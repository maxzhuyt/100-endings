# The Palindrome

## I.

The map had been taped together so many times it no longer lay flat on its own. Mirren used a coffee mug on one end and the old cast-iron owl doorstop on the other, and still the paper humped and buckled along its seams, sixteen sheets of graph paper joined edge to edge with Scotch tape turned amber with age, the whole thing spanning the kitchen table and cantilevering off both sides. She sat before it in the particular posture of a woman who has sat this way many hundreds of times—elbows planted, reading glasses low on her nose, a felt-tip pen uncapped in her right hand though she had not written anything new in eleven days.

The timeline ran left to right. Year one through year eighty, her full actuarial expectation, which she had calculated once from public tables and then revised downward when her mother died of a stroke and revised upward when her father made it, astonishingly, to ninety-six. The current edge of the line—where the pen stopped, where the future began—sat at year seventy-eight, three-quarters of the way across the rightmost sheet.

On the left half, in black ink: the events of her first four decades, each noted with a brief annotation and a small perpendicular hash mark. On the right half, in blue: the mirror.

She had discovered the first correspondence by accident, in her late fifties, sitting in a hospital waiting room while her second husband underwent a surgery that would reveal nothing wrong with him, a surgery she would later understand was *itself* the mirror—an echo of the surgery her mother had undergone when Mirren was twenty-two, the one that *had* found something, the one that had started the long, irreversible cascade of her mother's dying. Twenty-two and fifty-eight. She had been reading a magazine and the thought had arrived with the quality of a bell struck in an empty church, clear and sourceless and still ringing long after whatever had struck it was gone. She had written the two dates on the back of a parking receipt. She had put the receipt in her wallet. She had gone home and, that night, unable to sleep, had taken out the receipt and added a second pair.

House fire at seventeen. House fire at sixty-three.

The first fire had started in the basement of the house on Elm Terrace, an electrical fault in wiring her father had done himself because he did not trust contractors and did not have money for them anyway. She had been upstairs, asleep, and had woken to a sound she later understood was the window in her bedroom fracturing from heat differential—the glass simply sighing and falling inward, admitting a November wind that carried, absurdly, the scent of the neighbor's woodsmoke. No one was hurt. The damage was largely structural.

The second fire had started in the attic of the house on Calder Street—the house she and Gerald owned by then, the house where the children had been raised and left from—and it was also electrical, also wiring, also done by a man who did not trust contractors, though Gerald's reason was pride rather than poverty. She had been downstairs, awake, and had heard a sound she recognized instantly across four and a half decades as glass surrendering to heat. A bedroom window. Not hers—the guest room, the one that had been Lena's, the one they never called Lena's anymore. No one was hurt. The damage was largely structural. The fire investigator used the word *origin* four times in his report, and each time Mirren heard it the way you hear a word in a foreign language that sounds like a word in your own but means something entirely different.

From those two pairs she had built outward, and the map had grown the way coral grows—slowly, sessile, accreting structure without seeming to move. She worked on it the way other women her age worked on quilts or genealogies, which is to say compulsively and in secret and with a dedication that occasionally frightened her. The correspondences multiplied. They were, at first, easy to dismiss individually—the kinds of coincidences any long life accumulates—but they were not individual. They were *patterned*. They fell on either side of a central axis with a regularity that was not statistical but *geometric*, as though her life were a sentence being read forward and backward simultaneously and yielding, impossibly, the same meaning in both directions.

Her brother Patrick born when she was three. Patrick dead—a fall from scaffolding on a construction site outside of Cork—when she was seventy-seven.

Her first kiss, age fifteen, behind the equipment shed at the recreation center, a boy named Daniel Vos whose hands had tasted of copper when she put his fingers in her mouth. At sixty-five, in the parking lot of a Unitarian church after a grief support group she attended exactly once, a man named David Vosten had kissed her with such unexpected gentleness that she had pulled away not from displeasure but from *recognition*, the sudden sense that she had been here before, that her mouth knew this mouth, that the copper taste was something her body had been waiting for without her knowledge or consent.

The Tuesday the elm fell through the nursery ceiling—she had been four, asleep in the room, and the tree had missed the crib by less than a foot, and her mother had carried her out through plaster dust that hung in the air like flour, and for years afterward Mirren could not pass a bakery without flinching. At seventy-six, a storm had brought down the oak in Gerald's garden—Gerald, by then, had been dead three years, but she still thought of it as Gerald's garden the way she thought of certain drawers as Lena's drawers—and it had fallen through the roof of the room that had been the nursery, the room they never called the nursery, and the plaster dust had risen in the dawn light with such precise, such *faithful* resemblance to her earliest memory that she had stood in the hallway and wept. Not from sadness. From confirmation. From the terrible relief of no longer being able to pretend she was imagining it.

The correspondences were annotated in colored inks: black for the left side, blue for the right, and, connecting each pair, a line in green that arced over the top of the timeline like the curve of a bridge spanning a river. There were forty-one bridges. Forty-one pairs. She had used red ink only once, for the circle she had drawn around the midpoint year.

The midpoint was not precisely a year. It was a date she had calculated by dividing her projected lifespan into two equal halves and identifying the week, then the day, then—in a fit of numerological fervor she recognized as unhinged even as she indulged it—the hour that divided the halves. The red circle sat near the center of the eighth sheet, the paper there worn thin from handling, the ink of the circle itself almost embossed into the grain from the pressure of the pen. It was the only mark on the map that had been made in a single sitting, in a single gesture, without revision.

She had not yet reached it.

The midpoint was six weeks away. Six weeks and two days. She had taped a small calendar to the refrigerator and crossed off each day the way prisoners in films cross off days on cell walls, which was not, she understood, merely a simile but a statement of condition.

It was a Wednesday in October, seven in the morning, the kitchen dim because the overhead light had burned out and Mirren had not replaced it because she had stopped maintaining the house with any seriousness, directing her maintenance instead toward the map, which she tended the way medieval monks tended illuminated manuscripts—with reverence, anxiety, and the unshakable conviction that the thing she was preserving was more real than the room that contained it. The coffee was cold. The owl doorstop stared at her with its brass eyes.

She looked at the map. She looked at the red circle. She looked at the space around it, which was blank—annotated on the left with the event it mirrored, annotated on the right with nothing, because the corresponding event had not yet occurred. She knew what the left side said. She had known for years. It was the correspondence she had been unable to write on the right side. The one she had left blank not out of ignorance but out of the hope that if she did not write it, it would not come.

On the left side of the timeline, at the exact mirror-distance from the midpoint, in black ink, in handwriting that had been steadier then:

*Lena. 11 months. November 14.*

## II.

Lena had died on a Tuesday. Mirren remembered this because she had been hanging laundry when the sound came from the nursery—not a sound, in fact, but an *absence* of sound, the sudden void where breathing had been, a silence so specific and so shaped that she had known what it meant before she dropped the sheet and ran. Lena was in the crib on her back. Her mouth was slightly open. Her fists were closed. She was eleven months and six days old. The paramedics came and worked on her with a professionalism that Mirren later understood was kindness of a particular, structured sort—they had known she was dead, they had known for some time she was dead, and they continued working so that Mirren could watch them try and could later tell herself that everything possible had been done. The coroner wrote *Sudden Infant Death Syndrome* on the certificate, which is a medical way of saying: we have named this so that the silence has a shape, so that the absence of an explanation can itself function as an explanation, so that you have a word to put in the blank.

Mirren had carried that word for forty years. She had buried a daughter and divorced a husband and married another husband and buried him too and grown old around the word the way a tree grows around a nail—not healing, exactly, but enclosing.

Then her living daughter, Claire—Lena's older sister, who had been three when Lena died and who remembered nothing of it and who had built a life that was, in all visible respects, unharrowed by the loss she was too young to have registered—had a daughter of her own. And named her Lena.

Claire had meant it as a gift. "We want her to carry the name forward," she had said at the hospital, holding the infant, radiant with the particular confidence of a woman who believes she has performed an act of healing. "We want it to be a living name again." Mirren had held the baby and felt two things at once: a grandmother's reflexive, annihilating love, and a chill so specific it occupied a single vertebra at the base of her spine, a cold that did not spread but *waited*.

The baby was now ten months old.

Mirren sat across from Claire at Claire's kitchen table, which was white and modern and unburdened by paper. The kitchen was bright. There was a bowl of clementines on the counter. The baby was napping. The monitor on the table emitted the soft, rhythmic static of an infant's breathing, and Mirren watched the green light pulse with each breath the way a person watches a candle in a draft.

"I need to talk to you about something," Mirren said.

"Okay," Claire said, in the voice of a daughter who has learned, over many years, to modulate her tone when her mother begins a sentence this way.

"You need to take the baby away from here. You need to go somewhere I've never been."

Claire did not reply immediately. She wrapped her hands around her mug and looked at Mirren with an expression that was worse than disbelief—it was *recognition*. Not recognition of the truth of what Mirren was saying but recognition of a pattern Claire had seen before: her mother's obsession spiraling inward, her mother's map consuming another room, another year, another relationship.

"Mom."

"I know how it sounds."

"You don't, actually. You don't know how it sounds because you're inside it."

"I've been inside it for forty years, Claire. Being inside it is the only way to see it."

"See *what*?"

And here, Mirren faced the chasm. She could describe the map. She had described the map before. She could lay out the correspondences, name the dates, draw the bridges in the air between them. She had done this. Claire had listened politely and then changed the subject, or listened impolitely and said the things doctors say—*pattern recognition is a cognitive bias, Mom; apophenia, Mom; the brain finds shape in noise, it's what brains* do—and Mirren had absorbed these objections the way she absorbed everything, by noting them on the map and finding, inevitably, that they too had mirrors.

"The midpoint is November fourteenth," Mirren said.

Claire's face changed. Not because she understood but because she recognized the date—every member of the family recognized the date, it lived in them the way a scar lives in skin, part of the body's permanent text.

"Mom, *don't*."

"The baby will be eleven months and six days old on November fourteenth."

"Stop."

"I know you think I'm—"

"I think you're *grieving*," Claire said, and her voice cracked on the word in a way that told Mirren the crack had been there for a long time, that Claire carried her own version of the word *Lena* the way Mirren carried hers, except that Claire's version was a name she had chosen for her living daughter in a conscious act of reclamation that she could not, would not, allow her mother to poison with pattern and dread. "I think you have been grieving for forty years and you have turned your grief into a system because systems feel like control, and I love you, and I am not taking my daughter away from her home because your system says a date."

They looked at each other across the white table. The clementines sat in their bowl. The monitor pulsed green.

"You need to go somewhere I've never been," Mirren said again, quietly.

Claire stood. She picked up her mug and carried it to the sink and rinsed it with a thoroughness that was itself a communication—a way of performing normalcy, of insisting that the world was solid, explicable, and not mirrored.

"I think you should talk to Dr. Ashworth again," Claire said, with her back to her mother. "I think you should tell her about the map."

"She's seen the map."

"Then you should let her *help you with* the map."

Mirren looked at the baby monitor. The green light pulsed. She counted four pulses, then five, then six. The light did not waver.

She left. Not the city. Claire had left the conversation long before Mirren left the kitchen—had departed the shared space of mutual intelligibility the way you depart a room that has become too hot, not by walking out but by ceasing to breathe its air. Mirren drove home in her car, which was old and which she drove slowly, and she parked in the driveway and she sat in the dark for a long time, and then she went inside and stood at the kitchen table and looked at the map and looked at the red circle and looked at the blank space on the right side of the timeline where the mirror-event had not yet been annotated, and she picked up a blue pen and wrote, for the first time:

*Lena. ?*

Then she crossed it out. Then she wrote it again. Then she put the pen down and went to the telephone.

She did not call Claire. She called a number she had found three years ago in a footnote in an article in a journal she had accessed through the county library's online database, an article titled "Recursive Temporal Structures in Autobiographical Cognition: Four Case Studies," published in a journal so obscure it had ceased to exist the year after publication. The footnote read: *For correspondence regarding the Vásquez-Trotter Conjecture, contact Dr. Emil Brandt, emeritus, care of Chesterton Hospice, Chesterton, MA.*
