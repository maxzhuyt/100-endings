# The Future Language

## I.

The laptop was on the kitchen table where the laptop always was, next to the place where the coffee ring had stained the wood in a perfect overlapping sequence, like a Venn diagram of every morning she had failed to use a coaster. Elena Marconi opened it and closed it three times before she opened it the fourth time and pressed play.

Her mother's voice filled the kitchen.

It came out in a long, unbroken pour—syllables tumbling over each other with a cadence that rose and fell like someone explaining something vital to a very slow child. There were clicks in it. There were vowels that pooled and elongated. There were moments where the pitch dropped into a register her mother had never used in English, a basement note, almost ceremonial, before the stream picked up again and rushed onward. Eleven seconds. Elena pressed pause.

She sat with her hands flat on the table on either side of the laptop and breathed in through her nose, out through her mouth, the way the therapist had taught her to do when the thing she was feeling had no top to it, when it was just a shaft that went down and down. The therapist had a phrase for this. Bottomless affect. Elena had almost laughed the first time. She studied language for a living. She knew what *bottomless* meant. She also knew what *affect* meant. Putting them together like that was like saying *infinite sadness*, which was like saying nothing at all, which was, she supposed, the point. Some things could only be described by the failure of their description.

Her mother had been dead for nineteen days.

Gianna Marconi. Seventy-eight years old. Cause of death: aspiration pneumonia, secondary to advanced frontotemporal dementia, which had been doing its slow and total work for seven years, or eleven years, depending on when you started counting. If you started counting from the diagnosis, it was seven. If you started counting from the afternoon Elena had called home from a conference in Reykjavík and her mother had answered the phone and said, brightly, *I'm sorry, who is this?*—not with the polite confusion of someone who hadn't recognized a voice, but with the genuine, bottomless curiosity of someone who had never heard a telephone before and was delighted by the concept—then it was eleven.

The archive was on a shared drive. 2,481 files. Audio and video, ranging from eleven seconds to four hours and six minutes. The earliest was dated six years ago; the most recent, nine days before the death. Facility staff had recorded most of them. There was a memo Elena had found in the paperwork, between the insurance forms and the incident reports: *Resident in room 14 exhibits continuous vocalizations—does not appear distressed—not responsive to redirection—recording for clinical review.* Clinical review had concluded what clinical review always concluded: perseverative vocalization consistent with advanced neurodegenerative disease. The neurologist had a phrase too. *Non-meaningful verbal output.* Elena, who had spent twenty-two years studying the structures by which the human animal makes meaning out of sound, had read that phrase in the chart and thought: *Yes. I know. She is beyond meaning now.*

She also knew, in the part of her that was not a linguist and not a professional and not a person with coping strategies and a therapist and a system for organizing her grief into manageable units, that whenever she had sat beside her mother's bed in the facility and her mother had turned to her and unleashed that ceaseless, alien sound—urgent, rhythmic, with the unmistakable prosody of someone who *needs you to understand*—she had not been able to keep herself from searching her mother's eyes for the message. Every time. Even at the end, when the neurologist and the speech pathologist and the hospice counselor had all explained, with overwhelming patience, that there was no message. That the vocalizations were to language what the grasping reflex of a dying hand was to intention: the body's echo of a pattern long since emptied of its cause.

Elena had nodded. She had signed the DNR. She had held her mother's hand and listened to the sounds and said, *It's okay, Mom. I'm here. You don't have to explain anything to me.*

In the kitchen, with the coffee rings and the laptop, she pressed play again. Her mother's voice cascaded out of the speakers for eleven seconds. Elena listened to all of it. Then she closed the laptop and poured three fingers of bourbon into a mug that said *World's Okayest Syntactician*, which her graduate student Tomás had given her as a birthday present two years ago, which was the last birthday her mother had been alive for, which her mother had not, by that point, been aware of.

She drank the bourbon. She washed the mug. She went to bed in the clothes she was wearing. She lay in the dark and listened to the silence of a house that had once had two voices in it—first her own and David's, then just her own, then no one's, because she had spent the last year and a half going to the facility every evening and coming home only to sleep. The house had the particular silence of a place that has given up on being occupied. She listened to it for a long time.

She was not listening professionally. She was not listening at all. She was just a woman in the dark with the specific gravity of her loss pressing her flat against the mattress, and if she heard her mother's voice still running in the back of her skull—those liquid syllable chains, that impossible music—it was only because the brain replays what it cannot resolve, which is what the therapist would say, what the neurologist would say, which is what everyone would say, because what other explanation was there, and why would she, of all people, go looking for one.

## II.

She went back to the archive on a Tuesday. It was five weeks after the funeral, which had been attended by thirty-one people, which was fewer than Elena had expected and exactly the right number, because her mother's world had been contracting for years before the disease had even started, and by the end the woman's entire universe had been a single room with a window that faced a parking lot. Thirty-one was generous. Thirty-one was a small miracle.

Elena did not think of what she was doing as analysis. She thought of it as accompaniment. She had always worked with voices—she had spent a decade recording speakers of endangered languages in northeast Brazil and central Sulawesi, sitting across from elders in kitchens not unlike her own, capturing sounds that would outlive the mouths that made them. She knew what it meant to listen to a voice that had nowhere left to go. So she sat at the kitchen table with coffee instead of bourbon and she played the files sequentially, starting from the earliest, and she let her mother's voice fill the room as a kind of company.

The sound was different when you listened in sequence. She noticed this the way you notice a shift in the wind—not as a thought but as a sensation on the skin. In the earliest recordings, her mother's voice had a quality Elena could only describe as *searching*: halting, with long pauses, as if the woman were reaching for each sound individually, pulling it from some deep and uncertain place. By the recordings from two years later, the voice was fluid. By the final year, it was *torrential*. It moved with the speed and confidence of someone who had found their idiom and could not stop using it.

This was not what deterioration sounded like.

Elena knew what deterioration sounded like. She had studied aphasia at McGill before her interests shifted to fieldwork. She had sat through hundreds of hours of clinical recordings—Broca's aphasia with its effortful telegraphic fragments, Wernicke's with its fluent, meaningless, cheerfully unmoored output, the anomias and paraphasias and circumlocutions that map, with terrible precision, the landscape of a brain losing its grip on the lexicon. She knew the signatures. Wernicke's was the closest analogue to what her mother produced—fluent, phonologically complex, seemingly unconcerned with whether the listener understood. But Wernicke's output had a specific acoustic profile: it borrowed from the patient's native phonemic inventory, distorting real words into jargon through predictable substitution patterns. The underlying skeleton was always the first language, warped but recognizable, the way a funhouse mirror still shows you your own face.

Her mother's output did not sound like a funhouse mirror. It did not sound like Italian, distorted. It did not sound like English, distorted. It did not sound like anything Elena had a frame for, and she had spent her career building frames.

She was on file 0047—a four-minute recording from a Wednesday afternoon in March, three years before the death—when she noticed the first thing she could not explain. Her mother was speaking rapidly, and there was a sound she kept returning to: a voiceless velar fricative followed by a high front rounded vowel, something like /xy/, a combination that existed in no language her mother had ever spoken. Elena had heard this sound in Mandarin and Turkish; she had heard it in the Papuan languages she'd studied in graduate school. She had never heard it from a woman raised in Calabria who had spoken Italian and English her whole life and nothing else.

She rewound. Played it again. There it was: /xy/. And not once—she counted seven instances in four minutes, each one produced cleanly, with the casual precision of a native speaker.

She got up. She made more coffee. She sat down again. She told herself: the brain is creative in its collapse. Novel phonologies emerge in jargon aphasia. This was documented. This was known.

She played another file. File 0193, from fourteen months later. She listened for the /xy/. She found it within fifty seconds. Seven instances per four minutes in the early file; in this one, she counted twelve in three minutes. The sound was more frequent, but that was not what caught her. What caught her was that the /xy/ was now appearing in what sounded like derivational contexts—attached to different roots, modified by what might be suffixes. It was behaving the way a phoneme behaves in a living system: combinatorially, productively, as part of a generative grammar.

She put her coffee down. She picked up a pen and reached for the nearest piece of paper, which was the back of a condolence card from the department. She began to chart. She moved through file 0193 in thirty-second increments, pausing, rewinding, transcribing in IPA. After an hour, she had a preliminary inventory. She counted the symbols on the card.

Twenty-six distinct sounds. No. Twenty-seven. She had missed a retroflex. Twenty-seven distinct sounds, organized into what appeared to be a system: a set of stops, a set of fricatives, a set of nasals, three lateral approximants, and a vowel space that was larger than Italian and differently shaped than English. She had done this hundreds of times in the field—the first pass, the rough phoneme chart, the thrill of a new inventory emerging from raw audio. Her hands knew this work the way a carpenter's hands know wood.

She stopped. She put the pen down. She put her hands in her lap and sat very still.

This was not what she was supposed to be doing. She was supposed to be mourning. She was supposed to be letting her mother's voice wash over her as a last act of proximity, the way the grief books said to do, the way the therapist said to do, the way every well-meaning colleague who had sent flowers and casseroles said to do. She was not supposed to be transcribing her dead mother's dementia babble into the International Phonetic Alphabet on the back of a sympathy card.

And yet.

She picked the pen up. She pulled up file 0004—one of the earliest recordings, from just months after the vocalizations began. She listened and charted.

Twenty-one sounds.

She pulled up file 0412—one of the latest. She listened and charted.

Thirty-one sounds.

She lined the three inventories up in front of her. 0004: twenty-one. 0193: twenty-seven. 0412: thirty-one. The phonemic inventory had *expanded*. Over the course of years, new sounds had been introduced, each one integrated into the existing system, each one participating in the same combinatorial logic. This was not what happened in any model of aphasic deterioration Elena had ever studied. In aphasia, the inventory contracted. The brain shed its rarest and most complex articulations first and retreated toward the core. This was basic. This was *textbook*.

Her mother's vocalizations were going the other direction.

Elena stared at the three inventories on the condolence card and felt something she had not felt in so long she almost didn't recognize it: the cold, electric flicker of not understanding. Not the grief-based incomprehension of the past year, the *I don't understand why this is happening to her*. Something older, cleaner, more dangerous. The disciplinary vertigo of data that should not exist.

She closed the laptop. She put the card in a drawer. She went to bed.

She did not sleep. At 3 A.M. she got up and opened the drawer and looked at the card again. The inventories were still there. They were still wrong.

She left the card on the table where she would see it in the morning and went back to bed and lay in the dark and did not think the thought that was waiting to be thought, because she was a serious person, a tenured professor, a careful scholar, and the thought was not careful, was not serious, was the kind of thought that ended careers and concerned friends and confirmed the suspicion that grief had finally done what grief does when you let it run too long without intervention.

The thought waited. It was patient. It had nowhere to go.

## III.

She took a leave of absence from the university. She told the chair it was for bereavement, and this was true in a way that contained so many other truths inside it that she could say it without any sense of dishonesty.

She converted the living room into a workspace. She moved the couch against the wall. On the long table where she and David used to eat dinner before David, she set up two monitors, a speaker system, a backup hard drive, and a legal pad that would eventually become seventeen legal pads. She printed out the IPA vowel chart and the IPA consonant chart and taped them to the wall. She opened a fresh spreadsheet. She titled it *GM Phonemic Inventory — Complete.* She did not title it *Mom.* She could not afford to title it *Mom*. If she thought of the voice as her mother's, she would hear only the loss, and she needed to hear the structure.

She began with the phonology because the phonology was safest—the farthest from meaning, the most amenable to formal analysis. She listened to every file. She transcribed 342 hours of continuous speech into IPA notation. It took her six weeks. She lost nine pounds. She developed a tremor in her right hand from writing, which she ignored, and a ringing in her left ear from the headphones, which she treated by switching ears. Tomás, her graduate student, emailed twice to ask if she was all right. She responded to neither email. Her sister, Angela, called on Sundays. Elena answered and said she was fine and asked about the kids and hung up and went back to the recordings.

The phonemic inventory, fully mapped, contained thirty-one distinct consonants and fourteen vowels, with two tones—high and low—that appeared to carry morphological rather than lexical function. The consonant system was typologically unusual but not impossible: it combined the retroflex series common in South Asian and Australian languages with the click consonants of southern African languages and the voiceless lateral fricative found in Welsh and Nahuatl. No known natural language combined all of these features. But every feature, individually, was attested. The system was not *alien*. It was *convergent*—as if it had drawn from a wider range of human articulatory possibilities than any single language lineage had yet produced.

She moved to morphology. This took longer. A phonemic inventory is a list; morphology is a logic. She was looking for the rules by which sounds combined into units of meaning, and she was doing it without any semantic ground truth—without a single confirmed word-meaning pair. She was working blind, by distributional analysis alone, the way cryptographers break codes: by looking at which elements appear together, in what orders, with what frequencies, and inferring structure from pattern.

The patterns were there.

She identified fourteen prefixes and nine suffixes. She identified three infixes—sound units inserted into the middle of a root—which was cross-linguistically rare but richly attested in Austronesian languages. She found compounding rules that were agglutinative in some domains and fusional in others, which reminded her simultaneously of Turkish and Latin, a combination that should have been contradictory and was not. She found reduplication—partial and full—used in ways she tentatively coded as iterative aspect, meaning the marking of repeated or ongoing action. She found what she believed were case markers, at least six, possibly eight, with a distinction between witnessed and unwitnessed evidence that reminded her of the evidentiality systems in Quechua and Tibetan.
