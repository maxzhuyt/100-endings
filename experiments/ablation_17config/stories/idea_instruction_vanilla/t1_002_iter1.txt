# Every Mind You Enter

## I. The Mop Knows Things

The mop has a name. It is embossed on the handle in letters worn to Braille by three decades of Palmer Briggs's grip: *Libman Wonder Mop, Model 2001*. Palmer calls it nothing because Palmer calls nothing anything, has not called anything anything since the morning in 1994 when he opened his mouth to say *coffee, black* at a Waffle House in Meridian, Mississippi, and the counter stool beside him rose six inches off the floor and hung there, trembling, until the waitress screamed and Palmer's jaw locked shut around the word *black* and never opened again.

He mops.

He has mopped the east corridor and the west corridor and the sub-basement training suite they call the Acre, though it is not an acre, it is four thousand square feet of reinforced concrete overlaid with holographic emitters that cost, Palmer estimates from the invoices he has swept around, somewhere north of eleven million dollars. He has mopped Rorschach-shapes of blood and bile and once a carbon-black stain that smelled of ozone and was all that remained of a boy named Keith Somebody who had tried to teleport through six inches of load-bearing wall. Palmer mopped Keith Somebody into a dustpan. He mops.

This morning — a Tuesday in March, the light through the clerestory windows the color of old milk — Palmer pushes his bucket down the east corridor and feels, in the particular way he feels things, the thirteen minds distributed through the building's three floors. He does not read them. He is not that. He is the other thing — the thing that moves objects, that could, if he unclenched whatever he has kept clenched for thirty years, peel this building like a tangerine. He feels minds the way a man standing in a river feels the fish: not their thoughts but their *displacement*, the weight they exert on the medium of the world.

Twelve of the thirteen minds are in motion. Dressing. Brushing teeth. One is performing what Palmer recognizes, from displacement pattern alone, as a covert series of push-ups beside a bunk — the desperate calisthenics of a teenager who has been told he can shatter concrete with his voice but cannot do a single pull-up in front of his peers.

The thirteenth mind is still.

Palmer has felt this particular stillness before. He felt it in 2005 with the Yoon boy, and in 2011 with the Chastain girl, and in 2019 with the twins from Baton Rouge whose last name he never learned because no one at the Waring Academy for Exceptional Development wears last names where Palmer can see them. The stillness means: *today they go into the Acre*.

Palmer dips the mop. Wrings it. The floor accepts the water and gives back a dim reflection of his face — sixty-two years old, the color of walnut heartwood, eyes that have witnessed nine cohorts of gifted prodigies enter the program and seven-point-five of those cohorts leave it burned out, blanked, or voluntarily sedated into a life of numbed mediocrity, and the remaining one-point-five leave it functional but altered in ways that make them, in Palmer's silent estimation, parsing the word as carefully as a man can parse a word he will never say aloud, *worse*.

He mops toward the thirteenth mind.

## II. The Founder's Teeth

Dr. Josiah Waring has the teeth of a man who has been smiling for forty years without interruption. They are white. They are numerous. Palmer has sometimes felt, in the telekinetic sense, the small porcelain bridges that anchor the ones Waring lost to a stroke in 2016 — a stroke Waring never disclosed to his staff, which Palmer knows only because he felt, that November afternoon, three of Waring's teeth hit the bathroom floor, and then felt Waring's hand reach down and collect them with the calm of a man picking up loose change.

Waring is in his office now. Palmer feels him there — the particular density of a mind that has trained itself to operate in layers, the outermost layer a bright and readable stream of benevolence, the layers beneath it packed and dark as shale. Palmer has mopped past that office door eleven thousand times. He has never once felt Waring's mind shift in response to his proximity. To Josiah Waring, Palmer is furniture. Palmer is the mop. This is, Palmer understands, the usual arrangement between men who move things with their minds and men who move *people* with theirs, and if there is bitterness in the understanding, it is old bitterness, well-mopped, faded to the faintest watermark on the linoleum of Palmer's interior life.

The door to the Acre is propped open. Palmer can see, from the corridor, the holographic emitters cycling through their boot sequence — soft, tessellated light crawling across the concrete in geometric patterns that suggest, if you squint, a cityscape. Palmer does not squint. He has seen a thousand cityscapes blossom and burn in the Acre. He has mopped the aftermath of every one.

He feels them assembling. Six minds converging on the sub-basement stairwell, each one trailing its own atmospheric signature of anxiety: one stomach clenching, one knee bouncing, one mind rehearsing, over and over, a breathing technique it learned from a YouTube video it is not supposed to have accessed on the Academy's restricted network.

And one mind — the thirteenth, the still one — descending.

Dahlia Voss.

Palmer has read her file. Not psychically — literally. He read it off Waring's desk the way he reads everything in this building: by waiting until the room is empty and the document is lying face-up and his eyes, which are his own, which require no gift, do the rest. He knows: age seventeen. Telepath, class unrestricted. First manifestation at nine. Read her mother's mind during a custody hearing and announced, in open court, that her mother was thinking about the Safeway cashier with the beard. Read her father's mind and found only a white static she later understood was the sound of a man thinking about nothing because he had practiced, for years, thinking about nothing whenever his daughter entered the room.

Waring's handwriting in the margin, in green ink Palmer has come to associate with the founder's highest enthusiasm: *She hears everything. Let's find out what that costs.*

Palmer mops around the door frame. He does not enter the Acre. He has never entered the Acre. He is custodial staff. But the linoleum in the corridor outside the Acre is, by a convenient coincidence, always filthy.

## III. Simulation One: The City

The emitters build a city. Palmer can feel their heat through the doorway — the particular warmth of focused light constructing, from nothing, a four-block radius of simulated urban environment: brownstones, mailboxes, a bodega with a flickering Open sign, parked cars whose license plates are algorithmically generated and therefore, Palmer has noticed over the years, occasionally obscene.

The six students take positions. Palmer feels them spread across the Acre's floor — their minds bright, dense, afraid. He does not need to see what Waring's briefing screen shows. He has watched this simulation unfold in eighteen variants across nine cohorts. The scenario is always some version of: *hostage. Extract. Time limit. Complication.* The complication is always the same. The complication is always the other students.

Dahlia stands near the door. Palmer is eleven feet from her. He can see, past the mop handle, the edge of her profile — dark hair sheared close on the left, falling long on the right, a topology of asymmetry that Palmer suspects she chose because it gives her one exposed ear and one curtained ear and she has learned, in her seventeen years, that the symbolic architecture of listening matters to her the way breathing matters to a diver, that you must have a way to close the channel or the channel closes you.

The simulation starts. Palmer feels it in the emitters' frequency shift, in the students' collective intake of breath, in the way the holographic city snaps from translucent to opaque and the Acre's concrete floor becomes, to every eye inside, wet asphalt.

He mops.

Six minutes in, Dahlia's knees buckle.

Palmer knows why. He has watched this part. The simulation is designed to be unsolvable at the tactical level. The hostage cannot be reached through the building's front. The building's rear is guarded by a simulated threat that adapts to each student's specific ability. The rooftop access requires flight, and no one in this cohort flies. The only path forward is coordination — a precise, interlocking sequence of actions that requires each student to trust the others' timing absolutely, the way a flock of starlings trusts the air.

And Dahlia, because she is what she is, takes the shortcut: she reaches into her teammates' minds to synchronize them.

Palmer does not feel thoughts. He feels displacement. But he can read the displacement now the way a seismologist reads a needle: the sudden spill of Dahlia's mind outward, the expanding radius of her attention as she opens herself to five other consciousnesses simultaneously, and then — the stagger. The buckle. The hitch in her posture that Palmer has seen before, in the Yoon boy, in the Chastain girl, in every telepath Waring has ever put through this machine.

She is finding out what is inside them.

Palmer imagines it. He has had thirty years to imagine it. You open a door expecting a war room — strategies, courage, the focused intent of young people on a mission — and you find instead: a half-remembered jingle for a breakfast cereal. A fantasy about a girl on the third floor. A festering resentment over who was assigned the bottom bunk. The irreducible, humbling, *stupid* banality of the human mind at any given moment, the way even the most gifted brain is, at its fundament, a monkey in a room full of typewriters, and the monkey is not writing Shakespeare, the monkey is thinking about a horse in a necktie.

Palmer knows about the horse in the necktie. He knows because the student who thinks it — a pyrokinetic named Ellis, seventeen, with an arm-span like an albatross and an IQ that Palmer, from the test scores left face-up on Waring's desk, knows to be 151 — once drew the image on a napkin in the cafeteria and left the napkin on his tray, which Palmer collected. A horse. In a necktie. Windsor knot. The horse is smiling. There is no context. It is the kind of image that the human brain produces the way a radiator produces heat — involuntarily, purposelessly, a byproduct of the mechanical fact of being a mind with nothing better to do at that particular synapse-firing than concatenate *horse* and *necktie* and label the result *funny*.

This is what Dahlia has opened herself to.

She is on her knees now. Palmer can see her through the doorway — a silhouette against the holographic cityscape, head bowed, one hand on the simulated asphalt. The other students are still running the scenario. They have not noticed her. The hostage countdown is at three minutes and falling. Somewhere in the fake city a fake threat is advancing with fake menace, and no one has looked back to see that their telepath is kneeling in the street, not because she has been struck but because she has heard the horse.

Waring's voice comes over the intercom — the only spoken words in the Acre, and Palmer catches them not as language but as vibration, as the particular frequency of Waring's vocal cords through the building's steel infrastructure. He does not process the content. He processes the *tone*, which is the tone Waring always uses at this moment: warm, encouraging, calibrated to extend the exercise ninety seconds past the breaking point.

The simulation ends. The city dissolves. The emitters cycle down. Six students stand in the Acre's concrete emptiness, breathing hard, and Dahlia is still on her knees with a thread of blood descending from her left nostril.

Palmer dips the mop.

He will be needed shortly.

## IV. Between Simulations

The blood on linoleum. Palmer studies it while he mops. It has fallen in a pattern he has seen before — the specific Rorschach of a telepath's nosebleed, which differs from a regular nosebleed the way a river delta differs from a spilled glass. The blood branches. It reaches. It maps the shape of the effort that produced it, and Palmer, who has mopped nine cohorts' worth of these maps, can read in the rivulets and archipelagoes a precise record of what happened inside Dahlia Voss's skull: the initial extension, the recoil, the secondary push, the collapse.

He mops it away. He always mops it away. The linoleum keeps no record. This is, Palmer sometimes thinks, the function he serves: erasing the evidence of what this place does to people. If he were a different man — a man with a voice, a man with standing, a man who had not spent thirty years making himself into furniture — he might object. He might intervene. He might, in a moment of compressed fury, use the gift he has clenched for three decades to rearrange the Acre's emitters into a sculpture of his displeasure.

He mops.

Dahlia is in the corridor. She is sitting against the wall nine feet from where Palmer is working, knees drawn up, hair curtaining her face on the right side, exposed ear turned toward the empty hallway as though listening for something she hopes not to hear. There is blood on her upper lip. She has not wiped it. Palmer knows this gesture, too — has seen it in every telepathic student Waring has broken. The blood is a flag. It says: *Look what you did to me.* It says: *I am injured and the injury is visible.* It says, in the semaphore of the body, what the mouth cannot or will not say.

Palmer pushes his bucket past her. The wheels squeak. Dahlia does not look up. Palmer does not look down. This is their protocol — the protocol of the invisible and the overwhelmed, two people who have each, for different reasons, opted out of the transaction of being perceived.

But Palmer's hand, on the mop handle, makes a motion he has not planned. The mop pauses. Lifts a quarter-inch off the floor. Sets itself back down.

It is nothing. It is a tremor. It is the telekinetic equivalent of a caught breath, a stammer, a flinch — the body's involuntary acknowledgment of something the mind has decided not to address. Palmer tightens his grip. The mop stays down.

He has eleven more feet of corridor to mop before the second simulation begins.

He mops them.

## V. Simulation Two: The Labyrinth

Palmer has always understood that Waring designs his simulations the way a jeweler cuts a diamond: each facet precise, each angle calculated to direct the light toward a single, predetermined brilliance. The first simulation teaches the telepath that other minds are not what they expect. The second teaches them something worse.

The Acre reconfigures. Palmer feels the emitters shifting frequency — lower now, colder, producing not a city but a labyrinth, a lightless maze of corridors that Palmer recognizes, from the thermal signature alone, as Waring's adaptation of the Minotaur scenario. In this version there is no hostage. There is only the maze, and the five teammates scattered within it, and the telepath stationed at the center, and the rule that Palmer has seen printed on the briefing screen in fourteen-point Garamond: *Guide them out. You have their minds. Use them.*
