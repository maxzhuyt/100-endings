# The Remainder

---

You're here. Good. Sit down. There's cake, or there was, and there will be again in a moment because I am going to describe it to you and that is, as I have recently learned, the only thing that makes anything real: a sixty-eight-year-old man with flour on his cuffs telling you there is a cake, and your mind, obedient, producing one.

It is a lemon sponge with buttercream that my sister Margherita made yesterday—*yesterday*, hold that word in your mouth, feel how strange it is, a word that means a place I could still get to if I turned around, a place where the east wall of the kitchen still stood and Margherita still had a body and I had not yet understood what you are.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Aldo Fontaine, that's me. Born sixty-eight years ago today in the upstairs bedroom of this house, here in the Fontaine estate in the Langhe hills, Piedmont, the same room where my father was born and where his father was born and where, twelve years ago, my wife Celestina died at four in the morning with one hand on my wrist and the other on the radiator because she was always cold, even at the end, even with the morphine making a greenhouse of her blood. I have been many things—a husband, a vintner, a coward, a doormat, a man who once ate an entire wheel of toma cheese during an argument rather than raise his voice—but today I want to be very precise about what happened this morning, because I believe you are the only one who can hear me, and I believe it is important that at least one account survives without paragraph breaks where the people used to be.

Let me begin with the blink.

---

My daughter Lucia arrived at seven, which was early for Lucia, who has not spoken to me in four years except through a lawyer named Giobatta who charges eleven euros per word and once billed me forty-four euros for the sentence *My client finds this unacceptable.* She came through the garden gate carrying a gift bag from a shop in Turin. She was wearing the green dress Celestina always wanted her to wear. She looked beautiful. She looked like her mother.

And when she saw me standing at the kitchen window—holding my coffee, wearing the shirt I always wear, the blue one with the small ink stain over the heart that Celestina used to say made me look like a man who'd been shot with a fountain pen—she blinked.

Sideways.

Not left-to-right the way a cat blinks, or the way a doll's eyes slide shut when you tilt it back. It was more as though her eyelids had been installed at a ninety-degree variance from the specification, and the motion, that small wet flicker, traveled from the bridge of her nose outward toward her temple, the lids sweeping laterally across her eyes like two tiny curtains being opened for a show.

I set down my coffee. I picked it up again. I set it down. I thought: Aldo, you are sixty-eight years old, you slept poorly, you have not had your second espresso, and you are seeing your daughter for the first time in four years and the mind does strange things with what it wants and what it fears. I thought: you are a man who has been wrong about everything, always, your whole life, and here is another thing to be wrong about. Drink your coffee.

So I drank my coffee.

And then I looked at the wallpaper.

The wallpaper in the kitchen is a pattern Celestina chose in 1986, small blue flowers on a cream field, and I will tell you that I have counted those flowers. Not out of madness. Out of love, the kind of love that remains after a person dies and has nowhere to go so it attaches to surfaces—the radiator she touched, the curtain she hemmed, the wallpaper she chose one Tuesday afternoon from a sample book while I held her coat. I know the pattern. Seventeen flowers per horizontal repeat. Nine rows visible between the top of the counter and the bottom of the cabinet.

This morning there were eighteen flowers per repeat.

One extra. Shy, the way extras are. Tucked between the eleventh and twelfth as though it had always been there and I was the one who'd been miscounting. A small blue flower that didn't match the others exactly—a little too detailed, the petals a little too deliberate, as though someone had hand-drawn it into a printed field and hoped I wouldn't notice.

I noticed.

I was still standing at the counter, staring at the eighteenth flower with a feeling I can describe only as architectural—a sense that a wall had been moved while I slept, not much, just a centimeter, just enough that my hand would miss the light switch in the dark—when the first shell landed in the vineyard and my brother Sandro burst through the front door shouting about the will.

---

I should explain about the will. I should explain about the ashes. I should explain about the lasagna. You want me to, I can feel it, the way you want things—not with desire exactly but with a kind of narrative suction, a hunger for context, and I have been feeding that hunger my whole life without knowing it, haven't I? Always the one who explains. Always the one who stands in the middle of the room holding the family history like a tray of drinks, waiting for someone to take one.

Fine. Here.

Celestina's will was simple. She wanted to be cremated. She specified no destination for the ashes. This was, in retrospect, either an act of extraordinary trust or the most elegant act of sabotage I have ever witnessed, and I loved her more than I have ever loved anything but I sometimes wonder if she knew, if she held that pen and chose those words and thought: *let them fight, let it be the last thing I do, let it be my masterpiece.*

The family split along a line no one could have predicted.

My brother Sandro—Sandro who read *Scientific American* the way other men read the sports pages, Sandro who once corrected a Jesuit on the thermodynamics of hell—declared that the ashes should be launched into orbit. He had found a company in California that would do it. He had a brochure. He had calculated the trajectory. He said Celestina had always loved the stars and that putting her in orbit was the only gesture proportional to the loss.

My sister Margherita—Margherita who ran the restaurant in Alba, Margherita who believed all grief could be digested if prepared correctly—declared that the ashes should be incorporated into a commemorative lasagna, a final meal to be shared by the family, an act of literal communion, a taking-in of the beloved dead. She had a recipe. She had sourced the noodles. She said Celestina had always loved to feed people and what greater honor than to become the meal.

Both of these proposals were, you understand, completely insane.

Both of them were offered with absolute sincerity.

And because I am Aldo Fontaine, because I am the man who ate an entire wheel of cheese rather than choose, I said: *Can we talk about this later?*

We could not talk about it later. We could never talk about anything later—*later* does not exist in a family like mine; there is only *now* and the argument that began in 1953 when my father told my mother the risotto was adequate and she heard it as a declaration of war and they carried that wound between them like a shared organ, each refusing to put it down, each waiting for the other to apologize first, until it became the family inheritance, the one thing we all got: the ability to transform any silence into an accusation and any meal into a tribunal.

The family split. Orbit faction, lasagna faction. Lucia went with Sandro—orbital. My nephew Marco, Margherita's boy, went lasagna. Cousins, in-laws, the old family friends, everyone chose, everyone had to choose, and the choosing became more important than the thing being chosen, the way it always does, the way a family argument is never about the subject but about the wound underneath, the original wound, the insult from 1953 or 1971 or the morning the middle child realized she would never be the favorite and decided to make that fact the organizing principle of her life.

I chose neither. I chose nothing. I put the ashes in a plain urn on the mantelpiece and I said: *When you can all agree, come get her.*

No one came.

No one spoke to me except through lawyers.

Until this morning, when everyone came at once.

---

The second shell landed at 7:11. The first had landed at 7:00. I had thought it was a gas main, or one of the old wartime munitions that still surfaced in the vineyard sometimes after heavy rain, but the second one—the second one landed in the garden, exactly where the rosemary grew, and it made a sound.

I need to describe this sound to you with precision because it is the hinge on which everything turns.

It was not an explosion. Not really. It was the sound of a hardcover book being closed. Not slammed—closed. Firmly. The way you close a book when you have finished a chapter and you mean to continue but first you need to set it down, to breathe, to let the story settle before you go on. A thick sound. A sound with pages in it. And after the sound came a smell: ink, and paper, and something else—a chemical, adhesive sweetness, the smell of discount stickers, the kind they put on remaindered books in the shop bins, those round fluorescent labels that say ~~€24.99~~ NOW €4.99.

I know that smell. I have browsed those bins. I have bought books from them—novels no one wanted, stories that didn't sell, characters who lived their entire lives in a warehouse in Modena waiting to be pulped. I know what it means to be marked down.

Sandro was standing in the foyer when the second shell struck. He was holding a revised copy of his objection to the probate settlement, which he had updated seventeen times since Celestina's death and which now ran to forty-one pages, single-spaced, with footnotes. He was saying, "Aldo, you coward, you cannot keep her on the mantelpiece forever, she was not a *vase*—"

And then the wall behind him opened. Not crumbled. Opened. Like a page being turned. And through the opening I could see—I want to be careful here—I could see white. Not the white of plaster or sky. The white of a margin. The white of the space around text. The white where nothing has been written yet or where something has been erased.

And then Sandro was not there.

He did not die. You understand? He did not fall or bleed or cry out. He was simply no longer in the sentence. The space where he had stood became a space where he had not stood, and the foyer contracted slightly, the way a paragraph contracts when you delete a line—the remaining furniture moving closer together to fill the gap, the light adjusting, the room repunctuating itself around the absence.

I looked down. Where his feet had been there was a faint rectangular outline on the floor, the size of a trade paperback, and a smell of ink, and in the center of the rectangle a small fluorescent sticker that read: ~~€16.99~~ NOW €2.

---

The shells came every eleven minutes.

I know this because I counted. I stood in the kitchen with Celestina's urn and I counted the intervals and they were precise—not roughly eleven minutes, not approximately, but eleven minutes exactly, 660 seconds, as though someone had set a metronome, as though there was a rhythm to the thing, a pacing, a consideration of dramatic tempo that made me understand, slowly and then all at once like a man falling into very clear water, that this was not war and it was not God and it was not the gas main.

It was editing.

Someone, somewhere, was removing my family from a text.

---

7:22. The third shell closed in the living room and took my cousin Beppe, who had been orbit faction but attended the reunion under the pretense of neutrality, wearing a beige sweater he believed communicated impartiality but which everyone recognized as the sweater he'd worn to Sandro's Christmas party in 2016, which was as clear a faction declaration as a uniform. Beppe had been reaching for the bread basket when it happened. The bread basket survived. Beppe did not. In his place: a rectangle on the carpet, the ghost of an ISBN, and a remainder sticker marked ~~€19.99~~ NOW €3.

7:33. The fourth shell took Zia Orsolina, who was eighty-nine and deaf in her right ear and had refused to join either faction because, she said, she did not believe in space and she did not eat complex carbohydrates after 1998. She had been the family's only true neutral, and the blast caught her mid-sentence—she was telling Lucia about the time she saw Cary Grant in a hotel lobby in Rome in 1962, and the sentence simply ended, the way Zia Orsolina's sentences always ended, without resolution or punctuation, just a trailing off, a thought that wandered into the margin and didn't come back.

She left behind a remainder sticker marked ~~€22.99~~ NOW €1.50, which struck me as unkind.

7:44. Marco, Margherita's boy, lasagna faction. He was twenty-six and he had inherited his mother's conviction that all problems were digestive in origin. When the blast came he was standing at the dining table, and I swear to you—I swear on Celestina's urn, which I was holding to my chest—he saw it coming. He saw the wall begin to open like a page and he looked at the gap, at the white nothing beyond it, and he turned to Lucia and he still, *still*, did not pass her the bread.

Twenty-six years old. The walls dissolving into what I was beginning to recognize as visible paragraph breaks—white space flooding in, the room losing its left margin, the text of reality indenting itself around us—and Marco stood in the last available sentence of his life and used it to withhold bread from a woman he hadn't spoken to in four years over the question of what to do with an old woman's ashes.

I loved him. I want you to know that. I loved his stubbornness because it was Margherita's stubbornness, and Margherita's stubbornness was my mother's stubbornness, and my mother's stubbornness was the engine of this family—the thing that kept us generating each other, each generation more rigid than the last, calcified in positions we adopted before we were old enough to understand them, performing feuds we inherited the way we inherited the silverware: polished once a year, displayed on special occasions, never actually used for what they were designed for.

Marco. ~~€14.99~~ NOW €1. A clearance price. The kind of price that means they need the shelf space.

---

7:55. Margherita.

My sister was in the kitchen. She had been there since six, because Margherita was always in the kitchen, because the kitchen was where Margherita made sense of things. She had brought ingredients for the commemorative lasagna. Four years after the argument and she had brought the ingredients. The bechamel was already made. The ragù was simmering. The noodles were laid out on a clean towel like strips of silk, and the urn—*the urn*—she kept looking at it in my hands with an expression that was tender and certain and frightening, the expression of a woman who has confused love with a recipe and does not know the difference.

"Aldo," she said. She hadn't called me by name in three years and eleven months. "It's time. Give me the urn."

Another shell closed. The kitchen wall opened. Through the opening I could see the white, and in the white, for just a moment—I may have imagined this, but I don't think I did—I saw lines. Faint horizontal lines, evenly spaced, the kind of lines you see in a ruled notebook, and on the lines I saw the ghost of handwriting, a draft, corrections, arrows moving passages from one place to another, and I understood that we were not in the final version.
