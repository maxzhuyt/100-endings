# Optimal Placement

**Quarterly Home Visit Report — Foster Placement #2019-0347**
**Caseworker: Dana Reeves, MSW**
**Date of Visit: March 14**

---

I want to say I noticed something wrong the first time. I want to say there was a flicker, a wrongness in the air, the way people always claim in retrospect—*I knew, I just knew*—as though intuition were a professional credential and not the thing we spent four years of graduate school learning to distrust. But the truth is I walked into Ludmila Kask's house on a Tuesday in November and thought: *Finally.*

Finally a placement I wouldn't have to worry about.

The house was a two-story colonial on Harwick Lane, white with green shutters, the lawn edged with a precision that suggested either a landscaping service or a personality type. Mrs. Kask met me at the door. She was tall, fine-boned, somewhere in her late fifties, with silver hair pinned at the nape of her neck and the kind of posture that implied she had never once leaned against anything in her life. She wore a cardigan buttoned to the throat despite the heat she kept in the house, and her handshake was dry and calibrated—firm enough to communicate competence, brief enough to preclude warmth.

"Miss Reeves. You're three minutes early. I appreciate that."

She said this as though punctuality were a moral stance, and I remember thinking she was right.

The child—Lily Braden, age seven, removed from parental custody following what the file called a "pattern of environmental neglect," which is our way of saying the apartment had no food, no heat, and a mother who kept leaving—was sitting at the kitchen table doing a worksheet. Not a school worksheet. A worksheet Mrs. Kask had designed. It was titled **"My Feelings Today: A Self-Assessment"** and featured a grid where Lily could rate her emotional states on a scale of one to five, with anchoring descriptions for each number. A score of three on "Sadness," for instance, was defined as "Manageable. Responsive to approved coping strategy. Duration under forty minutes."

I remember picking it up and studying it. I remember thinking how thorough it was.

"She's adjusting beautifully," Mrs. Kask said, standing slightly behind me as I reviewed the document. "We had a minor attachment regression in week two—she asked to sleep with the light on, which I've logged—but I implemented the graduated exposure protocol from the Jensen-Whitfield framework, and we're seeing real improvement. I have the data if you'd like."

She had the data.

She had *all* the data.

The binder came out—three-ring, white, two inches thick, with laminated tabs in six colors. I'd been doing home visits for nine years. I'd seen foster parents who couldn't remember the names of the children they were housing. I'd seen refrigerators held shut with bungee cords. I'd stood in a living room in Brexton where a foster father had converted a closet into a bedroom and was genuinely offended when I cited him for it, because he'd installed a nightlight. So when Ludmila Kask opened that binder and I saw the section headers—**Medical, Nutritional, Behavioral, Developmental, Emotional, Attachment**—each one populated with charts, dated entries, cross-references—I felt something I almost never feel on home visits.

Relief.

"This is... incredibly thorough," I said.

"I believe in accountability," Mrs. Kask said. "The child has been failed by systems before. I refuse to be another system that fails her."

And I wrote that down. I wrote it in the margins of my intake form because it seemed like something I should remember, something I might quote in a staff meeting when someone asked whether the system was working. *I refuse to be another system that fails her.* I underlined it.

Lily looked up from her worksheet. She had those translucent green eyes that some children have, the kind that look like they're processing information slightly faster than the rest of the face. Her hair was braided. Two braids, evenly spaced, secured with matching elastics. She was wearing a clean white shirt and corduroy pants with no stains, which for a seven-year-old is either a miracle or a warning, though I didn't know that yet.

"Hello, Lily," I said.

"Hello. Are you the review specialist?"

I looked at Mrs. Kask. Mrs. Kask looked at me with an expression I interpreted as modesty.

"She knows you're someone who checks on how things are going," Mrs. Kask said. "I believe in transparency."

"I'm Dana," I told Lily. "I'm here to make sure you're happy and safe."

Lily considered this for a moment with an intensity that seemed practiced. Then she said: "I'm integrating well."

**Second Visit: January 9**

The temperature log was the thing that impressed my supervisor.

I mean, it impressed me too. I brought it back to the office—a photocopy, because Mrs. Kask had offered photocopies of everything, collated and stapled, with a cover page—and I showed it to Gerald, who runs our unit, and he held it up like an artifact from a civilization more advanced than ours.

"She records the temperature every fifteen minutes?"

"Every fifteen minutes. Every room the child uses. She has a target range based on pediatric sleep research. She adjusts it by season and by Lily's activity level."

"Jesus. Can she foster all of them?"

We laughed. We both laughed.

The temperature log was twenty-four pages long and covered the preceding two months. Each entry listed the room, the time, the thermostat reading, and the ambient air temperature as measured by a secondary device Mrs. Kask had purchased to verify the thermostat's accuracy. In the margins, she had noted deviations and their causes. *12:15 PM, kitchen: 73.2°F, +1.2° above target, attributed to oven use during meal preparation (see Nutrition Log, entry 11/14-L).* There were footnotes. The footnotes had footnotes.

"This woman is a saint," Gerald said, and I filed my report.

The nutrition spreadsheet was even more elaborate. It was color-coded by micronutrient. Not macronutrient—not just protein, carbohydrates, fat—but the specific vitamins and minerals in each meal, tracked against the USDA Dietary Reference Intakes for female children ages four through eight, adjusted for Lily's weight, which Mrs. Kask recorded weekly. Iron was red. Calcium was white. Vitamin D was yellow. The spreadsheet was beautiful in the way that madness is sometimes beautiful before you recognize it as madness, when it still just looks like diligence.

On this second visit, Lily was sitting in the same chair, which Mrs. Kask informed me was "her designated activity station." The house was immaculate. The house was always immaculate. Not in the way that some houses are clean—scrubbed and bleached and smelling of effort—but in a way that suggested dirt had never been invited and wouldn't know how to find the place. The couch cushions had no indentations. The carpet showed vacuum lines in parallel rows, like a freshly mowed infield. There were no toys on the floor. There were toys—a prescribed selection, rotated weekly per a schedule I later found in the binder under **Stimulation Protocol**—but they were on shelves, arranged by developmental category and labeled.

"Lily, can you show Miss Reeves your reading corner?"

Lily stood. She placed her pencil parallel to the top edge of her worksheet. She pushed her chair in until it was flush with the table.

"This is my designated reading area," she said, leading me to an alcove off the living room. "I'm allotted forty-five minutes of self-directed reading per day, with an additional fifteen minutes of guided reading before my sleep preparation sequence."

"Her sleep preparation sequence?"

Mrs. Kask materialized at my elbow. "Bath, tooth brushing, pajamas, fifteen minutes guided reading, five minutes of guided breathing, lights out. The routine provides stability. Children from chaotic backgrounds crave structure."

That's true. I learned that in graduate school. Children from chaotic backgrounds do crave structure. It's one of the first things they teach you, and it's become one of those truths that functions like a key card—swipe it and any door opens.

"Do you like reading, Lily?" I asked.

Lily looked at Mrs. Kask. It was quick—a glance, barely a quarter-second—but I noted it. I noted it and then I un-noted it, because children in new placements often look to their caregivers for cues and this is a sign of developing attachment, which is what we want.

"Reading is a positive engagement activity," Lily said. "I enjoy it within the prescribed parameters."

I laughed. I actually laughed. I thought it was cute. I thought she was parroting a phrase she'd heard, the way children parrot things, and that it was charming and a little funny and evidence that the placement was working—that she was internalizing the structure, making it her own.

I noted "positive language modeling" in my report.

---

**Third Visit: March 14**

This is the visit I keep returning to. Not because it was when I understood—I didn't understand yet, not really—but because it was when the understanding should have started, and instead I documented my way around it.

The binder had grown. It was now two binders. The second one was labeled **Attachment Milestones** and it was organized with the same laminated tabs, the same precise handwriting, the same ontological certainty that the interior life of a seven-year-old could be captured in a spreadsheet if you just made the columns narrow enough.

Mrs. Kask walked me through it at the kitchen table while Lily completed her afternoon Structured Free Play session in the next room. I could see her through the doorway. She was building with blocks—not wildly, not joyfully, not the way children build things in order to knock them down. She was building according to a diagram. Her movements were careful and sequential. She looked like a technician performing an assembly.

"We've made excellent progress in the attachment domain," Mrs. Kask said. She turned to a page titled **Physical Affection Tracking** and I saw a table with dates, times, and descriptions of physical contact between her and Lily. Each entry was categorized: *Initiated by child. Initiated by caregiver. Spontaneous. Prompted. Duration.* There was a column for Lily's "receptivity rating," scored on that same one-to-five scale.

"February 22nd," Mrs. Kask read aloud, tracing her finger down the page. "Lily initiated a lateral embrace at 18:42, duration approximately four seconds, receptivity rating four. I consider this a breakthrough."

"That's wonderful," I said, and I meant it, because I was reading the chart the way I'd been trained to read charts, which is to say I was looking for the numbers to go up. The numbers were going up. The numbers were always going up.

"She also asked permission to initiate a hug sequence yesterday," Mrs. Kask added. "Unprompted."

I wrote *child demonstrating increased attachment behaviors, including self-initiated physical affection requests* and I did not hear what I was writing. I did not hear that a seven-year-old had used the phrase "initiate a hug sequence" because it appeared nowhere in my training that a child requesting a hug in the language of a software protocol was a symptom of anything other than progress.

"Can I talk to Lily alone for a few minutes?"

"Of course. I'll be in the kitchen. I'll start her 15:30 snack prep. We're doing apple slices with almond butter today—she's slightly below target on monounsaturated fats this week."

I sat on the floor next to Lily. She was on block forty-six of what appeared to be a seventy-block structure.

"Hey, Lily. How are you doing?"

"I'm functioning within normal parameters."

"Are you happy here?"

She placed a block. Aligned it with her fingertip. "My well-being indicators are positive."

"Lily. Are you *happy*?"

She looked at me then, and here is where I failed—am still failing—to translate what I saw. Her face did something that wasn't in any rubric. It opened for a fraction of a second, like a door blown ajar by wind, and behind it was something vast and unfurnished, a room with nothing in it, and then the door closed and she said:

"Mrs. Kask says happy is when your chart goes up."

I wrote: *Child demonstrates sophisticated understanding of emotional self-monitoring. Placement continues to exceed benchmark expectations.*

---

It was somewhere around the fourth visit that I began to feel it—not know it, feel it—the way you feel a sound too low to hear, a vibration in the floor that could be the furnace or could be something beneath the foundation shifting.

I brought Gerald the binders. I brought him the spreadsheets. I brought him, specifically, the section of Binder Two labeled **Spontaneous Laughter (Prompted)** and I laid it on his desk and waited for him to see what I was beginning to see.

He read it carefully. He's a careful man.

"What am I looking at?"

"She's tracking prompted laughter and categorizing it as spontaneous."

"Where?"

"Here. Column C. It says 'spontaneous,' but then in the notes—" I pointed. "—she writes, 'Achieved following presentation of humorous stimulus (see Appendix D, joke rotation schedule).'"

Gerald took off his glasses and cleaned them, which is what Gerald does when he's going to disappoint you.

"Dana. She told a joke and the kid laughed."

"She told a joke from a *scheduled rotation* and then recorded the laughter as a developmental milestone."

"And?"

"And doesn't that seem—"

"Thorough?"

"—mechanical?"

Gerald put his glasses back on. "Do you know what I had cross my desk this morning? A four-year-old in the Hensley placement with a cigarette burn on his inner thigh. Circular. Deliberate. He told the ER nurse his foster dad was teaching him about 'hot and cold.' I've got three cases in active crisis, a judge who won't return my calls, and a kid in Brexton whose foster mother's boyfriend is selling the child's Ritalin. So when you bring me a foster parent who tracks nutritional intake by micronutrient and schedules jokes to make a child laugh, I don't see a problem. I see a miracle."

"But the language, Gerald. Have you listened to how Lily talks?"

"She's seven."

"She talks like an intake form."

"Kids pick up language from their environment. That's development."

"She described a nightmare to me. She called it 'an adverse sleep event.' She asked if it needed to be 'reported and catalogued.'"

Gerald leaned back. "Was it reported and catalogued?"

"Of course it was. Mrs. Kask has a Nocturnal Disturbance Log."

"Then it sounds like the child knows her concerns are taken seriously and documented. That's responsive caregiving."

I sat there, in Gerald's office, with its inspirational poster about every child deserving a safe space (an image of hands holding a smaller pair of hands, which itself looked like an intake brochure), and I understood something I couldn't articulate: that the language I needed to describe what was happening to Lily was the same language that was happening to Lily, and that if I tried to file a report about a foster mother who was replacing a child's inner world with compliance metrics, I would have to do it using compliance metrics, on a form designed to measure exactly the things Mrs. Kask was optimizing for.

I said, "Something feels wrong."

Gerald said, "That's not a finding, Dana."

He was right. It wasn't a finding. You can't file a feeling. A feeling is not an assessment instrument. A feeling does not have validity or reliability or inter-rater agreement. In the entire taxonomy of child welfare documentation, there is no code for *this home is a perfect machine and the child is becoming a part of it.* There is no checkbox for *the care is impeccable and the child is disappearing.*

I went back to my desk and filed a glowing report, because the report asked questions and the answers were all correct.

**Fifth Visit: July 2**

Mrs. Kask had started laminating things.

Not just the binder tabs—those had always been laminated—but the daily schedules posted in every room, the behavioral expectations listed on the inside of Lily's closet door, the "Affirmation Cards" that Lily was given each evening as part of her Positive Self-Concept module.
