# The Ballad of the Lurching Table

## I. The Mouth That Moved

The mouth belonged to Dr. Gerald Faust, DVM, and it had always been his least governable organ.

It was a mouth that had, over the course of a forty-one-year career, promised clients that their cats would stop urinating on clergy, guaranteed the recovery of a greyhound whose legs had been arranged by a highway median into a shape no greyhound's legs were meant to take, and once—at a conference in Zurich, before an audience of six hundred—confidently described a surgical technique he had invented during the previous sentence. The mouth operated, it must be understood, not in concert with the brain but in a kind of cheerful, adversarial parallel, the way two musicians might play different songs in the same bar and each believe the other was accompanying them.

On the afternoon of October 11, 1987, Dr. Faust's mouth found a camera.

The camera found it back.

The occasion was the Three Rivers Livestock Exposition in Welden, Colorado, a town of fourteen hundred souls wedged into a crease of the Sangre de Cristo Mountains like a dime in a sofa. Faust's surgical team—five veterinarians of varying distinction—had been invited to demonstrate bovine laparoscopy techniques in a tent that smelled of sawdust and the particular musk of animals who have been bathed against their convictions. The demonstration had gone well. A local CBS affiliate had arrived to film thirty seconds of filler for the evening broadcast, the kind of segment that exists between a house fire and the weather to remind viewers that the world also contains non-burning things.

The reporter was a young woman named Dana Sprague, who had been in television for six weeks and had not yet learned to fear a microphone held near a man who enjoyed the sound of his own name.

"Dr. Faust, incredible work today," she said, and this was a generous characterization of what had been, at best, competent work performed on a sedated heifer. "Tell us—is there any procedure you *couldn't* perform?"

What Dr. Gerald Faust's brain intended to say was something modest and forgettable, a throwaway line about the marvels of modern veterinary medicine, a sentence that would evaporate before it reached the ear.

What Dr. Gerald Faust's mouth said was: "Dana, my team could take out an appendix at a dead sprint. Put us in the back of a truck doing sixty and we'd have the job done before you hit the county line."

He said it with the specific grin of a man who believes he is being charming.

Dana Sprague, who did not know enough about cattle anatomy to know that bulls do not have appendixes in any clinically meaningful sense—or rather, they have a vermiform appendix-like structure so vestigial and irrelevant that removing it would be like performing surgery to extract a rumor—tilted her microphone with the instinct of someone who senses the segment has just become promotable.

"Is that so?"

"Hell," said the mouth, "we'd welcome the challenge."

Behind him, four veterinary surgeons stood in a loose semicircle. Not one of them spoke. In the silence that followed, an entire catastrophe was conceived, gestated, and born.

## II. The Five

Let us meet them as God would, which is to say all at once and with full knowledge of the contents of their hearts.

**Dr. Gerald Faust**, sixty-three, team leader. A man whose confidence had the tensile strength of bridge cable and the structural foundation of meringue. He had not performed surgery without supervision in four years because his hands had begun, at unpredictable intervals, to betray him—a tremor so faint that only he and his wife knew about it, and his wife only because she had watched him try to butter toast one morning and weep. He was, in spite of this, not a fraud. He had been magnificent once. He had saved animals that other veterinarians had measured for coffins. But the distance between *had been* and *was* had become a canyon, and he had been filling it, as canyons are often filled, with noise.

**Dr. Patricia Moreno**, fifty-one, second-in-command. A woman who had authored nine peer-reviewed papers, four of which had changed the way abdominal surgery was performed on large ruminants across the Western Hemisphere. She was the actual reason the team had been invited to the exposition, a fact that Faust had never acknowledged and she had never demanded he acknowledge, because Patricia Moreno did not demand things. She collected grievances the way other people collected stamps—meticulously, privately, and with a mounting sense that the collection would one day be worth something. She had a steel-trap mind and an ulcer that could have been classified as a dependent. In the moment after Faust's declaration, her face did not change. Inside her body, her stomach lining caught fire.

**Dr. Hank Olafsson**, forty-four, the largest veterinarian in the state of Colorado and possibly the largest veterinarian anywhere. Six foot five. Two hundred and sixty pounds, distributed with the aesthetic discretion of a landslide. Hands like dinner plates. A voice so deep it could be felt in the molars. Hank Olafsson had built a career on the principle that if you were enormous and quiet, people assumed you were competent, and if you were also occasionally competent, you became legendary. He was, in fact, a perfectly adequate surgeon—no more, no less—but he had been called "brilliant" so many times by so many people who were actually just impressed by how gently such a large man could handle a calf that he had begun to wonder if the word meant something different than he thought. His private fear, the one he carried like a stone in his shoe, was that one day a situation would arise that required actual brilliance, and his hands—so large, so praised—would be revealed as merely big.

**Dr. Raymond Chen**, thirty-eight, the team's most technically precise surgeon and its most comprehensively anxious human being. Raymond Chen prepared for procedures the way other people prepared for wars. He arrived early. He read. He diagrammed. He practiced on models made of silicone and prayer. He had, in his office at the veterinary college in Fort Collins, a filing cabinet containing contingency plans for scenarios that ranged from the probable (power outage during surgery) to the astronomically unlikely (earthquake during surgery) to the functionally impossible (surgery during the Rapture). He did not have a contingency plan for performing surgery in a moving livestock trailer because the idea had never occurred to him, not because he lacked imagination but because even his imagination, vast and morbid as it was, had standards. When Faust's mouth made its promise, Raymond Chen felt something inside him go very quiet, the way a computer goes quiet just before it displays a screen it has never displayed before and will never be able to explain.

**Dr. June Ketteridge**, twenty-seven, the newest member of the team by a margin of eleven years. She had completed her residency four months earlier. She had been invited to join Faust's team because she was cheap, available, and had once, during a particularly dire calving, held a prolapsed uterus inside a thrashing cow with her bare hands for forty-five minutes while the senior surgeon went to find a tool he had left in another building, a performance that Faust had witnessed and described afterward as "the best goalkeeping I've ever seen in a barn." June Ketteridge was, by every metric that mattered, good. She was better than good. She had the hands of a jeweler and the stamina of someone who had grown up bucking hay bales on a ranch outside of Salida, which she had. But she did not know she was good, because no one had told her, because the profession of veterinary surgery in 1987 was not in the business of telling young women that they were good, and because she had spent every day of the last four months comparing herself to the four people who surrounded her and finding herself, inevitably, shorter.

These were the five.

Not one of them wanted to get in the trailer.

## III. How the Trailer Came to Exist

The bull's name was Regent's Valor, and he was worth, by the estimate of his owner, "more than your house, your car, your wife, and whatever your wife is hiding in the coffee can she thinks I don't know about." His owner was a rancher named Floyd Dinsmore, a man who had been sunburned so continuously for so many decades that his face had achieved the color and texture of a catcher's mitt. Floyd Dinsmore loved two things: Regent's Valor and the sound of his own problems being described to sympathetic listeners.

The bull had been off his feed for two days. He had been listless, which for a bull of Regent's Valor's temperament—ordinarily a creature of such volcanic irritability that he had destroyed three fence sections and a surveyor's tripod in a single afternoon—was like watching a hurricane sit down and take up knitting. Floyd had called his own veterinarian, a man named Potts, who had examined the animal, palpated its abdomen, and said something noncommittal about "gastrointestinal upset."

Then Floyd had seen the broadcast.

He had seen the broadcast because his wife, Marlene, had turned on the television to watch the weather, and instead of the weather she had gotten Dana Sprague, and instead of Dana Sprague she had gotten Dr. Gerald Faust promising to perform surgery at sixty miles per hour, and instead of changing the channel she had said, "Floyd, come look at this fool," and Floyd had come to look at this fool and had seen not a fool but a savior.

He was at the exposition within the hour. He was at Faust's elbow within the minute.

"I seen you on the TV," Floyd said.

"Well," said Faust, in the tone of a man accustomed to being seen on things.

"My bull's gut-sick. Won't eat. Won't charge. Something's wrong in there," Floyd said, pointing at his own abdomen as a rough anatomical reference. "And I got to get him to Walsenburg by morning for the auction inspection. That's seventy miles and it's all mountain road. You said you could operate in a truck. I got a truck. I got a trailer. Hell, I'll drive slow."

Dr. Faust's brain—which had been, even in its reduced capacity, doing admirable work keeping the man alive and upright for six decades—sent an urgent memorandum to the mouth. The memorandum read: *Say no. Say it was a joke. Say you have a prior commitment. Say you are dead. Say anything. Say no.*

The mouth received the memorandum, considered it briefly, and said: "What kind of trailer?"

And so a Titan 24-foot gooseneck livestock trailer, 1979, white with rust, was backed up to the loading chute of the exposition barn while five veterinary surgeons stood in a line and watched the way passengers watch a plane they suspect is being held together by optimism.

The bull was loaded first. He went up the ramp with the weary resignation of an animal that had stopped believing things would improve. He was enormous—twenty-three hundred pounds of registered Charolais, the color of heavy cream, with a head like a boulder that had been given opinions. Floyd's ranch hand, a teenager named Kyle who appeared to communicate exclusively through shrugging, secured the bull in a makeshift chute made of welded pipe and ratchet straps. The chute would, in theory, restrict the animal's movement enough to allow surgical access to the abdomen. In practice—but we are getting ahead of ourselves.

A portable surgical table was bolted to the floor with lag screws. Surgical lamps—two of them, clamp-mounted, salvaged from the exposition tent—were attached to the ceiling rail with C-clamps and baling wire. An instrument tray was placed on the table. A portable anesthesia machine was strapped to the wall with cargo webbing.

Patricia Moreno looked at the arrangement and saw death.

Hank Olafsson looked at the arrangement and saw a very small space for a very large man.

Raymond Chen looked at the arrangement and saw the absence of approximately four hundred things he considered essential for surgery, including, among others: a floor that did not move, walls that did not vibrate, a patient that was not a bull, and the continued existence of a God who would permit such a thing to be attempted.

June Ketteridge looked at the arrangement and thought: *They must know something I don't.*

This was the thought that would carry her further than any of them.

## IV. The Mouth Makes One More Promise

Before they boarded, there was a moment—a sliver of time so thin you could have missed it if you'd blinked—when the whole enterprise might have been called off.

They were standing at the base of the ramp. The late-afternoon light had turned the mountains the color of a bruise. The air smelled of diesel and hay and the particular electricity that precedes a very bad idea. Faust was at the front of the line, one boot on the ramp, and he paused, and for just a moment his face did something it had not done in front of other people in forty-one years: it showed doubt.

Patricia saw it.

She could have said something. She could have said: *Gerald, this is insane. We are not getting in that trailer. You made a promise on television that no sane person would keep, and we are all going to walk away now, and tomorrow you will call this rancher and refer him to an equine surgical center, and in six months we will laugh about this, or we won't, but either way we will be alive and un-trampled.*

She did not say this. She did not say it because in thirty years of working alongside Gerald Faust, she had never once told him he was wrong, and the habit of silence is a muscle like any other—the more you use it, the stronger it gets, until it is stronger than you.

Hank saw the doubt too. He thought about saying something. Then he thought about how it would look—the biggest man on the team, the one everyone assumed was fearless, being the first to flinch. He said nothing. He adjusted his belt.

Raymond saw it. He had already calculated the odds of someone being seriously injured at somewhere between forty and seventy percent, depending on road conditions, the bull's anesthetic response, and whether or not there was a God, and if so, whether that God was paying attention. He opened his mouth. He closed it.

June saw nothing. She was looking at the bull.

The moment passed.

"All right," said Faust, and his voice did not waver, because the mouth was back in command, and the mouth did not waver, had never wavered, would not waver if the tongue were on fire. "Standard left-flank approach. Pat, you're on anesthesia. Hank, retraction. Ray, you're my second pair of hands. June, you assist Ray and stay out of the way. We'll have this animal opened, cleaned, and closed before La Veta Pass. Floyd—drive smooth."

"You bet," said Floyd, which was, under the circumstances, the most reckless lie anyone told that day, and the competition was stiff.

They climbed the ramp.

Kyle shut the gate behind them.

The truck started.

The story started.

## V. The First Three Minutes
