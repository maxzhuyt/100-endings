# The Skip

You want to know what eleven seasons of losing sounds like, I'll tell you. It sounds like a forty-two-pound granite stone released a half-second late, the handle still turning when it shouldn't be, the running band hissing against the pebble in a frequency I have come to know the way a mother knows her own child's cry in a crowded room—slightly flat, slightly mournful, the acoustic signature of a stone that will overcurl by exactly enough to miss everything that matters. I have heard this sound four hundred and six times. I have not been counting. The ice has been counting for me, because ice remembers everything, and I should know, having driven a 1987 Zamboni Model 552 across every sheet in this building for the better part of three decades, listening.

My name is Herschel Knaack. I resurface the ice.

That's what they pay me for. What I actually do is something else entirely.

---

The morning of the charity bonspiel at Brighton Beach, the air was fourteen degrees and the sky was the color of a old nickel—that greenish-gray you get over Lake Superior in January when the weather can't decide whether it wants to kill you outright or just make you profoundly uncomfortable. I rode out in the equipment van with Donnie Pfaff and his team because my nephew Gary, who was supposed to drive, had called in with what he described as a "stomach situation," and what I suspected was a Jägermeister situation, and so there I was behind the wheel of a Ford Econoline that smelled like rubber matting and decades of ambition, ferrying the worst curling team in the recorded history of the Duluth Community League to a fundraiser for the Humane Society on a frozen lake that, I will note for the record, I did not trust.

I did not trust it because I have spent my life on ice and I know what ice is. People think ice is a solid. People are wrong. Ice is a negotiation. It is water that has agreed, temporarily and under specific conditions, to hold still. The moment those conditions change—a warm current underneath, a shift in wind, the weight of four men and their stones concentrated on a patch that looked thick in November but has been slowly, treasonably thinning since the second week of December—the negotiation is over, and water goes back to being what water has always wanted to be, which is honest.

But nobody asked me about the ice. They asked me to drive.

---

Donnie Pfaff is forty-three years old. He sells HVAC systems for Northern Comfort out of a showroom on West Superior Street, and he is, by every measurable standard and several immeasurable ones, the worst skip in the history of organized curling in the state of Minnesota, a state that takes its curling seriously enough that this distinction carries real weight. His record entering that Saturday morning stood at zero wins and one hundred and fourteen losses, spanning eleven seasons, a streak so comprehensive and so devoid of even accidental success that it had transcended sport and become, in a way I don't think Donnie fully understood, a kind of art.

I say this with love. I say everything with love now because I am seventy years old and it is the only thing I have left that is worth spending.

His teammates:

Carl Tenhaken, lead. Fifty-one. Retired postal worker. Had a mustache that looked like something you'd find behind a radiator. Threw with a violence that was philosophically at odds with the game's demand for precision. Carl was the one who, three weeks prior, had sent the group text that read—and I know this because Donnie showed it to me in the parking lot, holding his phone out with the solemnity of a man presenting evidence at a war crimes tribunal— *"I think we should hang it up boys. This isn't fun anymore and honestly I don't know if it ever was."*

Ray Tenhaken, second. Carl's younger brother by two years, though he looked older because he carried his stress in his face, which was the face of a man who had been arguing with his brother since the Eisenhower administration. Ray's stones tended to be heavy because Ray himself was heavy—not in body but in spirit, in the way he lowered himself into the hack like a man climbing into his own grave. Ray was the source of what the team referred to, with varying degrees of venom, as the Dinner Situation, about which more later.

Pete Kovacic, vice-skip—or "third," depending on your nomenclature, and Pete had opinions about the nomenclature, Pete had opinions about everything, Pete was a man constructed almost entirely of opinions stacked on top of each other in a trench coat. He taught high school chemistry at East and was responsible for the team's only strategic innovation, a weight-control theory he'd derived from thermodynamic principles that had never once produced a positive result but which he continued to espouse with the fervor of a man who had confused being wrong a hundred times with being right in a way the universe had not yet recognized.

And Donnie. Always Donnie. Standing in the house at the far end, broom raised, tapping the ice where he wanted the stone to go, which was a kind of prayer, really, the way all target-setting is prayer—you point at the spot on earth where you need grace to land and then you wait to see if the universe is listening, and in Donnie's case, for eleven seasons, the universe had been occupied elsewhere.

The team was called the Sheet Show.

This was Carl's idea. He had thought it was hilarious in 2014. Nobody thought it was hilarious anymore, but the jerseys were paid for and you cannot outrun a name once it's been embroidered.

---

I need to tell you about the smell, because the smell is where the truth lives.

The Duluth Curling Club, where I have worked since 1996, where I have resurfaced the ice before dawn more times than I have told my wife I love her, which is a thing I think about sometimes on the long diagonal passes—the club smells like wet wool and old beer and the chemical sweetness of the pebbling water and, beneath all of that, something mineral and clean that I have never been able to name but which I associate with the word *patience.* The ice shavings that my Zamboni scrapes up—and she is a 1987 Model 552, bottle green, named Dolores after my mother because they are both temperamental and loud and have outlasted everything that tried to replace them—those shavings smell like nothing and everything at once. Like the inside of a cloud. Like the moment before snow. You put your face close enough to a fresh ribbon of shaving, curling off the blade, and you will smell what I can only describe as the ghost of the water the ice used to be—stripped of memory, stripped of dissolved minerals and fish and the slow brown tannins of Superior's depths, reduced to its simplest, coldest self.

This is the smell I have loved my whole life. This is the smell that was absent on that lake, replaced by wind and pine sap and the vast indifferent musk of open water lurking beneath a shell of dubious ice, and I knew—I knew the way you know a stone is going to overcurl before it crosses the hog line, the way you know a marriage is over before anyone says a word—that the day was going to go sideways.

---

The bonspiel was organized by Terri Lundeen, who ran the Humane Society with the tactical precision of a field marshal and the emotional range of a golden retriever, which is to say she loved everything and everyone and could not conceive of a scenario in which her plans might fail, because her plans were born of love, and love, in Terri's cosmology, was a structural material—load-bearing, weather-resistant, immune to the laws of physics. She had arranged four sheets on the lake ice, marked with spraypaint and bordered by hay bales, and she had tested the thickness in November—fourteen inches at the center, she said proudly, as if she had made the ice herself—and this was fine, this was more than adequate, except that November was two months ago and the lake does not answer to Terri Lundeen or her clipboard.

Eight teams showed up. The Sheet Show was seeded last, which was not a seeding so much as a geological classification—they were bedrock, the lowest stratum, the thing everything else rested on.

Their first-round opponents were the Duluth Brewing Company Brooms, sponsored by the brewery and composed of four men who had all curled competitively in college, who wore matching jackets with their names embroidered on the breast, who stretched before games. *Stretched.* Like it was a sport that required hamstrings. This bothered Donnie. Everything about competence bothered Donnie, though he would never say so, would only tighten his jaw and pick up his broom and walk to the house with the bearing of a man approaching a lover who has wronged him but whom he cannot leave.

I sat on an overturned five-gallon bucket near Sheet 3, wrapped in a blanket that smelled like the van, and I watched.

---

Here is the thing about the Sheet Show that I need you to understand before anything else happens, before the ice opens up and everyone starts screaming and the day becomes a story that people tell at the club for years, polishing it smoother and less true with every retelling:

They loved each other.

They would deny this. Carl would rather chew glass than admit he loved his brother. Ray would fold his arms and turn away. Pete would deliver a nine-minute lecture on the neurochemistry of male bonding that somehow circled back to thermodynamics. And Donnie—Donnie would just shake his head and say, "We're a team, that's all, we show up," as if showing up for eleven seasons of unbroken defeat was a small thing, a minor commitment, the equivalent of remembering to take out the trash.

But I saw them after games. Every game. Ten thousand small defeats across eleven seasons, and after each one, the four of them in the lounge, wedged into the corner booth underneath the 1983 U.S. Championships pennant—the wool-and-beer smell rising off them like incense, Carl's terrible mustache catching foam, Ray slowly demolishing a basket of cheese curds, Pete narrating the loss with the clinical detachment of a man performing an autopsy on someone he loved, and Donnie sitting at the center of it all, quiet, running his thumb around the rim of his glass the way he ran his thumb along the handle of his stone before every throw, which was the only tender thing I ever saw him do.

They were a ugly dog, this team. Matted and crooked and missing something essential. And I loved them the way you love a ugly dog, which is more than you love a pretty one, because a ugly dog doesn't have anything to hide behind, and neither did they.

---

The game started at 10:17 a.m., which I know because I checked my watch, which I do compulsively, because when you have spent your life on a Zamboni, moving at four miles per hour in rectangles, time becomes the only variable, the only thing that distinguishes one pass from another.

Carl threw first. His stone was heavy—Carl's stones were always heavy, Carl threw stones the way he'd delivered mail, with a grim efficiency that disdained subtlety—and it sailed through the house and came to rest against the back board with a thud I could hear from forty feet away, a sound like a door closing.

"Classic Carl," Ray said, from the hack end, and Carl, who was already sweeping for no tactical reason, just out of habit, just because his body didn't know what else to do on a sheet of ice, said nothing.

But then: the Brewing Company's lead overthrew too. His stone ran long and heavy, kicked off the back wall, and came to rest at an angle that invited nothing. And I watched their skip's face—Marcus Tremblay, twenty-seven years old, ponytail, insufferable—crumple briefly, a microsecond of confusion, because Marcus Tremblay was not accustomed to things going wrong.

Something was different about the ice. Lake ice is not arena ice. Lake ice has memory—it remembers the currents below, the fish, the slow geothermal argument between water and winter. It plays unpredictably. The Brewing Company, for all their stretching and matching jackets, had never curled on anything but pristine club sheets, and the lake was throwing them. Their stones reacted to invisible ridges, ghosted left on surfaces that looked flat, stuttered across patches of crystalline roughness that my trained eye could see from the bucket but that their trained eyes could not, because there is a difference between reading ice from above it and understanding ice from *inside* it, from having pressed your face into its shavings, from having felt its moods through the handlebars of a machine that has been married to it longer than most humans stay married to each other.

The Sheet Show, on the other hand, had been playing bad ice their entire lives. Not literally—the Duluth Club's ice was excellent, I made sure of that—but metaphorically, spiritually, in the sense that nothing had ever gone right for them and so they had developed, without knowing it, a kind of feral adaptability, the survival instinct of organisms that have evolved in hostile environments. They expected the ice to betray them. They planned for it. Their entire strategy—to the extent Pete's thermodynamic theories constituted a strategy—was built on the assumption that every stone would do something wrong, and so when lake ice *actually did* something wrong, they were, for the first time in their careers, playing the conditions they'd always been training for.

By the fourth end, the Sheet Show was ahead. 5–3.

I checked my watch. I checked it again. I looked at the scoreboard—hay bales with numbers spray-painted on cardboard, hung from a wire—and I felt something move in my chest, something tectonic, something I associated with the first time I heard Coltrane or the last time I saw my father alive, a feeling too large for the container it was in.

Donnie's face was absolutely terrifying. It was the face of a man who has been told the tumor is benign, that the house didn't burn down, that the child has been found alive—wide open, defenseless, stripped of the eleven years of scar tissue he'd built up against hope. He stood in the house with his broom like a man holding a flag on a hill he never expected to take, and his eyes were wet, and I thought: *This is the most dangerous moment of his life.*

Because losing is a country Donnie knew. He had citizenship there. He spoke the language, knew the customs, could navigate its terrain in the dark. But winning—winning was foreign soil, and he had no map, no currency, no phrase book, and I could see him looking around at this new landscape with the bewildered wonder of an immigrant who has arrived somewhere beautiful and has no idea how to stay.

---

"We need to talk about this," Carl said, during the break between the fourth and fifth ends. He was standing at the edge of their staging area—a patch of cleared snow near some equipment bags—with his arms folded, which was Carl's version of raising a white flag.

"We're winning," Pete said. "We're literally winning. Can it wait?"

"It's been waiting three weeks."

"The text," Ray said, flatly.

"The text," Carl confirmed.

Donnie was studying the house. He hadn't looked up. His lips were moving. He was calculating something—angles, weights, the geometry of stones that hadn't been thrown yet, a future that was, for the first time, a future he wanted.

"I meant what I said," Carl told them. "After this season, I'm done."

"You say that every year," Pete said.

"This year I mean it."

"You say *that* every year."

"I'm fifty-one years old. My knees sound like a bag of gravel. I've given this—" Carl gestured at the ice, the lake, the hay bales, the sky, the totality of their shared futility "—everything I had, and what has it given me back? What?"

"Friendship," Pete said, with the precise, arid tone of a man conducting a controlled experiment.

Carl stared at him. "Is that what this is?"

"Broadly?"

"Because friendship doesn't usually make me want to drive into traffic."

"Depends on the friendship."

"And it doesn't usually cost me four hundred dollars a season in league fees and another two hundred in bar tabs for a man—" Carl pointed at Ray "—who has never once, in eleven years, reached for the check."

And there it was. The Dinner Situation.

I should explain. In October of 2019, the four of them went to dinner at the Pickwick, which is a restaurant in Duluth that thinks very highly of itself, and at the end of the meal, the check arrived, and Ray did not reach for it.
