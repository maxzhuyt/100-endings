# The Hateful Immortals

## A Documentary History of the Greenbriar-Chen Marriage, 1847–2024

### by Dr. Amelia Thornton
#### Harwick Chair of Applied Historical Biography, University of Edinburgh

---

## I.

There are 2,307 surviving letters.[^1] There are seven court filings across five jurisdictions, spanning three centuries and two legal traditions.[^2] There are forty-six known addresses, distributed across nineteen cities on four continents, each maintained for an average of 3.8 years before abandonment. There are fourteen recorded aliases — "Mr. and Mrs. Brennan" being the most frequently recycled, though "the Ashfords" held for a remarkably stable eleven years in Nairobi, and "Dr. and Mrs. Soo-Yun Park" was used precisely once, in Seoul, in 1997, for reasons neither party has satisfactorily explained.[^3]

There are two people.

I should, at this point, establish the rules under which the world has operated since at least the early modern period, though the historical record is too fragmentary to fix a precise origin. The phenomenon — colloquially "manifestation," formally classified under the International Registry of Enhanced Individuals as "spontaneous phenotypic divergence" — occurs during puberty in roughly 0.003 percent of the global population. It is not inherited. It is not predictable. It is not, despite the best efforts of several governments and no fewer than forty-one pharmaceutical companies, replicable. A child in Johannesburg wakes one morning and discovers she can taste copper through walls — not metaphorically, not synesthetically, but with the specific, granular precision of an assayer's tongue, so that she can tell you whether the pipes behind the plaster are drawn or extruded, and whether the solder is lead-free.[^4] A man in Reykjavik steps into a crosswalk and his shadow remains on the curb, independently, watching him go.[^5] A teenager in São Paulo sneezes and every bird within a quarter mile changes direction simultaneously. The powers, if that is the right word — and the literature has never settled whether it is — do not follow a logic. They follow a grammar of non sequitur. They are party favors distributed by a universe with no evident sense of thematic cohesion.

In 1840s Boston, two such manifestations occurred within eighteen months of each other, in the same parish, to two children who already knew each other by sight.

Margaret Greenbriar, age thirteen, woke on March 7, 1840, and did not forget anything again.

James Chen, age fifteen, fell from the roof of his father's warehouse on the docks on September 14, 1841, struck the cobblestones headfirst, and stood up without a bruise. He would not receive one for the next 183 years.[^6]

There is a wedding notice in the *Boston Evening Transcript*, dated October 11, 1847. It reads, in full:

> MARRIED — On Thursday, the 7th inst., by the Rev. Elijah Wainwright, at the Second Congregational Church, Mr. JAMES CHEN, of this city, merchant, son of Mr. Samuel Chen and the late Mrs. Anne Chen, to Miss MARGARET GREENBRIAR, daughter of Mr. William and Mrs. Dorothea Greenbriar. No cards.

"No cards" meant no formal reception. They were young and not wealthy. He was twenty-one. She was twenty. He was the son of a Chinese immigrant merchant and a Boston Irishwoman, which was unusual enough in 1847 to draw comment but not unusual enough, evidently, to prevent the Reverend Wainwright from performing the ceremony, though this may have had more to do with Samuel Chen's generous donations to the church's building fund than with the Reverend's advanced views on matrimony.[^7]

There is also a photograph, taken in Lisbon, Portugal, in 2019. It accompanied a rarely granted interview for a Portuguese cultural magazine — one of the few occasions the couple have spoken jointly on the record — and it shows two people sitting slightly too far apart on a blue sofa in what appears to be a nondescript rented apartment. She has red hair and a face that would be called handsome rather than pretty, with the sharp, assessing quality of someone who is looking not just at you but at the last four hundred people who sat where you are sitting. He is East Asian with what one interviewer described, in a phrase I have never quite been able to forgive, as "the easy handsomeness of a man who knows he can't be damaged."[^8] They appear to be in their late twenties. They are wearing the kind of unremarkable casual clothing — her cardigan, his open-collared shirt — that people wear when they have been choosing outfits for long enough that the entire enterprise has become exhausting.

The photograph is dated 172 years after the wedding notice. They appear to be roughly the same age in both.

This is a love story. It is also a horror story. It is, by any available metric, the longest-running domestic argument in recorded history, and my purpose in presenting it is neither to arbitrate that argument nor to sentimentalize it, but to document, with the best tools available to the discipline, what happens to a human union — to the ordinary mechanisms of memory, grievance, compromise, desire, and boredom that constitute a marriage — when the single structural assumption upon which all marriages rest is removed.

They promised "till death do us part." One of them cannot die. The other cannot forget.

I ask the reader to consider the mathematics of that conjunction, and then to consider the wedding notice again — the plain typeface, the spare language, the hopeful October morning, the two young people who had no idea what they were promising — and to hold it in mind as we proceed.

It will be needed.[^9]

## II.

The earliest surviving argument — by which I mean the earliest argument for which we have a detailed, first-person account from at least one participant — dates to approximately November of 1852. We owe its preservation to Margaret's correspondence with her younger sister, Constance Greenbriar Holloway, who was herself enhanced (a modest but persistent bioluminescence that made her, by all accounts, extremely easy to locate at parties) and who lived, with the benefit of a moderately decelerated aging process, until 1931.[^10] In a letter dated March 12, 1903, Margaret writes to Constance at length about the state of her marriage, and in the course of the letter provides the following account:

> You ask, as you always do, how things stand with James. They stand as they have always stood, which is to say at a slight angle to the ground, giving the appearance of stability while requiring continuous effort to prevent collapse. I will tell you about last evening, but first I must go back, as I always must go back, to the evening in November of 1852 — I believe it was the 14th, a Tuesday, and cold, though not yet deeply cold — the evening he called Mother a pestilent harridan.
>
> We had dined with Mother and Father. Mother had, I grant you, been difficult — she had remarked upon the quality of the beef, which was in fact good beef, and upon the arrangement of the table linen, which was in fact poorly arranged, but which I had arranged myself and which therefore fell, by the laws of matrimony, under the protection of my husband's loyalty. James said nothing at the table. He was, as you know, always correct in company. It was afterward, in our parlor, the one on Milk Street with the green curtains — the curtains were a shade of green I would later see high in the Andes, on mosses near the treeline, though at the time I had not traveled and could only think they were the color of James's mother's jade brooch, which I never liked — that he said it. He was removing his cravat. He did it with that particular motion he has, the impatient tug as though the fabric has personally offended him, and he said, without looking at me: "Your mother, Margaret, is a pestilent harridan, and I include the word 'pestilent' advisedly, because her company is a disease from which I do not believe even I can recover."
>
> I did not speak to him for eleven days. He apologized on the twelfth. I accepted. We continued. But Connie, here is what I need you to understand: I am standing in that parlor now. The fire has gone to embers. The clock on the mantel — the brass one Father gave us, with the shepherd and the missing lamb — reads quarter past ten. The curtains are that precise green. His hand is on his cravat. And it is also 1903, and I am sitting at this desk, and I have lived fifty-one years since that evening, and the evening has not moved. It has not softened. It has not become the kind of memory one can hold up to the light and find the sharp edges worn down. It is as it was. Every memory is as it was. I do not have the luxury of the long view, Connie, because there is no long view. There is only the original view, in perpetuity.
>
> James does not have this problem. James remembers, as all people remember, a *version*. His version of himself is invariably kinder, more reasonable, and less inclined to use the word "pestilent" than the version I have on file.
