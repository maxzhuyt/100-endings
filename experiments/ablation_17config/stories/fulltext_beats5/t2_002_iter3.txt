# The HOA Board Will Now Come to Order

## I.

The ramp was ugly. Meredith Chen had known it would be ugly before the contractor laid the first board, knew it the way she knew that a nonsmoking woman of sixty-two with controlled hypertension and no family history of cardiac events had a 4.7 percent probability of dying within ten years, which was to say: with the clear, unsentimental vision that had made her, for thirty-one years at Midwestern Mutual, very good at her job. The ramp was built of pressure-treated southern yellow pine that had not yet had time to weather from its chemical green to the reputable gray of age. It ran seventeen feet from the front porch to the driveway at a grade of 1:12, which was the ADA standard, which was the grade at which a man with late-stage ALS whose left arm still retained perhaps forty percent function could, with assistance, wheel himself from the front door to the yard to sit in the sun that the occupational therapist said was good for his mood, though not, obviously, for anything else.

The ramp had cost four thousand two hundred dollars. The ramp had been finished at 11:40 a.m. on a Wednesday. By 2:15 p.m. on the same Wednesday, a laminated notice had been affixed to the front door of 414 Briarwood Court with a 3M Command strip—the clear kind, designed for renters, gentle on paint.

Meredith read it standing in the driveway with her car keys in one hand and a bag of pharmacy prescriptions in the other. She read it the way she read everything: once through for the shape, then again for the load-bearing clauses.

> **WILLOWBROOK COMMONS HOMEOWNERS ASSOCIATION**
> **NOTICE OF VIOLATION — §6.2(a): Unauthorized Exterior Structural Modification**
>
> Dear Homeowner:
>
> An inspection conducted on this date has identified the following violation of the Willowbrook Commons CC&Rs: construction of a wooden ramp structure attached to the front porch and extending across the front yard setback without prior submission of an Architectural Review Application (Form ARA-1, available at willowbrookcommons.org or from the management office).
>
> Per §6.2(d), a daily assessment of $50.00 will accrue beginning 72 hours from the date of this notice until such time as (1) a retroactive Architectural Review Application is submitted and approved by the Board (estimated review period: 60–90 business days), or (2) the unauthorized structure is removed and the property restored to its prior condition.
>
> Please direct questions to the HOA Management Office, M–F, 9:00–12:00.
>
> Sincerely,
> Douglas R. Whitmore
> President, Willowbrook Commons HOA

Below the signature, in blue ballpoint, in a hand she recognized from twelve years of Christmas cards:

*Meredith—so sorry to hear about Richard. Banana bread on your porch! —Doug*

She looked. The banana bread was there, wrapped in foil on a paper plate, resting on the bench beside the ramp's top landing. It was still faintly warm. She could smell the walnuts. Doug's banana bread was, she had to admit—had always had to admit—exceptional. He brought it to every HOA function, and at some point over the years it had become his signature, the thing people mentioned about him first: *Doug, the one with the banana bread.*

She picked up the plate. She tucked the laminated notice under her arm. She unlocked the door, left the prescriptions on the hall table, put the banana bread in the kitchen, and sat down in the chair by the bedroom where Richard was sleeping with his mouth open and his hands curled on the quilt like a child's and the BiPAP machine cycling its slow mechanical breath beside him.

Fifty dollars a day. She didn't reach for a calculator. She didn't need to. It was the kind of arithmetic that performed itself in the foreground of her mind while the rest of her sat very still and watched her husband's chest rise on borrowed air.

At seventy-two hours, the accrual would begin. At thirty days: fifteen hundred. At sixty: three thousand. At ninety days—the outside edge of the Architectural Review timeline, if she submitted the application tomorrow, if she could find Form ARA-1, if the board processed it without delay, which it would not, because the board was Doug—at ninety days, four thousand five hundred dollars, which was roughly the cost of the ramp itself.

Ninety days.

The hospice nurse, Janelle, had sat in this same chair last week and spoken to Meredith in the unhurried, carefully unmedical language that hospice nurses used when the clinical reality had simplified itself to a question of months versus weeks. Janelle had not said *ninety days*. She had said *the trajectory we're seeing*, and *usually at this stage*, and *I want you to be prepared for*—and the number Meredith's mind had built out of those phrases was approximately the same number that now sat in the accrual column of an HOA fine for a wheelchair ramp that existed because her husband wanted to sit in the sun.

She did not cry. She had not cried since the diagnosis, which was not bravery but professional deformation; actuaries did not cry about probability. They modeled it. They priced it. They found the clause in the contract that allowed for it and then they managed the exposure.

At 9:00 p.m., after she had fed Richard the thickened liquid that was all he could swallow now and had changed the pad beneath him and had set up the nighttime medications in their labeled cups and had checked the BiPAP settings and had stood in the hallway for ninety seconds with her forehead against the wall, Meredith went to the filing cabinet in Richard's old study and pulled out the Willowbrook Commons CC&Rs.

She had received the document when they closed on the house in 2011. She had read it then—she read everything—but quickly, a skim for red flags, the way you might review the terms of service on a software update. Now she sat at the kitchen table with a highlighter and a legal pad and read it the way she had once read the Society of Actuaries' mortality tables: not for the headline numbers but for the assumptions buried in the structure, the places where the model could be broken.

She read for three hours. She filled nine pages of the legal pad. She ate two slices of the banana bread, which was, as always, very good.

By midnight she had found what she needed. Not in the architectural review provisions—those were airtight, or at least tight enough to require a lawyer she couldn't afford on the timeline she had. What she found was in Article IV: Governance. Specifically, §4.3 (Board Elections), §4.6 (Annual Meeting Quorum Requirements), §4.7 (Parliamentary Authority—*"All meetings shall be conducted in accordance with the current edition of Robert's Rules of Order Newly Revised, except as otherwise specified in these Bylaws"*), and §4.9 (Financial Audit Provisions—*"The Board shall commission an independent financial audit of all Association accounts at intervals not to exceed five years"*).

The last independent audit of the Willowbrook Commons HOA had been conducted in 2009.

Meredith set down the highlighter. She looked at the legal pad. She looked at the banana bread, half-eaten, its foil peeled back like a surgical drape. She thought about Doug Whitmore's handwriting—the practiced warmth of it, the loops and curves of a man who believed that kindness and procedure occupied separate rooms in the same house, and that you could lock someone in one room while waving to them from the other.

She turned to a fresh page on the legal pad. At the top, in her small, precise handwriting, she wrote: *Annual Meeting—Nov. 14. Need: quorum, proxies, parliamentarian, record.*

Then she went to bed. She slept on her side of the mattress with her hand on Richard's arm so she could feel the rhythm of the BiPAP through his skin, and she dreamed of nothing.

## II.

Vivian Okonkwo lived in the unit on the corner of Briarwood and Mulberry, the one with the herb garden that Doug had once measured to verify it fell within the approved dimensional parameters for front-yard container plantings. (It had. By one inch.) Meredith knew her from the neighborhood, from polite nods at the mailbox, from one conversation at the summer block party two years ago in which Vivian had mentioned, in the careful, pre-emptive way of someone accustomed to explaining gaps in a résumé, that she worked from home.

Meredith knocked on a Thursday morning. Vivian answered the door in a flannel shirt and reading glasses, her gray locs gathered in a loose knot.

"I have a strange question," Meredith said.

"I prefer those."

"Do you still have your steno machine?"

Vivian's expression did something complicated—a flinch rearranged into a smile. "I keep it in practice shape."

"Can you still take verbatim at speed?"

"Two hundred forty words a minute. On a good day."

"I need an official record of the annual HOA meeting. A real one. Every word."

Vivian leaned against the doorframe. She studied Meredith for a long moment, and Meredith understood that the woman was not deciding whether she could do it but whether she would. There was a difference. Meredith waited. She had learned, in the weeks since the diagnosis, that most of the important things happened in silences, in the space between a question and its answer, in the pause before a hand reached for a doorknob or didn't.

"Helen Park has been recording those minutes for eight years," Vivian said. "She once summarized a forty-five-minute debate about the pool hours as—and I quote from the published minutes—'The board discussed pool scheduling.'"

"I know."

"You want a real record because a real record is a kind of weapon."

"Yes."

Vivian looked past Meredith toward the street, toward the neighborhood that had been carefully curated into a particular vision of order—the approved earth tones, the clipped hedges, the uniform mailboxes. Her eyes settled on Meredith's house at the end of the cul-de-sac, where the raw lumber of the ramp glowed pale and conspicuous against the façade.

"I read the notice on your door when I walked past yesterday," Vivian said. "I'm sorry about Richard."

"Thank you."

"I'll do it. But I need to practice. I haven't taken live testimony in—" She stopped. "I haven't taken anything live in eleven years."

"The meeting is November fourteenth."

"I know when the meeting is. Everyone knows when the meeting is. Nobody goes."

"This year they'll go," Meredith said.

She went home. She printed a Form ARA-1 from the website. She filled it out in complete, scrupulous detail, noting the ramp's dimensions, materials, load-bearing specifications, and ADA compliance documentation, and she submitted it to the management office by certified mail. Then she opened her laptop and began a different kind of search.

---

The National Association of Parliamentarians maintained a public directory of its credentialed members. Paul Mazzara, PRP—Professional Registered Parliamentarian—lived eleven miles away in a ranch house in unincorporated county territory, which meant he was not a Willowbrook Commons resident, which meant he had no conflict of interest, which meant he was exactly what she needed.

She called. He answered on the second ring.

"Mr. Mazzara, my name is Meredith Chen. I'm a homeowner in Willowbrook Commons, and I'd like to hire a professional parliamentarian to advise me on procedure for our annual meeting."

A silence. Then a sound she could not immediately identify. It was, she realized, the sound of a man pressing the phone to his chest so she wouldn't hear the noise he made before he collected himself.

"Mrs. Chen," Paul Mazzara said, and his voice was measured now, professional, but beneath it ran an electricity she recognized because she had heard it in her own voice when she'd found §4.7 in the bylaws. "Tell me everything."

She drove to his house the following Saturday. Paul was sixty-eight, compact, bald, with rimless glasses and the posture of a man who believed sitting up straight was a moral commitment. His living room contained a sectional couch, a television he clearly never watched, and an entire wall of bookshelves devoted to parliamentary procedure. Not one shelf. A wall. Meredith counted four editions of *Robert's Rules of Order Newly Revised*, including a 1970 first printing in a clear archival sleeve. There were annotated copies of *Demeter's Manual of Parliamentary Law and Procedure*, *The Standard Code of Parliamentary Procedure*, and *Mason's Manual of Legislative Procedure*. There were spiral-bound competition guides from the National Association of Parliamentarians. There were trophies.

"You competed," she said.

"Nationals. 2012, 2015, 2018." He pointed to a small plaque on the shelf between two editions of *Robert's*. "Third place, 2015. Parliamentary Procedure Contest, Professional Division."

"What's the contest like?"

"They present you with a scenario—a meeting in progress, motions on the table, nine things wrong—and you have to identify the procedural errors and propose the correct sequence of remedies. Timed. Under observation." His eyes were bright. "It is the closest thing to justice ordinary people ever get."

She opened her bag and spread the documents across his dining table: the CC&Rs, the violation notice, nine pages of legal-pad notes, and a printout of the last fifteen years of HOA meeting minutes, which were publicly filed and available through the county clerk's office and which Helen Park had rendered into documents of such strategic vagueness that they could have described any HOA in any suburb in America.

Paul put on a second pair of glasses—reading glasses, over the rimless ones—and bent to the papers. He read in silence for forty minutes. Meredith sat across from him and drank the coffee he'd made, which was terrible, and watched his index finger move down the columns of the financial disclosures.

"Meredith," he said.

"Yes."

"What's the approved annual budget for landscaping and grounds maintenance?"

"Forty-two thousand."

"And the actual expenditures in the last five fiscal years?"

"I haven't added them yet."

He had a mechanical pencil. He did it longhand, on the back of one of her legal-pad pages, his handwriting small and mechanical. When he finished, he turned the page around so she could see.

Year one: $89,400. Year two: $94,200. Year three: $91,800. Year four: $102,600. Year five: $97,100.

She looked at the numbers. She looked at Paul. He was already pulling up something on his phone.

"Green Valley Landscaping LLC," he said. "The vendor listed on all major grounds invoices since 2013. Registered business address: 1740 Orchard Park Drive."

"Which is?"

"Give me a moment." He typed. He waited. He tilted the screen toward her. County assessor records. 1740 Orchard Park Drive. Owner: Gerald Toomey.

The name didn't register. Paul saw it in her face and shook his head.

"Gerald Toomey is married to Patricia Toomey, née Whitmore."

Meredith sat very still. The math was already happening, unbidden, automatic—five years of excess expenditures at an average overage of roughly fifty-three thousand per year, times the full fifteen years Paul's documents covered, and the number that landed was not precise because she was working from incomplete data but it was *large*, it was a number with weight, and it sat in her mind the way the accrual of the ramp fine sat in her mind, the way Janelle's estimate sat in her mind, and for a moment all three quantities occupied the same cognitive space—$50/day, ninety days, three-quarters of a million dollars—and she felt something that was not grief and not anger but was perhaps the sensation of a woman whose entire professional life had been spent calculating the probability of adverse events now realizing she was standing inside one.

"Section 4.7.3,"
