# The Last Watch of the *Mara Celeste*

I was the cook. That's what I was. Cook aboard the *Mara Celeste* for eleven months and the better part of a twelfth, and I can tell you what happened on her because I was there, and because nobody else is going to.

The thing I keep coming back to is the tea. Third day of the typhoon, down in the Sargasso depths where the water goes that color like old wine, I brought Captain Wen a cup of black tea with no sugar because that's how she took it, and I set it on the chart table next to the dividers, and it sat there and went cold. I watched it go cold. She was standing not two feet from it, holding the edge of the table with both hands, reading the barometer, and I said the tea was there, and she said right, and she did not pick it up. I stood in the doorway of the chart room and watched the steam stop rising from that cup and I thought, well. That's that, then.

That was the third morning. But I need to go back further than that if any of this is going to make sense, and I'm not sure it will even then. Some things don't make sense. They just happen one piece after another and you're left holding the pieces.

---

The *Mara Celeste* was Wen's grandfather's boat. He'd built her—well, he'd had her built. Had her built at the Kwong Yip yard in sixty-two and sailed her freight runs for thirty years, and when he died she went to Wen's mother, and when Wen's mother died she went to Wen. That's a thing that weighs on a person, a boat like that. Three generations of hands on the same wheel.

But there was money owed. There was money owed on the refit, and money owed on the engine work, and there was a note with the bank that Wen's mother had signed that Wen had inherited along with everything else. I don't understand finance. I'm a cook. But I understood the letters.

They came in bundles at every port. Manila, four letters. Surabaya, six. They were from the bank and they were from attorneys and they were from a company whose name I saw on the envelopes but won't try to repeat because I'd get it wrong. What I can tell you is that when we made Bermuda—when we were supposed to make Bermuda—there were men waiting there. Repo men. Insurance people. Men with papers. Men who were going to walk onto the *Mara Celeste* and take her.

Wen read those letters at my galley table. She didn't read them in her cabin or in the chart room. She came down to the galley at odd hours—two in the morning, four—and she sat at my table under the lamp and she read them one by one and then she folded each one and slid it back into its envelope and pressed the crease flat with her thumbnail. Neat as you please. Every letter back in its envelope. Every envelope back in the stack. She never crumpled one. She never swore. She never cried. She pressed the creases flat with her thumbnail and the look on her face was the same look she had when she was checking tide tables or tallying cargo weight. Like it was work. Like it was just the next piece of work to be done.

That neatness frightened me more than anything in the typhoon ever did. I've seen angry people. Angry's normal. Angry means there's still something to fight about. But a person who folds the letter that says they're going to take everything from her, folds it back neat and square—that's a person who's already past it. Past all of it.

She was not going to hand them that boat. I'll say it plain because it was plain. Captain Wen did not stay aboard the *Mara Celeste* because she was brave. She was brave, but that's not why she stayed. She stayed because giving the boat to those men in Bermuda was worse to her than anything the ocean could do. That's not courage. I don't know what it is. It's something older than courage. It's the thing that makes a person lock the door and sit down inside a burning house because the house is hers.

We were three days out from Bermuda when the typhoon caught us. Or we caught it. The weather reports were clear enough. Wen read them. I saw her read them. She changed heading twice and both times it was toward the storm, not away, and if any of the crew noticed—there were only four of us left by then, the others having quit at various ports as the money thinned—they didn't say so. Or maybe they did say so the way sailors say things, with a look, with a silence, and I didn't catch it because that kind of language was never mine.

---

Every morning at six bells we played cards. This was established practice from my first week aboard. Wen came to the galley, I poured coffee, and we played gin rummy at the table with a deck so old the queens were faceless from handling. She kept score on the back of whatever paper was nearest—usually a provisions list, so if you had turned those lists over you would have found months of tallied points in Wen's small square handwriting running between notations for rice and cooking oil and salt.

We played on the first morning of the typhoon. We played on the second morning. On the third morning the ship was heeled over so far to starboard that I had to wedge a dishrag under the coffee pot to keep it from going over the table's edge, and the cards slid if you didn't hold them flat with your palm, and we played.

The hull was making sounds. I don't mean the usual sounds of a boat working in a seaway. I mean sounds that had weight and length to them. Groans. Low, drawn-out groans from somewhere below the waterline, like the metal was being bent by hands. And under that, water. Not the ocean outside—you could hear that too, the waves hitting the hull like something dropped from a height—but water *inside*, moving beneath the deck plates, sloshing when the ship rolled, a sound like a living stomach. The pumps were going. You could hear the pumps too, a thudding rhythm under everything, and as long as the pumps were going the water was being fought, and as long as the water was being fought you could sit at a table and play cards.

Wen played the same way she always played. Focused. Exact. She caught me miscounting my deadwood once—I'd said twelve and it was fifteen—and she corrected me, and I said sorry, and she marked the right number on the provisions list and drew from the stock pile and we continued. She didn't mention the storm. She didn't mention the pumps or the angle of the deck or the sound the hull was making. I didn't either. We talked about the cards. I said gin, or I said I'll knock, and she said show me, and that was the conversation. That was all the conversation there was.

I think about people who talk about love as a thing you say. A declaration. An event. They should have been in that galley. They should have seen Wen mark fifteen on the back of a provisions list while the ship groaned under us and neither of us said a word about the water or the wind or the men in Bermuda. That was not silence. That was the most I have ever said to another person.

The dishrag under the coffee pot. I think about that. A folded dishrag to keep a pot from sliding on a table aboard a ship that was sinking. If that's not—I don't know. I don't have words for what that is. But it's something. It's something human. It's the thing we do. The world is ending and you fold a dishrag under a coffee pot because the pot shouldn't slide. Because things should be where they belong.

We played every morning. We played even on the third morning. We would have played on the fourth.

---

The pumps stopped at a quarter past two on the afternoon of the third day. I know the time because I had just looked at the galley clock to see how long the bread had been in the oven. I was baking bread in a typhoon. I mention that because it's true and because I think it matters, though I couldn't tell you what it proves. Two loaves in the oven. I looked at the clock at two-thirteen and the pumps were running. I looked again at two-fifteen because the bread should have been done and the pumps were not running.

The quiet came in like a creature. It didn't just happen. It entered. It came through the deck and the bulkheads and it filled up the spaces where the thudding had been, and every other sound got louder around it—the hull, the water, the wind, the engine still running for no reason at all now—and I stood there with my hand on the oven door and I knew.

When I came up the companionway, Wen was already on deck.

She had line. I could see it coiled on the deck beside her, already cut to length, and I understood then that she had cut it before. Not that afternoon. Before. Days before, maybe. She had measured it and cut it and coiled it and set it aside the way she folded those letters back into their envelopes, with that neatness, that terrible ordinary neatness that meant the decision had been made a long time ago and everything since was just the working out of it.

She lashed herself to the wheel. Not the mast. I've said mast before—I said it to the man from the maritime board who asked me the first time, and I said it again to someone else after that, and it became mast in my memory because mast sounds right, mast is the story, the captain lashed to the mast like it's an old painting. But it wasn't the mast. It was the wheel. It was always the wheel. She put her back against the wheel housing and wrapped the line around her waist and through the spokes and she tied it off with a knot I could see was good from forty feet away. A bowline. Maybe two.

Her hands were steady. The deck was washing. The wind was the kind of wind that takes your hearing and replaces it with one long note. And her hands were steady. Not calm—I don't say calm, because I don't know what was happening inside her. Steady. The way hands are steady when they've done a thing ten thousand times. She handled that line like she was tying off at a dock on a clear day.

I was standing in the companionway. Half in, half out. The water was at my shins when the deck pitched towards the bow and at my ankles when it pitched back, and I stood there and I watched her, and she did not look back at me.

I need to say that again so you understand it.

She did not look back.

She finished the lashing and put her hands on the wheel and she looked forward, into the storm, into that wall of water and dark that was where the horizon used to be, and she did not turn around to see if I was there. She did not check whether I was going to the life raft. She did not wave me off. She did not call anything out to me. She looked forward.

I have turned that over more times than I can tell you. What it meant. Whether it was a kindness, her not looking—sparing me the having to respond to whatever I might have seen in her face. Whether it was just that I wasn't important enough to look at. Whether she even remembered I was there. Whether it would have changed something if she had turned around, if I had seen her eyes, if something would have passed between us that would have been—I don't know. A permission. An invitation. An apology.

I don't know. I didn't know her well enough to know and I knew her better than anyone alive.

---

The sky was very low. I can tell you that. It was the color of a bruise five days old, that green-grey-yellow, and it was close enough it felt like a ceiling, and the ocean was almost the same color reaching up to meet it, and the line between them was going. The last seam of light was to the west—a thin line, the width of your finger held out at arm's length, bright enough to hurt, like looking at a door closing from inside a dark room.

Wen was watching that line of light.

The water came over the bow steady now, not in waves but in a constant, like something the ship was agreeing to. The *Mara Celeste* was low in the water and getting lower. I could feel her under my feet, the way you feel a living thing that's tired, that's giving in, and it wasn't violent. That's what I want to say. It wasn't violent at the end. The violence was earlier. The end was quiet. The end was the ship settling into the water the way a person settles into a chair at the end of a day.

Wen looked at the horizon where the last light was going and she smiled.

I saw it. I was still in the companionway and the water was at my waist and I saw it. It wasn't a big smile. It was barely a movement. The corners of her mouth. The lines at her eyes. But I'd been watching that face across a card table every morning for eleven months and I knew it. I knew every version of that face—the focused version, the tired version, the version that read the bank letters, the version that checked the barometer. And this was the first time she had smiled since the letters started coming. Since Manila, maybe. Since before that.

It was not a peaceful smile. I won't give you that. It was not a woman at peace with the sea and God and the great beyond. It was the smile of a woman who had won. That's the only word. Won. The *Mara Celeste* was going down into the Sargasso and those men on the dock in Bermuda could stand there until their shoes rotted and they would never set foot on her grandfather's boat. Nobody was going to take it. Nobody was going to have it. It was hers and she was taking it with her and that was the smile—a hard, real, human smile, the smile of a person who has beaten you at something even if the price of beating you was everything.

I saw that smile and I understood that every morning of cards, every cup of coffee held in place by a dishrag, every pencil mark on the back of a provisions list, had been Wen's way of sitting inside her decision. She had decided before the typhoon. Maybe she had decided when the first letter came. The storm just gave her what she needed. The storm was not the enemy. The storm was the instrument. And the three days were not a fight. They were a watch. Her last watch. Ours.

The horizon took that last line of light, and the water came over the bow, and the *Mara Celeste* went down and it was like watching something breathe out for the last time.

That was the last watch of the *Mara Celeste*. I was the cook. I made her tea and it went cold on the chart room table. I played her cards and kept the coffee pot from sliding. I baked bread in the oven while the pumps were failing. I stood in the companionway and she did not look back.

I'm telling you this. I'm still telling you this.
