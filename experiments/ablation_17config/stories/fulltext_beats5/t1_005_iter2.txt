# The Shot Clock

## I.

The ball rack had fallen the way ball racks don't fall—backward, away from the weight, as if someone had pulled it down on top of themselves or someone else had shoved it into them with the force of a short, furious argument. The balls had rolled to their resting places across the hardwood with the patience of objects that do not know what has happened nearby, and by the time the Eastland Fury arrived for six a.m. practice, three of them had come to rest against Darren Kiersey's body like planets finding orbit around a cooling sun.

Amara Oyedele saw him first. She had come through the double doors at full push—she was always first through doors, first onto the floor, first to touch the ball, the kind of point guard whose internal clock started before anyone else's and ran slightly faster—and her chair was halfway to the free-throw line before her hands locked the wheels. The chair skidded two inches on the polyurethane. Behind her, she heard Devon's intake of breath, then Mattie's, then the slow rhythmic squeak of Soo-Yun's chair stopping well short of all of them, as though Soo-Yun had already understood from the geometry of everyone's stillness what was on the floor.

Darren Kiersey, forty-one years old, equipment manager for the Eastland Fury for the past six seasons, was dead in the key. His lanyard had twisted around his throat in a way that could have been incidental to any number of causes but looked, to anyone who paused long enough to look, deliberate. One hand still gripped a clipboard. His reading glasses had landed six feet from his face and the left lens was cracked in a starburst pattern that caught the gymnasium's overhead lights and threw a small, prismatic flare onto the baseline.

The police came. The paramedics came and were not needed. The athletic director came and made a phone call in the corridor that she clearly did not want overheard, and the omniscient record of that call—preserved here because it matters—includes the sentence, "I need this to not be about us." The gym was taped off. Practice was cancelled. The championship was in seventy-two hours.

By noon, a man named Russell Kovacs, formerly the head coach of Westridge Prep's wheelchair basketball program, fired two seasons ago amid allegations of recruiting violations, had confessed from a holding cell where he was already sitting on an unrelated disorderly conduct charge. He told the police he had driven to Eastland's gym the previous night, entered through the loading dock, confronted Kiersey about an old financial dispute, and strangled him with the lanyard when the confrontation turned physical. He described the cause of death—ligature asphyxiation with the woven nylon cord of an institutional lanyard—with a specificity that the detectives found convincing and the district attorney found sufficient. The case, they announced, was functionally closed before the body was fully cold.

The coroner's report was not released until 4:47 p.m.

Kovacs had confessed at 11:15 a.m.

Amara sat alone in the locker room at six o'clock that evening, in front of the bank of steel cubbies that smelled of adhesive tape and disinfectant, and the game clock on the scoreboard in the gym—visible through the propped-open door because no one had thought to close it, because this was a building that assumed it would be occupied by people doing normal things—was still running. It had been running since someone turned it on for practice that morning and no one had turned it off, and it now read 4:47:33, 4:47:34, 4:47:35, counting upward in a game that would never end because it had never begun.

She spoke to no one. She spoke to the room.

"The loading dock was chained at nine p.m. Security logs show it. Kovacs says he entered at ten. The access-card reader at the main entrance shows three swipes between eight and midnight—Darren's, Coach Walton's, and one from a student card that was reported lost in September. Kovacs doesn't have an access card. Kovacs says he strangled Darren with the lanyard, and Kovacs described the lanyard correctly, but the lanyard is standard issue—every school in the league has them, same supplier, same forest-green cord—and Kovacs described the cause of death five hours and thirty-two minutes before the coroner said the words out loud."

She stopped. She pressed her palms flat against her thighs, which were thin and still beneath the compression pants she wore to play, and her fingers spread wide, and she stared at the clock through the open door.

She did not say aloud—and would not have said aloud if the room had been full, and would not say aloud for another sixty-one hours—that three weeks ago Darren Kiersey had found her original classification paperwork in the filing cabinet he maintained with a diligence that bordered on compulsion, and that he had compared it with the paperwork submitted to the league, and that the two documents disagreed on the matter of her functional classification by a full two points. She did not say that Darren had confronted her quietly, after practice, in this very room, and told her that the discrepancy was either an error or a fraud and that he intended to report it to the league before the championship because that was, he said, simply what you did. She did not say that the reclassification was not her idea—that it had been arranged by a person she trusted, for reasons she had been told were administrative—or that she had agreed to it because agreeing was easier than asking questions and because the questions, if asked, would have led to answers that threatened everything: her place on the team, her scholarship, the version of herself that she had built in this gym over four years of practice and competition and the daily, grinding work of being extraordinary at something.

Her fear sat inside her chest like a hand gripping a ball before a free throw—fingers placed, weight centered, everything still, the whole body organized around the single question of whether to release.

She released nothing. She sat in the locker room and let the clock run.

What she did instead was investigate.

## II.

She called the meeting for the following morning, seven a.m., in the film room, which was a repurposed storage closet with a mounted screen and eight chairs and a whiteboard so old the surface had gone chalky in the corners. They came because she asked. She was their point guard. When she called a play, you ran it, and when she said *be somewhere*, you were there, and this fact had never been a decision anyone remembered making—it was simply the physics of the team, the understood gravitational order, Amara at the center, everyone else in orbit.

"We owe him this," she said.

She stood—she did not always use her chair; her condition permitted standing and walking for limited periods, which was, in fact, part of the problem with her classification, though no one in this room knew that yet—she stood at the whiteboard and uncapped a dry-erase marker and wrote DARREN across the top in block letters that were too large for the board, so that the final N ran off the edge and down the aluminum frame.

"The confession doesn't hold. I went through it last night. The timeline is broken, the access doesn't work, and the cause-of-death detail is impossible unless Kovacs had information he couldn't have had. Which means either he was there and the story is wrong, or he wasn't there and the whole thing is wrong. Either way, it's wrong."

She had prepared roles. She presented them with the brisk, tabulated certainty of a scouting report—the kind she distributed before every game, printed on green paper, each opponent reduced to tendencies, weaknesses, habits that could be exploited if you watched closely enough.

"Soo-Yun." The center. Six-foot-one seated, arms longer than anyone's on the team, the best rebounder in the conference, and—this was the reason Amara needed her—profoundly deaf since birth, a lip-reader of extraordinary precision, the kind of person who watched the world with the focused, unblinking attention of someone for whom every face was a text being read in real time. "You told me once that you saw Darren on the phone outside the gym Tuesday night. Through the window. I need you to reconstruct that call. Every word you caught."

Soo-Yun nodded. Her hands were folded in her lap. Her face was the face she wore during free throws—still, composed, the muscles around her eyes slightly tightened in concentration. She did not say—and the narration records it here with the same dispassionate precision with which it records everything—that she had already reconstructed the call in her memory within minutes of learning of Darren's death, that she had lain awake the previous night cycling through it frame by frame the way she cycled through game film, and that she knew the call was to Coach Marcus Walton, their head coach, and that among the phrases she had read on Darren's lips were *I know what you've been paying him* and *this is over* and *Friday*. She had decided, with the deliberate care of a person who understood that information was a kind of currency and that spending it rashly was a form of recklessness, to keep the coach's name out of her account until she understood why it belonged there. She said yes. She said she would try.

"Devon." The shooting guard. Slight, fast, a three-point specialist with a release so quick that opponents routinely failed to close on her in time. Her mother was a records clerk at Mercy General. "I need you to find out if Darren was treated at Mercy in the last month. Anything. An admission, an ER visit, anything."

Devon leaned back in her chair—the wheelchair, the everyday one, not the sport chair, her legs crossed at the ankle beneath a plaid blanket she kept draped over her lap in a habit she'd maintained since she was thirteen. Her jaw moved slightly, a lateral shift, the way it did when she was calculating whether to shoot or pass. She did not say that she already knew Darren had been at Mercy, because her mother had mentioned it three weeks ago in the offhand way her mother mentioned things—*your school's equipment guy came in last night, seemed upset, wouldn't say what happened*—and that Devon had filed this information in the back of her mind the way she filed defensive tendencies, waiting for the moment it became relevant. She did not say that she suspected the hospital records would show something about the nature of Darren's injuries that would be difficult to explain, and she did not say that she was frightened of what her mother might find. She said, "I'll ask tonight."

"Mattie."

Mattie Huang. The bench player. She had been on the team for two years and had logged a total of forty-seven minutes of game time, nearly all of it in the fourth quarter of games already decided. She was small—both in stature and in presence. Coaches forgot to call her name during drills. Opponents forgot to mark her on the rare occasions she entered a game. Administrators, custodians, teachers, and security guards looked past her in hallways with a consistency that could not be attributed to rudeness alone—she was simply the kind of person the human eye had learned to skip, the way the eye skips the gap between floorboards. She had spent two years navigating Eastland's building in a state of near-invisibility, and in that time she had learned the location of every maintenance corridor, every propped-open fire door, every bathroom where the lock didn't catch, every route through the school's backstage architecture. She fit into spaces that were not designed for wheelchairs and, when they were too narrow, she got out of her chair and crawled. She did this without embarrassment or complaint. She had never told anyone on the team about these routes because no one had ever asked.

"I need someone who can get into places," Amara said, and she looked at Mattie, and Mattie understood that she was being seen, perhaps for the first time since she'd joined this team, and the feeling was complicated—warm and frightening in equal measure—and she nodded.

She did not say that she had already been in Coach Walton's office. The previous afternoon, during the confusion following the discovery of the body, while the police were in the gym and the administrators were in the corridor and the team was in the locker room, Mattie had wheeled herself down the east hallway, past the booster-club trophy case, through the maintenance door that was always propped open on Tuesdays because the custodian, Gerald, brought in supply deliveries on Tuesdays and did not like making extra trips, and into the coach's office through the connecting door that opened into the equipment storage room. She had not planned to do this. She had simply gone where no one was watching, which was what she always did, and once she was inside the coach's office she had looked at the desk, and the filing cabinet, and the drawer that was slightly ajar, and inside the drawer she had found envelopes.

Eleven of them. White, letter-sized, unsealed. Each containing cash in denominations of fifty and one hundred. Each with a date written on the front in blue ballpoint—game dates, she realized, though she didn't know yet what the dates meant—and on the back of each envelope, in the same handwriting, a number. A score. Not the final score of the games, but close to it: a point spread, Mattie would later understand, a target, the margin by which a game was meant to be won or lost.

She had photographed each envelope, front and back, with her phone, and then returned them to the drawer, and pushed the drawer to the position in which she had found it—slightly ajar, exactly slightly ajar—and left through the same route she had come.

She told no one.

In the film room, she sat in the corner closest to the door, which was where she always sat, and she said, "What do you want me to find?"

"Anything in the gym that the police might have missed. The maintenance corridors. The coach's office if you can get in. Filing cabinets. Anything that connects Darren to someone other than this Kovacs person."

"Okay," Mattie said.

The room was quiet. The screen behind Amara showed a frozen frame of game tape—their opponent for the championship, Westridge Prep, a team that had been rebuilding since Russell Kovacs's firing, a team they were expected to beat—and in the frozen frame, a Westridge player was caught mid-shot, the ball suspended above her fingertips, the image slightly blurred by the pause, and no one in the room was looking at it.

They all said yes. They all held back. The narration, which has no loyalty to any one of them and which answers only to the architecture of what happened and why, notes that in this moment the Eastland Fury was performing teamwork with the seamless coordination of athletes who had spent years learning to move as one body—and that the performance was, like all performances, constructed on top of the things the performers chose not to reveal. A team is a system of trust. Trust is a system of incomplete information. They trusted each other because they did not know each other entirely, and the investigation Amara had just launched would, within forty-eight hours, change that permanently.

The whiteboard said DARREN in letters that ran off the edge, and no one erased it, and the marker was still uncapped, and the room smelled faintly of its chemical evaporation, and they left one by one to do what they had agreed to do.

## III.

Thirty-six hours before the championship game, Soo-Yun went back to the window.

It was the window at the east end of the corridor outside the gym—a tall, wire-reinforced pane that looked onto the sidewalk where the ramp met the parking lot. She had been passing this window on Tuesday evening, on her way back from the athletic trainer's office, when she had seen Darren standing outside under the sodium light with his phone pressed to his ear and his free hand cutting the air in the sharp, emphatic gestures of a person losing an argument.

She had stopped. She had watched. This was not unusual for Soo-Yun. Watching was her primary mode of engagement with a world that had never offered her sound. She had been reading lips since she was four years old, and the skill had become, over the years, less a compensatory technique than a kind of fluency—she read faces the way hearing people read tone, with an intuitive, almost musical sensitivity to rhythm, emphasis, and the small muscular betrayals that accompanied deception. She could tell when a person was lying not because she caught the lie but because she saw the face *working* to produce it, the tiny lag between intention and expression, the way the mouth shaped words it did not fully believe.

Darren's face, under the sodium light, had not been lying. It had been terrified and resolute, which is a combination that is difficult to fake and unmistakable when it is genuine. She had read:

*I don't care what it costs you.*

And: *I know what you've been paying him.*

And: *It ends Friday or I go to the league.*
