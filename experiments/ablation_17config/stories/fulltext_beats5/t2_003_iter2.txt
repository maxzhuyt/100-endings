# Perdition Gulch

## I.

Here is the town.

It sits in a depression between two ridges of friable red stone, and depending on the season it is either drowning in dust or baking in a stillness so absolute that the heat becomes a sound—a low, tidal hum that rises from the ground and presses against the undersides of roofs. Forty-seven occupied structures. A well that has never gone dry, though no one can account for the aquifer. A single road in, the same road out. Population: enough to need a post office; not enough to need a sheriff. This has been, for twenty-three years, a matter of deliberate design.

It is a Tuesday. The month is either September or October—already the narrator must confess a certain imprecision about things that do not matter, to better persuade you of precision about things that do.

In the long low building at the eastern end of Main Street that serves as land office, deed registry, and, on Saturdays, a kind of court, Alma Royce is settling a dispute between two men who have drawn their property lines through the same stand of cottonwoods. The men are not calm. One has a hand on his belt, close enough to the hip that the gesture is a grammar everyone in Perdition Gulch can read. Alma sits between them on a cane-back chair, legs crossed at the ankle, eyes half-closed, and recites.

She recites the deed of Thomas Fenton, filed November ninth, eighteen hundred and fifty-one, and she recites it complete: the surveyor's hash marks, the directional bearings, the clerk's margin note about a blotted seal requiring re-impression. She recites the deed of Samuel Obst, filed three months later, and where it abuts Fenton's she produces the discrepancy from her mouth with the flat, inarguable authority of typeset. The men listen. The man with his hand on his belt lets the hand drift down to his thigh, then to his knee, then into his lap, where it rests with the sudden uselessness of a tool returned to its hook. They accept her verdict. They always do. To argue with Alma Royce you would have to argue with the original document, and Alma Royce *is* the original document, and the copy, and the amendments, and the recording clerk's breakfast that morning, if she chose to include it.

Across town—simultaneously, which is a word the narrator uses with full knowledge of its weight—the healer is pulling a bullet from his own left shoulder.

His name is George Ware, and his hands are steady in the way that a man's hands are steady when pain has been, for most of his adult life, a kind of weather: always present, shifting only in intensity, not meaningfully different from rain. The bullet is a .36 caliber ball, fired two hours ago by a man who no longer remembers firing it, because the man is dead, and because George Ware absorbed the dying, too, walking toward the drawn pistol as one walks into a river, letting the damage break around him and through him and find, somewhere in the architecture of his body, a room to occupy. He does not heal others so much as he inherits their suffering. The bullet entered him after he stepped between the shooter and a boy of fourteen carrying a flour sack across the road. The boy is home now, unaware. George sits in the back room of his own house, a pair of needle-nose pliers in his right hand, a rag between his teeth, and operates on himself because to ask for help would require explaining why the wound does not behave the way wounds behave—why the flesh around it is already closing, pushing the ball toward the surface like a splinter the body knows does not belong.

He drops the slug into a tin cup. It rings once. He flexes the shoulder, watches the entry wound seal to a puckered line, and reaches for a clean shirt. He does not find one. He puts on the same shirt, badge of blood and all, because in forty minutes he is due at the saloon to meet two men from the Central Pacific's surveying division who wish to discuss the possibility of a spur line through the gulch, and George Ware is the only person in Perdition Gulch who can sit across from men like that and make them feel, in a way they cannot articulate and will not remember clearly afterward, that the cost of pressing the matter will be extracted from their own bodies. They will have headaches when they leave. They will feel a heaviness in their limbs, a half-memory of pain they never experienced. They will file a report recommending an alternate route. They will not know why.

On the porch of the general store, the child sits.

Her name is June, and she is twelve, and she has been twelve for what feels to her like a very long time—not because she does not age but because childhood, when you can hear the inside of every skull within two hundred yards, is not an experience that tracks with calendars. She sits with her legs swinging above the dust, a stick of horehound candy in her cheek, and she watches Mrs. Ελένη Papadimitriou examine a bolt of calico with two hands and a heart full of theft. Mrs. Papadimitriou does not know the child can hear her. No one does, except the council. June turns her head a quarter inch toward the shopkeeper, Mr. Feld, who is stacking tinned peaches with his back to the room, and she whispers: *The blue calico. She'll tuck it under the shawl when you turn to the coffee grinder.*

Mr. Feld does not turn to the coffee grinder. He asks Mrs. Papadimitriou if she'd like two yards measured and cut, and she says yes, and pays for it with coin, and the theft dissolves like a weather pattern breaking up before it reaches the ground. June moves the horehound candy to the other cheek. She does not feel proud. She feels the way a gate feels, if a gate could feel: always open, always admitting traffic, always in the path.

And at the two ends of Main Street, facing each other across the full length of the town—one seated on the bench outside the livery, one standing at the window of the boarding house with a cup of coffee she has not sipped—the Ybarra twins maintain the ceaseless, silent exchange that has been, for as long as anyone in Perdition Gulch can remember, the town's peripheral nervous system.

Constance and Cordelia. They have different faces, different heights, different ways of entering a room—Constance leading with her shoulders, Cordelia with her hands—but when one thinks, the other finishes. Not the sentence. The thought. They are, in the language the narrator might use if it were being precise rather than poetic, a single consciousness housed in two bodies, and what this means for Perdition Gulch is that the town has no blind spots. Constance sees a man she doesn't recognize hitch his horse outside the saloon; Cordelia, six hundred yards away, knows the man's posture, his dust-line, the way his coat falls wrong over the right hip, and she has already left the boarding house and is walking toward the saloon before Constance has finished the thought *armed, unfamiliar, moving with intent.* They converge on problems the way the two halves of a jaw converge on food. The town is chewed and swallowed and digested by their attention, and the town does not know it, and the town is safe.

This is the inventory. This is what holds.

And the narrator offers it to you the way a doctor reads vitals aloud in a calm and ordered voice, not because the patient is well but because the reading of vitals is a ritual that keeps the doctor's hands from shaking, and the doctor's hands must not shake, because what comes next—

But not yet.

For now: the five of them, performing the functions that keep one hundred and six people alive in a landscape that would prefer them dead, and performing those functions with the particular exhaustion of people who have been load-bearing for so long that they have forgotten the distinction between what they can do and what they owe. Alma's perfect memory is the law. George's body is the wall. June's invaded mind is the watch. The twins' shared self is the net. Remove any one and the structure does not gradually weaken. It fails completely, the way a bridge does not lose a cable and sag but loses a cable and falls.

(Once, years ago, in the arroyo south of town, the bridge did fall. Or rather: the bridge had not yet been built, and what happened in the arroyo is the reason it was built, and the reason it was built on guilt instead of stone. But the narrator is not ready to say more, and you are not yet ready to hear it, and these two unreadinesses are more similar than you think.)

It is Alma who notices first.

She is in her house that evening, alone with her journals—sixty-eight clothbound volumes lining the walls of a room she does not let others enter—and she is recording the day's deed dispute as she has recorded every event in her life since the age of nineteen: from memory, complete, without error, in a hand so consistent it could be mistaken for typeset. She is writing the word *cottonwood* and she stops.

The word has left her.

Not the word on the page—that remains, in her handwriting, half-finished. The word in her mind. The tree. She can see it: the pale bark, the shivering leaves, the way the roots buckle the ground. But the *name* is a white space, a gap in the catalog, and she is staring at that gap the way you stare at a place on a shelf where an object has always been and now is not.

It comes back. Three seconds, perhaps four. *Cottonwood.* There it is. She writes the remaining letters.

She does not tell anyone.

She sits with her pen above the journal and she feels the thing she knows she must not feel, the thing the narrator sees and does not name, because to name it now would be to intervene, and the narrator does not intervene—has not intervened—will not—

Relief. What Alma Royce feels, in the space where the word should have been, is relief. A gap in the record. A moment of silence in a life that has never, not once, not for an instant, known forgetting. The word returned, yes. But for four seconds she was no one's archive. For four seconds the deed of Thomas Fenton did not exist inside her skull, and neither did anything else, and the emptiness was as vast and clean as the desert at dawn, and she wanted to stay in it the way a woman dying of thirst wants to stay in water.

She closes the journal. She caps the ink. The candle on her desk gutters briefly—though the room is sealed, and no window has been opened, and no wind has entered.

The narrator notes this. The narrator notes everything. The narrator, if it were the sort of entity given to self-reflection, might recognize in Alma's relief a mirror of its own impossible position: to be the one who remembers, to be the one who cannot stop, to understand that the remembering is the sickness and the cure at once and to go on remembering anyway, because—

Because.

Here is the town. The vitals are stable. The patient is dying. Let us proceed.

## II.

Depending on the year you imagine, they were settlers.

A group of eleven, maybe fourteen, in two wagons that had come overland from a route nobody in the territory would have recommended, because nobody in the territory had been asked, because the group had been told—by a man in St. Louis, or Westport, or Council Bluffs, the origin shifts—that the southern pass through the ridges would cut three weeks off the journey, and what they had not been told, because the man did not know or did not care, was that the arroyo at the base of that pass was a place where certain other parties conducted certain kinds of business that did not benefit from witnesses.

Depending on the year you imagine, they were Comanche. A hunting party, seven or eight, trailing pronghorn into a wash where they had no reason to expect violence because this had been, in the grammar of the land, their wash, their pronghorn, their passage, for longer than the territory had been a territory or the nation had been a nation.

Depending on the year you imagine, they were Chinese rail workers. Twelve men released from a Central Pacific crew two hundred miles west, walking toward a rumor of silver wages in the mountains, carrying nothing but the clothes and the English they had and the knowledge that both were insufficient and the determination that insufficiency was not the same as impossibility.

It has depended and continues to depend.

What does not depend—what is fixed, the way the arroyo itself is fixed in the landscape, a scar the water cuts and the dust fills and the water cuts again—is what the five of them saw.

They were not yet a council. They were not yet anything to each other. They were five people living in or near a settlement that did not yet have a name, each carrying a capacity they had told no one about, each maintaining solitude as a species of camouflage, and on the morning in question each was, for their own reasons, within sight of the arroyo.

Alma was walking the ridge with a surveyor's map she had memorized and was correcting in her head, noting every error the way a musician notes every wrong pitch in an orchestra.

George was down in the wash itself, a quarter mile east of where it would happen, gathering mud-clay for a poultice he did not need but made as performance, the way he performed all his healing—slowly, with herbs and cloths, so that no one would notice the real work happening beneath.

June was nine years old and had followed a javelina and was crouched behind a tumble of sandstone with her hands over her ears, as she often was, because the javelina's mind was louder than she'd expected, all hunger and heat and directionless aggression, and she was trying to get the animal out of her head when the human minds arrived and were worse.

The twins were sixteen and had climbed the opposite ridge to watch the sunrise and were sharing the sunrise between them, which is to say that Constance was watching the eastern sky and Cordelia was watching the western sky and together they held the full horizon in a single continuous perception, and what entered that perception from the northwest was a column of riders moving with the particular unhurried speed of men who know exactly what they are about to do.

The riders came into the arroyo. The other party—whoever they were, in whatever year you are imagining—was already there. What happened next took between four and eleven minutes, depending on which of the five you later asked, and none of the five was later asked, because none of the five ever spoke of it in full, not even to each other, not even in the years when their shared guilt was the mortar that held the council together.

Here is what the narrator can tell you, assembled from fragments that will continue to surface throughout this account like bones the desert pushes up after rain:

There was shouting. Then there was not. The interval between shouting and not-shouting was occupied by sounds the narrator will not reproduce, because the reproduction of certain sounds is not narration but something else, something closer to the original violence, and the narrator has committed enough sins already.

The riders left. In the arroyo, there was stillness of the kind that only follows annihilation—not silence but the *negative space* where sound has been amputated.

And the five of them, from their separate vantage points, each performed the same calculation. Each arrived at the same answer. And the calculation was this:

Alma could have run. She had memorized the face of every territorial marshal within four hundred miles—their names, their jurisdictions, the locations of their offices. She could have provided testimony so detailed, so irrefutable, that it would have constituted not a witness account but a *reconstruction*. She could have built the crime in a courtroom the way God built the world: from perfect memory, leaving nothing out.

George could have walked into the wash. The bullets would have entered him and he would have stood and the bullets would have continued to enter him and he would have continued to stand and the shooters—even men like that, men for whom killing had become a kind of employment—would have faltered, because there is a limit to what even brutalized men can do to a body that will not fall. He could have absorbed every shot. He would have suffered. He would have lived. Some of the others might have, too.

June could have screamed. Not a child's scream, not a scream of fear, but the particular scream she had never screamed and had always known she could: the scream of *what the killers intended*, broadcast not from her throat but from her mind, a psychic alarm that would have—might have—reached the settlement, reached someone, alerted anyone.
