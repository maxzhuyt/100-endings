# THE SUTTON COUNTY TRANSCRIPTS

---

## Compiled and annotated manuscript, provenance: Sutton County Clerk's Office, General Filing, Box 1149-C

---

**To whoever is assigned to file this, or to read it, or to discard it:**

I have assembled the following materials in the order I believe makes them most legible. They include: excerpts from the official trial transcript of *State of Arkansas v. Eleanor Vance* (Docket No. 87-CR-4421, Sutton County Circuit Court, the Honorable R. Aldiss Whitmore presiding); my personal audio diary entries, transcribed by my own hand from cassette recordings made between September 14 and December 6, 1987; photocopied personnel and public records obtained through lawful request; one page from *A Concordance of Latin Liturgical Texts, Vol. III: Monastic Rites and Ordinations* (University of Arkansas Press, 1961); and my own marginalia, reproduced where it appeared, in context.

I have deposited copies of this manuscript with the Sutton County Clerk's Office, the Arkansas State Bar Association's Committee on Judicial Conduct, and a post office box rented in Fayetteville under a name I do not disclose here.

I do not claim to be in danger. I do not claim the Latin is real, or that it is not. I have rendered the record as faithfully as I know how. A stenographer does not interpret. A stenographer records. What is done with the record is not her office.

I ask only that the record survive.

**AUDIO DIARY — ENTRY 1**
**Recorded: September 14, 1987, 5:47 p.m.**
**Location: Parking garage, Sutton County Courthouse**

&nbsp;

This is Carol Gibson. I'm recording this in my car, level two of the garage. Engine off. I can hear the elevator shaft through the wall — that grinding sound it makes. I want a record of this case from the start because I was not the original assignment and I want it noted, for my own purposes, how I came to it.

I was pulled from *Garner v. Garner*, a custody matter, at ten o'clock this morning. Helen in the clerk's office handed me the reassignment sheet and told me Mrs. Dreher had taken sudden medical leave. I asked what was wrong. Helen said she didn't know, that Mrs. Dreher had called in Friday afternoon and that was that. I asked if anyone had spoken with her. Helen gave me the look she gives and said, "Carol, the case starts in forty-five minutes."

So I went.

Courtroom B. The old one, with the balcony they closed off in '79. It smelled like wet plaster, the way it does when the humidity gets into those walls. The AC unit behind the bench was rattling, and Judge Whitmore had someone from maintenance in there trying to fix it before we started. It wasn't fixed. It rattled all day. My steno mask picked it up on every take, this low hum underneath the testimony, like the room itself was producing a frequency.

The defendant is Eleanor Vance, forty-one years old, employed as — well, formerly employed as — a child care provider. Licensed home day care out of a house on Pryor Road. Three counts of first-degree murder. Three children. I'm not going to describe the charges here. They're in the transcript. What I will say is that the prosecution's theory involves — and I am choosing my words carefully because I want to be precise — it involves allegations of ritualistic practice. Occult activity. That's the word Mr. Pike used in his opening. *Occult*.

Eleanor Vance does not look like anything. She looks like she could work in a bank. She looks like someone who might sit next to you at a church supper and ask how your mother was doing. She was wearing a beige cardigan that was too big for her, and she kept her hands flat on the table in front of her the entire morning session, palms down, fingers spread, as if she were checking whether the table was still solid.

The first day was mostly procedural. Jury instructions. Pike's opening. Odom's opening, which was brief and not good. Then prosecution witness one, a forensic technician, and the cross-exam.

I want to talk about what happened during cross.

Odom was asking the technician about the condition of the house — the physical site. Whether the markings described in the police report could have predated Mrs. Vance's tenancy. It was a reasonable question. The technician was hedging. And then Eleanor stood up.

She didn't scream. I want to be clear about that. She stood and she spoke in a normal volume, not shouting, and what she said was sustained and phonetically consistent and I captured every syllable of it on my steno take. Every syllable. My hands did what they always do. They kept up.

Judge Whitmore gaveled her down. Odom pulled her back into her seat. Whitmore instructed me to strike the outburst and render it as unintelligible. His exact words: "The record will reflect an unintelligible outburst. The witness is admonished."

She wasn't a witness. She was the defendant. He called her "the witness."

I rendered it as instructed:

&nbsp;

---

[*Excerpt from official transcript, p. 34:*]

&nbsp;

THE COURT: Let the record reflect — counsel, control your client.

MR. ODOM: Eleanor — your Honor, I apologize —

THE COURT: The record will reflect an unintelligible outburst. The defendant is admonished. The jury will disregard. Ms. Gibson, strike the preceding.

COURT REPORTER: Yes, your Honor.

[UNINTELLIGIBLE OUTBURST — DEFENDANT ADMONISHED]

&nbsp;

---

I struck it, as directed. It is struck. The official record reads as it reads.

But I am making this recording because what she said was not unintelligible. My hands knew it. My tape knew it. If the word means "cannot be made intelligible" then the word does not apply, because I made it intelligible. I got every syllable. I wrote it in my personal shorthand in the margin of my steno paper, in pencil, before I left the courtroom, because I did not want to lose it.

I'm looking at it now. It doesn't look like English. It doesn't look like any language I recognize. But it has structure. Repeating phonemes. What sounds like conjugation. It has the rhythm of someone saying something *to* someone.

I don't know what it means. That isn't my job. My job is to record.

But I want it noted: it was not unintelligible. It was not struck because it was meaningless. It was struck because the judge decided the meaning didn't belong in the record.

Those are different things.

I'm going home. I have leftover soup and the first two days of *Garner v. Garner* to finalize before the reassignment goes through. The elevator is still grinding. I don't know why they don't fix it.

This is Carol Gibson, September 14, 5:47 p.m. End of entry.

**AUDIO DIARY — ENTRY 4**
**Recorded: October 1, 1987, 12:22 p.m.**
**Location: Ladies' room, second floor, Sutton County Courthouse**

&nbsp;

Lunchbreak. I'm in the second stall with the door locked and I feel ridiculous making a recording in a bathroom but I don't want to wait because I'll lose the details.

She looked at me. I keep going back to that. When everyone else has an outburst they look at the judge or the jury or they look at the ceiling or they close their eyes. She turned in her chair and looked directly at the stenographer's station. At *my* hands.

The phoneme string today was longer by roughly twelve seconds. Same initial cluster — the hard "k" followed by the aspirated vowel — but then a new section I haven't heard before. Three distinct syllable groups. I got them. They're in the notebook.

I want to compare all three instances side by side tonight. I think there's a progression. It's not the same phrase repeated. It's — I don't know. Accumulating.

I need to eat something. I haven't eaten.

End of entry.

**AUDIO DIARY — ENTRY 7**
**Recorded: October 9, 1987, 3:14 a.m.**
**Location: Home, kitchen table**

&nbsp;

[*Sound of tape rewinding on a second device. Click. Playback of previous audio diary entry, distorted, at half speed.*]

I've been up since one. I moved the tape deck from the bedroom to the kitchen because I kept getting up anyway and I didn't want to pretend I was going to sleep.

I've laid out all three phoneme sequences on the table. Written in my shorthand, then transliterated into approximated Roman characters. I've been reading them aloud. I've been — I know how this sounds. I've been reversing them.

[*Pause. Sound of paper being arranged.*]

The first thing I noticed was the vowel pattern. When you read the second sequence backward — syllable by syllable, not letter by letter, *syllable* by syllable — the stressed vowels form a pattern I recognized from somewhere. I couldn't place it. It bothered me all week. Today, sitting in the courtroom while Pike was doing his redirect, it came to me. My grandmother's funeral. The priest singing something. That rise and fall. I thought I was imagining it, so I came home and I tried something.

I played Entry 4 — the one where I read the second sequence aloud — backward. On the second tape deck, just physical reversal, holding the reels.

[*Long pause.*]

It's Latin. Played backward, read in reversed syllable order, the second sequence is Latin. I don't — I took French in high school. I don't speak Latin. But I know what Latin sounds like, and I know what conjugated verbs sound like, and this has both.

I wrote it out. I'm going to the library tomorrow. The university extension in Ridgeline has a reference section. There has to be a Latin dictionary, a concordance, something.

[*Pause.*]

I also need to say this, for the record: I am aware that I'm sitting alone in my kitchen at three in the morning playing tape recordings backward and claiming to find Latin. I am aware of how that sounds. I am aware that the prosecution's entire case rests on the claim that Eleanor Vance engaged in ritualistic behavior, and that I am now — in the privacy of my own home, on my own time — engaging in exactly the kind of pattern-seeking that Mr. Pike would call evidence of susceptibility.

I am aware of that.

But my hands captured what they captured. The tapes contain what they contain. I am not interpreting. I am transliterating. There is a difference.

End of entry.

**AUDIO DIARY — ENTRY 8**
**Recorded: October 10, 1987, 11:38 p.m.**
**Location: Home, kitchen table**

&nbsp;

I found it.

The Sutton County Library, not the university extension — the extension was closed for Columbus Day, I'd forgotten — but the county branch on Maple had a reference copy of *A Concordance of Latin Liturgical Texts*, three volumes, University of Arkansas Press, 1961. I almost laughed when I saw the press. I'm in Arkansas, finding Latin in Arkansas, and the concordance was published in Arkansas. That doesn't mean anything. I know it doesn't mean anything. But I noticed it.

Volume Three. Monastic rites and ordinations.

The reversed second sequence, when I cleaned up my transliteration, was close enough to match an entry on page 412. I photocopied the page. I'm looking at it now.

It's from a rite of *ordinatio*. The concordance translates this as a consecration — a rite of selection and appointment. Specifically, it's from a variant attributed to a North African monastic community, fifth or sixth century, a rite for *choosing* someone. Not ordaining a priest. Earlier than that. Selecting the person who *would be* ordained. The concordance notes that the rite was unusual because the selection was not made by the community or the abbot. The selection was made by — the concordance says "by the office itself." The role chose its occupant.

It is not satanic. It is not a curse. It is not anything the prosecution has described in its case against Eleanor Vance. It is a consecration. An *appointment*.

[*Long silence. Approximately fifteen seconds.*]

I need to say something else.

The third sequence — the one from Day 6, the longest one, with the five syllable groups — I've only partially decoded it. The first two groups match the *ordinatio* structure. Groups three and four are — I think they're a naming formula. The concordance shows a pattern where the rite inserts the name of the selected person, Latinized, in the vocative case. A direct address.

[*Silence. Seven seconds.*]

It has my name in it. The second phrase. It has my name in it.

[*
