# Form 27-B: Application for Continued Existence

## I.

The error was on line 4,096.

Gladys Thibodaux found it on a Tuesday—or what the colony ship *Adastra* designated a Tuesday, which was to say a day on which the corridor lighting shifted toward a slightly warmer kelvin and the cafeteria served reconstituted scrambled eggs instead of reconstituted oatmeal. It had been a Tuesday for seventeen years, every seventh rotation, and Gladys had spent every one of those Tuesdays the same way: in the records bay on Deck 11, surrounded by filing cabinets that hummed faintly at a frequency just below human hearing, indexing the documents that constituted, in the legal and ontological sense recognized by the governing cosmic authorities, humanity's continued right to exist.

There were many such documents. The Species Continuity Extension alone ran to forty-seven pages, each requiring signatures from the captain, the chief medical officer, and a notary whose license was recognized by at least three non-terrestrial administrative bodies. The Biological Substrate Verification required cellular samples from no fewer than two hundred living humans, catalogued by phenotype, filed in septuplicate, and annotated with a chemical-composition summary cross-referenced against the Auditors' Periodic Table, which contained eleven elements not found in human science and omitted carbon entirely from its noble listings. The Transit Corridor Occupancy Permit had to be renewed every 4.7 standard galactic cycles, which did not map onto any human unit of time, and which Gladys had learned to calculate using a formula she kept taped to the side of Filing Cabinet 7 in handwriting so small it resembled, from a distance, a prayer.

She had done all of this correctly. She had done all of this correctly for seventeen years. She had maintained the filing schedule, met every deadline, submitted every form through the ship's quantum-entangled document relay—a device the size of an industrial refrigerator that the Auditors had installed in the cargo bay during the initial processing of Earth, which accepted documents fed into a slot on its left side and, after a grinding pause that lasted between nine seconds and three weeks, returned a stamped receipt from a slot on its right side. The receipts were in a language no one on the ship could read, but they were consistent, and they came, and Gladys had filed every one of them in chronological order in Cabinet 12, Drawer 3, subdivided by form type and cross-indexed by galactic cycle date.

She had done all of this correctly. She had done all of this using Form 27-B.

Line 4,096 of Form 27-B read: *Applicant substrate classification (select one):iteite-ite mineralite-compositeite-ite-hybrid crystalline-ite-ferrous-it-it-composite-it-it-mineral-deposit-standard.*

There was no option for carbon-based life.

There was no option for carbon-based life because Form 27-B was not designed for carbon-based life. Form 27-B—full title: *Application for Species Continuity Extension (Silicon-Based Biomes, Non-Sapient or Pre-Sapient, Mineral and Mineral-Adjacent Substrates)*—was for rocks. It was, in the most generous possible reading, for entities that were to human beings what a gravel quarry was to a cathedral. And Gladys had been filing it, in septuplicate, with full notarization, at every required interval, for seventeen years.

Three thousand human beings had been traveling toward a sanctuary planet, at a velocity that compressed their experience of time into something manageable but made communication with any fixed point in the galaxy a bureaucratic impossibility, classified in the records of the governing cosmic authority as a mineral deposit in transit.

Gladys sat at her desk in the records bay and looked at line 4,096 for a long time. The filing cabinet next to her hummed. The lighting was warm. It was a Tuesday.

Then she called the captain.

---

Captain Aaron Wyckoff was a man who had been, before the processing of Earth, a naval aviator, a test pilot, and briefly and unhappily a congressional aide. He had been selected to command the *Adastra* not because of any particular qualification in interstellar navigation or species-level crisis management but because, during the seventy-two hours between the Auditors' initial notice of processing and the completion of that processing, he had been the highest-ranking military officer who had (a) not gone catatonic, (b) not attempted to launch nuclear weapons at an entity that existed partially outside of three-dimensional space, and (c) been within driving distance of the launch facility in what had been, for another few hours, west Texas. He was a competent man. He was a serious man. He had held three thousand people together through seventeen years of travel toward a planet none of them had seen, through grief that had no geography and loss that had no shore, and he had done it by believing,eli every day, that the journey was real and the destination was reachable and the paperwork was someone else's department.

He arrived in the records bay in eleven minutes. He was a tall man. He had to duck to enter. He found Gladys standing in front of Filing Cabinet 7 with a look he had seen only once before—on the face of a radar operator who had just realized that what he was tracking was not a weather system.

"Show me," he said.

She showed him.

He read line 4,096. He read the full title of Form 27-B. He read the substrate classification options. He looked at the filing cabinets—all fourteen of them, spanning seventeen years, containing every document that established humanity's legal existence in the eyes of the only authority that had ever demonstrated the capacity to end it.

"This can't matter," he said.

Gladys looked at him with an expression that combined the patience of a woman who had spent twenty-three years at DPS Window 9 in Lubbock, Texas, with the pity of a woman who understood that the man in front of her had never, not once in his life, been told that his registration was invalid because he had checked the wrong box on a form he had not been told existed.

"Captain," she said. "I once watched a man lose his vehicle because he wrote 'blue-green' instead of 'teal' in the color field."

"That's a car."

"It's a system."

"This is a different—"

"It's always a system."

She pulled a second document from the drawer she'd been searching when she found the error. It was thinner than Form 27-B. The paper—or the material that resembled paper, produced by the document relay in the cargo bay—had a slightly different weight, a slightly different sheen, as if it had been printed on stock from a supply chain that no longer reached the ship's location.

"Form 27-B-Revised," she said. "Application for Species Continuity Extension—Carbon-Based Sapient Populations, Self-Aware, with Cultural and Biological Archive Requirements."

"How long has that existed?"

"It was included in the original document package the Auditors provided during processing. Page three thousand, four hundred and twelve of the supplementary appendix. Subheading nine."

"And no one—"

"No one."

The captain looked at the form. It was, in most respects, identical to Form 27-B. It asked the same questions in the same order in the same language that had required four years of collective human effort to decode. But line 4,096 read differently: *Applicant substrate classification (select one): carbon-based-sapient carbon-based-sapient-with-archive carbon-based-sapient-with-archive-and-cultural-appendix carbon-based-sapient-emergency-provisional.*

"What happens," the captain said slowly, "if they realize what we've been filing?"

"I don't know."

"What do you think happens?"

Gladys had, in her years at Window 9, seen what happened when the system discovered a misclassification. She had watched a woman learn that her deceased husband's estate had been processed under the wrong tax code for eleven years and that the state of Texas considered this not the state's error but the estate's fraud. She had watched a man learn that his commercial driver's license had been issued to a different person with the same social security number and that resolving this would require him to prove, using documents he did not have, that he was himself. She had watched the system do what systems do: apply the rules as written, without malice, without pity, without any mechanism for recognizing that the rules, as written, could not accommodate the situation before them.

"I think," she said, "that we have been a mineral deposit for seventeen years, and mineral deposits do not get reclassified. They get *mined.*"

The captain's jaw tightened. It was the expression he used when the recycling filters failed and the oxygen mix shifted two percent toward unbreathable, the expression that meant *I understand the problem and I reject its authority over us.*

"Then we don't tell them," he said.

"Captain—"

"We keep filing what we've been filing. The forms are accepted. The receipts come back. We are seventeen years into a approved journey. Whatever box we checked, they approved it."

Gladys looked at him with something that was not quite disagreement and not quite pity and not quite the expression she had worn every day for twenty-three years when she had tried to explain to someone at Window 9 that the rules were not a conversation.

"They approved a mineral deposit," she said. "Mineral deposits don't apply for sanctuary. Mineral deposits don't request habitability permits. When we arrive, Captain, and we file the next form—the landing form, the occupancy form, whatever they call it—we will file it as three thousand carbon-based sapient beings, and the file will say we are a silicon-based mineral deposit, and there won't be two versions of us. There will be an error. And errors get—"

"Corrected?"

"Processed."

They looked at each other in the warm Tuesday light. The filing cabinets hummed. Somewhere in the level below them, in the communal bay where families had made seventeen years of constrained metal into something that resembled life, children were at lessons. One child—the captain knew her name, everyone on a ship of three thousand knows every child's name—was not at lessons. She was sitting in a corner of the bridge, where the duty officer let her sit because she was quiet and because her drawings, scattered around her like shed feathers, made the sterile space feel briefly like a room in which someone lived. She was drawing the filing cabinets. She had never seen them, but she drew them anyway—tall rectangles with thin dark drawers, humming, she said when anyone asked, because they were thinking.

Neither Gladys nor the captain was thinking about the child.

Gladys was thinking about Form 27-B-Revised and the seventeen years of filings that would need to be amended and the process for amendment, which she did not yet know but which she knew, with the certainty of a woman whose life had been spent in the space between a rule and its application, would be the only thing that mattered.

"I need to refile everything," she said. "From the beginning."

"You need—"

"Seventeen years. Every form. Every attachment. Every notarized supplement. The substrate classification has to be corrected on every filing, and each correction has to be submitted as an amendment, and each amendment has to reference the original filing, and if there's an amendment form—"

"Gladys."

"—which there will be, because there is always an amendment form—"

"*Gladys.*"

She stopped.

"Is this what will keep us alive?" the captain asked.

It was, she understood, the only question he knew how to ask. He was a man who maintained a ship and the three thousand lives inside it. He thought in terms of survival, of trajectory, of whether and how to continue. The distinction between Form 27-B and Form 27-B-Revised was, to him, a matter of life and death—and he was right—but it was also something else, something he did not have the training to see. The forms were not about life and death. The forms were about *category.* They determined not whether humanity would survive but *what humanity would survive as.* A mineral deposit could survive indefinitely. It could travel the galaxy. It could occupy a planet. It could exist, in the administrative sense, forever. But it could not be *human.* It could not have a cultural archive, a biological record, a continuity that recognized what it was and insisted on being recognized in return. The wrong form had kept them alive for seventeen years, but it had kept alive something that was not, in any sense the universe recognized, them.

Gladys did not say any of this. She was not a woman who made speeches. She was a woman who filed forms.

"I'll need the captain's signature," she said. "On every amendment."

"How many?"

She looked at the cabinets.

"All of them," she said.

## II.

The summons arrived on a Wednesday.

It materialized in the ship's communal printer—a device that, until that moment, had been used exclusively to print meal schedules, lesson plans, and the occasional birthday banner—at 0347 ship-time, unaccompanied by any data transmission the communications officer could identify. It printed on standard ship paper, in standard ship ink, in a font that did not exist in the ship's typographic library. It was triple-stamped, each stamp appearing to have been applied physically to the paper, with ink that was a slightly different shade of not-quite-purple and that did not smear when touched.

It read:

>**INTAKE PROCESSING NODE 7734**
>**RE: SPECIES CONTINUITY EXTENSION — PETITION NO. 00000000000000000000000000000000000000000000000000000000003**
>**APPLICANT: [MINERAL DEPOSIT / SEE FILE — RECLASSIFICATION PENDING]**
>
>Your presence is requested for administrative review of the above-referenced petition, including but not limited to: substrate reclassification query, filing amendment reconciliation, habitability pre-assessment, and miscellaneous outstanding items (see attached Form 992-C, Itemized Hearing Agenda, 118 pages, not attached, available upon request, requests processed in order received).
>
>**APPOINTMENT: NOW.**
>
>Please take a number upon arrival. Do not lose your number. Numbers are non-transferable, non-duplicable, and non-refundable. Failure to present your number when called will result in rescheduling. Rescheduling is available at the end of the current queue cycle. The current queue cycle began approximately 4.7 billion standard galactic years ago and is estimated to conclude in [FIELD NOT POPULATED].
>
>This notice constitutes your first, second, and final reminder.

No one touched the navigation console. The *Adastra* changed course. It did this without engaging its engines, without altering its power output, without producing any sensation of acceleration or deceleration. One moment it was traveling along a trajectory that the navigation officer had calculated and the captain had approved and the stars had confirmed for seventeen years. The next moment it was somewhere else. The stars outside the observation ports were different—not rearranged, not shifted, but *different*, as if the ship had been picked up and set down in a region of space that had never been on any chart because it had never been in any space.

The observation ports showed a structure.

"Structure" was the word the first officer used, and it was adopted immediately by everyone who saw it, because there was no other word, because the human linguistic apparatus, confronted with the thing outside the observation ports, reached for the nearest available container and jammed the perception inside. It was a structure. It had walls—or surfaces that behaved as walls, in that they separated inside from outside, though the geometry of the separation changed depending on the angle of observation and, some crew members reported, depending on how long you looked. It had an entrance—or an area of increased permeability that the ship was drifting toward at a speed that felt bureaucratically predetermined, not fast, not slow, the speed of a conveyor belt in a government building that moves you toward the counter whether or not you are ready.

The ship entered. The observation ports filled with beige.

It was a specific beige. Not warm, not cool, not comforting, not hostile. It was the beige of a wall that had been painted by a committee that had been asked to select a color that would not provoke complaints, and had selected this one, and had been right—it would not provoke complaints, it would provoke nothing, it existed in a chromatic region that the human visual system processed without engaging any emotional response, so that looking at it was like hearing the hum of the filing cabinets, a sensation present but unregistered, a color whose purpose was to assert that nothing was happening in a place where everything was happening.
