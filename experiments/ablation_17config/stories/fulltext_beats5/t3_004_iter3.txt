# The Commissar's Confession

## I.

The antechamber smelled of wax and old paper and the particular variety of dust that collects in rooms where men decide things. Citizen Inspector Marzin stood with his hands at his sides while the clerk—not Robespierre, never Robespierre directly, always the clerk, a man named Géraud whose left eyelid trembled when he was conveying something he understood to be irreversible—opened a drawer and produced a folded sheet. Géraud slid it across the desk with two fingers, as one slides a bill of sale or a requisition for candles, and returned his attention to the ledger in front of him.

Marzin picked up the paper. He unfolded it along its existing crease, a crease that had already been pressed and reopened several times, so that the fibers along the fold had gone soft and white, like a scar.

Seven names. He read them from top to bottom at a pace that could be called efficient. Desmulins, J.-F.—not the famous one, a cousin, a clerk of courts who had once, at a dinner Marzin attended in another life, described the sound of his daughter's laughter as "the republic's only reliable currency." Aubert, P., former priest, who had renounced his orders in the Year One with such evident relief that Marzin had believed him completely and arranged his lodging on the Rue de Bièvre. Vignon, R., schoolmaster, who corrected everyone's grammar, even in fear, even in hiding, and who had once told Marzin that the subjunctive was "the tense of things hoped for but not yet real," and then looked startled, as though he'd said something political. The printer's widow was fourth from the top: Lessard, C., née Bourdon.

He read the remaining three names. He refolded the paper along its crease and placed it inside his coat, on the left side, against the lining where the stitching had loosened and created a shallow pocket he had sewn himself, at night, by the light of a tallow stub, three years ago when he had first understood that he would need a place to keep things close to his body.

"The transfer should be arranged before evening," Géraud said, without looking up. "The tribunal has confirmed the sentences."

"I will see to it."

"Citizen Saint-Just has expressed interest in the efficiency of the process. It would be well to be thorough."

"I am always thorough."

Géraud's eyelid trembled. Marzin turned, crossed the antechamber in four steps—the room was not large; the Committee did not waste space on ceremony—and opened the door to the corridor. The hinges needed oil. They had needed oil for months. No one had attended to them because the men who passed through this door generally had other preoccupations, and the men who might have oiled them had learned that proximity to this particular door was a poor investment of effort.

The door closed behind him.

In the corridor, which was dim and which smelled of lime wash and the river, Marzin's hands were steady. His right hand hung at his side. His left pressed the fold of paper lightly against his ribs through the fabric of his coat. Both hands were perfectly still. He had practiced this stillness for longer than he had practiced anything—longer than his penmanship, which was excellent and which had first recommended him to the Committee; longer than his manner of walking, which was brisk but unhurried, the gait of a man who has tasks but no anxieties; longer than his way of meeting another man's eyes, which conveyed a fidelity so absolute it could afford to be mild. He had practiced the steadiness of his hands late at night, alone, holding a cup of water and watching its surface for any tremor, training the muscles in his forearms and his wrists the way a violinist trains, through repetition and the slow erasure of involuntary response, until the stillness was no longer a performance but a fact of his body, until his hands did not shake because they had forgotten how, and this forgetting was the thing he had worked hardest to achieve, and was perhaps the only expertise he possessed that could be called, in any honest accounting, his own.

He walked toward the stairs.

## II.

The street was loud. July had settled over Paris like a hand pressed flat against a face—the heat was blunt, vegetal, thickened by the smell of the river and of horse dung drying on the cobblestones and of bread from a bakery on the corner where a woman in a blue apron was shouting at a boy who had dropped a tray of loaves into the gutter. The boy was perhaps eleven. He did not cry. Children in Paris in the Year Two of the Republic had learned, through a pedagogy more effective than any the Convention had devised, that crying attracted attention, and attention was a resource it was better to let others spend. Marzin turned right, toward the Pont au Change, and the clatter of a cart loaded with something wooden—chairs, perhaps, or disassembled shutters, the load covered by a canvas that flapped in a wind that smelled of chalk—

—and it was the smell of chalk, or no, not chalk, calcium powder, no, it was printer's ink, and the room was not large, and the window gave onto a courtyard where someone was singing a song about a fisherman, and the voice was a woman's voice and it threaded through the open window like a needle pulling something bright through dark cloth, and across the table the man who had introduced himself as Dufresne—a name so ordinary it sounded elected, the kind of name a man might choose if he wanted to pass through the world without snagging on anything—Dufresne was pouring wine into two cups that did not match, one clay, one tin, and saying:

"You understand what I am asking you."

"You are asking me to become two things at once."

"I am asking you to remain one thing. The thing you already are. But to let them believe you are another."

"That is two things."

"It is one thing and its shadow. The shadow is not real. You would know which is which."

The wine was sour. The song from the courtyard had stopped. Dufresne's face: narrow, pockmarked along the jaw, eyes of a color Marzin would later try to recall precisely and fail—gray, perhaps, or a brown so pale it looked gray in poor light—and across the bridge of his nose a faint constellation of ink spots, because Dufresne was a printer by trade, or had been, before the trade became political and politics became fatal and he had moved into the back rooms of other people's shops, carrying with him always the smell of that ink, soot-black, animal-fat-based, a smell that lived in the creases of his hands regardless of how many times he washed them.

"What the Revolution is becoming," Dufresne said, and stopped. He turned the clay cup on the table: one quarter turn, then another, as if calibrating something. "You marched. I know you marched. I know you were at the Bastille."

"Yes."

"You wept."

Marzin said nothing.

"I am not accusing you of sentiment. I am establishing that you were sincere. That you believed—"

"I believed."

"—and that what you believed in was not *this*." Dufresne gestured: not at anything in the room but at the air itself, at the night outside, at the city in which, even as they spoke, lists were being compiled in rooms smaller than this one by men whose handwriting was less careful than Marzin's. "This is not the thing we built. This is the thing that ate the thing we built. And it is still hungry. And it has learned to use the vocabulary of the thing it replaced."

Something cracked. Not in the room. Not a sound. Something inside the architecture of what Marzin had believed, a hairline fracture along a load-bearing wall, the kind of structural failure that does not announce itself with noise but with a faint settling, a door that no longer closes flush, a window frame that admits a draft where no draft existed before. He had sung the Marseillaise in the streets with his arm around a stranger's shoulder and the stranger had been weeping too and the tears had been real, the joy had been real, the sense that history was a gate that had been locked and was now swinging open was real, and now Dufresne was telling him that the gate had swung open onto a room where men with excellent penmanship were writing names on lists, and the lists were getting longer, and the names were of people who had also marched, also wept, also believed, and the only difference between the people on the lists and the people writing the lists was that the people on the lists had believed too specifically, or too loudly, or in a version of the future that differed from the version currently authorized by men who had themselves believed a different version only six months prior—

A cart rattled past on the cobblestones.

Marzin was on the Pont au Change, mid-stride, his left foot landing on a stone that was darker than its neighbors, stained by something he chose not to identify. The river below was the color of pewter. Ahead, the towers of the Conciergerie rose against a sky that was too blue, offensively blue, a sky that had not received the memorandum about what was happening beneath it.

Six steps. The entire memory had occupied six steps. He counted backward to verify: the step before the cart was the step where Dufresne had gestured at the air; the step before that was the sour wine; the step before that was the window, the courtyard, the song. Three years in six footfalls. His body had not broken stride. His hands had not moved. The fold of paper inside his coat had not shifted. To any observer—and there were always observers; the Revolution had achieved, if nothing else, a perfect saturation of observation—he was a man crossing a bridge on professional business, walking briskly but without haste, his face composed into the expression of moderate purpose that was the approved expression of the Republic, a face that said *I have tasks* and did not say *I have a dead man's voice inside my skull and the dead man is still pouring wine and the wine is still sour and I can taste it now, on this bridge, three years later, as though the interval between that night and this afternoon is not a duration but a fold in a piece of paper that has been creased and opened too many times and has gone soft and white along the line where it bends.*

He reached the far side of the bridge. He did not look back. The Conciergerie was ahead. The list was against his ribs. The names were warm with body heat.

## III.

The smell of the Conciergerie was made of straw and damp stone and the particular human odor that accumulates in places where bodies are kept too close together and washed too infrequently—not the sharp smell of labor or exertion but a slower, sweeter smell, the smell of skin that has had time to become acquainted with despair, a biochemical process the Republic had not yet found a way to regulate. Marzin showed his credentials to the guard at the first gate, then the second, then the third. The guards examined the paper from the Committee. They did not examine Marzin. He had been here often enough that his face was its own credential, and this fact—that the place where people were kept before being killed recognized him as a regular—was a fact he stored in the part of his mind where he kept things he could not afford to examine during working hours, a constantly expanding archive that he had begun to imagine, in the small hours of the night, as a room with a door that did not close flush, that admitted a draft, that—

He passed through the third gate.

The holding cell for tomorrow's condemned was a long room on the ground floor that had once, implausibly, been part of the medieval palace kitchens. The ceiling was vaulted. The stone was old enough to have settled into its own geology. There were seventeen people in the room, though only seven appeared on his list; the others belonged to different lists, different clerks, different processes of administrative selection that would converge tomorrow at the same blade in the way that rivers of different origin converge at a single mouth.

He saw Desmulins the cousin first, sitting against the far wall with his knees drawn up, not sleeping but occupying the posture of sleep, a man who had decided that the most dignified thing remaining to him was unconsciousness and was doing his best to approximate it. Aubert the former priest was standing near the window—there was a narrow window, high up, almost decorative, admitting a bar of golden light that fell across the floor like a plank—and his lips were moving without sound, and Marzin understood that he was praying, that the renunciation had un-renounced at the end, that the body's oldest habits were also its last, and this did not surprise him. Vignon the schoolmaster was sitting on a bench, perfectly upright, his hands on his knees, his posture so precise that Marzin thought, absurdly, *he is conjugating something in his head, he is declining nouns, he is holding the structure of language between himself and what is coming the way a man holds a plank between himself and the sea.*

The printer's widow was sitting on the floor near the bench. Her skirts were arranged around her with a care that seemed both automatic and deliberate, a woman's lifelong habit of managing fabric surviving into circumstances where fabric was no longer the relevant concern. She was not looking at the window or the wall or any of the other faces in the room.

Marzin saw her hands first.

They were folded in her lap—the right over the left, the fingers loosely interlaced. The nails were short. The skin of her fingers was stained along the cuticles and in the creases of the knuckles with ink that had become, over years of daily contact, not a coating but a quality of the skin itself, the way certain dyes will fix permanently into wool if the mordant is strong enough. The ink had turned the creases of her hands into small dark lines that looked, from a distance, like the lines on a map, like a terrain rendered in miniature, and Marzin knew these hands, had watched these hands set type and pull proofs and break bread and pour soup from a pot into a bowl with a chipped rim, had sat across a table no wider than a writing desk—

—and the shop was on the Rue de la Huchette, and it was autumn, and the press stood in the back room like a skeletal animal, its iron frame greened and worn at the handle from generations of hands that had gripped it at the same place, and the pamphlets were hung on lines strung between the beams to dry, and they hung there like laundry, like small white shirts, and the ink on them was still wet enough to smell, and the smell was the smell of Dufresne's hands, and this coincidence—the same ink, the same chemical compound of soot and fat and gum arabic, connecting the man who had recruited him to the woman whose shop he had been sent to watch—was a coincidence he had noted on his first visit and then, deliberately, never noted again, because to note it would be to create a connection that could be traced, and connections that could be traced were the things he was supposed to be looking for, not the things he was supposed to have—

"You report on me," she had said. Not a question. The second week.

"I report on many things. Your shop is one of them."

"And what do you report?"

"That the presses are idle. That you print nothing seditious. That you are a widow of no political consequence."

"All of which is true."

"All of which is true."

She had looked at him then with an expression he would later spend many hours trying to disassemble—not gratitude, not suspicion, not warmth, something that combined elements of all three with something else entirely, something he eventually decided was *recognition*: the recognition of a person meeting another person who was doing a thing that cost them something, even if the nature of the cost was not yet visible to either of them. She poured soup into the chipped bowl, pushed it across the table, and said, "You should eat. You look like a man who forgets to eat."

He ate. The soup was thin, potato and leek, with a taste of bay. He ate it and she watched him eat it and neither of them said anything and the pamphlets dried on their lines above them and the press stood in the corner and the window darkened and the courtyard outside filled with the sound of a dog barking and then not barking and then barking again, and in this silence, or rather in this particular texture of sound—the spoon against the bowl, the distant dog, the settling of the building's old timbers—something began that neither of them would ever name.
