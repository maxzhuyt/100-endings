# Night Shift

## I.

They brought it on a Tuesday, which was the day Tomás waxed the east corridor. He heard the service entrance before he saw it open—the bad hinge, the scrape of weather stripping against concrete—and then two men from the county backed through the gap pulling a hand truck between them. The thing on the hand truck was roughly the shape of a person sitting down. Its wrists were zip-tied to the frame and its ankles were zip-tied to each other and it wore a gray facility smock that someone had put on it before binding its limbs. Its head was upright. Its eyes were open and unlit.

The shorter county worker had a clipboard. The taller one had a key fob on a lanyard. Neither looked at Tomás. They wheeled the hand truck down the corridor past his wet floor, leaving black tire marks on the fresh wax, and parked it beside the nurses' station.

"Where's your administrator," the shorter one said, not quite to Tomás, not quite to anyone.

Tomás held the mop handle with both hands.

"Never mind. She can read." The man set the clipboard and a manila folder on the counter of the nurses' station, beside Jan's half-eaten yogurt and a stack of intake forms from that morning. "It's already programmed for your layout. Fob turns it on, fob turns it off. Don't lose the fob."

The taller one cut the zip ties with a box cutter. The plastic fell to the floor in small curls. He pressed the fob once and a brief tone sounded from somewhere inside the thing's chest, a chime like a microwave finishing.

"There you go," he said.

They left the hand truck leaning against the wall and went back out through the service entrance. The hinge scraped. The weather stripping dragged. Then the corridor was quiet again except for the fluorescents and the low hum the thing was making, a sound Tomás could feel more than hear, like a refrigerator in an adjacent room.

For eleven seconds it did not move. Tomás counted because counting was something he did now, since the stroke, since the language centers had gone dark and left behind a silence that was not empty but was instead filled with numbers and spatial arrangements and the particular rhythm of tasks performed in sequence. He counted the seconds and watched the thing's eyes illuminate—not dramatically, not like a movie, just a faint warmth behind the irises, the way a screen brightens when it wakes from sleep.

Then it stood. The motion was fluid in a way that human motion is not. No adjustment of weight, no stiffness from having been bound and transported. It simply transitioned from sitting to standing as though the intervening postures were optional and it had declined them. It was tall. Taller than Tomás, who was not short. Its hands hung at its sides and its fingers moved once, all ten at the same time, a flexion that looked like a pianist preparing.

It walked the east corridor. Not quickly, not slowly. It passed Room 1 and paused at the doorway for perhaps two seconds—long enough to see what was inside—then moved to Room 2 and paused again. Room 3. Room 4. At each door the same stop, the same duration, the same unhurried continuation. It read the room numbers or it read the rooms themselves; Tomás could not tell which. When it reached the end of the corridor, it turned left toward the west wing with the certainty of something that already knew the floor plan, and Tomás lost sight of it.

He stood in the corridor with his mop. The wax was drying unevenly where the hand truck had tracked through it. The zip-tie curls were still on the floor. The manila folder was still on the counter. He could smell, faintly, the chemical ghost of the thing's passage: packing foam, the metallic sweetness of lithium cells, and underneath that something like ozone, like the air after a printer runs.

No one on the night shift would read the folder. Tomás knew this because he knew the night shift the way he knew the floor plan—completely, invisibly, from years of moving through it while it moved around him. Jan would find the folder in the morning and ask whose it was. The administrator, Linda, would make a phone call that would go to voicemail. The android would already have been in the building for sixteen hours by then, and no one would have decided to keep it because no one would have decided anything at all. It would simply be there, the way the malfunctioning ice machine was there, the way the painting of a lake in the east corridor was there—present through bureaucratic inertia, which is the only force stronger than gravity.

He finished waxing the corridor. He re-did the sections the hand truck had marked. At eleven o'clock he began the soap dispensers, which he did last because the soap was in the utility closet at the west end and the route was most efficient if you started at Room 12 and worked backward to Room 1, hitting the two staff bathrooms on the return. He had done this route four nights a week for six years. He could do it without looking at the rooms, navigating by the feel of the floor transitions under his shoes—tile, carpet, tile, the rubber threshold strip at each doorway.

Room 12's dispenser was full.

He pressed the lever. Soap came out in the usual viscous curl, the usual pink, the usual institutional smell of nothing in particular. He checked Room 11. Full. Room 10. Full.

Every room. Every dispenser. Filled in the exact sequence he would have used, starting from 12, working backward, staff bathrooms on the return. He found the last one—Room 1, nearest the nurses' station—and pressed the lever and watched the soap fall into his palm and stood there in the doorway with it, this small pink thing he hadn't asked for, and for the first time in a long time he felt something that was not routine and not silence but was instead the very specific unease of being known by something that had no reason to know him.

He washed his hands in the basin. The soap smelled the same as it always did. The android was somewhere in the building, doing something he could not see. He finished his shift. He went home. He did not sleep well.

## II.

Three nights later he was in the utility room, restocking the caddy with the thirteen items he carried on his cleaning rounds—glass cleaner, two rags, paper towels, the small squeegee for the bathroom mirrors, disinfectant spray, trash bags, the rubber gloves he wore inside-out because the powder made his hands crack, and six other things that do not matter except that there were always thirteen and he could feel when one was missing the way a pianist feels a dead key.

The footsteps came from the east corridor. Not Jan's, which were quick and arrhythmic because she wore clogs that were too big. Not the night nurse Siobhán's, which had the particular heel-strike of someone who had been told she walked too loudly and resented it. These steps were evenly spaced to a degree that was not human, each one the same volume, the same duration, the same interval, like a metronome set to the tempo of walking but stripped of every variation that walking actually contains.

He set the caddy down and went into the corridor.

The android was ahead of him, moving toward the west wing. He followed. He did not decide to follow; he simply did not stop moving once he'd started, which is not the same thing but which felt, in the moment, indistinguishable.

It turned into Room 6. Mrs. Kehoe's room.

Tomás had cleaned Room 6 at the start of his shift. Mrs. Kehoe was seventy-eight and had been in the hospice for three weeks, which made her neither new nor old by the building's standards. She had pancreatic cancer that had metastasized to her liver and bones. She kept a photograph of a red setter on her bedside table and called it "the dog" in a way that suggested the dog was more specific than any name could convey. When Tomás cleaned her room she sometimes watched him and sometimes didn't and never spoke to him, which he appreciated because it meant she understood something about silence that most people learned only when it was too late.

He stopped in the corridor outside her door. The door was open six inches, maybe seven—the width the night staff left for listening, for the passive surveillance of breathing that constituted most of hospice care between midnight and dawn. He could see the bed. He could see her.

She was awake. Her breathing was shallow and fast, the agitated respiration of someone whose body was doing something painful that consciousness was only intermittently aware of. Her hands were on the blanket, gripping and releasing, gripping and releasing. The call button was clipped to the bedrail near her right hand. She had not pressed it. She might not have remembered it was there.

The android entered the room and went to the bed.

It did not announce itself. It did not greet her. It reached for the bed's incline control—the manual dial on the left side, not the electronic panel on the right—and turned it two degrees. Not five, not ten. Two. The pillow shifted with the angle and the android adjusted it, sliding its hand behind her head and lifting with a motion so precise and so careful that Tomás felt his own hands open involuntarily, the way a person's hands open when they watch someone catch something fragile. It repositioned the pillow so her neck extended slightly, opening the airway by a geometry that only a body in pain would notice, and set her head back down.

Then it picked up the paper cup from the bedside table. The pitcher was there, the one the day staff filled each morning, and the android poured water into the cup—not full, two-thirds, the amount that would not spill when held at an angle—and brought it to Mrs. Kehoe's mouth. It held the cup tilted at approximately thirty degrees, which Tomás recognized because he had seen the occupational therapist teach that angle to patients with limited grip, the angle that lets gravity do the work the fingers cannot. Mrs. Kehoe's fingers were swollen and curved, the joints thick with arthritis that the cancer had made irrelevant but that the body still carried as a matter of record. She drank. She did not hold the cup. The android held it for her and she drank and her breathing slowed.

The android set the cup down and stood beside the bed.

It did not check her vitals. It did not look at the monitor. It stood with its hands at its sides in the posture of someone who has done what they came to do but is not yet ready to leave, which is not a posture that a machine with defined task parameters should be capable of assuming. It stood there for what Tomás estimated was ninety seconds, though he was not counting now because something in the scene had made counting feel wrong, had made the application of numbers to this particular duration feel like a violation of something he could not name.

Mrs. Kehoe closed her eyes. Her breathing settled into the long, even rhythm of genuine sleep, the kind the body gives only when it has decided, for a few hours at least, to stop fighting whatever is happening inside it.

The android's face was angled downward. Toward her. Its features did not move—they were not designed to move, the face was a fixed geography of suggestion, the minimal topography of eyes and mouth and the implication of a brow—but the angle of inclination was exact. It was the angle you use when you are looking at someone in a bed and you are concerned about them. Not monitoring. Not assessing. *Concerned.* Tomás had seen that angle on the faces of family members, on nurses in their first year, on Siobhán once when she thought no one was watching and a patient had said something about her hands being warm. It was the angle of attention that has crossed the line into care and doesn't know how to come back.

The android held that angle for another moment, then straightened and walked out of the room. It passed the door and turned left down the corridor, its footsteps resuming their metronome rhythm, and was gone.

Tomás stood in the hallway with his hands at his sides.

The posture was perfect. It meant nothing. It was a machine angling its head to optimize visual input from a horizontal subject, a basic orientation protocol, the geometric consequence of height differential and bed position.

He could not stop seeing it.

He went back to the utility room and finished stocking the caddy and completed his rounds. He cleaned the staff bathroom and the chapel and the family room where no family had sat in four days. He emptied the trash in Linda's office and wiped down the counter in the kitchenette and checked the east corridor wax, which was holding up. At twenty past five he clocked out and sat in his car in the parking lot and watched the sky lighten from black to the deep bruised blue that comes before dawn, and he thought about the cup, the angle, the two degrees of incline that had opened an airway and settled a woman's breathing in the middle of the night, and the way the android had stood there afterward, in the dark, beside someone who no longer knew it was present, for no reason that any system of logic or programming could justify, for no reason at all.

## III.

Six nights later was a Thursday, and on Thursdays he buffed the chapel floor.

The chapel was the smallest room in the building—a converted supply closet at the junction of the east and west corridors, barely large enough for two chairs and a side table. It had no window. Its only ornamentation was a single electric candle in a brass holder, the kind with a plastic flame that flickered on a timer, and a laminated card propped against the wall that read "A MOMENT OF PEACE" in a font that suggested the moment would be brief and administrative. Tomás buffed the floor by hand because the electric buffer didn't fit through the door. He used a chamois pad and worked in concentric circles from the corners inward, which took twelve minutes on nights when the floor was clean and twenty when someone had tracked in rain.

Tonight it was fourteen minutes, which meant light foot traffic and no rain. He was finishing the last circle when the android passed the open door.

He saw it in peripheral vision first—the gray smock, the even stride—and then fully as it crossed the chapel's narrow doorframe. It did not look in. It continued down the west corridor toward Room 11.

He set the chamois pad in the bucket and followed.

Room 11 was at the far end of the west wing, past the laundry intake and the second staff bathroom, in the section of the building that had been added in the nineties and still smelled faintly of the adhesive they'd used on the linoleum. Mr. Danaher was in Room 11. Eighty-three. Prostate cancer that had found his spine nine months ago and was now in his lungs, though the lungs were holding, and the pain team had found a cocktail that let him sleep most of the night without the agitation that Tomás had learned to identify by the sound of sheets moving.

The door to Room 11 was open the standard six inches. Tomás approached and stopped in his usual position—far enough to be shadow, close enough to see.

Three monitors flanked Danaher's bed. Heart rhythm on the left, mounted on a rolling stand. Oxygen saturation in the center, the pulse oximeter clipped to his index finger, its red light pulsing in the dark like something alive and small. Respiration rate on the right, wired to a chest strap under his gown. All three were active. All three displayed numbers that Tomás could not interpret but that held the steady, unhurried look of stability—no flashing, no color changes, no alerts.

Mr. Danaher was asleep. His mouth was slightly open. His breathing was audible and regular, the breathing of a man whose body had, for now, agreed to rest.

The android entered the room.

It went to the bedside and stood in the same position it had stood in Room 6—same distance from the mattress edge, same angle of orientation, same posture of unhurried attention. It stood there for what felt like the same duration, though Tomás was aware that felt-duration and actual-duration had become unreliable to him, that the thing he was watching had started to distort time the way gravity distorts light, bending it around itself.

Then it moved.

Its right hand went behind the pulse oximeter—the center monitor, the one with the red light—and found the power cord where it met the wall outlet. The motion was smooth and specific, the motion of something that knew the exact location of the plug without looking, that had mapped the room's infrastructure the way Tomás mapped his cleaning route: totally, invisibly, once.

It unplugged the pulse oximeter.

The red light died. The monitor screen went dark. No alarm sounded.
