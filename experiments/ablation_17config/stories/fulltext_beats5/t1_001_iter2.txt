# The Thirty-Seven Letters of Monk Hideo, As Compiled by His Opponent

---

## Prefatory Note by the Editor

I will not give my name. Those who were present at the 1976 All-Japan Invitational Kendō Tournament in Osaka will know who compiled this volume, and those who were not present have no need of the information. It is enough to say that I was Hideo's opponent in the final match, and that for forty years I have been the recipient of the letters published here. I am publishing them now, with my own annotations, as — and I choose this word deliberately — my final move.

Some facts of provenance. The first letter is dated March 3, 1977, approximately three months after the tournament. The last arrived at my home on the morning of October 9, 2017, three days before I delivered this manuscript to the publisher. I have verified the calligraphy against authenticated samples from Hideo's years at the Eiheiji monastery; the hand is consistent across all thirty-seven letters, allowing for the expected degradation of age. The paper varies. The early letters are written on standard stationery. The middle letters, beginning with Letter 9, use handmade washi of a kind produced in limited quantities by a single papermaker in Echizen. The final letters are written on what appears to be the reverse side of sutras.

I have preserved the letters in the order of their receipt. Where Hideo's text is legible, I have reproduced it without alteration. Where damage, water staining, or the deterioration of ink has rendered a word or phrase uncertain, I have indicated this with brackets. My annotations appear beneath each letter. I have tried to restrict myself to matters of context and clarification.

I should state three things plainly.

First: the 1976 final was expected, by every informed observer, to result in Hideo's victory. He was the superior technician. This was not a matter of opinion. It was the settled consensus of every senior instructor in the All-Japan Kendō Federation, several of whom are quoted to that effect in the newspaper coverage I have included as Appendix B.

Second: Hideo did not lose the match. He forfeited. The distinction matters. He stepped to the center line, bowed, assumed his stance, and at thirty-eight seconds — before a single decisive strike had been scored by either competitor — lowered his shinai, removed his men, and walked from the court. No explanation was offered to the judges. None was given to the press. The official record lists the result as a withdrawal, which is the federation's administrative euphemism for an act that has no administrative category.

Third: in forty years, I have never written back. I have never attempted to contact Hideo by any means. I have never visited the monastery. I have never asked him, directly or through any intermediary, why he did what he did.

I am aware of how these three facts appear when placed beside the act of publication. I am aware that the reader will ask why a man who maintained silence for four decades should choose, at the end of his life, to break it in this particular fashion — not by answering the question himself, but by making public the letters of the man who could not quite ask it.

I have no answer that would satisfy the reader, and I will not manufacture one.

I will say only this: readers will form their own conclusions about why a man who chose silence would write so much of it to the one person from whom he was supposedly hiding.

The annotations are my own. The errors are my own. The letters belong to Hideo. This belongs to no one.

— October 12, 2017

## Letter 1

*March 3, 1977*

The monastery is colder than I expected. I do not know why I expected otherwise. I knew the elevation. I knew the latitude. I knew that the meditation hall at Eiheiji has no heating and that the monks sit zazen before dawn in winter with nothing between their skin and the air but a single layer of cotton. I knew all of this and still I was surprised, which tells me something about the difference between knowledge and experience that I imagine a man in your profession would find obvious.

I sit zazen for four hours each morning. The cold enters through the soles of the feet and rises. By the second hour it has reached the hips. By the third, the shoulders. By the fourth, it is no longer cold. It is simply the body, understood completely.

The barley tea here is made by a monk named Daitō who over-roasts the grains by exactly the same margin each morning, so that the tea tastes always of a small and consistent burning. I have come to depend on this flavor. It is the most reliable thing in my life.

We chop wood before dawn. The sound carries differently in cold air. A struck log rings. I had not known that wood could ring.

I am well. I did not write to say that I am well, but I am.

— H.

**Editor's annotation:** The letter arrived without a return address. Subsequent letters were posted from various towns near the monastery but never from the monastery itself. I do not know whether this was Hideo's choice or the monastery's policy. I read this letter in my office, standing, still in my coat, having come directly from the mailbox. I read it four times. I placed it in a drawer. I did not tell my wife.

## Letter 2

*October 19, 1977*

Autumn now. The persimmons are heavy on the branch, and when they fall they make a sound that is nothing like the sound of wood being struck and nothing like the sound of a shinai connecting, though the mind — my mind, still — reaches for that comparison before any other. I wonder how long it will take before the body stops translating everything into the language of the thing it has left behind.

A new monk arrived last week. Young, perhaps twenty-three. He moves the way young men move who have never been made aware of their movement — loose, easy, unguarded. His shoulders are open. Watching him cross the courtyard is like watching a man walk through a battlefield without knowing it is a battlefield.

I have been assigned to the kitchen. The work is simple. Rice, soup, pickled radish, tea. The same meal repeating. It is not so different from training, which was also the same motion repeating, except that in the kitchen the repetition does not pretend to be going somewhere.

The abbot said something to me last week that I have been turning over. He said: "You did not come here to find something. You came here because you found something and did not want it." I told him he was wrong. He said, "Good. Tell me that again in ten years."

— H.

**Editor's annotation:** Seven months between the first letter and the second. I had assumed the first was an anomaly. The arrival of the second informed me that it was not.

## Letter 3

*November 2, 1977*

Two letters in the same season — forgive me. Or rather, do not forgive me, as forgiveness implies I have asked for it, and I have not.

The young monk I mentioned. He began training with the kitchen knife, learning to cut daikon into uniform rounds. I watched his wrist. There is a motion the wrist makes when it is doing what it has been told to do, and a different motion — almost indistinguishable, a difference of perhaps two degrees in the angle of rotation — when it is doing what it has *taught itself* to do. The first is obedience. The second is habit. They look the same. At speed, even a master would not see the difference.

But at the moment of failure, the difference becomes everything. The obedient wrist can adjust. The habituated wrist cannot. It will complete its motion even as the daikon rolls, even as the blade descends wrong, because the body has made a promise the mind no longer supervises.

The young monk cut his finger. One and a half centimeters, shallow, between the first and second knuckle. He was surprised.

He should not have been surprised.

— H.

**Editor's annotation:** No annotation.

## Letter 4

*June 14, 1978*

Summer. The heat is the inverse of the winter cold — it enters from above, through the crown of the skull, and settles downward. By midday the body is not hot but *full*, as though heat were a substance that displaces thought.

I have been thinking about the hip. The hip is where every motion originates, though we speak of the hand, the foot, the shoulder. A man who understands his own hip understands his own intention. A man who does not is at the mercy of a mechanism he cannot see.

Consider: the left hip in a man who favors his right side. It carries less weight. It knows this. Over years, it compensates not by becoming stronger but by becoming *more efficient in its weakness* — by finding the precise angle at which its reduced load-bearing can mimic the stability of full engagement. The compensation is so elegant that it becomes, in time, invisible. The man stands. He appears balanced. An observer, even an experienced one, sees equilibrium.

But in the moment of full extension — when weight commits entirely to one vector — the compensation reveals itself. Not as imbalance. As *geometry*. The hip describes an arc that is two degrees shallower than the arc of true stability, and those two degrees translate, at the point of impact, into an opening. A gap. A place where force, applied correctly, would meet no resistance.

There are postures that contain their own ending. The body makes promises the mind does not know it has made.

The persimmon tree has flowered. Small white blossoms. I did not know persimmons flowered before they fruited, though of course they must, as all fruit trees must. Another case of knowledge arriving late.

— H.

**Editor's annotation:** I read this in 1979, a year after it arrived, having left it unopened for eleven months. I did not sleep for three nights. On the fourth night, I went to my dojo at two in the morning and stood before the mirror in the stance I have held since I was fourteen years old. I stood there for one hour. I was looking at my left hip.

## Letter 5

*March 22, 1979*

The monastery has asked me to teach calligraphy to the younger monks. I agreed, though I wonder if one must be at peace with one's own hand before presuming to guide another's.

The brushstroke, like the sword stroke, is a commitment. Once the brush touches paper, the line is already finished — the hand merely enacts what the body decided in the instant before contact. I tell the young monks: do not watch the brush. Watch the breath before the brush. The stroke lives there, in the pause, in the moment of fullest intention. What happens on the paper is only the record.

They nod. They do not understand. Understanding of this kind takes years, and then it arrives not as comprehension but as grief — the grief of realizing that every motion you ever made was the motion you were *always going to make*, that freedom was only the name you gave to the interval before you recognized the pattern.

I am being too abstract. I promised myself I would write to you about concrete things. The barley tea. The wood. The sound of the bell at four in the morning, which does not wake us so much as *collect* us — we are already awake, scattered across our separate darknesses, and the bell gathers us into one.

I wonder if you still rise early.

— H.

**Editor's annotation:** This is the first time he addresses me directly. "You." "I wonder if you still rise early." Previous letters were written as though to the air, or to a journal. This sentence changes the nature of the document. It is no longer a monologue. It is one half of a conversation in which the other half is silence. My silence. I note that he does not ask me a question — "Do you still rise early?" — but expresses a wondering. The distinction may seem trivial. It is not. A question demands an answer. A wondering permits one to remain exactly where I was: receiving, reading, saying nothing.

## Letter 6

*December 1, 1979*

Winter again, and the body remembers. Not last winter — *every* winter. The body is a library of winters, and each year it opens to the oldest pages first.

I watched two monks sparring yesterday. Not with swords — the monastery doesn't train in that — but with words, in the formal dharma combat the abbot stages each month. One monk posed a koan. The other responded. Back and forth, faster and faster, until one of them paused.

The one who paused had seen something. I could tell by the way his weight shifted — left foot drawing back, perhaps four millimeters, a motion so small he could not have known he made it. A retreat. Not from the other monk's argument, but from his own next sentence. He had seen where his own logic was taking him, and he could not go there.

The abbot rang the bell. The exchange was over. The monk who paused was declared the loser.

I thought: no. He was the only one in the room who was fighting.

The weight distribution in that moment — the four-millimeter retreat of the left foot — I have seen this before. Not in monks. In a man who understood, at the level of his bones, that his next movement would be decisive, and who chose, for reasons the bones could not name, to interrupt the motion before it arrived.

The winter deepens. I sleep well, sometimes.

— H.

**Editor's annotation:** "I have seen this before. Not in monks." He does not say where. He does not say in whom. The annotation one is tempted to write here is the annotation I will not write. I will say only that by December 1979, three years after the tournament, the pattern of these letters had become clear to me, and I understood that I was being addressed with a patience and an indirection that I had encountered only once before in my life — in the thirty-eight seconds before Hideo lowered his shinai, during which the two of us stood at the center line and something passed between us that I have never described to any living person.

I understood the letters. I did not write back.

*Editor's note on Letters 7 through 11: These letters, dated 1980–1983, concern principally the daily routines of the monastery, Hideo's reading in Dōgen and the classical Ch'an masters, and descriptions of natural phenomena — the weight of snow on particular branches, the behavior of a specific crow that returned each spring to the monastery's persimmon tree. They are of interest to scholars of Sōtō Zen practice and I have included them in Appendix A. They do not advance the subject with which the reader is, by now, concerned. I note only that Letter 9 contains the sentence "I dreamed last night of two men on either side of a river, each waiting for the other to build the bridge," and that beneath this sentence I have no annotation to offer, or rather, no annotation I am willing to make public.*

## Letter 12

*September 7, 1984*

I have been reading Musashi.

This will not surprise you. What may surprise you is that I have not read him before — or rather, that I read him when I was young and understood nothing, and that reading him now, after eight years of sweeping meditation halls and chopping wood and burning barley, I find that the text has changed. It has not changed. I have changed, and the text, which was always there, which was always saying what it says, now says it to a man who can hear it.

Musashi writes in the *Book of Five Rings* that the way of strategy is the way of nature. That a man who understands the rhythm of his own body understands the rhythm of his opponent, and that from this understanding comes the ability to strike at the moment of maximum vulnerability. He calls this "the crossing of distance." Not the physical distance — the *temporal* distance. The gap between one man's intention and another man's awareness.

But Musashi does not address the question I am interested in, which is this: What happens when the crossing of distance reveals not a tactical opening but a *structural* one?
