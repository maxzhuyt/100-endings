# The Last Watch of the *Mara Celeste*

---

The kettle wouldn't sit right on the burner. It kept walking itself toward the port side, an inch at a time, like something ashamed trying to leave a room. I braced it with the heel of my hand, waited for the water to find some version of level inside, and lit the flame on the third match because the first two wouldn't keep their heads up in the draft coming through the companionway seal. The seal had been good in April. It was October now and the rubber had a lip to it, a little curl of separation you could fit a dime into, and through that dime-width the wind came in not as wind exactly but as a pressure that made the matches lean.

The water took too long. I stood with both palms flat on the counter edge because that was how you read the ship's angle when your eyes had stopped being reliable—through the small bones of the wrist, the way the counter pushed back harder on the left hand, then traded, then found a sickening pause at the top of each roll where for one full second neither hand bore weight and the kettle slid and the dishtowel I'd wadded under it compressed to nothing and was useless and I thought very clearly *this is the time it goes over* and then it didn't. It traded back. The water started to mutter in the bottom of the kettle, the small preliminary sounds before boiling, the sounds like a conversation in another room you're not supposed to hear.

I had the tea tin wedged behind the gas pipe fitting where it met the bulkhead, the space exactly right for the circumference of the tin, as though the ship had been built to keep this one thing safe. I had to reach across the stove to get it and the reach put my forearm over the burner flame and I held it there one second too long because the ship swung and I had to grip the pipe to stay standing. The hair on my forearm went to nothing and the smell was the same as the smell of the singed rope ends the bosun used to seal with a lighter, standing on the aft deck in Havana with his feet crossed, casual about it in the way you're casual about a thing you've done nine thousand times. Everyone had been casual about something on this ship. That was what made it a crew.

I spooned the tea into the strainer. Two scoops. Wen took two scoops and no milk and one sugar and I knew this the way I knew the path from my bunk to the head in the dark, which is to say not as knowledge but as the body's own sentence, a thing my hands would do if I were asleep or dead. The sugar tin was not where I'd left it. I found it seventeen minutes later behind the fire extinguisher mount in the corridor outside the galley, on its side, lid off, a trail of white granules making a kind of crude map on the deck—here is where the ship rolled to port, here is where it paused, here is where it fell starboard and the sugar went with it and stopped against the frame of the engine room door. I scooped what I could back into the tin. Some of it was wet. I used it anyway. Wen would not notice the clumping. Wen was not noticing things in the order she used to.

I carried the cup with both hands the way you carry a thing you've been told is holy. The corridor to the bridge was thirty-two steps and I had walked it twice a day for six years, breakfast tea and evening tea, a ritual so fixed it had worn a kind of groove not in the deck but in me, a groove-shaped expectation in the muscles of my calves and the particular attentiveness of my fingers around ceramic, and the thirty-two steps that afternoon took four minutes because every third step the ship went somewhere I didn't expect and I stopped and braced the cup against my sternum and felt the hot liquid find the rim and retreat and find it again, a small mouth trying to speak and being silenced, trying and being silenced.

The bridge was dark except for the instrument panels. Green light on every surface. Wen was at the chart table with both hands spread on the paper as though she could hold the lines in place, as though the contour intervals of the ocean floor would stay where she'd last agreed to them. Her rain jacket was on but unzipped and beneath it her shirt was soaked in a way that meant the bridge windows had been open at some point or the spray had come through the seals—the same seals, the rubber going, everything on this ship slowly unsealing itself from itself.

I put the cup on the chart table. It slid four inches to starboard and stopped against the parallel ruler.

"Tea," I said.

Wen looked at the cup the way you look at something you recognize from a previous life. She nodded. She did not reach for it.

I stood there. I don't know how long. Long enough to count the sounds: the instrument panel ticking, the hull making its deep-gut sound that was not a groan and not a whine but something between the two like a dog dreaming of being hurt, and the rain which by that point was hitting the glass not as rain but as a continuous thing, a wall of water being leaned against us by whatever was out there that I did not look at and would not describe because I am a cook and what I saw was the cup on the chart table with the tea cooling inside it and the surface tilting left, right, left, right, a tiny sea inside a cup inside a ship inside a sea, and the way the liquid moved it looked as if even the tea was trying to leave.

I waited.

Wen traced something on the chart with her finger. I think it was a route. I think it was a route she had already decided against.

I retrieved the cup. Tested the temperature against the inside of my wrist the way you test a bottle of milk before you give it to an infant, that gesture of care so automatic it embarrassed me, standing on the bridge of a ship that was dying with a cup of tea no one wanted held to my skin like a question I already knew the answer to. It was cold. Not cool. Cold. The ship had taken the warmth out of it or the time had or I had by standing there waiting for a woman to drink something she was never going to drink while the world outside the glass arranged itself into a shape I was not willing to look at directly.

I went back to the galley. I boiled the water. I braced the kettle. I spooned the tea, two scoops, and the sugar, one, clumped, and I carried it thirty-two steps in four and a half minutes this time because the ship had learned a new direction to fall in, a yaw that came after the roll and before the pitch like an extra syllable crammed into a word that already had too many, and I set the cup down and it slid and I caught it and set it down again and it stayed.

Wen was in the same position. Hands on the chart. Eyes on a depth sounding off the Sargasso that meant nothing to either of us now.

The second cup also went cold.

I held it to my wrist and the liquid was exactly the temperature of the ship, which was exactly the temperature of the water coming in, which I knew not from any instrument but from the fact that the warmth had left the cup with the same patience the warmth had left everything else—the engine room, the galley stove between meals, Wen's voice over the last forty-eight hours, all of it going to the same temperature, equilibrium, the thermodynamic term for *giving up*.

I poured both cups into the galley sink. The drain was slow. Everything was slow now except the things that were too fast.

---

The crew went off in the boats twelve hours before the second cup of tea. That is the order it happened in, though not the order I've given it, because it didn't seem important at the time and that is a lie but it is the lie I am telling, so: the crew went off in the boats.

Wen gave the order at 1800 on the first day, which was a Tuesday, though by then the days had stopped having names and started having only weather. She pulled Macedo and Fontaine and little Guo aside on the main deck where the wind had dropped to something merely horrible rather than impossible, and she said things to them I could not hear from the galley porthole but could read from the geometry of their bodies—Macedo stepping back, Fontaine leaning in with that jaw he got when he thought he was being brave, Guo looking at his boots. Lifeboats. EPIRB. Bearing for Hamilton. She gave them the heading the way she gave all orders, with the flat certainty of someone reading a tide table, and they took it the way they took all orders, which is to say with the various small resistances of people who know they're being managed and are grateful for it.

There were nine. Nine crew and two officers and me, which made twelve, which made the *Mara Celeste* a small ship with a full complement or a medium ship with a skeleton one depending on which registry you believed, and I had been feeding all twelve of them for six years and I knew what each of them took for breakfast and how they held their forks and which ones came back for seconds when they thought no one was counting and I want to say their names now because this is the part where I am supposed to say their names but I find I don't want to. They went. They took the good lifeboat and the adequate lifeboat and the EPIRB and four cases of water and the medical kit and the flare box and they went, and that is the verb that matters. *Went.* Past tense. Finished. The lifeboats dropped into water I did not look at and moved away from us in a direction I did not track and I went back to the galley and did the evening prep because it was 1830 and at 1830 I do the evening prep.

The *Mara Celeste* was built in 1974 at a yard in Kaohsiung for a man named Wen Zhaolong, who was Captain Wen's grandfather, who was dead. He had been dead for eleven years. The ship had been alive for forty-nine. In between those two facts was a story made of paper: registration transfers, holding company amendments, a maritime lien filed by Pacific Basin Finance Ltd. on the fourteenth of March, a demand letter dated June, another dated August, a third dated September with the word FINAL in a typeface large enough to read from across the cabin, and all of them rubber-banded together in a stack on the desk in Wen's quarters next to a photograph of the old man standing on the foredeck in Keelung with his hand on the rail and his face pointed into a wind that, from the angle of his collar, must have been about fifteen knots, which is a pleasant wind, a wind that does not kill anyone.

I know about the letters because I cleaned the cabin. I know about the lien because I read the letters. I know about the holding company—Celeste Maritime Holdings, registered in Monrovia, beneficial owner Wen Lian, sole director Wen Lian—because the third letter named it four times and each time the font got smaller as if the company were shrinking under the pressure of being mentioned. There was a clause about salvage rights. I am not a lawyer and I did not understand the clause entirely but I understood the word *forfeit* and I understood the word *surrender* and I understood that Wen had underlined both words with the same mechanical pencil she used on the charts, the 0.5mm with the cracked barrel I had glued once with epoxy from the engine room kit, and the lines she'd drawn under those words were not the lines of someone reading but of someone being read to by a voice she couldn't make stop.

Bermuda was 340 nautical miles to the northwest. In Bermuda there were men from the insurance company and men from the bank and men from the flag state and all of them wanted the same thing, which was the ship, and Wen wanted the same thing, which was the ship, and the difference was that their wanting was backed by paper and hers was backed by whatever it is that makes a woman stand on a bridge in a typhoon with her hands on a chart she isn't reading while tea goes cold beside her.

She sent them all off. She stayed. That is the sentence. That is the whole story of her decision and it has the shape of a noble thing if you don't look too closely and the shape of a spiteful thing if you do and the truth is probably not a shape at all but a temperature, the temperature of the letters on the desk, the temperature of the photograph, the temperature of a granddaughter who would rather give the ship back to the ocean that her grandfather had sailed it on than let a man in a Hamilton office write *REPOSSESSED* on a form.

I did not go.

The evening prep was rice and tinned mackerel and pickled vegetables from the stores and I made it for two instead of twelve and the portions were the same size they had always been, which meant I had made too much, which meant I could not yet count correctly in the new numbers of our lives.

---

The water came into the galley on the third morning with the slow confidence of something that had been invited. It came under the door and through the deck seam where the linoleum had lifted and up from below through a crack in the sole that I had reported in August and that Macedo had looked at and said *it's cosmetic* and that was now admitting the sea in a thin, patient line, a line of water like a sentence that would not end.

Ankle-deep by 0700. I know because I put my foot down off the bunk at 0700 as I have every morning for six years and instead of the deck my foot found water and the water was not cold—that was the wrong thing about it. It was warm. It was warmer than the cup of tea had been because it had come through the engine room and taken whatever heat was left there, the last warmth of a ship that had stopped trying to be warmer than the sea.

I put on the rubber boots from the locker. I went to the galley. I lit the stove—the gas still worked, which seemed implausible, a small municipal kindness in the middle of an apocalypse. I boiled water. I made tea. Two cups. I carried one to the bridge, thirty-two steps in the water now, each step a small splash that was nothing like the sound of walking and everything like the sound of a child in a bathtub who doesn't know yet that bathtime is almost over.

Wen was sitting in the captain's chair. She had not been sitting the night before. Sitting was new. Sitting meant something I noted and did not examine.

"Cards," she said.

I went back for the deck. The cards were in the drawer under the galley clock where they had been for six years, a pack of fifty-two with a joker on top that had a water stain on its face from a spill in Guayaquil that made the joker look as though it were weeping through its grin. The drawer was stiff. The wood had swelled. I pulled and it gave with a sound that was exactly the sound of the stuck drawer in my mother's kitchen in Cebu, the one with the knives in it, the one you had to lift and pull at the same time, and for a moment I was in both kitchens at once, the one that was sinking and the one that had simply aged, and I took the cards and closed the drawer and went back.

We played at the chart table because it was the only flat surface on the bridge that was bolted down. I dealt. Gin rummy. We had played gin rummy at 0730 every morning we were at sea for six years. The ritual had survived calms and squalls and engine failures and the time Fontaine broke his wrist falling down the forepeak ladder and we diverted to Colón, and it survived the departure of nine crew members and two officers and it survived the water in the galley and it survived the sound the hull was making, which had changed overnight from a dreaming dog to something else, something I will call a cabinet hinge because that is what it sounded like—the long, high plea of a hinge that has been asked to hold a door that is too heavy for it, a sound of metal that has been patient and is now finished being patient.

I wrote the score on a napkin. It was a cloth napkin, one of the good ones I'd hemmed myself in the first year because the ship's napkins were paper and paper is an insult to a meal, even a meal of tinned mackerel and rice, even a meal served to two people on a ship that is taking water in the galley, because there are things you do not stop doing just because the reasons for doing them have changed, and the napkin lay on the chart table and the corner closest to the starboard edge began to wick seawater up from a puddle that had formed in the gutter of the table where the chart was supposed to sit, and the wetness crept up the cloth fiber by fiber, number by number, eating the score from the bottom.

Wen drew a seven of spades
