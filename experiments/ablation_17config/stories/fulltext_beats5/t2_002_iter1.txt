# The HOA Board Will Now Come to Order

## I.

The violation notices arrived in a stack, which suggested they had been accumulating somewhere — Doug's desk, perhaps, or a tray beside his printer — before being delivered all at once, rubber-banded, in a gallon Ziploc bag to protect against the morning's rain. Meredith appreciated the Ziploc. It was a considerate touch.

She spread them across the kitchen table, seventeen notices covering seventeen days at fifty dollars per day compounding, each printed on Meadow Glen Commons letterhead — the little line drawing of a sycamore tree in the upper left corner, the sans-serif font that someone on the original board had chosen in 2006 because it looked, in their words, "clean and community-forward." Section 4.11(c), Exterior Structural Modifications: *No homeowner shall construct, install, or permit to remain any structural addition, extension, or modification to the exterior of a dwelling unit, including but not limited to ramps, decks, pergolas, or permanent awnings, without prior written approval from the Architectural Review Committee.* The fine schedule was printed on the back of each notice. Fifty dollars per day for the first thirty days; seventy-five per day thereafter; after ninety days, the HOA reserved the right to place a lien.

Beside the Ziploc bag, also on the porch, also protected from rain — this time in aluminum foil with a ribbon — was a loaf of banana bread. The handwritten note was on a card with a watercolor hummingbird: *Thinking of you both during this difficult season! — Doug & Linda.* The bread was still warm.

From the back bedroom, Ray's oxygen concentrator hummed its two-beat rhythm. He was asleep. He slept fourteen hours a day now, sometimes sixteen, the disease taking his waking life in increments the way a tide takes a beach — not all at once, not dramatically, just a little more each time until you looked up and the sand was gone. The ramp had cost four thousand two hundred dollars and had been installed in an afternoon by a contractor who had not asked about permits because he built medical-accessibility ramps for a living and had never encountered a neighborhood that required them to go before review. He'd said this to Meredith with the particular exhaustion of a man who had, in fact, encountered it many times.

Meredith set the banana bread on the counter beside the coffee maker. She did not throw it away. She sat back down at the table and read Section 4.11(c) again. Then she read 4.11(a) and 4.11(b) and then the entirety of Article IV, which governed the Architectural Review Committee's composition, meeting schedule, and appeals process. She found her pencil — the mechanical one, the 0.5mm Pentel she used for actuarial notations when she still worked, before she'd taken leave, before the leave had become permanent in everything but paperwork.

She underlined: *Prior written approval.*

She turned to Article IV, Section 3: *The Architectural Review Committee shall make available to all homeowners, upon request or at the time of purchase, the Modification Request Form (Appendix D). The Committee shall act on any properly submitted request within fourteen (14) business days.*

She turned to Appendix D. Appendix D was not there. The bylaws, downloaded from the HOA website, jumped from Appendix C ("Approved Exterior Paint Colors — Revised 2019") to Appendix E ("Holiday Decoration Guidelines and Removal Deadlines"). Appendix D had either never existed or had been removed from the digital version. She made a note of this on the margin. She wrote the date.

On the counter, the banana bread sat in its foil, already cooling into something solid.

Meredith worked through the bylaws for another forty minutes. She marked eleven provisions. She made a list. At one point Ray's concentrator cycled louder and then quieter, a sound she had learned not to react to because it happened six or eight times a night and if she reacted every time she would never stop reacting. She turned to Article IX, Records and Financial Transparency, and read it with the particular attention she'd once reserved for mortality tables — the attention that comes when you suspect the numbers are going to tell you something the people who compiled them did not intend.

At the bottom of Article IX, Section 4, in language so standard it was almost invisible: *Any homeowner in good standing may, upon written request delivered to the Board Secretary, inspect and copy the financial records of the Association, including but not limited to annual budgets, receipts, vendor contracts, and disbursement ledgers, during normal business hours and at a reasonable time and place to be designated by the Board.*

She circled it twice.

## II.

Gloria Maye lived in unit 34, the one with the ceramic garden gnomes that had survived three separate Architectural Review challenges because Gloria, whatever her professional failings, understood the power of an appeal filed on time and in triplicate. She answered the door in a housecoat and readers, holding a mug that said WORLD'S MOST ACCURATE.

"I know who you are," Gloria said. "You're the ramp."

"I'm Meredith Chen. I live on — "

"You're the ramp. Doug wouldn't shut up about it at the last board meeting. Said it looked — what was the word. 'Institutional.' He said it made the cul-de-sac look institutional."

Meredith took a breath. She had prepared for this conversation the way she prepared for everything: with an outline, a fallback position, and an actuarial understanding that the probability of any human interaction following your outline was roughly eleven percent.

"I need a stenographer," she said.

Gloria's eyes narrowed, then widened, then narrowed again — a facial journey that covered suspicion, interest, and the suppression of interest in about two seconds. "I'm not certified anymore."

"I know."

"What happened was — they made it sound like I editorialized, but what I did was provide *context*. The transcript is supposed to be a complete record. How is it complete if it doesn't capture that the witness was obviously lying through his — "

"Gloria."

"What."

"I need someone who will create an exact, verbatim, indisputable record of the annual meeting on March fifteenth. Not because I want to add anything to the record. Because I want to make sure nothing gets taken out."

Gloria leaned against the doorframe. Behind her, the house smelled like Earl Grey and old paper. "Doug writes the minutes himself, you know. Has for years. Last annual meeting, Terri Fitzpatrick raised a question about the snow-removal contract — asked why we were paying twice the county average — and in the published minutes it came out as 'A homeowner expressed appreciation for the board's diligent winter maintenance efforts.' I was sitting right there. Terri said *diligent* like she was going to choke on it."

"Will you do it?"

"I'll need to bring my machine. It's a Diamante — I bought it with my own money after the certification board took back the office equipment, which, by the way, they had no legal basis to — "

"Bring the machine."

"I'll be there at six-thirty. Doors open at seven?"

"Seven."

"I'll be there at six-thirty." Gloria took a sip from her mug with what Meredith recognized as the precise, calibrated dignity of a person who has been waiting for someone to need her specific skill again.

Phil Keener did not answer the door. He answered the garage, which was open, because he was at a folding table in the middle of it, surrounded by three-ring binders and a whiteboard on an easel. On the whiteboard, in four colors of dry-erase marker, was a flowchart. At the top it read: MAIN MOTION → SUBSIDIARY MOTIONS → PRIVILEGED MOTIONS → INCIDENTAL MOTIONS. There were arrows, decision trees, and, in the bottom right corner, a small drawing of a gavel with motion lines around it, as if it had just been struck.

"Mr. Keener?"

Phil looked up. He was sixty-eight, bald in a way that suggested not loss but *completion* — as if hair had been a draft and this was the final version. He wore a polo shirt tucked into khakis with a braided belt, and his posture was so upright that Meredith's lower back issued a sympathetic complaint.

"I'm preparing." He gestured at the binders. "Regionals are in six weeks."

"Regionals?"

"National Association of Parliamentarians, Mid-Atlantic Region, Mock Parliamentary Competition. I've come in second twice. The man who beat me — " Phil stopped. Something moved across his face like weather. "Gerald Forsythe. He chairs out of Annapolis. His command of the subsidiary motion stack is — technically proficient. I'll say that much for him."

"Can I ask you a procedural question?"

Phil Keener looked at Meredith Chen the way a doctor looks at a patient who has finally agreed to discuss the lump. "Sit down," he said, and pulled over a second folding chair.

She told him. All of it. The ramp, the fines, the bylaws, the missing Appendix D, the Article IX records request she intended to file, and — in broad strokes that she would fill in only after the audit was complete — the financial questions she expected the records to answer. She told him what she wanted to accomplish at the annual meeting. She told him it had to be procedurally airtight, survivable against any point of order, consistent with the governing documents, and compliant with the state nonprofit corporation code. She told him it had to be done in one meeting because she did not know how many meetings she had left before the fines became a lien and the lien became a legal battle she could not afford to fight while also keeping her husband alive.

Phil listened without interrupting. He did not take notes. When she finished, he was quiet for eleven seconds — Meredith counted, because the actuary's reflex under stress is to measure — and then he said:

"You're describing a beautiful meeting."

He said it the way someone might say *you're describing a beautiful building* or *you're describing a beautiful proof*. Without irony. Without qualification. With the reverence of a man for whom the orderly sequence of motions, seconds, debates, and votes was not bureaucracy but architecture — a cathedral made of rules, and inside it, the closest thing to fairness he had ever found.

"I can build this," Phil said. He was already reaching for a fresh binder. "We start from the end. The vote of no confidence. We work backward. Every motion needs a second — you have seconds?"

"I have seconds."

"The chair will try to rule you out of order. We need to anticipate every objection and have the bylaw citation ready. Not paraphrased. Exact. Page and section. Can you get me a copy of the governing documents?"

"I'll bring them tonight."

"Bring two copies. And the state code, Title Twenty-Three, Chapter Eleven, Sections One through Forty-Seven. I have it, but mine's annotated for tournament use and the margin notes might confuse you."

The book club met on Thursdays at Janet Moriarty's house, and had met on Thursdays at Janet Moriarty's house for eleven years without interruption — through Janet's knee replacement, through Bev Okafor's daughter's wedding, through the Great Alchemist Schism of 2018, which had cost them a member (Diane Polley, who had called it "a life-changing fable" and whose name was no longer spoken) and strengthened the remaining four in the way that shared contempt for a common enemy strengthens any alliance.

They had not read a book in three weeks. They had been canvassing.

"Forty-three," said Janet, setting a manila folder on the coffee table beside the Trader Joe's snack platter. "Forty-three proxy votes, signed and dated. Lorraine got six from the Sycamore Court cul-de-sac alone."

Lorraine Kim, who was seventy-one and looked fifty-five and had the particular social gift of making every interaction feel like a confidence between old friends, shrugged modestly. "They were easy. The Nguyens have been angry about the parking enforcement since 2021. The Hendersons' son got fined for his basketball hoop. I just asked if they were planning to attend the meeting, and when they said no, I said, 'Well, would you like your voice to count anyway?'"

"That's terrifying," said Meredith.

"That's canvassing," said Janet.

Bev Okafor laid out the proxy forms. Each was on the proper HOA form — they had found it in Appendix F, one of the appendices that *did* exist — and each designated Janet, Bev, Lorraine, or Sue as the proxy holder. The forms were organized by street. They had coverage in every section of Meadow Glen Commons except The Arbors, the newer construction on the north side, where Doug's support was strongest because he'd fast-tracked their drainage easement in 2020 and they felt they owed him.

Sue Park, who had said the least and canvassed the most — fourteen proxies, the highest individual total — sat in the corner of Janet's sofa and ate a cracker and said nothing. She had a way of listening that made you certain she was forming a judgment and equally certain she would not share it until the judgment was complete.

"We have enough," Meredith said. "If Phil's sequence works and the quorum holds, we have enough."

"If," said Bev.

"If," Meredith agreed.

On the drive home, she passed Doug Whitmore's house. The lights were on. Through the front window she could see him at what appeared to be a desk, writing. The garage door was up, and inside, beside a riding mower and a tower of mulch bags, she could see a small desk with a filing cabinet and a label maker. The label on the filing cabinet's top drawer, visible even from the street, read: HOA — CURRENT YEAR. The second drawer: HOA — ARCHIVE. The third: WHITMORE & FAMILY LAWN.

She drove on.

At home, the banana bread had not moved. It sat on the counter in its foil with the hummingbird card propped against it. It looked, in the kitchen's dim light, like a small monument to something — she was not sure what. Goodwill. Obliviousness. The space between what a person does and what a person thinks they are doing.

From the back bedroom: the two-beat hum.

## III.

Article IX, Section 4 turned out to be the kind of provision that works exactly the way it's supposed to, which is to say it worked only when someone was stubborn enough to invoke it. Meredith filed her written request on a Monday. By Wednesday, she'd received an email from Doug:

*Hi Meredith! I got your records request — first one we've ever had, I think! 😊 I'm happy to make our financials available. As you probably know, the Treasurer's summary is posted on the website every quarter. Can I point you to that? It's very comprehensive. — Doug*

She replied:

*Thank you, Doug. Per Article IX, Section 4, I am requesting inspection of the complete financial records, including annual budgets, receipts, vendor contracts, and disbursement ledgers. Please let me know the reasonable time and place you'd like to designate. — Meredith*

Forty-eight hours passed. Then:

*Hi Meredith — Talked to the board and we're happy to set something up! Linda reminded me I should mention that some of our older records are in storage and might take a bit to pull together. Would sometime in April work for you? After the spring mulch cycle settles down. 😊 — Doug*

Meredith consulted the state nonprofit corporation code — Title 23, Chapter 11, Section 29, which Phil had highlighted and tabbed with a green flag. She replied:

*Doug — Under state code § 23-11-29, the Association is required to make records available within five (5) business days of a written request. My request was submitted Monday. I am available Thursday and Friday of this week at any time convenient to the Board. — Meredith*

The records were made available Thursday, in a folding chair in the community center's storage room, where Doug had set up a card table, a desk lamp, and — Meredith noted this with something between admiration and disbelief — a small plate of cookies.
