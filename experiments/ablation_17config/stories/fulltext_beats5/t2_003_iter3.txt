# Perdition Gulch

## I.

The well outside the assayer's office was three feet lower than last year, and the five of them sat around a table with their backs to the window as though the light coming through it were something they had agreed, without discussion, to ignore.

The rancher's name was Moss. He stood turning his hat in his hands and his accusation was simple: the railroad surveyor, a man called Dunlevy, had poisoned his well. Dunlevy sat in a cane-back chair with his ankle crossed over his knee and his face composed in the particular expression of a man who believes he is smarter than everyone in the room and has not yet been given cause to reconsider.

Ada Reyes opened her journal—though she did not need the journal, had never needed it, the journal being a performance of labor meant to make the knowing look like work—and recited: "November second, Dunlevy purchased three-quarters of a pound of white arsenic from Kessler's General Supply at fourteen minutes past two in the afternoon, paying in coin, exact change, no conversation. November third through seventh, Dunlevy surveyed the eastern ridgeline from the Moss property fence to the rail spur, arriving each morning before dawn and departing after dark. November eighth, Dunlevy visited the Moss well at approximately eleven at night. He carried a glass jar. He did not carry it on the return."

Every date, every minute, every detail retrieved from the architecture of a memory that did not decay, that held everything it had ever received the way a cathedral holds sound, and Dunlevy's ankle uncrossed itself, and the composed expression slipped by perhaps a quarter of an inch.

The child was sitting on the floor between the twins' chairs, drawing something on a piece of brown paper with a stub of pencil, and without looking up the child said, "He's thinking about the second well."

Dunlevy went white.

"He's thinking that you don't know about the second well," the child continued, in the low, distracted murmur of someone repeating sounds heard from an adjacent room. "The one on the Kimball property. He did that one first. Practice. He's thinking—" A pause. The pencil stopped. "He's thinking he should have brought his gun."

Lena and Leta Coe sat beside each other with their hands folded on the table in mirror postures that were not mirror postures because there was no imitation involved, only a single intent expressed through two bodies the way a single word is expressed through multiple letters. They looked at Dunlevy—both of them, at the same instant, with the same tilt of the head, one degree to the left—and what passed between them was not a glance and not a decision but something more like a current changing direction in a wire. Leta's hand moved to the table's edge. Lena's moved to her lap. This meant something. No one in the room knew what it meant except the two of them, who were not two.

Elias Cane, the healer, rolled his left sleeve to the elbow. The skin of his forearm showed a wound the size of a half-dollar, still pink and tightening, the flesh visibly knitting as Dunlevy watched—the bullet from that morning's altercation at the rail camp when a foreman had shot Cane through the arm at close range and Cane had looked down at the hole and then up at the foreman and said nothing and walked to the assayer's office with his blood dripping into the dust where it dried in spots the exact color of rust. The wound was closing now. It closed the way a mouth closes. There was nothing theatrical about it. Cane did not flex or wince. He simply showed the arm and let the arm speak its one fluent sentence: *You cannot kill me, and what you cannot kill you cannot frighten, and what you cannot frighten you cannot control.*

Dunlevy confessed.

He confessed in a tumbling, graceless rush, his voice cracking upward into a register that would have been funny if Moss had not been standing there with the hat crushed in his hands and his cattle dead and his children sick with what the doctor in Ridley had called "mineral stomach." Dunlevy named the railroad company. He named the agent who had paid him. He named the price, which was forty dollars, which was less than the value of one of Moss's dead heifers, which was a fact that nobody pointed out because pointing it out would have required a cruelty that the five of them had learned to leave in the room's silence rather than in anyone's mouth.

Moss thanked them. He did not understand what had happened. He understood that the council knew things, and the knowing was sufficient, and he left.

Dunlevy was escorted out by no one in particular. He would leave town by morning. They knew this because the child had heard it in his mind — the horse already saddled in the image behind his eyes, the road east, the intention to never return — and the child mentioned it to no one because the child had already returned to drawing, which depicted a house with five chimneys, each chimney producing a different color of smoke, and in the sky above the house a large bird or a small cloud, and the child did not know which.

The five sat alone in the assayer's office. The light through the window had shifted. Dust lay on the sill in a thickness that Ada could have told you to the day — eleven days' accumulation, meaning no one had wiped it since the last rain, meaning there had been no rain. The well outside was three feet lower than last year and two feet lower than it should have been for November and the water that remained tasted of chalk and something beneath chalk, somethingite or mineral or simply old, water that had been underground so long it had forgotten the taste of sky.

Someone — no one among the five, no one anyone could name — had scratched a word into the assayer's door at a height that suggested a child or a kneeling man:

*PERDITION*

The letters were not deep. A season of weather would erase them. But for now they held, as words do, the way a scar holds the memory of a blade without holding the blade itself, and the five of them walked past the word every morning and every evening and not one of them, in fourteen years, had mentioned it aloud.

The narrator — patient, omniscient, already grieving in the way that only those who know the end can grieve while the middle is still unfolding — noted the dust, and the well, and the word, and the five chimneys in the child's drawing, and said nothing, because saying nothing was, for now, the kindest thing.

## II.

The morning the silence came, the child was eating porridge.

Not good porridge. Lena made the porridge and Lena cooked the way she did everything that did not involve her sister — with competence but without instinct, following steps she had memorized rather than felt, and the porridge reflected this: edible, nourishing, blank. The child was eating the porridge and hearing, as always, everything: Lena's mild irritation at the stove's damper, which stuck; Leta's dream, not yet fully dissolved, about a river they had crossed as girls in Missouri, the water brown and the current stronger than it looked; a man outside thinking about his horse's hoof; a woman across the street composing a letter to her sister in her mind, choosing and discarding words the way a woman chooses and discards dresses — *I hope this finds you well* no *I have been meaning to write* no *Dear Helen you would not recognize this place* — and beneath all of this, the deep, tidal hum of the town itself, three hundred and forty-one minds producing a sound like wind through a stand of aspens, each leaf distinct if you listened for it, the whole merging into a single quaking if you did not.

The child was nine years old and had been hearing this since birth and understood silence the way a fish understands dry land: as a theoretical condition, terrible and abstract, something that happened to others.

At seven minutes past eight — Ada would have known the exact time, would have annotated it, would have placed it in the long ledger of the town's chronology — the sound stopped.

Not faded. Not diminished. *Stopped.* The way a hand stops a bell. The way a knife stops a throat. One moment the child's mind was full of three hundred and forty-one voices and the river in Leta's dream and the letter to Helen and the sticky damper and the hum beneath the hum, and the next moment there was nothing, a nothing so total and so sudden that the child dropped the spoon and the porridge and stood up and opened their mouth and could not speak because the silence was not merely in the world. It was *in.* It was interior. It had replaced something essential, the way a stone replaces a heart in the old stories, and the child stood in Lena's kitchen and for the first time heard only the sound of their own breathing, their own blood, the horrifying privacy of a single mind with no exits.

"Jesse?" Lena said. She was looking at the child with an expression the child could not read, because the child had never needed to read expressions — had always simply *known* the feeling behind the face — and now the face was a landscape without a map, features arranged in a pattern that could mean concern or anger or love or the particular tender exasperation of a woman looking at a child who had dropped porridge on her clean floor, and the child could not tell the difference, could not tell any difference, was standing on the far side of a wall that had not existed five seconds ago and would, as it turned out, exist forever.

The child ran.

Out the door, down the porch steps, into the street where the man was still thinking about his horse's hoof except the child could not hear him thinking about it, could only see a man standing beside a horse, and the child ran to the man and pressed both palms against his face — the stubble, the warmth, the smell of tobacco and leather — and heard nothing. Pressed harder. Nothing. The man swore and pushed the child away and the child was already running to the next person, a woman carrying a basket of eggs, and the child reached for her face and the woman pulled back and an egg fell and broke in the dust, the yolk orange and vivid against the gray dirt, and the child heard *nothing nothing nothing nothing.*

Lena found the child behind the livery stable, sitting in the straw with both hands over their ears, pressing inward as though the silence could be compressed back into sound, and the child was not crying because crying was the expression of an emotion and the child did not know yet what this emotion was—this vast, landless, borderless thing that was not sadness and not fear but something prior to both, some original, underlying condition of the human self that the child had been shielded from since birth and was only now meeting, the way a person raised in a palace might one day step outside and discover that the palace was built on a swamp and the swamp had been there the whole time, beneath every marble floor, every gold-leafed ceiling, every crystal glass of wine.

The child pressed and pressed and heard only the sound of pressing.

The wind changed that night, and the story changed with it.

Fourteen years. Different dust, the same canyon. 1873, autumn, the light in the territory falling at the particular amber angle that made the scrub cedars look dignified and the bare ground look gilded and everything look briefly like a painting of itself, beautiful and already elegiac, already past.

They were younger then. Ada Reyes was twenty-six and her memory was six years old — she had noticed it at twenty, the perfect retention, the inability to forget even what she wished to, the accumulation of detail that was not like a library and not like a machine but like a flood, like living in a house where the water rose an inch each day and you could not bail and could not swim, could only learn to breathe with your mouth closer and closer to the ceiling. She was lean, sharp-faced, and she kept her journals already, three of them, and the handwriting was the same handwriting it would be in forty years: steady, small, precise, a hand that could have engraved watchplates.

Elias Cane was thirty-one and had known since he was fourteen, since the horse kicked him in the chest and he felt the ribs break and then felt them unbreak, felt the bones reaching for each other like hands in the dark, knitting and fusing while he lay in the hay and stared at the rafters and understood that something in him refused to stay damaged, that his body treated injury the way other bodies treated insult — as a temporary condition to be corrected and forgotten. He did not know why. He had stopped asking.

Lena and Leta Coe were twenty-three and had been one mind in two bodies for as long as either could remember, which was the same memory, the same long thread of experience viewed from two slightly different angles, the way a man with two eyes sees a single world in depth. They had come west from Missouri — the river in the dream, the brown water, the current — and they had come because the territory was big enough that two women who completed each other's sentences and moved in unison and sometimes, unsettlingly, breathed in unison might be taken for eccentric rather than uncanny. They had arrived in Perdition Gulch three months before. They had rented rooms. They had begun, tentatively, to call it home.

The child was an infant, parentage unclear, found by one of the twins — Leta — on the road outside town in a basket with no note, which was not unusual in the territory, where children were sometimes left the way one leaves a letter, hoping the right person finds it. Leta carried the baby. The baby did not cry. The baby seemed, to both twins, to be listening — though to what, at a week old, could a child be listening with such intensity, head tilted, eyes wide and unfocused, as though the visible world were a distraction from a more interesting one?

The word about the wagon train reached them separately, the way all news moved through small towns: in fragments, through partial mouths, reassembled in the hearing like a shattered plate glued back together from shards that didn't quite fit. A supply rider came in from the eastern road and said he'd seen smoke in the canyon. A man at the saloon said he'd heard gunfire that morning, distant, rhythmic, the kind that meant not a fight but a slaughter — one side shooting, the other side dying. A woman at the dry-goods store said the Comanche had been raiding south of the river for weeks and that the cavalry was three days away, at minimum, which was the same as saying the cavalry did not exist.

Ada Reyes knew the canyon. She had ridden through it in April and her memory held it the way a daguerreotype holds a face: the walls of red sandstone, the narrow defile at the eastern end where two wagons could pass abreast but not three, the open bowl at the western end where you could make camp but could not defend against an attack from the rim, because the rim was higher than a rifle could reach and the walls were too sheer to climb and you would sit in the bowl and the shots would come down on you like rain.

Elias Cane could have walked into the gunfire. This was not bravery. It was arithmetic. Bullets did not kill him. His body repaired itself faster than a bullet could damage it. He could draw fire, absorb it, and by drawing it give others time.

The twins could have coordinated a flanking approach without speaking, without signals — one mind reading the terrain from two positions, adjusting in real time to a geometry of rescue that only a shared consciousness could compute — and they knew this, both of them, simultaneously, the way they knew everything simultaneously, which was not like agreement and not like discussion but like a compass needle settling on north: not a choice but a recognition of the direction that was already true.

The infant, held by Leta, was broadcasting. The twins could feel it — a wash of emotional noise, formless and overwhelming, that seemed to carry with it the positions and intentions of every mind within range. The raiders' fear, because they were afraid too. The settlers' despair. The particular bright, terrible focus of a man aiming a rifle.
