# The HOA Board Will Now Come to Order

## I.

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Meredith would later consider appropriate because Tuesdays were the days things happened to you, as opposed to Mondays, which were the days you braced for things to happen, and Wednesdays, which were the days you dealt with what had happened on Tuesday. She found it in the mailbox between a credit card offer and a catalog for adaptive clothing she had not yet opened because opening it would mean something she was not ready for it to mean.

The envelope was cream-colored, heavyweight, with the Greystone Terrace Homeowners' Association logo embossed in the upper left corner—a stylized oak tree inside a shield, designed by someone's nephew in 2007, never updated, vaguely feudal. Inside:

---

*Dear Meredith and Family,*

*First, I hope you and Richard are doing as well as can be expected. Donna and I think of you often. I still have your casserole dish from the banana bread I brought over in October—remind me and I'll drop it by.*

*I'm writing in my capacity as President of the Greystone Terrace HOA Board of Directors to inform you that a structural modification has been observed at your property (lot 34, 1412 Ashburn Lane) that does not appear to have received prior approval from the Architectural Review Committee as required under Section 4.11(b) of the Greystone Terrace CC&Rs ("Structural Modifications to Exterior Facades"). Specifically, a wheelchair-accessible ramp has been installed at the front entrance of the home.*

*I want to emphasize that the Board recognizes and respects the medical circumstances your family is facing. Nobody is questioning the need. But as I'm sure you understand, the CC&Rs exist to protect the community as a whole, and if we make exceptions to the approval process—even well-intentioned ones—we undermine the framework that keeps Greystone Terrace the neighborhood we all chose to live in.*

*Per Section 7.03(a), a compliance assessment fee of $50.00 per day will be assessed beginning 14 days from the date of this notice unless the modification is (1) removed, or (2) submitted for retroactive ARC approval with the required documentation (architectural drawings, material specifications, contractor licensing, and proof of conformity with Sections 4.11(b)(i) through 4.11(b)(iv)).*

*Please don't hesitate to reach out. I mean that sincerely.*

*Warm regards,*
*Doug Whitmore*
*President, Greystone Terrace HOA Board of Directors*

---

Meredith read the letter standing in the driveway. Then she read it again. Then she folded it along its original creases and placed it back in the envelope and placed the envelope between the credit card offer and the adaptive clothing catalog and carried all three inside.

Richard was in the living room, in the recliner, which was where Richard was now. The recliner had been his chair for twenty years, but it had become his *location* only in the last four months, since the diagnosis progressed from the abstract language of referrals and imaging to the concrete language of a hospital bed they did not yet need and a wheelchair ramp they did. Their son, Kevin, had built the ramp over a weekend in November. He'd driven down from Tacoma with lumber in the bed of his truck and a video tutorial bookmarked on his phone and a look on his face that Meredith recognized as the particular determination of a man who could not do anything about the disease but could do this one thing about the stairs.

The ramp was pine. It was sturdy. It had a slight rightward drift that Kevin had compensated for with a handrail on the left side. It was not beautiful. It was not ugly. It was a ramp. It was the difference between Richard leaving the house and Richard not leaving the house, and because Richard's world had contracted in recent months from the size of a life to the size of a neighborhood to the size of a house, the ramp was the difference between a small world and no world at all.

Meredith picked up the phone and called Doug Whitmore.

He answered on the second ring. He always answered on the second ring. First ring was too eager, third was too casual. Doug had calibrated this.

"Meredith, hi. How are you. How's Richard."

There was no question mark in his delivery. These were not questions. They were the sounds a kind person makes before saying the unkind thing the kind person has already decided to say.

"Doug, I got your letter."

"I figured you'd call. I told Donna—I said, Meredith's going to call, and I want to be available for that call, because this is exactly the kind of thing that I think we can figure out together if we just—"

"The ramp, Doug."

"I know."

"My husband can't get out of the house without it."

"I know that. Believe me, Meredith, I know that. And it—I'll be honest with you—it made me sick to write that letter. It genuinely did. Donna will tell you. I was up the night before just going back and forth about it. But here's the thing, and I think you know this because you're a reasonable person, which is what I've always admired about you—"

"What's the thing, Doug."

"The thing is the bylaws are the bylaws. And I don't make them. I didn't write Section 4.11(b). I wasn't even on the board when that section was ratified. But I swore—and I don't use that word lightly—I swore when I took this position that I would apply the rules uniformly, because the minute I start making exceptions, even exceptions that my heart tells me to make, I'm not a president anymore. I'm just a guy picking favorites. And then Bob Halloran comes to me and says, well, you let the Chens skip the approval process, how come my patio extension has to go through committee? And I don't have an answer for that, Meredith. I don't have an answer for Bob Halloran."

There was a long silence.

"Bob Halloran's patio extension," Meredith said.

"I'm using an example."

"You're comparing a patio to a wheelchair ramp."

"I'm comparing a process to a process. That's what I'm comparing. And I think if you sit with that—"

"Doug."

"Yeah."

"What does the retroactive approval require?"

He walked her through it. Architectural drawings, to scale, prepared by a licensed professional. Material specifications consistent with the community aesthetic guidelines. A contractor affidavit. Conformity review by the three-member Architectural Review Committee, which met quarterly, the next meeting being in March. This was December. Fourteen days until the fine began. Ninety-one days until the next ARC meeting.

Meredith thanked Doug. Doug said again that he meant it when he said don't hesitate to reach out. Meredith hung up.

She looked at the letter. She looked at Richard, who had fallen asleep in the recliner with the television on—a nature documentary, something about elk. She looked at the ramp through the front window. The ramp Kevin had built with lumber and a YouTube video and the specific love of a son who needed to do one fixable thing.

Then she sat down at the kitchen table and opened her laptop and she did what she had done every working day for thirty-one years at Pacific Mutual Life, what she had done so instinctively and for so long that it had become not a skill but a mode of perception, not something she *chose* to do but something that happened to her when the world presented her with uncertainty. She opened a spreadsheet. She began to calculate.

$50 per day.

Dr. Petrosyan had said six to fourteen months, in September. That was three months ago. So three to eleven months remaining, if the estimate held, which estimates often didn't, but you had to start somewhere.

Three months minimum: $50 × 90 = $4,500.

Eleven months maximum: $50 × 334 = $16,700.

Expected value, assuming a roughly normal distribution and adjusting for the recent decline in Richard's appetite and mobility, which moved the probability mass leftward: call it seven months. $50 × 213 = $10,650.

Ten thousand six hundred and fifty dollars. The cost of Richard being able to leave his own home for the remainder of the time in which he would be alive to leave it.

Meredith stared at the number. She highlighted the cell. She changed the font to bold, then changed it back. She saved the file. She named it *ramp.xlsx*. She closed the laptop.

In the living room, the elk were crossing a river. Richard's chest rose and fell under his blanket, which was the green wool one from the hall closet because the heating in the house was never quite even and the recliner was in a draft. His feet, in thick socks, were propped on the leg rest Kevin had fixed with a replacement spring in October, during the same visit he'd scouted the front steps and quietly measured the drop from the threshold to the walkway. His mouth was slightly open. His hands, which had once done fine carpentry, which had built the bookshelves in the den and the raised beds in the backyard and a toy chest for each of Kevin's children, were folded on his chest, and they were thinner now than they had been, visibly thinner, and this was the thing about the disease that no one told you—not the large decimations but the small ones, the way the body subtracted from itself incrementally, ledger-line by ledger-line, a reverse compounding that she recognized because she had spent her career modeling exactly this kind of decline, only in tables, only in populations, never in a single man in a single chair in a single house that was becoming too large and too small simultaneously.

She did not cry. She had not cried, or not much, or not yet—she wasn't sure which. She recognized this about herself without judgment. Some people processed grief as emotion. Meredith processed grief as math. The math was not a defense mechanism, or it was, but it was also the only language she trusted to tell the truth, because numbers did not have Doug Whitmore's warm tone or his neighborly cadence or his banana bread. Numbers simply were what they were, and what they were, in this case, was $50 per day, accumulating, while her husband's body subtracted from itself, declining, and the two curves—the fine rising, Richard falling—would intersect at a point that she could calculate but did not want to name.

She opened the laptop again. She opened a new tab. She typed *Greystone Terrace HOA bylaws*.

The PDF was 94 pages long. Last amended 2011.

She began to read.

---

## II.

She read the bylaws the way she had once read NAIC model regulations—not for meaning, not at first, but for structure. Where were the load-bearing walls. Where were the seams. What had been drafted with care and what had been copied from a template and what had been added later, in haste, by someone responding to a specific incident that the language attempted to generalize but couldn't quite conceal. (Section 6.14, "Recreational Vehicles and Non-Standard Conveyances," had clearly been written about one person's boat. Section 8.02(c), governing "audible disturbances of a repetitive nature between the hours of 10:00 PM and 7:00 AM," was clearly about one person's wind chimes.)

But the structural elements—the ones that governed governance itself—were what she needed, and they were buried in Articles III and IV, the articles no one ever read because they concerned quorum thresholds, amendment procedures, officer removal protocols, and the parliamentary authority governing meetings, which was (Article IV, Section 2) Robert's Rules of Order, Newly Revised, "in all cases to which they are applicable and in which they are not inconsistent with these bylaws."

Meredith read Article III, Sections 1 through 9, a second time. Then a third. Then she took out a yellow legal pad and began to diagram the power structure of the Greystone Terrace HOA the way she had once diagrammed risk pools: who could call a meeting, who could set an agenda, what constituted a quorum, how amendments could be proposed, what voting thresholds were required, where the president's authority ended and the membership's began.

The picture that emerged was, to Meredith, immediately recognizable. It was the same picture she had seen dozens of times in her career, in pension plans and insurance trusts and any organization that had written its rules during a period of optimism and then let a single administrator interpret them for a decade without challenge. The rules were fine. The rules were, in fact, robust—written by someone (probably a lawyer on Birch Court who'd been on the original board in 2003) who understood that community governance required checks and balances. But rules that no one enforces against the enforcer are suggestions, and Doug Whitmore had been president for fourteen years, and in that time the gap between what the bylaws permitted and what Doug permitted had widened to a size that could be exploited.

Specifically:

Article III, Section 4: A quorum for the annual meeting consisted of 15% of eligible voting households, present in person or by proxy. Greystone Terrace had 142 lots. Fifteen percent was 22 households. Annual meeting attendance had averaged, according to the minutes Doug posted on the community website, between 23 and 28 people. The margin was thin.

Article III, Section 7: Amendments to the CC&Rs required a two-thirds vote of those present at a properly convened meeting with a quorum established. Not two-thirds of the total membership. Two-thirds of those present. With proxies counted.

Article IV, Section 5: Any officer could be removed by a majority vote of those present at a meeting where the removal had been placed on the agenda at least 30 days in advance—or, and this was the clause that made Meredith sit very still for a very long time, during any meeting where the matter arose from the floor under new business and was supported by a two-thirds procedural vote to consider.

In other words: if you filled the room, and if you had the votes, and if you knew the rules better than the man with the gavel, you could do almost anything.

Meredith put down the legal pad. She made a pot of coffee. She stood at the kitchen window and looked at the ramp and drank the coffee and did not think about what she was about to do so much as she observed herself already doing it—the way you observe the first row of a spreadsheet populating itself once you've entered the formula, each cell filling in with logical inevitability.

She needed people. Not many—this could not be a movement; movements were visible and Doug would see a movement—but the right ones. She needed someone who understood procedure not as a means but as a calling. She needed someone who could create a record. And she needed numbers: warm bodies or signed proxies, enough to fill the room and hold the vote.

She made three phone calls.

---

The first was to Colin Marsh.

Colin Marsh lived on Birch Court in a colonial revival whose shutters were always exactly the approved shade of Forest Dusk (Sherwin-Williams 7623). He was 71 years old. He was a retired high school civics teacher. He had attended four national parliamentary procedure conferences and seven regional ones. He owned the 12th edition of *Robert's Rules of Order, Newly Revised*, the 11th edition, the 10th edition, and—his pride—a sixth edition from 1967 with Walter Cronkite's autograph on the flyleaf, acquired at a television-studio estate sale in circumstances Colin would describe in full if you let him.

Colin Marsh had been waiting for this phone call for his entire life. He simply hadn't known it would come from Meredith Chen about a wheelchair ramp.

"Robert's Rules," he said, and she could hear him already moving—standing up, probably, pulling a book from a shelf. "The parliamentary authority. Tell me the citation."

"Article IV, Section 2."

"'Robert's Rules of Order, Newly Revised, in all cases to which they are applicable and in which they are not inconsistent with these bylaws.' Standard incorporation clause. Good. Good. That means RONR governs everything the bylaws don't explicitly address. Floor procedure, debate limits, motion hierarchy, appeals from the chair—Meredith, do you understand what this means?"

"Tell me what it means."

"It means that if the chair—if Doug—makes a procedural ruling, any member can appeal, and the appeal is decided by majority vote. The chair doesn't control the room. The *assembly* controls the room. Most people don't know that. Most people think the person with the gavel is in charge. The person with the gavel works for the assembly. That's the whole—that's the entire foundation. The assembly is sovereign."
