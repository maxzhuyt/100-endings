# The Casserole Geometry

---

## I. The Terms

She had been dead for eleven days when the trickster found her standing in the kitchen, watching Gerald open foil-covered dishes he would not eat.

The trickster wore the shape of something between a fox and a tax attorney—long-faced, copper-furred, standing upright beside the refrigerator with its paws clasped behind its back. It smelled like ozone and peppercorns. It had been waiting, the way tricksters always wait: not for the dead, but for the specific dead, the ones still wet with living.

She—Margaret Anne Cowell, fifty-one, formerly of the body now boxed and planted beneath six feet of Illinois clay and a temporary marker the funeral home swore would be replaced within eight to ten business weeks—had not yet accepted the full architecture of her situation. She understood she was dead. She understood she was still here. What she had not yet reconciled was the distance between those two facts—the way *here* had become a word with no purchase, a place she occupied the way light occupies a room it cannot warm.

Gerald opened a casserole. Green bean. He stared into it with the glazed incomprehension of a man confronting a foreign alphabet. He put the lid back on.

Margaret reached for him. Her hand passed through his shoulder, disturbing nothing—not even the dust motes hanging in the four-o'clock light.

The trickster watched this with the particular satisfaction of a creature who understands that desire, left unmet long enough, becomes its own currency.

It extended one paw.

In the paw: a contract. Not paper—something older, something that looked like smoke pressed flat and made to hold still. The terms shifted and rearranged on its surface like ants building and dismantling the same hill.

Margaret could not read it. Not because she lacked the capacity—she had been a paralegal for twenty-three years, had parsed language designed specifically to obscure—but because the terms were not written in a language meant to be understood *before* signing. They were written in the language of *after.* Of consequence. Of the slow ugly dawn.

What she understood was this: the trickster offered contact. Not metaphorical. Not spiritual. *Contact.* The ability to be perceived. To exist, however briefly, in the same plane as Gerald's grief, his skin, his terrible lonesome breathing in the bed they had shared for twenty-seven years.

The cost, as presented, seemed abstract. A binding. A tethering of her visibility to moments of—the contract used a word that did not translate cleanly, something between *union* and *exposure* and *hunger.* She interpreted this as intimacy. She was not wrong. She was not right enough.

The trickster smiled. Its teeth were numerous and not designed for food.

Margaret kissed it.

Not because she wanted to. Not because the trickster was beautiful—though it wore its given shape with a certain vulpine elegance. She kissed it because the kiss was the seal, and the seal was the door, and behind the door was Gerald, opening casserole after casserole in a kitchen that smelled like other people's pity.

The kiss tasted like a struck match.

When it was done, the trickster folded the contract into a shape that could fit between ribs and pressed it into her sternum, where it dissolved like sugar into rain.

*Now,* its posture said. *Now you belong to the terms.*

And Margaret, dead and gleaming with the electricity of unused love, waited for the terms to show her what they meant.

## II. The Neighbor

Deborah Kessler—two doors down, recently divorced, perpetually in possession of freezer-ready sympathy—arrived on day fourteen with a chicken tetrazzini and a neckline Gerald's eyes fell into like a man tripping on a step he'd forgotten was there.

Margaret, formless and invisible, did not yet know what was coming.

She knew Deborah the way everyone on Mulberry Court knew Deborah: as a woman who brought food to crises with the reliability of weather. Every death, every diagnosis, every house fire and foreclosure—Deborah arrived with something creamy in a 9x13 pan, her hair recently done, her sympathy precise and practiced and somehow always delivered while leaning against the nearest available doorframe.

Gerald accepted the tetrazzini. He also accepted Deborah's hand on his forearm—a gesture that lingered two seconds past condolence and one second into something the geometry of which Margaret recognized with the precision of a woman who had spent twenty-seven years mapping the exact topography of her husband's weaknesses.

She recognized it, and she thought: *No.*

Then: *Not yet.*

Then: *Surely not yet.*

---

Seventeen days dead. Deborah returned. No casserole this time. A bottle of wine and a softness in her posture that communicated availability the way a porch light communicates to moths—not through language but through the simple, annihilating physics of *here I am, still lit.*

Gerald poured two glasses. He had not shaved. He wore the blue shirt Margaret had bought him for their anniversary—the one she'd chosen because it made his eyes into something women noticed across rooms—and he had not worn it intentionally, had pulled it from the closet the way grief pulls anything from anywhere, without thought, without meaning, and yet.

And yet.

Deborah sat on the couch. Gerald sat beside her. The distance between them was the length of a forearm, then a hand, then fingers, then—

Margaret felt the contract ignite.

It began as heat in the space behind her sternum where the trickster had pressed its terms. Then: a pull. Not gravitational—more specific, more surgical, like a hook set deep in the architecture of her attention, hauling her toward the couch with the irresistible patient force of a riptide.

She materialized.

Not fully. Not in the way she had been alive—three-dimensional, casting shadows, displacing air. She materialized the way a stain materializes on a ceiling: slowly, then all at once, something that had always been there finally asserting its presence.

She was standing in the corner of her own living room, behind the armchair she'd reupholstered the summer before she died, and she could *see*—not as a ghost sees, which is to say dimly and from a great remove—but with the high-definition clarity of a surveillance camera, every pore and hair and hesitation rendered in excruciating focus.

Deborah's hand moved to Gerald's knee.

Gerald's hand covered Deborah's.

Margaret opened her mouth to scream, to object, to say *it has been seventeen days, Gerald, seventeen days and the ground over me hasn't even settled*—

No sound came out.

The contract's terms clarified themselves in the silence of her failed protest: she could see. She could not speak. She could be present. She could not participate. She was audience, not actor. Witness, not wife.

Gerald leaned in.

Margaret surged forward—through the armchair, through the coffee table, arriving at the couch with the desperate velocity of something falling—and her hands reached for Gerald's face, that face she had held ten thousand mornings, and her fingers passed through him like grief through glass, touching nothing, displacing nothing, and Gerald shivered.

He mistook the shiver for anticipation.

Deborah's mouth found his.

Margaret, six inches away, close enough to count the pores on his nose, watched her husband kiss another woman in the negative space her death had made, and she could not close her eyes—the contract would not let her close her eyes—and she could not leave the room—the contract would not let her leave the room—and she understood, with the full catastrophic weight of a woman who had spent twenty-three years parsing the fine print of other people's agreements, that she had signed something she had not read.

In the hallway mirror, the trickster appeared. It sat on its haunches, watching Margaret watch Gerald, and its expression was the expression of something being fed.

## III. The Geometry of the Farce

The narrator wishes to be precise about the architecture of what followed, because the architecture is the comedy, and the comedy is the cruelty, and the cruelty is the point.

The master bedroom: Margaret and Gerald's. Queen bed, Egyptian cotton sheets (the sage green set, her choice), nightstand still crowded with her reading glasses and the mystery novel she'd dog-eared at page 214, never to learn who'd poisoned the vicar. Gerald had not moved these objects. Had not touched her side of the bed. Slept curled on his half like a man guarding a border.

Until night twenty-two, when Deborah stayed past the wine, past the decorum, past the invisible margin that separates comfort from hunger, and Gerald—lonely and alive and full of the specific recklessness that grief lends to bodies that still have blood in them—led her by the hand up the stairs, past the photographs on the wall (wedding, vacation, vacation, anniversary, Christmas, Christmas, Christmas), and into the room where Margaret had died.

Margaret was already there.

The contract had dragged her up through the floor the moment Gerald's pulse shifted register—she'd felt it happen, felt the hook in her sternum engage like a winch—and she'd arrived in the bedroom before they did, materializing in the corner beside the closet, her ectoplasmic form flickering with an agitation the narrator can only describe as *seismic.*

Gerald and Deborah entered.

What followed was an exercise in spatial humiliation so precisely choreographed that the narrator suspects the contract itself possessed a dramaturgical sensibility.

Gerald reached for the bedside lamp. Margaret stood between him and the switch. His hand passed through her wrist—her wrist, which had worn his ring, which had rested on his chest through a thousand sleeping nights—and he felt nothing except the cool of the metal pull chain.

The lamp went off.

In the dark, Deborah's blouse made a sound like a question being answered. Gerald's belt buckle—the silver one Margaret had bought at that antiques fair in Galena—rang against the hardwood when it dropped, and the sound was so specific, so conjugally familiar, that Margaret's form brightened involuntarily, her grief generating a faint luminescence that neither living person perceived but that the trickster, now peering through the bedroom window from outside like a patron at a puppet show, consumed with visible relish.

They moved toward the bed.

Margaret moved to the ceiling.

This was not a power she had known she possessed. The contract, it seemed, granted a certain spatial flexibility within the room of manifestation—she could not leave, but she could *redistribute* herself, and in the panic of watching Deborah lower herself onto Margaret's side of the bed—*her side, the left side, the side with the better pillow and the nightstand and the reading glasses and page 214 of the vicar mystery*—Margaret flung herself upward with the frantic inelegance of a cat launched from a bathtub and clung to the ceiling, spread-eagled, staring down at the bed from directly above.

This was worse.

This was so much worse than the corner.

From above, the geometry was total. Every movement was laid out beneath her like a map of a country she no longer had a passport to enter. She could see the exact way Gerald's hand remembered how to find the curve of a waist—that gesture, learned on her body, now applied to another's. She could see Deborah's fingers in Gerald's hair, his hair, the hair that had thinned in exactly the pattern Margaret had tracked for a decade with a tenderness she'd never spoken aloud because how do you say *I've memorized the precise geography of your scalp* without sounding unhinged.

Gerald shivered again.

*It's been so long,* his body said to Deborah's body, in the language bodies speak when mouths are otherwise occupied.

Margaret, on the ceiling, understood that *so long* did not mean the twenty-two days since her death. It meant the months before—the time of treatments and appointments and the slow medical redaction of her from the physical world, during which touch had become clinical and tenderness had become careful and the distance between their bodies in this bed had widened into something neither of them had acknowledged because to acknowledge it would have been to admit that she was leaving.

She had left.

And now he was filling the space.

And the contract required her to watch.

## IV. The Catalog

What followed over the next weeks was a taxonomy of intimacy that the narrator will present with the detachment of a field researcher, because detachment is the only possible response to a sequence of events this precisely designed to annihilate a woman who is already dead.

**The Kitchen Counter (Day 25):**

A Sunday morning. Coffee and the Tribune spread across the table. Deborah in one of Gerald's shirts—not one of Margaret's shirts, the narrator confirms, because Deborah had brought her own overnight things by now, neatly packed in a weekender bag that sat in the hallway like a foreign embassy establishing its first diplomatic outpost.

The coffee maker gurgled. Gerald moved behind Deborah to reach the mugs—the mugs Margaret had bought at that pottery studio in Door County, each one slightly lopsided because she'd made them herself, her hands in wet clay on a Tuesday afternoon while Gerald read on the cabin porch—and as he reached, his body pressed against Deborah's back, and the ordinary physics of proximity did what ordinary physics does. His mouth found her neck.

Margaret materialized inside the pantry.

She pressed herself between the canned tomatoes and the pasta boxes, flattening her spectral form into the narrowest possible cross-section, because the pantry door was open and the angle of sight was direct and *she could see his hands on the buttons of the shirt Deborah was wearing in Margaret's kitchen using Margaret's mugs on Margaret's counter—*

The trickster sat on top of the refrigerator, grooming one paw with methodical pleasure, and the narrator wishes to note the particular obscenity of its patience, the way it did not rush or urge or intervene but simply *waited* for the anguish to reach its optimal concentration, the way a sommelier waits for a wine to breathe.

Gerald lifted Deborah onto the counter. The sugar canister fell. Neither of them noticed.

Margaret noticed. Margaret had bought that sugar canister at a craft fair in 2011. It had a hand-painted rooster on it. The rooster's name was Frederick. She had named the rooster on the sugar canister because she was the kind of person who named things—who invested objects with personality and history and affection, who could not occupy a space without populating it with meaning—and now Frederick was on the floor, lid off, sugar spreading in a white fan across the tile, and Gerald's attention was entirely and catastrophically elsewhere.

She watched from the pantry. Her form flickered at a frequency that, had anyone been equipped to measure it, would have correlated precisely with the cardiac rhythm she no longer possessed.

**The Guest Bathroom (Day 31):**

Deborah ran a bath. Candles—candles she had brought from her own house, votives in little glass holders, arranged along the tub's rim with an intentionality that Margaret recognized as *staging.* This was not spontaneous. This was *produced.* Deborah was directing a scene in which she was the lead, in a bathroom she had not earned, in a tub Margaret had picked out from the showroom on Route 14 after measuring the alcove three times because Gerald always said she over-measured and she always said you can never be too precise and they'd had the same argument for twenty-seven years and *she missed the argument—*

Gerald entered. Steam had fogged the mirror. In the fog, the trickster's face appeared—just the outline, just the suggestion of copper fur and endless teeth—and then was gone, replaced by the streak of Gerald's palm wiping a clear arc through the condensation so he could check his reflection, and for a moment his reflected eyes looked directly at the space where Margaret stood behind him and saw absolutely nothing.

He undressed. He stepped into the tub with Deborah. The water level rose. Candles flickered.

Margaret crawled across the ceiling.
