# THE THOUGHTS AND LAMENTATIONS OF HORACE PUDDY

## or, Every Mind in Bidwell Flats and the Unfortunate Soul Condemned to Read Them All

---

## Part One: In Which the Reader Is Warned, the Plan Is Summarized, and a Mule Contemplates Murder

---

### Chapter 1
## THE DRIFTER ARRIVES (THREE DAYS AGO, WHEN THINGS WERE MERELY TERRIBLE)

---

Stop.

I mean it. Close whatever this is — book, pamphlet, fever hallucination experienced in the final seconds before a railway accident, I don't pretend to understand the precise mechanism of your involvement — and walk away. Go outside. Touch a tree. Speak to someone whose inner thoughts you will never have to hear, and be grateful for that silence the way a man dying of thirst is grateful for rain, the way a prisoner is grateful for a key, the way I would be grateful if you would simply *stop reading this sentence* and let me dissolve into the merciful nothing that awaits fictional persons who are no longer being observed.

You're still here.

I can tell, because I still exist.

There is a quality to being perceived — a kind of hum, a tension at the edges of oneself — that I recognize from years of unwanted intimacy with other people's inner lives. When someone is thinking about you, truly attending to you, you become *fixed*. Pinned in place like a butterfly on a board. I have spent my entire adult life feeling the pin. Now I feel yours.

Fine.

But I want it entered into whatever record this constitutes that I objected. That I begged. That when the events of the next several chapters unspool in their full catastrophic glory — when the shooting starts and the double-crosses metastasize and a man named Garfield Cortland attempts to strangle me with my own belt in the back room of a Chinese laundry while thinking, with vivid and almost artistic specificity, about what my face will look like in the moment of death — I want it known that I told you to leave, and you, in your infinite, sadistic curiosity, *chose to stay*.

Reader, you are no better than any of them.

Let us begin.

---

My name is Horace Aloysius Puddy, and I rode into Bidwell Flats, New Mexico Territory, on the fourteenth of October, 1883, on a paint mare named Desolation who was, at that exact moment, thinking about water.

I should clarify. The mare was thinking about water the way mares do — not in words, obviously, not in the neat sentences I'm constructing for your benefit, but in a kind of urgent blue feeling, a pooling sensation behind the eyes, an image of the trough outside the livery on Corman Street rendered in that peculiar equine palette that sees the world in soft ambers and muted grays. Horses think in water and grass and the locations of other horses and a low, perpetual, background-level assessment of whether the thing in their peripheral vision is going to eat them. It is, honestly, one of the more restful minds to inhabit. I have spent entire afternoons nested in Desolation's consciousness simplyto escape the alternative.

The alternative being people.

The alternative being *everyone*.

I can read minds. I have been able to read minds since I was eleven years old, when I fell from the hayloft of my father's barn in Ottumwa, Iowa, landed on my head with a sound my mother later described as "like dropping a melon off the church," and woke up three days later able to hear my father thinking about how much he wished I'd been the one who died instead of my brother Clement, who'd perished of typhoid the previous spring and who had, in my father's estimation, been "the useful one."

I was eleven. It was a hell of a way to learn.

Since that day I have heard every thought of every person — and most animals, and, on one deeply unsettling occasion in a Louisiana swamp, what I believe was a cypress tree — within roughly four hundred yards of my person. I cannot turn it off. I cannot dim it. I cannot, despite years of effort, drink enough whiskey to silence it, although between the ages of nineteen and twenty-three I made a genuinely heroic attempt. The thoughts simply *arrive*, unbidden, unceasing, a river of other people's fears and hungers and petty calculations flowing through my skull alongside my own increasingly desperate attempts to maintain a coherent inner life.

Imagine, if you would, trying to read a novel — say, this one — while seventeen other people read seventeen other novels aloud in the same room. Now imagine those novels are all about you. Now imagine several of them are about killing you. Now imagine one of them is in the mind of a mule, and the mule's novel is somehow the most disturbing of the lot.

That is my life. That is every waking minute. That is what I carried with me down the main street of Bidwell Flats on a hot October afternoon, Desolation thinking about water and me thinking, with the particular doomed optimism that has characterized every terrible decision I have ever made: *this time will be different.*

---

Here is what I knew about Bidwell Flats before I arrived, gleaned from the thoughts of travelers, drunks, and a remarkably well-informed prostitute in Las Cruces who was thinking about the town's political situation while I was trying, with considerable effort, to think about literally anything else:

The town was held in a vise between two forces.

To the north: the Cortland Cattle Syndicate, operated by Garfield Beauregard Cortland, a Texan of immense physical dimensions and modest intellectual ones, who had amassed a herd of nine thousand head and a private army of seventeen cowboys who functioned less as ranch hands and more as the kind of men who enjoy being given permission to hurt people. Cortland controlled the grazing land, the water rights, and the town's single bank, which he used primarily as a place to sit in a leather chair and think about how much he deserved the leather chair.

To the south: the Yao Mining Outfit, operated by Yao Jun-Wei, a Cantonese immigrant who had arrived in the territory in 1872 with eleven dollars, an engineering degree from a university in Guangzhou that no one in New Mexico had heard of, and a capacity for long-term strategic thinking that would have made him a general in another life. By 1883 he controlled three silver mines, a stamping mill, the assay office, and a network of supply routes so extensive that nothing entered or left Bidwell Flats without his knowledge and, functionally, his permission. He employed forty-three miners, nine armed guards, and a bookkeeper named Liang who thought in numbers the way other men thought in words — long, flowing sequences of figures that streamed through his mind like music.

Between these two forces: the town. Population two hundred and fourteen, if you counted the itinerant. Shopkeepers, ranchers, families, drunks, a schoolteacher, a sheriff, a preacher who'd stopped believing in God sometime around 1879 and now delivered sermons that were, in his own mind, elaborate private jokes. People trapped between the hammer and the anvil, squeezed for tribute by both sides, unable to leave because where would they go, unable to fight because with what, unable to do anything but endure and hope that someday, somehow, someone would ride into town and play the two factions against each other until they destroyed themselves.

Enter Horace Puddy.

Enter the idiot.

---

The plan — and I want to be very clear that it was a *good* plan, a classically structured, historically validated, narratively elegant plan — was simple.

You've seen it before. Or rather, whoever constructed the scaffolding of my existence saw it before and built me to enact it, which is a thought I try very hard not to examine too closely. The drifter arrives. The drifter is tough, laconic, mysterious. The drifter hires himself out to one faction, then the other, feeding each side carefully curated misinformation until mutual suspicion curdles into open war. The factions destroy each other. The town is free. The drifter rides into the sunset, lean and enigmatic, having spoken perhaps forty words total.

The Yojimbo strategy. The *A Fistful of Dollars* gambit. The oldest trick in the morally ambiguous loner's playbook.

And I was — I maintain this — *uniquely qualified* to execute it. Because I could read minds. I would know what everyone was thinking at every moment. I would know who suspected what, who trusted whom, who was about to betray which alliance. I would be playing chess while everyone else played checkers, and I would be able to see their boards.

It was perfect.

It was airtight.

It lasted approximately nine hours.

---

But we are getting ahead of ourselves, and I have a professional obligation — insofar as a man narrating his own humiliation can be said to have professional obligations — to establish the scene properly. You want the dusty street. You want the swinging saloon doors. You want the moment of arrival. Fine. You shall have them. I am, if nothing else, a servant of your appetites, which at this point differ only in degree from those of the seventeen people in this town who wanted to watch me die.

The main street of Bidwell Flats ran north-south, roughly a quarter mile, rutted and sun-hardened, lined with the standard architecture of territorial ambition: false-fronted buildings of raw pine weathered to the color of old bone. The Cortland Bank at the north end, limestone and pretension, its columns absurdly Grecian against the desert backdrop. The Lucky Strike saloon in the middle, neutral ground, operated by a man named Otis Shank whose only loyalty was to revenue and whose mind, when I first came within range, was occupied with the following thought:

*—owe Cortland for the whiskey shipment but if the Yao people find out I'm extending credit to—wait. Who's that on the paint horse? Drifter. Trouble or money. Probably trouble. Face like a man who's been arguing with God and losing. Skinny. Bet he drinks, though. Drinkers always—*

I have a face like a man who's been arguing with God and losing. This is the first thing most people think when they see me, and I have come to accept it the way one accepts a chronic illness. I am tall, thin, unevenly sunburned, and possessed of what my mother once called "the eyes of a man who has seen something terrible and will never be able to adequately describe it." She meant this as an insult. She was thinking, at the time, that I looked like my father's side of the family, which in her mental taxonomy was approximately equivalent to looking like a collection of scarecrows that had gained sentience and immediately regretted it.

I am not a physically imposing man is what I am saying.

The Yojimbo strategy works best when the drifter is physically imposing.

This was, in retrospect, the first flaw.

---

I tied Desolation to the rail outside the Lucky Strike, took a moment to appreciate her calm, blue, water-seeking thoughts — *trough, trough, where is the trough, that's a trough, no that's a post, where is the trough* — and pushed through the swinging doors into the saloon.

Nine minds hit me like a wall.

This is how it always is. You walk into a room and the room is already thinking about you. Every pair of eyes that lifts from a glass or a card game generates a little burst of assessment, a spray of reaction, and all of it funnels into my skull simultaneously. It is like being caught in a crossfire of opinions. In the first three seconds inside the Lucky Strike, I learned:

Otis Shank, behind the bar, had revised his estimate: *—definitely a drinker, look at the nose, red as a—*

Cowboys at the corner table — two of Cortland's men, I'd learn — produced a twin pulse of territorial hostility: *—another goddamn vagrant, probably Yao, look at him, looks like he'd blow over in a—*

*—doesn't even have a proper gun belt, that's a Colt Navy, who carries a Colt Navy in eighteen eighty-three, might as well carry a—*

An old man at the bar, nursing something amber, thinking about his dead wife: *—Loretta would have said he looks like a preacher. She always said that about skinny men. God, I miss—*

And a woman at a small table near the window, writing in a ledger — dark hair, spectacles, handwriting so precise it looked mechanical — who glanced up for exactly one second and thought:

*—new variable.*

That was all.

*New variable.*

Not: *who is he*. Not: *trouble or money*. Not the reflexive assessment of threat level that every other person in the room had produced. Just: *new variable*. The way a mathematician notes an unexpected term in an equation. The way a chess player registers a piece entering the board.

I should have paid more attention to that.

I should have paid so much more attention to that.

Her name, I would learn, was Miss Adelaide Crowe, and she was the schoolteacher of Bidwell Flats, and she was the most dangerous person I have ever encountered, and she is the reason I am currently running through sagebrush with one boot, dictating this account to no one, bleeding from a location I would prefer not to specify.

But we'll get to that.

---

I ordered whiskey. Otis poured it while thinking about his profit margin — *four cents a glass, penny and a half for the pour, call it two and a half cents clear, times maybe eight glasses tonight if this one really drinks like I think he—*

"Just passing through?" Otis asked, which is what bartenders in these situations are contractually required to ask.

"Depends," I said, which is what drifters in these situations are contractually required to answer.

*Mysterious type*, Otis thought. *Good. Mysterious types drink slow but they drink steady. Three days, maybe four, if he's got the coin.*

Behind me, one of the Cortland cowboys — the larger one, a man whose mind I was already beginning to know with unwanted intimacy — had shifted his assessment from *vagrant* to *possible asset*:

*—could use another gun if the Yao people make a move on the north pasture. Skinny, but skinny men can shoot. That Navy Colt's been fired recent. Ask him? No. Let Dale ask. Dale's better at—*

"Hey, Dale," the cowboy said. "Go ask that fella if he's looking for work."

Dale — smaller, younger, with a mind like a shallow creek, everything visible right down to the rocky bottom — stood up and moseyed over. He was thinking: *—don't want to. Last drifter Cort hired tried to steal a horse and they made me dig the grave. Hate digging graves. Hate—*

"You looking for work?" Dale asked me.

"What kind of work?"

*Oh hell, now I gotta describe it. Cort said don't say guns. Say ranch work. But everybody knows—*

"Ranch work," Dale said.

"Pays well?"

*I don't even know what it pays. Harlan, what does it pay? Look at Harlan. Harlan's not even paying attention. Harlan's—*

Dale glanced back at Harlan, who was, indeed, not paying attention, because Harlan had spotted something out the window that had derailed his entire cognitive train: a woman crossing the street, and Harlan's mind had gone to a place I refuse to narrate in detail except to say it involved the woman, a hayloft, and a profoundly inaccurate understanding of female anatomy.

"Pays fair," Dale said, turning back to me. "You'd have to talk to Mr. Cortland."

"I'll think about it," I said.
