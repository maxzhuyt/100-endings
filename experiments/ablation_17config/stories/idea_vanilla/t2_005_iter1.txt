# The Unfinished Movement

## Being the Recollections of Einar Þórsson, Retired Accompanist, on the Life and Disappearance of Maren Sol

---

## I. Prelude in the Key of Absence

I have tuned a thousand pianos for Maren Sol, and every one of them was a prayer I did not know I was saying.

You must understand this first, before I tell you anything else: that a piano is not a single instrument but a congregation of them—two hundred and thirty strings pulling against a cast-iron frame with twenty tons of tension, every one of them drifting even as you play, every one of them trying to return to some native disorder that tuning only ever postpones. I spent forty years kneeling inside that disorder. I spent forty years pressing my tuning lever to the pins and listening for the point where two frequencies stopped their war and became, for one trembling instant, the same voice. And then I would move to the next string. And the string behind me would already have begun its slow migration back toward chaos.

That is all I know about music. That is all I know about Maren.

She has been gone from Reykjavik for three years now, or four—I lose the count the way one loses count of waves—and I have been asked by various people to say what happened, to produce a narrative with a beginning and a middle, and especially an end, because the world requires endings the way a dominant seventh requires resolution, and Maren's story resolves into nothing that anyone standing outside it can easily hear.

But I was not outside it. I was the accompanist. I was the man in the darkened wing adjusting the bench height, checking the sostenuto pedal, laying out her scores in the order she would need them while she stood in the corridor breathing into her hands. I accompanied her for thirty-one years and in all that time I played not a single note of my own devising, and I want you to know that I say this without bitterness, or with a bitterness so old and so thoroughly fermented that it has become indistinguishable from love.

So let me say what I can say.

Let me say it the way a hymn says it—not by arguing but by repeating, not by proving but by insisting, phrase after phrase, that something too large for language happened, and the only honest response is to sing it badly and mean it completely.

## II. Maren Sol, Before

She was born in 1961 in Ísafjörður, in the Westfjords, where the mountains come down to the water in such severe and humorless diagonals that the town receives no direct sunlight for two months of the year. I mention this because the biographers always mention it—*born in darkness, composed toward light*—and because Maren herself would sometimes laugh at the neatness of it, the way the biographical detail presented itself already shaped into metaphor, already doing the work of meaning before she had consented to mean anything at all.

Her father was a fisherman who sang. Not performed. Not practiced. Sang—the way the wind sings, which is to say involuntarily, and only because there was an opening through which the breath could pass. He sang on the boats. He sang gutting cod. He sang to Maren melodies that were not Icelandic and not anything else either, melodies he claimed to have heard in his sleep, and which vanished from his memory the moment she asked him to repeat them. She told me once—we were in Gothenburg, I was adjusting a Steinway D for a concert that evening, and she was sitting in the empty hall eating an orange with her shoes off—she told me that her entire life's work had been an attempt to reconstruct those melodies her father could not remember, and that the reason she could never finish anything to her satisfaction was that she was transcribing from a recording that did not exist.

I said nothing. I was listening to the interval between the A above middle C and the A an octave higher, waiting for the beats to disappear. That is what a tuner does. He listens for the ghost between two notes and then eliminates it.

She studied first at the Reykjavik College of Music, then at the Royal Danish Academy in Copenhagen, then in Vienna, then in Berlin. She studied with Jón Nordal and later with the Dane whose name I always forget and finally with Dóra Hjálmarsdóttir, which is where the real story begins, though I will not begin it yet, because a hymn does not begin with its crisis; a hymn begins by establishing the congregation, by reminding everyone in the room that they are in the room together, that they all have bodies, that the bodies are breathing, and that the breathing, if you listen carefully enough, already contains the melody.

She composed her first major work at twenty-four: *Vetrarsól*, *Winter Sun*, a piece for chamber orchestra that the Icelandic Symphony premiered in the old Háskólabíó cinema because the national concert hall had not yet been built. I was not her accompanist then. I was a student myself, twenty years old, sitting in the fifth row with my coat still on because the heating had failed, and I remember that the piece began with a single sustained note in the oboe—a B-flat, held so long that the audience began to shift in their seats, began to cough, began to feel that something was wrong, that the musicians had lost their place—and then, just at the point where discomfort was about to tip into irritation, the strings entered, not with a melody but with a texture, a slow chromatic accumulation that swelled from beneath the oboe's held breath like water rising in a basin, and I felt—

I do not know how to tell you what I felt.

I felt that someone had opened a door in a room I had not known was closed.

The critics called it luminous. The critics called it a masterwork of restraint. The critics used words like *pellucid* and *gossamer* and *architecturally rigorous*, and Maren, who was twenty-four and had a gap between her front teeth and wore her hair cut short as a boy's, read the reviews to me when I first came to work for her, years later, and she read them the way you read a letter from someone you once loved who turned out to have loved a version of you that never existed. "Architecturally rigorous," she said. "They mean cold. They always mean cold. They just don't know how to say it."

She was not cold. That is the thing I need you to understand before I tell you about the driving and the gospel churches and the crossroads and the children. Maren Sol was not cold. Maren Sol was a bonfire in a windstorm who had been told so many times that a bonfire was an inappropriate way to heat a concert hall that she'd learned to disguise herself as a radiator. The discipline the critics praised was not native to her. It had been installed, the way I install new strings—under tension, stretched beyond what the material wanted, held in place by pins that had to be checked and rechecked because the thing they were restraining was always, always trying to spring loose.

## III. Dóra

Dóra Hjálmarsdóttir was a jazz theorist, which in Iceland in the 1980s was roughly as respected a vocation as being a jazz theorist on the moon. She was fifteen years older than Maren and had studied at Berklee in Boston and at the Conservatoire de Paris and had played piano in a trio that recorded one album for ECM Records—the cover was a photograph of a frozen lake, as all ECM covers are photographs of frozen lakes or their spiritual equivalent—and then she had come home to Reykjavik and begun teaching.

I knew Dóra before I knew Maren. I tuned her piano—a battered Yamaha upright in a basement apartment in Vesturbær that smelled of coffee and manuscript paper and the particular mustiness of a room where someone has been chain-smoking Camels while reading Thelonious Monk transcriptions for the better part of a decade. The piano was abused in the way that only a piano belonging to a genius can be abused—not through neglect but through excess of use, the hammers flattened to hardness by ten thousand hours of impact, the dampers sluggish, the keys worn to a smoothness that your fingers registered as a kind of sadness, as if the instrument were exhausted and was submitting to your touch rather than receiving it.

Dóra never asked me to tune it to standard concert pitch. She asked me to tune it to itself—to find the instrument's own internal logic, the key in which its warped soundboard and aged strings most naturally wanted to resonate, and to honor that, even if it meant the piano would be useless for performing any piece written by anyone else. "The piano is not a democracy," she told me once, pouring coffee into a cup that had not been washed in what appeared to be several geological epochs. "It is a specific being with a specific voice, and the job of the tuner is not to make it conform but to help it confess."

I thought she was pretentious. I thought she was possibly insane. I tuned the piano her way because she was paying me, and because—I admit this now, with the freedom that age confers—because I was a little afraid of her. Dóra had a way of looking at you that made you feel she was listening not to what you were saying but to the overtone series of what you were saying, the phantom frequencies above your words that revealed the harmonic content of your actual meaning, and she would respond not to your statement but to its overtones, so that you always felt she was having a conversation with a truer version of yourself, a version you had not authorized to speak.

Maren came to study with Dóra in 1986. She was twenty-five. *Vetrarsól* had made her the most talked-about young composer in Iceland, which is to say that perhaps six hundred people knew her name, and of those, perhaps sixty had listened to the piece with genuine attention, and of those, perhaps six had understood what she was actually trying to do, and Dóra was one of the six.

What Dóra heard in *Vetrarsól* was not what the critics heard. The critics heard restraint, structure, the Nordic school, the long shadows of Sibelius and Pärt. Dóra heard a woman screaming in a soundproofed room. She heard the scream muffled by craft, by technique, by the entire apparatus of European formalism that Maren had learned to wield with such precision that everyone mistook the precision for the point.

"Your student has a problem," Dóra told me once. This was years later, when Maren was already famous, already the Reykjavik Composer-in-Residence, already producing one immaculate work after another that won prizes in Berlin and Helsinki and made audiences weep with their austere beauty. "Her problem is that she is writing *about* music. She is describing it from a great height, like a cartographer. She has never once been lost in it."

I asked her what being lost in it sounded like.

Dóra sat down at her ruined Yamaha—she was fifty-something then, her hair entirely white, her fingers beginning to stiffen with the arthritis that would eventually silence her—and she played a single chord. It was not a chord I recognized. It was not a chord I could name. It sat somewhere between a major seventh and a minor ninth, in a voicing that spread the notes so far apart they seemed to have nothing to do with each other, and yet when she held the sustain pedal and let the overtones bloom, the notes reached toward each other in the air above the strings and made a sound that was—

I am going to say it was the sound of someone admitting something they had kept secret for a lifetime. I am going to say it was the sound of a door opening onto a room that turns out to have been there all along, behind the wall you thought was solid. I know these are metaphors and not descriptions, and I know that a musician would tell me to name the intervals and the voicing and stop being sentimental, but I was a tuner, not a theorist, and what I heard was not a structure but an experience, and the experience was that of suddenly recognizing in a stranger's face someone you had loved and forgotten.

Dóra held the chord until it decayed, and then she looked at me and said: "That. That is what Maren will not let herself play."

## IV. The Residency Years

Maren became Reykjavik's Composer-in-Residence in 2004. She was forty-three. She moved into the studio on Laufásvegur that the city provides for its resident artist—a bright, modern apartment above a bakery, with a view of the harbor and a Bösendorfer Imperial that I tuned every two weeks without fail, adjusting for the temperature fluctuations that the Icelandic weather imposed on the soundboard like a restless editor making changes to a manuscript that was already perfect.

She composed there for twenty years. She composed *Eldfjall* (for brass quintet and electronics), *Norðurljós* (for string quartet and prepared piano), *Hljóðlaus Saga* (the "Silent Saga," for solo cello, in which the cellist is required at five separate points in the score to stop playing entirely and simply hold the instrument against their body in silence for durations ranging from eight seconds to one minute, letting the audience hear the room itself becoming the music). She composed the *Reykjavik Cantatas*, a cycle of seven vocal works that drew on Old Norse texts and transformed them through serial techniques into something that sounded ancient and impossible at the same time, like a medieval chant channeled through a particle accelerator.

Everything she wrote was brilliant. Everything she wrote won prizes. Everything she wrote was reviewed with the same vocabulary: rigorous, luminous, crystalline, austere. And I tuned the Bösendorfer every two weeks, and I said nothing about the fact that the scores she showed me grew more and more dense, more and more tightly controlled, the dynamic markings becoming more specific (*pianississimo al niente*, *morendo quasi senza suono*), the time signatures more esoteric (11/16, 7/20, once a passage in what she called "unmetered time" that was in fact so precisely notated that the performers had to use click tracks in their earpieces just to navigate it), as if the margin for spontaneity, for accident, for the kind of productive chaos that lives in the space between what the composer intended and what the performer feels, was being deliberately, systematically closed.

I said nothing because I was the accompanist. The accompanist does not offer opinions on the composition. The accompanist serves the composition. The accompanist makes himself so transparent, so subordinate to the music's needs, that the audience forgets he is there at all, and when they applaud they applaud the soloist, and when they review the concert they mention the soloist, and when they remember the evening years later they remember the soloist's name and the name of the hall and perhaps the name of the piece, but they do not remember the accompanist, because the accompanist's highest achievement is to become invisible.

I was excellent at being invisible. I was, if I may say so, the finest invisible man in Icelandic music.

But I saw things. From the invisibility of the piano bench, from the backstage shadows where I waited with her water glass and her rosin cloth and her special mechanical pencil (0.3mm, Pentel, she would use no other), I saw Maren Sol become more and more precise, and more and more unhappy, and I understood—the way a tuner understands the gradual flattening of a string that is under too much tension—that something was going to break.

## V. The Opus

She began the piece she called *Lok* in 2019. The title means "ending" or "closing" in Icelandic, though it also carries the connotation of a lid being placed on something, a container being sealed. She told me it would be her final major work—a symphony in four movements for full orchestra, choir, and two soloists, running to approximately ninety minutes, encompassing everything she had learned in four decades of composition and resolving it into a single, unified statement.
