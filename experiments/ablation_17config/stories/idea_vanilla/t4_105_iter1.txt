# The Therapeutic Properties of Steam

## A Story in Incident Reports

---

### I. Concerning the Strip Mall Known as "Mohawk Crossing Plaza," Its Tenants, and the Particular Quality of Light That Falls Upon Its Parking Lot in October

The strip mall sat on Route 11 like a dental bridge in a rotting mouth. Five units. Three occupied. The sign out front—MOHAWK CROSSING PLAZA—had lost its second O to a windstorm in 2019, so that it now read MHAWK CRSSING PLAZA, which the remaining tenants had ceased to notice in the way that one ceases to notice one's own heartbeat, or the slow death of one's aspirations, or the particular chemical odor that rose from the drainage ditch behind Unit 4 whenever it rained, which in Kessler Falls, New York, was constantly, the sky having apparently entered into a long-term contractual obligation with misery.

Unit 1: vacant, though a family of raccoons had established in its drop ceiling what could only be described as a governing body.

Unit 2: Nails by Nadine, owned and operated by Nadine Blotcki, fifty-seven, whose acrylic work had been described by the *Kessler Falls Pennysaver* as "adequate" in a 2016 review she had laminated and displayed by the register in the manner of a battlefield commendation.

Unit 3: also vacant, its plate glass window bearing a ghost-palimpsest of old signage—the faint outline of TAX MASTERS visible beneath the fainter outline of VAPE KINGDOM visible beneath the faintest outline of CURVES, each layer a sedimentary record of commerce attempted and abandoned, like the strata of some archaeological site dedicated not to civilization but to its opposite.

Unit 4: SuperCuts, staffed by two women named Brenda who had never, in six years of shared employment, discussed this coincidence.

Unit 5: and here we arrive at the heart of the matter, the lesion around which the body politic of Kessler Falls would soon inflame—*Zurab's Bathhouse & Wellness*, which had opened its doors on September 14th of the present year, and which had, in the seven weeks since, served exactly forty-one customers, thirty of whom were long-haul truckers who had stopped because the sign said WELLNESS and they had confused it, not unreasonably, with a massage parlor, and eleven of whom were a single retired postal worker named Art Koontz, who came every Tuesday and Thursday because the steam helped his sciatica and because Zurab gave him tea in a glass with a silver holder and called him "my friend" and Art Koontz had not been called anyone's friend since his wife died in 2021 and found that he did not mind being called it now.

Zurab Dolidze had arrived in Kessler Falls from Tbilisi by way of Brooklyn by way of Binghamton by way of a complex chain of economic calculations that need not concern us here except to say that commercial rent in the five boroughs had become, in Zurab's precise engineering vocabulary, "a crime against mathematics." He was fifty-three. He had degrees in civil engineering from the Georgian Technical University and in mechanical engineering from a polytechnic whose name Americans could not pronounce and therefore regarded with suspicion, in the manner of a dog confronting a vacuum cleaner. He had helped design a bridge in Kutaisi. He had helped design a water treatment plant in Rustavi. He had come to America eleven years ago because his daughter wanted to study biochemistry at Stony Brook and because he believed—with a sincerity so complete it functioned as a kind of armor, though armor, as we shall see, of the thinnest possible gauge—that a man who worked honestly and provided a clean service in a clean space would be left alone to do so.

He had built the bathhouse himself. This is important. He had laid the tile—hand-cut Georgian ceramic, blue and white, in a pattern his grandmother would have recognized—over the concrete subfloor, working on his knees for three weeks until his patella swelled to the size of a tennis ball, at which point he wrapped it in an Ace bandage and continued. He had constructed the steam room from western red cedar, joining the planks with a precision that would have been excessive in a spacecraft, let alone a wellness facility in a strip mall between a nail salon and a vacancy. He had installed the *kupeli*—the soaking tub—himself, running copper pipes through the wall with a plumber's flux and a blowtorch, and when the building inspector, a man named Phil, had come to examine the work, Phil had stood in the doorway for a long time and then said, "Huh," which in the idiom of upstate New York code enforcement constituted the highest possible architectural praise.

The bathhouse was beautiful. This is also important, and seldom noted.

It was beautiful in the way that a thing made by a person who loves the making can be beautiful regardless of its context—the way a cathedral is beautiful even in a war zone, the way a garden is beautiful even beside a highway, the way a perfectly tiled steam room with hand-forged copper fixtures and a cedar bench sanded to the smoothness of river stone is beautiful even when situated between a SuperCuts and a nail salon in a strip mall whose parking lot had not been resurfaced since the Clinton administration, the first one, and whose only landscaping was a single arborvitae that had died so long ago it had become essentially geological.

Zurab had opened for business on a Monday. He had placed a small ad in the *Pennysaver*. He had set out a tray of *churchkhela*—the walnut-and-grape confection that is to Georgian hospitality what the firm handshake is to American—on a table by the door. He had waited.

And he had been, for seven weeks, if not happy, then something adjacent to it. Something that shared a wall with happiness, the way his bathhouse shared a wall with Nails by Nadine.

It was through that wall that the trouble came.

### II. Concerning Nadine Blotcki, the Provenance of Her Suspicions, and the Ecosystem of the NextDoor Platform as It Pertains to the Dissemination of Unfounded Accusation in Communities Whose Primary Industry Is Grievance

Nadine had not always been vindictive. Rather, she had always been vindictive, but her vindictiveness had previously been distributed across a wide field of targets—the county assessor, her ex-husband Gerald, Gerald's new wife (also, through a coincidence that God had arranged specifically to torment Nadine, named Nadine), the Democratic Party, the Republican Party, gluten, and a specific cashier at the Aldi in Utica who had once questioned her coupon. The arrival of Zurab Dolidze had merely provided her vindictiveness with a fixed address.

The proximate cause was water. Water from Zurab's plumbing, which had, on October 2nd, seeped through the shared wall and dampened a box of acrylic nail tips, French style, which Nadine valued at sixty-seven dollars, though their wholesale cost was eleven. Zurab had apologized immediately, profusely, and in person, appearing at her door with a towel and an offer to pay for the damage and a plate of *khachapuri*—cheese bread—which Nadine had accepted, eaten in its entirety, and then used the empty plate as Exhibit A in the narrative she was already constructing, because the bread, she would later tell the zoning board, "tasted ethnic," a phrase she delivered with the gravity of a forensic finding.

But the water was not the true cause. The true cause was that Zurab's bathhouse, in its seven weeks of operation, had attracted more foot traffic than Nails by Nadine had seen in seven months, and that several of these feet—specifically the feet of Art Koontz, who had been one of Nadine's last regular customers, coming in biweekly for a pedicure he did not want but submitted to out of the same loneliness that now drew him to Zurab's tea—had defected.

The loss of Art Koontz was the loss of forty-four dollars a month. It was also the loss of something else, something Nadine could not have articulated and would not have wanted to—the loss of being needed, even minimally, even by a man who came to her only because there was nowhere else to go, which is, if we are being honest, the economic model upon which most of Kessler Falls operated.

And so, on the evening of October 19th, Nadine Blotcki sat down at her kitchen table with a glass of Barefoot Moscato and her HP laptop and opened the NextDoor application and began to type.

The post was titled: **"Has anyone else noticed what's going on at the new 'bathhouse' in Mohawk Crossing???"**

Three question marks. The typography of insinuation.

The post read, in its entirety:

> *Not trying to start anything but my friend who works at the hospital says they've been seeing "unusual medical waste" in the dumpster behind unit 5. Also my cousin's husband is a trucker and he says these kind of places are KNOWN for being fronts. I'm not saying what I think they're a front FOR but just Google "organ harvesting" + "bathhouse" + "Eastern Europe" and draw your own conclusions. We have a RIGHT to know what is going on in our community. Also he got some kind of permit to install a "soaking tub" which is NOT a normal thing to have in a strip mall. This is NOT the kind of business we need in Kessler Falls. Just my opinion!!! 🇺🇸*

The friend at the hospital did not exist. The unusual medical waste did not exist. The cousin's husband was real but had said nothing of the kind—he had merely observed, in Nadine's presence, that the *churchkhela* Zurab had given him looked "like a finger," which it did, slightly, in the way that many foods resemble body parts if one is determined to see body parts, which Nadine, by this point, was.

But accuracy, on NextDoor, is an inconvenience of the same order as parking regulations or the Geneva Conventions—theoretically binding, universally ignored. Within forty-eight hours, the post had accumulated 112 comments, a number that exceeded the total engagement of every other post in the Kessler Falls NextDoor community combined, including the one about the coyote in Helen Marsh's swimming pool and the one about the Domino's delivery driver who had been photographed urinating in a recycling bin, which had previously held the record.

The comments followed a trajectory familiar to anyone who has studied the fluid dynamics of paranoia in closed systems, which is to say they began with caution ("Hmm, interesting, thanks for sharing, Nadine!"), ascended rapidly to credulity ("My neighbor saw a refrigerated truck there at 2 AM!!"), crested at invention ("My daughter's friend is a nurse and she says there's a whole BLACK MARKET for kidneys from that part of the world"), and finally achieved a kind of escape velocity from reality entirely ("Has anyone checked if there are tunnels? There are ALWAYS tunnels"), at which point the accusations no longer required even the pretense of evidence, because they had become, in the communal imagination of Kessler Falls, not allegations but atmosphere—the sort of thing everyone simply *knew*, the way everyone *knew* the water tower was full of fluoride and the mayor's son had gotten away with something and the Olive Garden in Utica was a money-laundering operation, though no one could say for whom or why.

The zoning board met on the first Thursday of each month in the basement of the Kessler Falls Municipal Building, a structure that had been condemned in 2017 and then uncondemed in 2018 when it was discovered that the condemnation would require the town to find an alternative meeting space, of which there were none, the Elks Lodge having burned down and the church basement being occupied by a feral cat colony that had, through a series of bureaucratic oversights, been granted something perilously close to squatters' rights.

The board consisted of five members: Chair Dennis Trott, a retired insurance adjuster who ran meetings with the quiet authority of a man who knew that nothing mattered; Vice Chair Linda Peck, who was also the treasurer, the secretary, and, in a one-woman consolidation of municipal roles that would have impressed Caesar Augustus, the part-time librarian; and three at-large members—Bob, Bob, and a woman named Crystal whose last name no one could remember, including Crystal.

It was at the November meeting that Nadine appeared.

She had prepared a presentation. She had printed screenshots of her NextDoor post and its 112 comments. She had printed a Google Maps satellite image of Mohawk Crossing Plaza on which she had drawn, in red Sharpie, a circle around Unit 5 and, for reasons never explained, several arrows pointing to the dumpster. She had printed a Wikipedia article about organ trafficking that pertained to events in Kosovo in the late 1990s and had no connection to Georgia, bathhouses, or Kessler Falls, but which contained, on its second page, the word "harvesting," which she had highlighted in yellow with such force that the highlighter had torn through the paper, leaving a wound in the document that seemed, to Nadine, like emphasis.

She approached the podium. She cleared her throat. She said:

"I'm here tonight as a *concerned citizen*."

And the room—seventeen people, which was eleven more than the usual attendance, the surplus drawn by the same force that draws motorists to decelerate near highway accidents—leaned forward.

What followed was forty-five minutes of testimony that the recording secretary, who was also Linda Peck, transcribed with the weary professionalism of a woman who had once, in this same room, been obligated to transcribe a thirty-minute public comment about chemtrails. The minutes would later read:

> *Ms. Blotcki expressed concerns re: business operations at Unit 5, Mohawk Crossing Plaza ("Zurab's Bathhouse & Wellness"). Ms. Blotcki presented printed materials. Ms. Blotcki referenced "organ harvesting" (7 times), "front" (4 times), "those people" (11 times), and "common sense" (9 times). Board Member Bob M. asked Ms. Blotcki if she had contacted law enforcement. Ms. Blotcki stated that law enforcement was "part of the problem." Board Member Bob K. asked Ms. Blotcki what specific evidence she possessed. Ms. Blotcki stated that "you don't need evidence when you know." Chair Trott reminded Ms. Blotcki that the zoning board did not have jurisdiction over criminal matters. Ms. Blotcki stated that the zoning board "didn't have jurisdiction over 9/11 either but that still happened." Chair Trott called a recess.*

The recess lasted eleven minutes. During the recess, Chair Trott consumed two Tums and stared at the ceiling in the manner of a man doing long division with God. When the meeting resumed, Chair Trott made the following recommendation: that the matter be referred to the Kessler Falls Code Enforcement Office for investigation.

He made this recommendation not because he believed Nadine's accusations, but because he did not believe them and knew that an investigation would prove them baseless and the matter would end, which was, in Dennis Trott's experience, the function of government—not to solve problems but to process them, to pass them through the alimentary canal of bureaucracy until they emerged, on the other end, as paperwork, which could be filed, and filing, Dennis Trott believed, was the closest humanity would ever come to peace.

He was wrong, of course. He was catastrophically wrong. But he did not know this because he did not know Darlene Pfaff.
