# THE FESTA DELLA FIAMMA ETERNA
## Or: Concerning the Proper Filing of Form 27-Cuore (Revised), and Its Consequences

---

## PART ONE: INTAKE

**I.**

My name is Enzo Bianchi, and I have been the Deputy Clerk of the Comune di Pietra Rossa for twenty-three years, which is twelve years longer than I have wanted to be the Deputy Clerk of the Comune di Pietra Rossa. I state this not for sympathy but for context. Context is what bureaucracy replaces meaning with, and I have learned to work with the materials available.

I am telling you this story because no one else will. The five outsiders cannot tell it because they do not understand what happened to them, not fully, not in the way that understanding requires you to have watched the machinery operate from inside its gears for two decades while your knees deteriorated and your marriage ended and your cat died of what the veterinarian in Amalfi called "ambient spiritual exhaustion." The Signora cannot tell it because the Signora does not tell things. The Signora watches. And the thing that lives inside the Signora — I will get to that. I will get to all of it. But first I need to describe the forms, because the forms are where it starts. The forms are always where it starts.

Pietra Rossa sits on a cliff above the Tyrrhenian Sea in a stretch of the Campanian coast that the travel magazines have called, with remarkable consistency, "the most romantic place on Earth." I have seen this phrase in eleven languages. It is printed on the ceramic tiles we sell to tourists for nine euros. It is engraved on the iron railing of the Belvedere del Sospiro, where couples stand at sunset and photograph themselves in the act of experiencing what the railing's plaque promises they should be experiencing, and where, every April, I must file the incident reports for the two or three proposals that go wrong — the ring dropped into the sea, the answer that is not *yes* but a complicated exhalation that the proposer's face tells you they have been dreading for months.

I file these reports under Form 9-Lacrime, Subsection C: "Public Emotional Incidents Requiring Municipal Witness." There are seventeen subsections to Form 9-Lacrime. I wrote six of them. I am not proud of this, but I am not ashamed either. The forms exist. Someone must maintain them.

The village itself is a stack of pastel-colored buildings climbing the rock face like barnacles with terracotta ambitions. The streets are mostly stairs. The stairs are mostly crooked. There are eleven churches for a population of four hundred and six — four hundred and seven if you count Signora Volpe, which I have never been certain you should — and every single one of those churches has a different saint dedicated to a different aspect of love. San Cornelio for first love. Santa Beatrice della Soglia for love you find at your doorstep unexpectedly. San Rocco dei Lamenti for love that makes you want to lie down on the floor of your kitchen and press your face to the cool tile and stay there for a very long time. That one gets the most candles.

The Festa della Fiamma Eterna — the Festival of the Eternal Flame — has been held in Pietra Rossa since, according to our municipal records, 1397, though the records from before 1782 are written in a script that is not quite Latin and not quite anything else, and which gives our archivist, Dottore Fabrizi, migraines so severe he has to sit in a dark room for three days after consulting them. The festival occurs once every seven years, always in October, always under a moon that the astronomical charts insist should not be full but always is. It lasts five nights. On the first night, the bonfire is lit in the Piazza della Cenere. On the fifth night, couples process by candlelight to the Cortile della Luna — the Courtyard of the Moon — where they kiss, and where, according to every piece of lore this village has ever generated, their love becomes permanent. Irrevocable. A contract with the universe.

I have seen couples emerge from that courtyard. I have handled their paperwork. They do stay together. Every one of them. I have checked. This is not romantic. This is a data set, and data sets do not lie, and the fact that the data set in question has a one hundred percent success rate over six centuries is not heartwarming. It is statistically impossible. And statistically impossible things, in my experience, are not miracles. They are mechanisms.

But I am getting ahead of myself. The forms. Start with the forms.

**II.**

They arrived on the same day, which was unusual. Festival participants typically trickle into Pietra Rossa over the week preceding the Festa, filtering through our intake process with the dazed, reverent expressions of people who have read too many articles with titles like "This Secret Italian Festival Will Restore Your Faith in Love." They come in couples, mostly. Sometimes in hopeful pairs that are not yet couples but intend to be. Occasionally, a solitary pilgrim appears, armed with the belief that the festival itself will supply the other half of the equation — that Pietra Rossa, being the most romantic place on Earth (see: ceramic tiles, nine euros), will generate love from the coincidence of proximity and moonlight.

But five strangers, all single, all arriving on the same October Tuesday on the same bus from Naples — the 14:20, which was eleven minutes late because the driver, Massimo, had stopped to argue with a goat that he claimed was "looking at him with judgment" — this was different. I knew it was different because the intake forms I'd prepared — Form 1-Arrivo, "Declaration of Romantic Intent and/or Existing Emotional Entanglement" — were insufficient for them. They did not check any of the standard boxes. They did not fit the categories.

The categories, I should note, are extensive. I have been refining them for twenty-three years.

I was at my desk in the Ufficio Accoglienza — the Welcome Office, ground floor, though "ground floor" in Pietra Rossa is a relative concept since the entire village is built on a sixty-degree incline, and my office is simultaneously on the ground floor, the second floor, and, due to a peculiarity of the building's medieval foundations, technically underground — when the five of them came through the door in a clump, the way strangers enter a space when they have been forced into temporary solidarity by a shared bus ride and now do not know how to disperse.

I will describe them in the order in which they reached my desk, because order matters, because sequence is the spine of bureaucracy, and because I have been trained over twenty-three years to resist the narrative urge to characterize when I should be documenting.

The first was the actuary. Her name was Claire Benford, American, forty-four, from Hartford, Connecticut, which she mentioned three times in the first two minutes as though Hartford were a condition she was disclosing. She had hair the color of a form that has been left too long in a window — not quite brown, not quite blond, faded by sustained exposure to fluorescent lighting and the mathematics of catastrophe, which is what actuaries calculate, I am told. She placed her passport on my desk with both hands, centered it, and aligned it parallel to the desk's edge. I respected this. She said she was divorced. She said this the way one says the word *deceased* — factually, with the faintest tremor underneath the fact, a tremor she had clearly spent considerable effort learning to suppress.

"I'm here for the festival," she said.

"You are alone?" I asked, because the form required this.

"That is the point," she said.

I checked the box for *Solitary Participant, Subsection 7: Autonomous Romantic Aspiration*. It was the wrong box. I know that now.

The second was the K-pop logistics manager. His name was Park Joon-ho, Korean, thirty-one, from Seoul, though he had been living in Los Angeles for four years managing the tour logistics for a boy band whose name I cannot legally include in this account due to an NDA he signed that apparently extends to third-party narration. He was handsome in the way that people in logistics are sometimes handsome — efficiently, without waste, every feature serving a purpose. He wore a jacket that cost more than my monthly salary. He carried a leather portfolio. Inside the portfolio, I later learned, he kept a laminated spreadsheet ranking the world's reportedly most romantic destinations by quantifiable metrics: accessibility, success rate of proposals, median relationship duration post-visit, local Wi-Fi reliability. Pietra Rossa was ranked number one. He had highlighted this in yellow.

"I'm here for the festival," he said.

"You are alone?"

He considered this question longer than Claire had. "I am between situations," he said.

I checked the same wrong box.

The third and fourth came together but not *together* — a distinction I note because they made this distinction aggressively, with their bodies and their silence and the specific way they arranged themselves in front of my desk: side by side but angled outward, like two halves of a door that had been blown open and now refused to close.

They were the twins. Marta and Sofia Leone. Italian by blood — their grandparents had emigrated from a village not forty kilometers from Pietra Rossa — but they had grown up in Melbourne, Australia, and their Italian was the peculiar, preserved-in-amber Italian of diaspora grandchildren, grammatically meticulous and slightly archaic, as though they had learned it from people who had stopped updating their vocabulary in 1962. They were thirty-six. They had the same face worn differently — Marta's expression was a closed fist; Sofia's was an open hand that had been open so long it was trembling.

They had not spoken to each other in nine years.

I knew this because Sofia told me immediately, in a rush, before I had asked any questions, while Marta stared at a point six inches to the left of my head with an intensity that made me want to check whether there was something there.

"We're not here together," Sofia said.

"We are here at the same time," Marta said. "Which is different."

"It's completely different," Sofia said, and for the briefest moment they were in agreement, and the agreement seemed to cause them both physical discomfort.

"You are here for the festival?"

"Yes," said Sofia.

"Apparently," said Marta.

"You are alone?"

They answered simultaneously: Sofia said "Yes" and Marta said "Obviously" and then they both fell silent in a way that suggested the simultaneous speaking had been a violation of some carefully maintained protocol.

I checked the wrong box twice.

The fifth was the sweepstakes winner. His name was Doug Freely. American, fifty-eight, from Terre Haute, Indiana. He wore a windbreaker with a logo for a brand of cereal that I will not name but which features a cartoon animal on its box that I am told is meant to be a toucan but which resembles, in Doug's description, "a bird that has seen some things." He had won his trip to Pietra Rossa — airfare, accommodation, festival registration — from a code on the inside of a cereal box flap. He had been eating this cereal every morning for thirty years. He had never won anything before. He kept the winning box flap in his wallet, laminated, next to a photograph of a woman he did not on that first day explain.

"I'm here for the festival," he said, with a kind of wondering uncertainty, as though he was still testing the sentence's plausibility.

"You are alone?"

"I guess so," said Doug Freely, and smiled, and the smile was the kind that has a room behind it full of things the smiler has decided not to show you.

Five strangers. Five wrong boxes. One bus from Naples, eleven minutes late.

I should have known then. The forms should have told me. Forms always tell you, if you know how to read the silences between the checkboxes — the spaces where the truth lives that the categories cannot contain. But I was tired that day. I am always tired. I have been tired since 2013, which is when I first attempted to file my resignation and discovered that the Ufficio Dimissioni — the Resignation Office — was not where it was supposed to be.

It is still not where it is supposed to be. I check every Thursday.

**III.**

I must now tell you about the process.

The Festa della Fiamma Eterna is not a festival you simply *attend*. You do not buy a ticket. You do not show up and stand near the bonfire and kiss someone in the courtyard. The festival is a *process*, and the process is the festival, and this distinction is critical because it is the distinction between a door and a corridor — between entering something and being *conducted through* something, with the thing that conducts you having its own intentions that you will not perceive until you are already too far inside to retreat.

Upon completing Form 1-Arrivo, each participant receives the Dossier del Cuore — the Heart Dossier — a manila folder containing the fourteen forms that must be completed, notarized, approved, and filed before one is permitted to participate in the final procession to the Cortile della Luna. I have memorized these forms. I could recite them in my sleep. I have recited them in my sleep. My ex-wife, Giuliana, told me this was one of the reasons she left, though she also cited the cat's spiritual exhaustion and my inability to describe my feelings without referencing subsection numbers.

The fourteen forms are:

1. **Form 2-Desiderio** — Statement of Romantic Desire, not to exceed 500 words, single-spaced, in triplicate.
2. **Form 3-Precedente** — Complete Romantic History, with dates, durations, and categorized outcomes (see Appendix 3-A through 3-F: "Classifications of How It Ended").
3. **Form 4-Dolore** — Notarized Proof of Heartbreak.
4. **Form 5-Capacità** — Self-Assessment of Emotional Capacity, scored on a rubric of one to seventeen.
5. **Form 6-Fuoco** — Fire Code Inspection of Inner Passion (I will explain this).
6. **Form 7-Merito** — Justification of Worthiness to Receive Love.
7. **Form 8-Paura** — Disclosure of Fears Relating to Intimacy, with supporting documentation.
8. **Form 9-Promessa** — Oath of Romantic Intent, witnessed by a municipal official.
9. **Form 10-Perdono** — Evidence of Forgiveness Given or Sought.
10. **Form 11-Sogno** — Description of Recurring Dream Most Closely Associated with Longing.
11. **Form 12-Segreto** — Sealed Confession of the Thing You Have Never Told Anyone (to be filed unopened).
12. **Form 13-Specchio** — Self-Portrait in Words, as Seen by the Person Who Loved You Most.
13. **Form 14-Ombra** — Declaration of What You Are Willing to Surrender.
14. **Form 15-Fiamma** — Final Petition for Entry to the Cortile della Luna.

Each form requires approval from a different sub-office. There are seventeen sub-offices — yes, seventeen, though there are only fourteen forms, because three of the sub-offices exist solely to process appeals from the other fourteen, and their relationship to the other offices is recursive in a way that makes Dottore Fabrizi reach for his migraine medication even when he is not consulting the pre-1782 records.

The sub-offices are distributed across the village's municipal complex, which is not a single building but rather a network of seven buildings connected by covered walkways, exterior staircases, and, in one case, a rope bridge over a narrow alley that the structural engineers warned us about in 2008 and which we have been meaning to address. Each sub-office has a number, a name, posted hours, and a floor designation, and every single one of these attributes is, at any given moment, wrong.
