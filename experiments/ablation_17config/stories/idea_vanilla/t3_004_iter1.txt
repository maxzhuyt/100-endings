# THE ORNAMENT'S SIXTH SATURDAY

## Chapter VI: The Anaphylaxis Gambit

---

The napkins this week are swan-fold. Last week, bishop's mitre. The week before — I've been keeping a log, naturally, in the margins of the Trollope they've left in my room like a chocolate on the pillow of a man who cannot leave the hotel — the week before was a simple pyramid, which the staff call "the diplomat," presumably without irony, though in this house one can never be certain that anything is without irony, least of all the things that appear most sincere.

I note the swans because the fold tells me which of the housekeeping staff has laid the table, and because today the fold matters. The swan is Ketevan's work. Ketevan, who has a sister in Kutaisi with a printing business. Ketevan, who over three weeks of my most carefully rationed charm — a commodity I must spend now like a pensioner counting coins for bread, having wasted the reserve fund of it on the catastrophe at the embassy reception that delivered me here — has been persuaded that I am a man of sorrows, a man of quiet dignities, a man who merely needs to reach the Georgian border crossing at Larsi with documents that pass a fourteen-second inspection by a guard who, I am told, has a preference for speed over thoroughness, especially on Saturdays between one and three, when the football is on.

The documents are currently in the left breast pocket of my jacket. The jacket is currently on my person. My person is currently in the chair third from the end on the south side of a mahogany table that seats twenty-four and today hosts eleven, because the baroness — our hostess, my captor, the woman to whom I owe a debt so socially exotic that no Foreign Office protocol covers it — the baroness prefers odd numbers at table. She says they prevent the conversation from becoming bilateral. I suspect they prevent the guests from forming alliances.

I have been in this house for forty-one days.

The house is not a house. I should say that plainly, in case my assessments are ever recovered and read by someone at the Service who needs to understand the tactical situation. The house is a fortified estate called Tsinandali — not the famous one, a private joke or a private threat — set into a granite shelf eleven hundred meters above a valley whose name I have been unable to determine because every map in the library has been annotated in a hand I don't recognize with place names I suspect are invented. The walls are limestone. The gates are steel and decorative in the way that a beautiful woman's beautiful fingernails are decorative when they are also, one comes to understand, quite sharp. There are seventeen visible staff and an uncertain number of others who move through parts of the house I am discouraged from visiting, not by locks — nothing here is locked, that is the horror of it — but by the gentle, lethal architecture of hospitality. One does not wander into the east wing because one has not been *invited* to the east wing, and to go where one has not been invited would be *rude*, and to be rude in this house is to — well.

I haven't yet determined what it is to be rude in this house. The baroness has not shown me. The baroness, in fact, has shown me nothing but the most extraordinary, most immaculate, most progressively *quiet* generosity, and it is the quietness that makes my hands shake when I try to butter my bread.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. The bread hasn't arrived yet. We are still in the anteroom phase of the Saturday lunch, which unfolds each week with the ceremonial precision of a diplomatic function organized by someone who finds diplomatic functions amusing rather than necessary, which makes them worse, because the necessary ones at least have the decency to be boring.

I should describe the room. I should describe the company. I should describe the plan.

I will begin with the plan, because the plan is good. The plan, this time, is genuinely good.

---

**The Anaphylaxis Gambit (Summary for the Log)**

At 12:40, the second course arrives. The second course is, based on five weeks of observation, always a soup. The baroness's kitchen favors walnut-based soups — *bazhe*, *satsivi* stock, once a cream of walnut thing that had no name and tasted like the forest floor rendered edible by someone who understood forests better than I ever will. I am not allergic to walnuts. But the medical file that the embassy transmitted to the baroness's household physician — Dr. Lasha, a man so overqualified for this posting that his presence here is itself a form of threat — that file was prepared by me, at the embassy, before the reception, for unrelated reasons that I will not detail here except to say that a man in my financial position occasionally requires medical documentation that supports insurance claims of a creative nature.

The file says I am allergic to walnuts. Severely. *Anaphylactically.*

At 12:40, I will consume the soup. At approximately 12:43, I will exhibit symptoms. I have practiced the symptoms. The throat-clutching is, I think, convincing. The facial swelling I will approximate with a crushed capsule of niacin held in my cheek — Ketevan provided this, along with the documents, in exchange for a locket containing a miniature portrait of my maternal grandmother, which I presented as a token of emotional significance rather than what it is, which is nine-carat gold plate over pewter, worth perhaps forty pounds on a generous day in a dishonest shop.

At 12:45, Dr. Lasha will be summoned. Protocol — I have observed the protocol — requires that a medical emergency be treated in the clinic on the ground floor, east corridor. The clinic has an exterior door. The exterior door opens onto the service courtyard. Ketevan's cousin — not the sister in Kutaisi, but a cousin — drives the supply vehicle that departs from the service courtyard every Saturday at 1:00 PM precisely, bound for Stepantsminda, which is twenty-six kilometers from the Larsi crossing.

At 12:50, while Dr. Lasha is retrieving the epinephrine he will feel professionally obligated to administer, I will exit through the exterior door, enter the supply vehicle, present the cousin with the second half of the payment Ketevan has negotiated — the first half being the locket, the second half being my word as a gentleman that I will wire three hundred pounds to an account in Batumi, a promise I intend, in the moment of making it, to keep, which is the most dangerous kind of promise because it almost resembles integrity — and I will be at the border by 1:45.

By 2:00, I will be in Georgia proper. By evening, Tbilisi. By morning, a call to the consulate from a position of safety, where the baroque unpleasantness of my situation can be reframed as a minor diplomatic misunderstanding rather than what it is, which is something I still lack the precise terminology to describe.

This is the plan. The plan is good. I have stress-tested the plan. I have, as they say, war-gamed it.

The variable I have not accounted for is presently crossing the anteroom in a dress the color of Georgian amber, holding a glass of *Saperavi* she is too young for and carrying it with the authority of someone who has never encountered a thing she is too young for, and she is looking — Christ, she is looking directly at me with that particular expression, the one that is not a smile but performs all the mechanical functions of a smile while communicating something closer to the moment before a cat commits to the pursuit of a creature it has already, in some feline cognitive sense, parsing the physics of it, *caught*.

Nino.

The baroness's eldest daughter. Twenty-three. Educated in Geneva, or possibly Vienna — the details shift depending on which staff member one asks, another feature of this house, that facts are not fixed but contextual, like the weather. She has her mother's face, which is to say a face assembled from the intersection of three empires, Ottoman and Russian and Persian, combined in proportions that suggest God was, at the moment of her creation, showing off. She has her mother's voice, which is low and carries without effort and makes every room she enters slightly smaller. She does not have her mother's stillness. Where the baroness has learned to be quiet — has *become* quiet, these past weeks, in a way that I recognize, that I know, that I have *seen before*, in Astana, in that terrible last month — Nino is loud in the way that intelligence is loud when it has not yet learned to hide.

She calls me the ornament.

"The ornament looks well this morning," she says, arriving at my periphery with the glass of wine held like a small, dark lantern. "Someone's polished him."

"Miss Tamar," I say. I always use the wrong name first. It is a small rebellion that costs nothing and earns me approximately two seconds of her displeasure, which is preferable to her interest.

"Nino," she corrects, as she always corrects.

"Forgive me. One struggles with names when one has been introduced to so many charming members of the household."

"You've been introduced to eleven people. The same eleven people. Every Saturday. For six weeks." She sips the wine. "Is that the threshold at which British memory fails, or is it specific to your branch of the service?"

She says *your branch of the service* the way one might say *your particular strain of the infection*. I have never confirmed that I am in any branch of any service. She has never required confirmation. This, too, is a feature of the house: things are known here the way altitude is known, not because anyone has measured it but because the air is thin and your lungs tell you so.

"I'm a cultural attaché," I say, which is the thing I say, the way a man on a ledge says *I'm fine.*

"Of course you are. And today you're a cultural attaché with —" She leans closer. The wine on her breath is warm and sharp and geographic; it smells like the valley I cannot name. "— something in your left pocket that's making your jacket sit badly. Tskitishvili & Sons, unless I'm mistaken. The tailoring, I mean. Not whoever did your documents."

The room does not spin. I do not allow it to spin. A man in my position — a man in any position — must understand that the moment the room spins is the moment the room has won, and rooms in this house win often enough without my assistance.

"I carry a handkerchief," I say. "As one does."

"As one does," she agrees, with a musicality that makes the phrase mean: *I know what you carry and I know what it's for and I will now leave you in the amber of this knowledge for precisely as long as it takes to be unbearable.*

She moves away. She always moves away at this point. The cat, releasing the mouse. Not out of mercy — I abandoned the notion of her mercy somewhere around Week Three, when she deduced the business with the kitchen boy and the recopied laissez-passer and dismantled it over dessert with three sentences of such elegant social demolition that the boy himself didn't realize he'd been exposed until Wednesday — but because she understands timing the way her mother understands silence, which is to say *structurally*.

I touch the pocket. The documents are there. The niacin capsule is there, nested in a fold of tissue behind my upper right molar. The plan is intact.

She cannot know the plan. She can know the documents — she is, I have accepted, somehow omniscient about documents, as though forgery gives off a scent she can detect — but the plan has moving parts she cannot see. The soup. The allergy. The clinic door. The cousin and the vehicle. She is intelligent, but intelligence is not clairvoyance, and the plan — the plan is *good*.

I take my seat. Third from the end, south side. Across from me, Vakhtang, who manages the estate's accounts and speaks to me only in proverbs. Beside me, the empty chair that will be filled, as it is filled every week, by whichever guest the baroness has invited to remind me that the world continues to exist outside these walls and that I am not part of it. Last week, a Georgian MP who clapped me on the back and asked which London clubs I belonged to, and I said "White's," because I have been saying "White's" for eleven years, and it was true once, briefly, before the fees and the debts and the quiet conversation in the secretary's office that was not technically an expulsion because men like me are not technically expelled, we are *allowed to let our memberships lapse*, which is the institutional equivalent of being buried without a headstone.

Today, the guest chair is occupied by a woman I have not seen before. Fiftyish. French, or performing French. Hermès scarf. Eyes like a balance sheet.

"Madame Clairmont," she says, extending her hand in the European fashion. "And you must be —"

"The ornament," says Nino, from three seats away, without looking up from her wine.

Madame Clairmont's expression performs a complicated journey from confusion to amusement to something I cannot read, and she withdraws her hand half a degree — not a full withdrawal, not a snub, but a recalibration, the way one adjusts the angle of an umbrella when the wind shifts. She has been told something about me. Everyone who sits in that chair has been told something about me.

"Galbraith," I say. "Hugh Galbraith. Cultural attaché, British Embassy, currently — between postings."

"Between postings," says Nino, still not looking up. "Like a letter that hasn't been collected."

"My daughter has a gift for metaphor," says the baroness.

The baroness.

I have not described the baroness because the baroness resists description in the way that certain diplomatic incidents resist summary: one can list the facts, the sequence, the parties involved, and still fail to convey the quality of the air in the room when the thing was happening.

She is seated at the head of the table. She is always seated at the head of the table when I arrive, which means she arrives before me, which means she is either genuinely punctual or she wishes me to understand that in this house, all positions are established before I enter the scene, and my entrance is itself a concession, a door held open, and doors held open can be closed.

Manana Tamar. The newspapers call her a gas baroness, which is accurate in the way that calling the ocean "wet" is accurate: it conveys a single quality of a considerably more complex entity. She controls — through a web of holdings I have tried and failed to map on the blank pages of the Trollope — a significant portion of the pipeline infrastructure connecting the Caspian fields to the Turkish border. This is not the source of her power. The source of her power is that she controls these things *quietly*, and in the Caucasus, quietness is not the absence of power but its highest expression.

She is fifty-seven. She has the face of a woman who was once very beautiful and is now something more dangerous than beautiful, which is *exact*. Every feature has been refined by time into a kind of precision.
