# The Wound in the Axis

## I.

The last calibration drift had gone unlogged because there was no longer a meaningful standard against which to measure it. Sable noticed it the way she noticed everything now — precisely, dutifully, and into a silence that swallowed the observation whole. The gyroscopic array was off by a quarter-degree. Or the universe had shifted by a quarter-degree around the gyroscopic array. In the intergalactic void, without a single reference point burning in any direction, the distinction was theological.

She wrote it down anyway. She wrote everything down. That was the work.

The ship was called the *Lethe*, which had been a joke once, when the crew was nine and the mapping contract paid per annum and the name had the defiant irony of people who believed they would be remembered. Now it was five — five and a half, if you were generous about Okonkwo's condition — and the name was just a name, and no one remembered the joke, or chose not to.

The cartography suite took up a third of the habitable deck, a sprawl of instrumentation that suggested a universe worth measuring. Three holographic plotting tables, two spectral analysis rigs, a gravimetric array sensitive enough to detect the mass-shadow of a brown dwarf at forty light-years. All of it built for regions where things existed. Sable sat among them each morning — mornings being a fiction they maintained by dimming and brightening the overheads on a twenty-four-hour cycle, though the bulbs had yellowed unevenly so that "dawn" arrived now as a sickly, parchment-colored event, like the lights coming up in a room where someone had recently died — and she plotted nothing onto nothing, and named it. Void cell 1734-Null. Void cell 1735-Null. A grid of certified absences extending outward in every direction, each square a region she had personally verified as containing no matter, no energy, no residual radiation above the cosmic microwave background. She was typing the obituaries of the sky.

The work was important. The Cartographic Authority, when it still answered transmissions, required void-certification for navigational safety: ships needed to know which regions between the galaxy clusters held nothing at all, so that future routes could be plotted through confirmed emptiness rather than the merely probable kind. This was how Sable had explained it to herself for eleven years. That the blankness she charted was *for* something. That the absence had a use. That naming empty space void cell 1735-Null and entering its coordinates into a database that might or might not still exist was a contribution rather than an elaborate method of going mad.

The region they traversed now was called, in the Authority's terminology, the Boötes Expanse — one of the great supervoids, a bubble of nothing roughly three hundred million light-years across in which no galaxy had formed, or in which every galaxy that had formed had died. The distinction mattered to Sable, though she could not have articulated why. She preferred the second interpretation. It was lonelier but more companionable: the void as aftermath, not absence. A place that had known something and lost it, rather than a place that had never known anything at all. She charted it accordingly, her notes specifying not merely that Region 1736 contained nothing but that it contained the particular kind of nothing that came *after* — thermal residues too faint to be meaningful, fluctuations in the background radiation that might once have been the light of a star that might once have warmed a world that might once have held, briefly, improbably, the sound of someone calling someone else's name across a room. Or might not have. Probably not. But Sable logged the residues, the fluctuations, the *mights*, because the alternative was to log nothing at all, and if she did that she would be what she measured, and she was not ready for that yet.

On the deck below the cartography suite, Lécuyer was dividing water. This was a task that took him approximately two hours each day and that he performed with the grave, ritualistic focus of a man enacting a sacrament. Twelve hundred milliliters remaining in the potable reserve. Five crew members, or five and a half. Lécuyer solved the problem each morning as though it were new, as though he had not solved it identically the day before, writing figures in a small notebook with a pencil he sharpened with a knife because the electric sharpener drew too much power. He apportioned the water into containers of different sizes, each labeled in his meticulous handwriting: SABLE, MAREN, TSONG, OKONKWO, LÉCUYER. He did not make a container for Petrov, because Petrov was dead, though Petrov's body was still in the med-bay because no one had the caloric surplus to move it, and in the cold of the lower deck it was not urgent. Lécuyer had crossed Petrov's name off his notebook. A single line, very straight.

The containers were always the same size. The water was always divided equally. But the ritual of recalculation seemed to comfort him — the proof, each morning, that mathematics still worked, that division still divided, that the universe at least obeyed the jurisdiction of fractions.

Tsong was sleeping. Tsong slept fourteen hours a day now and called it efficiency. She had been the ship's navigator, back when navigation meant something — when there were destinations that could be reached and reasons to reach them. Now she slept in the pilot's chair with her mouth open and her hands folded over her stomach, and sometimes she murmured numbers in her sleep, headings and velocities and correction burns for a course no one was flying, her dreaming mind still solving the problem of how to get somewhere, anywhere, in a universe that had run out of somewheres. Sable had stopped waking her. The numbers Tsong murmured were no less useful than the coordinates Sable plotted. Both were systems of meaning applied to the null set. Both were prayer.

Maren was with Okonkwo. Maren was always with Okonkwo. Okonkwo's immune system had begun a civil war against his own connective tissue four months ago — some autoimmune cascade triggered by malnutrition and stress and the particular cellular despair that came from years in the lightless between — and Maren, who was a geologist and had no medical training beyond a first-aid certification she'd earned twenty years ago in a classroom that probably no longer existed on a planet she would never see again, tended him with the fierce, clumsy dedication of someone who could not bear to do nothing. She changed his dressings. She mixed electrolyte solutions from diminishing stocks. She talked to him — long, quiet monologues about rock formations she had studied, mineral compositions, the particular way feldspar cleaves along its crystal planes — because the sound of organized knowledge seemed to calm him, or because it calmed her, and the distinction was, like so much else out here, immaterial.

Okonkwo listened, or didn't. His joints had swollen to obscene proportions, his fingers fat and taut and shining like sausages in a casing, and the pain was such that Maren's mineral monologues reached him, when they reached him, as a kind of geological lullaby — the soothing murmur of a universe that at least had the decency to be solid, to have structure, to cleave along predictable planes.

This was the *Lethe*. This was its household. Five people in a metal tube drifting through a region of the universe that had no opinion about their existence, performing elaborate rituals of competence in a professional context that no longer existed, too deep in the void to turn back, too low on consumables to reach anything even if there were anything to reach, and too proud or too exhausted for the conversation about what, precisely, they were doing still. The ship's engines ran at eleven percent — enough for minimal course corrections, not enough for meaningful acceleration. The communications array had received nothing but static for nine hundred days. The food stores would sustain them at current rationing for another thirty-one to thirty-seven days, depending on whether Okonkwo could eat solids again.

Sable stood at her plotting table and watched the holographic grid extend in every direction: a blue lattice of coordinates superimposed on the observation feed from the external sensors, which showed nothing. The grid floated in blackness. The blackness was the blackness of between — not the star-punctured dark of a galactic neighborhood but the absolute, matte, depthless dark of a place where no photon had originated in millions of years. Looking at it was not like looking at emptiness. It was like looking at the surface of something. Like the dark had a texture, a weight. Like it was not the absence of light but a substance in its own right, an upholstery stretched over the frame of a cosmos that had, in these regions, nothing left to show.

She plotted void cell 1736-Null. She entered its coordinates. She verified its emptiness. She waited for the spreadsheet to save — the cursor blinking for three seconds, four, the processing cores straining even at this — and then she moved to 1737.

Below her, Lécuyer finished his division and placed two hundred and forty milliliters of water outside each bunk. The containers made small, precise sounds against the metal shelving. He returned the remaining supply to the locked cabinet and checked the seal twice. He sat down at the common table and opened his notebook and began, very slowly, to calculate tomorrow.

---

In the evening — another fiction, the overheads dimming to amber, the ship pretending to have a relationship with a star — Sable ate her ration at the common table with Lécuyer and Tsong. The ration was a paste extruded from a tube. It was gray. It had been engineered, years ago in a laboratory on a planet with sunlight and weather, to provide complete nutrition in a calorie-dense format. It tasted of almost nothing, a faint mineral tang at the back of the palate, and this minimal flavor had taken on an outsized significance: Tsong said it tasted like chalk, Lécuyer said aluminum, Sable said nothing because she had stopped tasting it months ago and this seemed like a defeat she shouldn't publicize.

They ate in silence. The silence had a specific quality — not hostile, not companionable, but *conservational*. They were people who had said the things there were to say. The remaining conversations — the useful ones, the recriminations, the admissions of terror, the practical discussion of how exactly this would end — required an energy none of them could afford to spend. So they ate, and the ship hummed at its reduced frequency, and the dark pressed against the hull.

"1736 is clean," Sable said, finally.

Lécuyer nodded. He wrote something in his notebook. He had begun, at some point, logging Sable's certifications alongside his water calculations, as though the two data sets were related, as though the confirmation that a region of space was empty belonged in the same ledger as the confirmation that a quantity of water had been divided. Perhaps it did. Both were accountings of what was left, how much of it, and how long it could last.

"Anything in 1737?" Tsong asked, without looking up.

"I'll survey tomorrow."

"Right."

The word *anything* had become their euphemism. It meant: a signal, a station, a derelict with salvageable consumables, a miracle. It meant rescue, though no one said rescue because rescue implied a rescuer, a civilization with reach and resources, and the last confirmed civilization in their records was eleven hundred light-years behind them and had not answered a transmission in almost three years. *Anything* was the polite word for the impossible. Tsong asked it each evening and Sable answered each evening and neither of them addressed the fact that the question and the answer were part of the same ritual as Lécuyer's water division: a formal acknowledgment that tomorrow would occur, that there would be a survey, that the grid of void cells would extend by one more unit, that the project of measuring nothing would continue for at least one more day.

Sable squeezed the last of the paste from her tube and placed the empty container in the recycling bin, where it joined a growing archaeology of spent tubes, each one a thin, collapsed skin bearing a date written in her handwriting — a record of meals consumed, of days successfully not-died, that resembled nothing so much as her void charts: a careful log of negation, each entry certifying that another unit of something had been used up and replaced by nothing.

She returned to the cartography suite. She did not return because the work was urgent. She returned because the suite was where she sat, and sitting was what she did, and the plotting table's blue glow was the closest thing she had to a hearth.

She pulled up her records for the past three years. Thousands of void cells, certified and logged, forming a lattice of verified emptiness that now stretched — if the measurements were accurate, and they were, or had been — across a span of forty-two light-years. Forty-two light-years of nothing, measured and named and entered into a database that drifted with them through the dark like a menu in an empty restaurant, a meticulous record of what could not be ordered, what would not arrive.

She scrolled through the entries. She did this sometimes — reviewed old certifications the way a writer rereads old letters, looking not for information but for evidence of a prior self who still believed the project mattered. Early entries were detailed, rich with spectral data and gravimetric readings and marginal notes speculating about the void's formation history. More recent entries were terse. 1601-Null. 1602-Null. 1603-Null. The progression from thoroughness to brevity was its own kind of chart — a cartography of morale, mapping the slow transit from professional rigor to the minimum motion required to keep the ritual alive.

She closed the records. She looked at the external feed: nothing. She looked at the plotting grid: void cell 1737, unsurveyed, a blue square in the lattice awaiting its certification of emptiness. Tomorrow she would scan it, measure it, and enter its nothing into the log. The grid would extend. The project would continue. The database would grow by one more zero.

She sat in the blue glow and listened to the ship, which creaked and murmured as its thermal systems cycled, adjusting and readjusting to the unchanging cold outside — a machine built for the universe, faithfully regulating itself for a cosmos that had moved on, like a clock still ticking in a house whose occupants would never return.

## II.

It was not a sound. It was the memory of a sound — the echo a bell leaves in a cathedral after the bell has stopped, not the tone itself but the shape the tone cut in the air, persisting.

Sable's first thought was instrument failure. A spectral analyzer stuttering in its final decline, producing a phantom return where no return existed. She had been running the standard sweep on void cell 1737 — broad-spectrum electromagnetic scan, gravimetric pulse, thermal survey — expecting, as she always expected, nothing. The sweep confirmed nothing in the electromagnetic range. Nothing on the gravimetric pulse. And then, on the thermal survey, a reading so faint it barely resolved above the noise floor: a point-source thermal emission at coordinates she had already certified empty.

She ran the sweep again. The emission was there. At the extreme edge of the sensor's degraded range, almost indistinguishable from the background — a warmth. Not a heat signature in any meaningful astrophysical sense. Not the remnant output of a stellar body or the thermal bleed of an active system. Something at approximately 2,900 Kelvin — the color temperature of warm light, of candlelight, of the light that falls through a window at the end of the day in a room where someone is waiting. A temperature that had no business existing in a region where the ambient was 2.7 Kelvin, the faint, exhausted warmth of the universe's own background radiation, the last thermal memory of the beginning of everything.

She adjusted the sensor array. Narrowed the field. Increased the integration time to compensate for the instruments' drift. The emission held. A point of warmth at bearing 042 by 117, distance indeterminate — the ranging systems couldn't triangulate on something this faint without a second reference point, and there were no second reference points, there was nothing, there was the void and the void and the void and, impossibly, insufferably, this: a warm thing in the empty.

Sable did not call the others. Later she would think about this and find nothing to think about, no decision she could locate. She simply did not call them. She adjusted the visual array — the direct optical feed, which she rarely used because it showed only blackness and because staring into the intergalactic dark was not good for the organism behind the eyes — and slaved it to the bearing of the thermal anomaly and looked.

Nothing. Blackness. The absolute matte dark of between.
