# THE DEED

## I.

The deposition lasted four hours and eleven minutes. Margaret Hargrove knew the exact duration because she billed in six-minute increments and had trained herself, years ago, to track time the way other people tracked weather — automatically, peripherally, the internal clock ticking beneath every interaction like a meter that never stopped running.

She also knew, by the three-hour mark, that opposing counsel had nothing.

Peter Olafsson, representing the Bearpaw Valley Ranchers' Cooperative, had come in with ninety-seven pages of counterclaim and three ring binders of documentary evidence that he clearly considered devastating. Margaret had read all of it the evening before — twice — and identified the structural deficiency within twenty minutes: every boundary description his clients relied upon derived from a 1934 BLM survey that had been administratively superseded in 1961. Olafsson either didn't know this or was hoping she wouldn't. Both possibilities insulted her.

She let him build his case for two and a half hours. Let him lay each document into the record with the careful pride of a man who believed in the weight of paper. Let his expert witness, a retired county surveyor with a white mustache and a bolo tie, narrate the boundary history that Olafsson had constructed from secondary sources and incomplete title abstracts. Margaret asked clarifying questions. She requested spellings for the record. She appeared, by every external measure, to be a woman receiving new information and finding it somewhat persuasive.

At 2:47 p.m., she introduced Exhibit 14 — the 1961 reclassification order — and asked the surveyor whether he had reviewed it during his preparation.

He had not.

She asked whether the reclassification order altered the legal status of the benchmark from which all of his boundary calculations originated.

It did.

She asked whether, in light of the reclassification, any of his boundary descriptions remained valid as a matter of law.

The silence in the conference room lasted eleven seconds. Margaret counted them.

"No, ma'am," the surveyor said. "I suppose they would not."

Olafsson requested a recess. Margaret agreed. She was magnanimous in the aftermath of precision — it cost nothing. In the hallway, she checked her phone: two emails from the paralegal team on the Sievert Resources file, one from Stuart Chandler's assistant requesting fifteen minutes on her calendar, and a text from her mother that said *Call when you can, it's about the house*. She archived the text without reading it again. The house was the house in Elk Fork, the one her grandfather had built, the one her mother could not stop talking about and Margaret could not bring herself to visit. There was always something — a fence, a foundation crack, a property tax reassessment. The house was a document that never stopped needing revision.

She answered the paralegals. She confirmed the meeting with Chandler. She did not call her mother.

Back in the conference room, Olafsson attempted to rehabilitate his case by pivoting to an equitable argument — longstanding use, community reliance, the character of the land itself. Margaret let him talk. Equitable arguments were like weather: they felt significant in the moment and had no bearing on the outcome. The law was the survey. The survey was the benchmark. The benchmark had been reclassified. Everything Olafsson's clients believed about their eastern boundary was, in the language she preferred, *legally inoperative*. The land they'd been ranching for three generations belonged to the entity that held the post-1961 title, which was, through a chain of assignments Margaret could recite from memory, Sievert Resources LLC.

Sievert wanted the land for lithium extraction. The brine deposits beneath the eastern pastures were, according to the geological survey Margaret had read in the bathtub on a Tuesday night, among the most concentrated in North America. Sievert's CEO had described it, in the intake meeting six months ago, as "the find of a generation." Margaret had not corrected his grandiosity because grandiose clients paid retainers without negotiating.

She closed the deposition at 3:18 p.m. Olafsson did not shake her hand. His clients, two men in clean denim who had driven down from the valley that morning, looked at her as men always looked at her when she won — as though she had broken something they didn't know was fragile.

Margaret gathered her files. She felt what she always felt after a clean win: not triumph exactly, but the deep structural satisfaction of correct assembly. The law was a machine. She was good with machines.

---

Stuart Chandler's office occupied the southwest corner of the fourteenth floor, which meant it received the last of the Denver light in a long amber stripe across his desk every afternoon at four. Stuart was sixty-three, the founding partner's son, and the closest thing Chandler Pryce had to a living institution. He wore his authority the way old buildings wear their stone — not as display but as fact.

"The Bearpaw deposition," he said, not looking up from a document he was signing. "How did it go?"

"Their boundary claim fails. The '34 survey is dead."

"Olafsson know that before today?"

"He does now."

Stuart signed the document, capped his pen, and looked at her. He had the assessing gaze of a man who had spent forty years determining what people were worth. "Sievert's going to be pleased. That eastern parcel was the last contested piece."

"I know."

"Which means the extraction permit moves forward. Which means the firm's largest client achieves its primary strategic objective. Which means" — and here he paused, performing a calculation he had clearly already completed — "the compensation committee is going to have a very interesting conversation at the end of Q1."

Margaret understood what he was not yet saying. The partnership. Her name on the door, or at least on the letterhead, which was the modern equivalent. She had been working toward it for seven years — longer, if you counted law school, the clerkship, the first grinding years as an associate when she billed twenty-three hundred hours and learned to read mineral rights agreements the way musicians read scores, hearing the silences between the clauses.

"I don't want to get ahead of the committee," she said, because modesty was a form of leverage when deployed correctly.

"You're not ahead of anyone. You're exactly where the work has put you." Stuart leaned back. Behind him, on the credenza, a row of framed photographs documented the firm's history: ribbon cuttings at mine openings, handshakes with governors, the long procession of men in suits standing on land that had been converted, by their efforts, into value. Margaret had walked past those photographs a thousand times. She noticed, now, one she'd never studied closely — a sepia image, older than the others, showing a group of men around a railroad survey table. The mountains behind them were unmistakable: the Crazies, north of Elk Fork. One of the men had his hand on the table, proprietary, and beneath the photograph a small brass plate read: *J.R. Hargrove, N.P.R.R. Land Office, Montana Territory, 1886*.

Her great-great-grandfather.
