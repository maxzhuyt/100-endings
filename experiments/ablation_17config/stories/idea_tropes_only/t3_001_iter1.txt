# The Avalon

## I.

Carthage, Mississippi, sits in Leake County the way a tooth sits in a dead man's jaw — still rooted, still there, signifying nothing to anyone but itself. The courthouse square is red brick. The oaks around it are two hundred years old and arthritic in their upper branches, and in the summer their canopy draws a green ceiling over the Confederate monument that no one has removed and no one defends anymore, at least not in public. It simply remains, the way so many things in Carthage remain — not because they are wanted but because removal would require an act of will, and acts of will have a way of exposing other things that have been allowed to remain.

The town extends from the square in four directions, each ray a little dimmer than the last. East along Highway 16 to the Dollar General, the Piggly Wiggly, the Valero station where the pumps are numbered but pump three has never worked. South toward Zion Hill Baptist, whose steeple is the highest point for miles and whose new fellowship hall was built on a slab poured by a Harmon contracting crew in 2006 at three times the county bid, a fact noted in the *Carthaginian* in a four-line item on page nine and never mentioned again. West to the industrial blight along the railway spur: the shuttered catfish processing plant, the operating soybean facility that fills the air with a sweetness that is not sweetness but a chemical cousin of sweetness — a smell that coats the sinuses and cannot be washed out of cotton, so that Carthage's people carry it with them as a kind of olfactory passport. You can always tell someone from Carthage. They smell like something pretending to be sugar.

North, past the newer subdivisions where middle management from the soybean plant lives in houses that all chose the same three exterior paint colors, the road narrows and passes through a corridor of loblolly pines before arriving at the Neshoba County line, where Carthage's jurisdiction and interest both terminate.

Two names have threaded through this town for as long as anyone under eighty can remember, and longer than that, though the memories past eighty have grown unreliable and anyway the people who hold them have learned the prudence of forgetting.

Delacroix: on the pharmacy at the corner of Center and Valley streets, gold serif letters on a green awning, *Delacroix Drug* — though no Delacroix has been a pharmacist since 1974. On the side of a long-haul trailer parked behind the Valero: *Delacroix Logistics, LLC.* On a bail bonds storefront two blocks from the courthouse, the name abbreviated: *DLX Bonding, 24hr.* And on a brass plate in the lobby of First Carthage Bank, among the founders: *J. Beaumont Delacroix, 1937.*

Harmon: on a placard in the county supervisor's office, third district — *Roy Harmon, Jr.,* though everyone calls him Little Roy, even to his face, even though he is sixty-three years old and taller than his father ever was. On a pew dedication plate at Zion Hill Baptist: *In Loving Memory of Elda Harmon, Given by the Harmon Family, 2011.* On a county road southeast of town, *Harmon Road,* which leads to a catfish pond and a locked gate and 240 acres of pine plantation that produce, according to tax filings, remarkably little timber revenue for their size. And on the masthead of the *Carthaginian,* not as owner but as the name everyone knows stands behind the owner: a Harmon married a Givens who bought the paper in 2002.

These two names do not appear on the same surfaces. They divide the town the way a cell divides: not through conflict but through the ancient arithmetic of replication. Each family has its own territory, its own operations, its own revenue streams — and they share, as all successful organisms do, a common interest in the environment that sustains them both. This common interest has, for as long as anyone bothers to remember, been maintained through a series of joint ventures so thoroughly embedded in the civic life of Carthage that they are indistinguishable from the civic life itself. The annual Leake County Law Enforcement Appreciation Dinner: jointly underwritten. The Fourth of July fireworks over the reservoir: jointly sponsored. The Christmas Toy Drive for the Choctaw Reservation: jointly administered, the funds passing through a joint account at First Carthage that, for two weeks in December, holds approximately nine times what the purchase of toys would require, then releases the toys' cost to the reservation contact and routes the remainder through channels so boring in their complexity — subsidiary transfers, intercompany loans, equipment purchases for Delacroix Logistics, consulting fees paid to a Harmon-owned LLC in Jackson — that the auditor in Meridian who technically oversees the county's books has never found it worth the drive.

These are the charity galas. Everyone knows. No one names.

And at the center of this architecture, both literally and operationally, stands the Avalon.

The Avalon Theatre occupies the north side of the courthouse square, between the former Carthage Hotel — now a law office on the ground floor and vacancy above — and a two-story building that has been, in succession, a dry goods store, a furniture showroom, a video rental franchise, and is currently being "developed" by an entity called CRX Properties into something that the permits describe as "mixed-use retail" and that has not progressed past the demolition of the interior walls in the fourteen months since the permits were issued. The Avalon's façade is glazed terra-cotta in a style that the Preservation Society's brochure calls Moorish Revival, though the architect — a man from Memphis named Sonnenberg who built four theatres across Mississippi between 1923 and 1929 — would have called it simply "modern." The terra-cotta is the color of dried blood. Sections of it have cracked and fallen, revealing the common brick beneath. The marquee juts over the sidewalk on a steel armature, its lightbulbs missing or dead, its movable letter-track holding, at present, the message:

**AV LON**
**VAIL BLE FOR PRI ATE EVENTS**
**CALL 601 – –**

The phone number lost its last four digits sometime after Hurricane Katrina, when the wind took the letters and no one replaced them.

Inside, the lobby retains its original terrazzo floor — cracked along a seismic vein that runs from the ticket booth to the first set of doors — and its original plaster ceiling, a medallion pattern of acanthus leaves and rosettes that were once painted in gold leaf and are now painted in a latex gold that is peeling in long, papery strips, so that the ceiling has the look of something molting. The concession stand is plywood over the original marble counter. Behind the plywood, a mouse has built a nest in the popcorn machine's heating element. It has been there for years. No one has turned the machine on since 2019.

The auditorium seats 620, or did. A third of the seats have been removed to make room for round tables during the gala events, and the removed seats were stacked in the sub-basement and forgotten. The stage is deep for a small-town theatre — Sonnenberg had ambitions — and the fly system above it still holds, in its iron cradle, a painted backdrop of a pastoral scene: a river, willows, a distant white house on a hill. The backdrop has not been lowered in decades. It hangs above the stage like a cataract over an eye: a vision of beauty that no longer transmits.

The orchestra pit was sealed with plywood in 1983, when Roy Harmon, Sr. — Little Roy's father, dead now fifteen years — decided the pit was a liability and ordered it covered. The plywood was painted black. Over the years the paint has chipped and the plywood has bowed, so that a soft, perceptible sag marks the center of what is now a flat extension of the stage floor. When a heavy person walks across it, the plywood flexes and breathes out a smell of damp earth and something older, something mineral and ferric, which anyone who noticed it would attribute to the pipes that run beneath the building's foundation, the original steam heating system that cracked sometime in the 1970s and was abandoned in favor of forced-air units bolted to the roof.

The balcony was condemned in 2014, after a support beam failed during a Rotary Club luncheon and dropped a section of plaster ceiling onto an unoccupied table. A chain and a hand-lettered sign now block the staircase: **NO ENTRY — UNSAFE.** Behind the chain, the staircase ascends into darkness. On the landing, a door leads to the projection booth, which still contains the original carbon-arc projector — a massive thing, nearly six feet tall, obsolete since the 1960s — and, beside it, a folding table on which someone has left a ledger book, a calculator, and a bottle of Wild Turkey with two fingers remaining. The ledger book contains numbers in neat columns, the handwriting alternating between two distinct hands, one looping and one angular. The numbers do not correspond to any business currently operating in Carthage. The bourbon has been there long enough to leave a ring on the table.

These are the rooms of the Avalon. There are others — dressing rooms behind the stage, a utility corridor beneath the auditorium, a boiler room that leads to a sub-basement that leads, in turn, to a crawl space beneath the foundation where the building meets the earth. But these rooms are locked, or sealed, or simply dark in the way that rooms in old buildings are dark when no one has reason to enter them, when the darkness itself has become a kind of occupant.

The Avalon is owned jointly. The deed lists both names — a Delacroix entity and a Harmon entity, 50/50 — and has since 1967, when the original owner's son sold it to pay debts that had more to do with poker than with real estate. The families maintain it to the exact degree that prevents condemnation and not one dollar more. It is the only property in Carthage that bears both names. It is the only place in Carthage where both families conduct business in the same room. The galas happen here. The meetings happen here. The appearances happen here.

People in Carthage call it neutral ground.
