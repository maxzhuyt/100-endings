# The Chorus

## I.

The wall behind him forgot what it was made of.

It happened the way it always did — not a dramatic dissolution, not the theatrical unraveling the recruitment posters warned about, but a quiet lapse, like a word on the tip of a tongue. The concrete went translucent for two seconds, maybe three. Through it he could see the shape of the street, the hulk of a burned-out transit station, a sky that was almost the right color. Then the wall remembered itself, thickened, became opaque and solid and trustworthy again, and Lieutenant Maro Fen pressed his back against it and felt the cold of it through his jacket and breathed.

Three.

He counted them the way he always counted them — not with thought, not with intention, but the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. The inventory was automatic, somatic, older than decision. He pressed inward, down past his own pulse, his own breathing, and found them.

The first was the hum.

It lived behind his sternum, slightly left of center, a vibration so constant he sometimes mistook it for his own heartbeat until he held his breath and felt the two rhythms diverge. The hum was warm and had always been warm, from the moment the transfer was completed — how long ago now, nine months, ten. It was the presence he was most accustomed to. On bad nights, when the patching ran long and reality frayed in ways that registered behind his eyes as a hot pressure, the hum steadied him. He did not know who the hum had been. He knew the hum had been someone. He tried not to lean on it. He leaned on it constantly.

The second was the fist.

That was how he thought of it, though it wasn't anger, exactly. It was pressure — a dense, restless knot that shifted through him, now in his throat, now in his gut, now pressing outward against his ribs like a hand testing the walls of a room it had woken up inside. The fist moved. It had always moved. It did not settle or integrate the way the field manuals said a consumed soul should within six to eight weeks. Three months in, the fist still pushed. Still paced. Fen had told the nurse about it once, early on. She had touched his chest, closed her eyes, listened. *Some of them take longer*, she had said. *Some of them don't stop being themselves as quickly.* She had said it gently, and the gentleness had carried inside it something he decided not to examine.

The third was the silence.

He found it now where he always found it — low, near the base of his spine, so faint it was less a presence than the memory of one. He reached for it the way you reach for a sound you're not sure you heard: holding still, holding breath, turning the whole apparatus of attention toward a single dim point. There. Not gone. Not yet. But quiet in a way that made his hands want to move, made his fingers curl against his thighs, made his jaw tighten. The briefings called it *integration decay*. The soul thinning into the host consciousness, losing its coherence, becoming fuel without signature. The manuals framed it as natural, expected, even desirable — a consumed soul fully integrated was a consumed soul fully available, its energy accessible without resistance or loss. Fen called it dying. He called it that only to himself and only late at night and only after pressing down past the hum and the fist to find the silence and spending whole minutes making sure it was still there, still something, still someone enough to register against the blankness.

Three.

He'd started the week with five.

The defensive action at the lace mill had cost two. Not in combat — the rival cartel's strikers hadn't breached the perimeter — but in the patching itself. The sector had been losing coherence for days, the mill district softening at its edges in ways civilians experienced as déjà vu, as the unsettling sense that a street they'd walked a hundred times was somehow wrong, shorter than it should be, the buildings not quite aligned. By the time Command authorized the patch, the dissolution had gone structural. Walls forgetting their materials. Gravity stuttering. A woman on Finch Street stepping off a curb and falling for two full seconds through a gap in the ground that existed and then didn't and then existed again, her ankle shattering on a surface that was asphalt except where it wasn't.

Patching was simple in principle. The soul-eaters of the lieutenant's unit pushed consumed consciousness outward, into the fraying architecture of the world, and the world fed on it and solidified. Reality was — and this was the part that no amount of field briefings made less grotesque — *hungry*. It ate the eaten. The universe had developed holes, and the holes could be filled only with the specific density of consumed human awareness, and so an economy had arisen around the harvesting and deployment of that awareness, and Fen was a mid-level operative in that economy, a man with people inside him who existed in part so that those people could be pushed outward and spent, burned, fed to the world's insatiable need to remain real.

Two souls consumed in the patching. He had felt them go. That was the part the manuals understated, the part the veterans drank to forget. Consumption during combat or patching wasn't like spending currency. It was like — and he had never found the right comparison, because the right comparison would require an experience no human was designed to have — it was like holding someone's hand and feeling their grip weaken and knowing they were falling and feeling the exact moment their fingers left yours, except the falling was not away from you but *through* you, outward, into the world, and for one vertiginous instant you felt what they felt, the consumed soul's last flare of coherent experience before it became structure, became load-bearing reality, became a street that held its shape, a wall that remembered it was concrete, a sky that displayed the correct gradient of blue.

The two he'd lost at the mill had gone fast. The first — a soul he'd carried for six weeks, a low melodic presence that pooled in his hands like warmth — had flared and dispersed in under a second, and the sector had stabilized twenty percent. The second — newer, sharper, a presence that tasted of copper when it surged — had taken longer, had resisted, and Fen had felt the resistance as a tearing in his diaphragm, a wrenching wrongness, because the soul did not want to be spent and he had spent it anyway and the sector had stabilized fully and he had stood in the restored street afterward with his hands shaking and the taste of copper filling his mouth and two absences inside him where people had been.

The sky above him now held its color. It held it too long, a beat past natural, the blue persisting through what should have been a shift toward the white-gray of the afternoon overcast. Then it corrected. The clouds arrived. The light changed. He watched it happen and felt the hum in his chest vibrate faintly — responding to the world's minor lapses the way a tuning fork responds to a struck note.

He should eat. He should report. He should check in with the rest of the unit, who were scattered through the district running perimeter verification, each of them carrying their own choruses, their own consumed presences, their own private inventories conducted by fingertip and breath. Instead he sat against the wall and counted. Three. One humming. One pressing. One silent.

He stayed until the wall behind him flickered again, a lapse so brief it was more like a blink than a failure, and he felt the cold disappear from his back for an instant, the concrete losing its memory of temperature before remembering, solidifying, pressing cold against him again. The hum in his chest rose slightly. The fist shifted. The silence remained.

Fen stood. His knees ached. His body felt heavier than its weight, which was the soul-eater's constant companion — the sense of carrying more than mass, of being inhabited, of housing a population. He walked toward the district's east checkpoint, where the nurse's team had set up the hospice, and with every step the three inside him moved: humming, pressing, silent. Three people who were not him. Three people who were only him. Three people riding the animal of his body through a world that would eat them if given the chance.

He counted them again at the checkpoint gate. He would count them again on the stairs. He would count them in his sleep.

## II.

The hospice had been a school. You could tell by the proportions of the rooms — ceilings too high for the cots that lined them, doorways wide enough for crowds that no longer came, a smell beneath the antiseptic that was chalk dust and floor wax and the particular institutional warmth of a building designed to hold many small bodies in rows. The nurse had chosen it for its east-facing windows. Light mattered in extractions. Not physically — the process had no photosensitive component — but psychologically, for the donor. The nurse had explained this to Fen once, early in his assignment, in the careful, patient tone she used for things she considered non-negotiable. *They should be able to see the sky. They should know what time of day it is. They should be in the world when they leave it.*

Her name was Diel Rassa and she was fifty-three and she had been doing this for eleven years and she carried no souls.

That was the fact about her that new transfers always stumbled over. A nurse — a harvester — who didn't carry. Who took the consumed consciousness from willing donors and passed it directly to others, never holding it, never integrating, keeping herself empty. The field manuals didn't have a protocol for it. Command didn't officially approve. But the nurse's extraction success rate was the highest in the sector, her donors reported less pain, and the souls she harvested arrived in their carriers more coherent, more intact, more *themselves* than anyone could account for. So Command looked away, and Diel Rassa continued to touch dying people and take what they offered and give it to soldiers and carry nothing inside her except what she'd carried before any of this began.

Fen found her on the third floor, east wing, in a room with four windows and one cot and one dying man.

She was sitting beside the cot. Not standing, not positioned in clinical authority over the body, but *sitting*, on a low stool she carried from room to room, so that her eyes were level with the donor's eyes. Her hands were in her lap. She was listening.

The donor was old. Fen could see that from the doorway — the white hair, the spotted hands on the blanket, the chest rising and falling in the stuttered rhythm of lungs that had been failing for a long time. The disease was called the dimming, though the medical term was longer, and it was endemic to sectors where reality had been unstable for extended periods. Something about prolonged exposure to ontological inconsistency broke the body down. Cells forgot their instructions. Organs lost coherence. You faded. You went translucent in the way the walls did, except slower, and with more pain, and with the knowledge that unlike the walls, no one was going to patch you back.

The dimming was terminal. Everyone in this wing was terminal. That was the architecture of the nurse's ethics, and it was the load-bearing wall of everything Fen had built his loyalty on: *the donors were dying already*. They were going to die. The consumed consciousness that would be extracted from them would otherwise dissipate — lost, wasted, unrecoverable. What Diel Rassa did was not kill. It was collect. It was redirect. It was — and this was the word the nurse used, and she used it without irony — *salvage*.

Fen waited in the doorway. The nurse did not acknowledge him. She was in the preparatory phase, which could last minutes or hours and which could not be rushed, because Diel Rassa would not take from someone who had not spoken and been heard.

The donor's name was Cael. Fen had read the intake report. Seventy-one years old. Former lineman for the municipal power grid, back when the grid had been physical infrastructure rather than soul-sustained projection. Wife, dead. Son, dead — not of the dimming, but of the last cartel incursion, a striker who had been consumed without consent, his soul ripped out and fed to reality by people who did not sit with their donors, did not ask, did not listen. Cael had volunteered three days ago. He had come to the hospice on his own feet, signed the consent forms, sat through the orientation, and asked only one question: *Will it hurt the person who carries me?*

The nurse had answered him honestly. Fen knew this because the nurse always answered honestly, and the honest answer was *yes*, and the honest answer was also *not the way you think*, and the honest answer was also *I will do everything I can to make you coherent enough that the carrying is bearable for both of you*, and the honest answer was also *I don't fully know*. Cael had nodded. He had signed.

Now the nurse was listening to him talk. His voice carried to the doorway in fragments — not a continuous narrative but the scattered, circling speech of a person who has been given permission to say what remains and is discovering that what remains does not organize itself into story. Fen caught pieces. The wife's name. A street he had lived on. The feeling of climbing a power pole in winter, the city spread out below, the particular quality of vertigo that comes not from height but from the sudden understanding that the city depends on you, personally, your hands, your weight on the pole, your willingness to be cold and high and necessary.

The nurse listened with her whole body. This was not metaphor. Fen had watched her work dozens of times and the thing that struck him every time was the *physicality* of her attention — the way she leaned forward, the way her hands opened in her lap, the way her breathing synchronized with the donor's as if she were learning their rhythm before she entered it. It looked like love. It looked like the most profound clinical competence he had ever witnessed. It looked like both of these things, and the fact that it was both was the thing that made Fen trust her and the thing that, in moments he did not allow himself to dwell on, made him uneasy in a place he could not point to on a diagram.

Cael finished speaking. Or did not finish — simply arrived at a silence that was not empty but full, the silence of a man who has put down a weight he has been carrying. The nurse let the silence be. She did not fill it. Ten seconds. Twenty. The room held its light. The windows showed a sky that was, for the moment, behaving.

Then the nurse said, "I'm going to touch your chest now. You'll feel warmth first. Then pressure. I'll talk to you the whole time. You tell me to stop and I stop. You are not committed beyond this moment. Every moment is a new choice. Do you understand?"

Cael said, "I understand."

"Are you afraid?"

A pause. "Some."

"That's correct," the nurse said. "That's the right amount." She did not say *Don't be afraid.* She never said that. She had told Fen once that the most violent thing a harvester could do was deny a donor their fear, because the fear was proof that something real was being taken, and to paper over it with comfort was to begin the process of pretending that consumption was not consumption.

She placed her right hand flat against Cael's chest.

Fen had watched this dozens of times and it never became routine. The physical mechanics were simple — contact, focus, the slow exertion of the harvester's awareness into the donor's failing consciousness, the identification of the coherent core that would survive extraction, the careful separation of that core from the biological substrate that could no longer sustain it. In training, they taught it as a technical procedure. In the nurse's hands, it was none of those things.

It was, in Fen's experience, most like watching someone pray. Not the rote recitation of learned words but the other kind of prayer — the raw, stripped-down, terrified kind, the opening of oneself to something that might not answer, the willingness to be changed by the asking.

The nurse closed her eyes. Cael's breath shifted. Fen saw it — the slight deepening, the slight hitch, the moment the body registered that something was reaching into it with intention. The dying man's hands tightened on the blanket. Then released. Then tightened again.

"I'm here," the nurse said. "I'm with you. I can feel the pain in your lungs. I'm going to move past it. You'll feel me move past it."
