# The Storage Unit

## I.

The plan was simple. Mara said it one more time, for everyone, leaning between the front seats of the idling Civic with the overhead light off and a printout of the facility map resting on the center console.

"Unit 2217. Second floor, east corridor. The freight elevator's faster but it's on camera, so we take the stairs at the south end. There's a blind spot between the stairwell door and the first turn — about forty feet. After that turn the corridor cameras don't resume until you're past 2230, which means we've got almost the full length of the hall unrecorded. Roy opens it up, we go in, we find the journals, we leave. Out by four at the latest. The cleanout crew is scheduled for seven."

Roy Aguilar, in the passenger seat, was cleaning beneath his fingernails with a flattened paperclip. He was fifty-three, thick-shouldered, and wore a fleece vest over a flannel shirt, as if he'd been called away from a fishing trip. He had been recommended through a friend of a friend with the understanding that he did not ask questions about the situations behind locks. He had not asked questions. He had quoted a price — eight hundred cash — and shown up on time, and Mara had respected both of those things.

In the back seat, Dani Reeves sat with her knee pressed against a nylon duffel bag containing packing tape, bubble wrap, two flashlights, nitrile gloves, and a collapsed banker's box. Dani was the only person in the car Mara actually knew. They had been close once, in their twenties, during the years Mara sometimes thought of as the loud years — bartending, bad apartments, the particular bond of women who are broke together. Dani had a practical streak that Mara had always relied on and that Dani, reliably, resented being relied on for. She'd agreed to come tonight as a favor, the kind of favor that uses up the last of something.

"And if there's more than journals?" Dani asked. "If it's a whole unit full of his crap?"

"I know what her handwriting looks like."

"That's not what I asked."

"We're not taking anything else. We go in, I find the composition books, we leave. That's it."

"You keep saying 'that's it' like you're trying to convince yourself."

Roy shifted in his seat. "Ladies."

"It's fine," Mara said. She folded the map and put it in her jacket pocket. The jacket was dark, lightweight, chosen for this. Her hair was tied back. She wore no rings, no watch, nothing that could catch light or snag. She had read somewhere that burglars dressed to move, not to hide, and the sentence had stayed with her — its calm professionalism, its implication that a crime was just a task performed under abnormal constraints.

She looked at the facility through the windshield. EZ-STOR, the sign said, the letters backlit in a sickly orange that made the whole façade look jaundiced. The parking lot was vast, mostly empty, bisected by speed bumps no one had ever needed to obey. A chain-link fence topped with privacy slats ran the perimeter. Beyond it, the building itself: three stories of corrugated metal, the color of a primer coat on a car that was never finished. Everything about the place was designed to be forgettable — a building for objects whose owners couldn't decide whether to keep them or throw them away.

"Tell me about the gate code again," Roy said.

"4-0-7-1. His birthday, reversed. He uses it for everything. He used it for the garage door when I was a kid. He used it for his voicemail, his ATM. He's not a creative man."

Roy nodded. He did not ask how she knew her estranged father's PIN codes. He was earning his eight hundred.

The dashboard clock read 1:47 a.m. April 14th. She'd been forty years old for one hour and forty-seven minutes. Dani had texted *happy birthday* at midnight, from the apartment where she'd been waiting to be picked up for this, and the text had sat on Mara's phone like a small wrong note in an otherwise well-rehearsed piece of music. She hadn't responded because she couldn't figure out the right register. *Thanks* was too casual. Anything else was an acknowledgment that this night was something other than operational, and she needed it to stay operational.

The restraining order had been issued in 2006, renewed in 2009, renewed again in 2012, and then left to lapse — not because anyone felt safe, but because Mara's mother had died, and the order had been in her mother's name, and renewing it posthumously required a kind of legal energy that no one in the family had left. By then Mara was in her late twenties and fully capable of maintaining her own perimeter. She hadn't spoken to her father in eighteen years. The restraining order had been the structure, but the silence was hers, and she maintained it the way she imagined he maintained nothing: with vigilance, with intent.

Her mother's journals — six or seven composition books, college-ruled, with green marbled covers — had been in the house when her mother died. By the time the estate was sorted, they were gone. Her aunt believed Don had taken them during the three-day window between the death and the lock change, when the house was porous with grief, when anyone could walk in and claim to be helping. Mara believed it because it fit. He had always needed to control the record, to own the version of events. The journals were her mother's real voice — unperformed, unmediated — and he could not allow that voice to exist outside his custody. So he'd taken them. And he'd put them here, in this unit, along with whatever else he'd hoarded over the decades, and in the morning a company called CleanStar LLC was going to back a truck up to the bay and throw all of it away, because the rent was three months past due and the lien had been filed and the law, in this case, was on the side of the landlord.

She had learned all of this from the facility's office manager, a woman named Trish, who had been willing to share a surprising amount of information over the phone when Mara had identified herself as Don's daughter and expressed concern about family heirlooms. Trish had been sympathetic. Trish had said, "Honey, you've got until morning." Trish had not meant it as an invitation to commit burglary, but language, Mara had found, was generous that way.

"Alright," Mara said. She looked at the clock one more time. "Let's go."

They got out of the car. The air was cool, thin, carrying the faint diesel musk of the interstate a quarter mile south. The lot's floodlights made everything shadowless and overexposed, like a crime scene already documented. Mara punched 4-0-7-1 into the keypad beside the pedestrian gate. It beeped, clicked, opened. No alarm, no alert.

Roy picked up his tool bag. Dani shouldered the duffel. They walked single file toward the south stairwell entrance, and Mara led them because she had memorized the route, and because this had been hers — this plan, this anger, this night — since long before she had hired anyone to help her carry it out.

## II.

The stairwell smelled like concrete dust and the faintly sweet chemical odor of rodent bait. Their footsteps echoed in a way that made the building sound hollow, as if the whole structure were an empty shell rather than a grid of small compartments holding the sorted and unsorted residue of other people's lives. Mara counted doors. The second-floor landing had a fire door with a push bar, and she opened it slowly, checking the corridor.

Fluorescent tubes overhead, half of them buzzing, a few dark. The hallway was long, wide enough for a furniture dolly, lined with orange roll-up doors, each one identical, each one numbered in stenciled white paint. The floor was polished concrete. There was no sound except the hum of the lights and, somewhere, the low cycling of climate control.

They turned the corner. No cameras, just as she'd mapped it. The corridor stretched ahead of them, and the unit numbers climbed: 2210, 2212, 2214. Odd numbers on the left, even on the right.

2217 was on the left. They stopped in front of it.

Roy set his bag down and knelt. He studied the lock — a disc padlock, circular, stainless steel — and the hasp it hung from, which was bolted through the door frame with carriage bolts. He didn't touch it immediately. He leaned in, tilted his head, then leaned back.

"Huh," he said.

"What?"

"This is an Abloy. PL 362."

"Is that a problem?"

"It's not a problem, it's a choice. This is a three-hundred-dollar lock. On a storage unit." He looked up at her. "These are high-security. Pick-resistant, drill-resistant. Most units, you get a Master Lock, maybe a disc lock from the hardware store. This is what you put on a commercial property. Or a gun safe."

"Can you open it?"

"I can open it. It's just going to take longer than I quoted you for." He was already unrolling his kit — a cloth sleeve containing picks, tension wrenches, a set of tubular tools, and what looked like a small electric hand drill with a specialized bit. "Fifteen, twenty minutes."

"Fine."

Roy went to work. His hands were steady and unhurried. He began with a pick and tension wrench, exploring the keyway of the lock by feel, his head cocked to one side like a man listening to a conversation in the next room. Mara watched his fingers. They were blunt, calloused, precise. He worked with the unselfconscious competence of someone who had long ago merged his identity with his skill, and she found it calming in the way she had always found competence calming — it was a refuge from the world of feelings, where nothing ever worked on the first try.

Dani leaned against the opposite wall, arms crossed, chewing gum. She hadn't put on her gloves yet, and Mara didn't remind her, because the reminder would start a conversation she didn't want to have.

The corridor was absolutely still. The building at 2 a.m. was a mausoleum of possessions, a place where objects went when they were too meaningful to discard and too painful to keep close. Mara had never rented a storage unit herself. She threw things away. She had always thrown things away. After her mother died, she had kept nothing — no clothes, no jewelry, no kitchenware. She had wanted the journals and only the journals, because the journals were not sentimental objects but *evidence*: her mother's interior life, recorded in her own hand, immune to revision. Everything else could be replaced or forgotten. The journals could not, because her mother had written them in the last years of the marriage, and whatever was in them — Mara had never read them, had only seen them on the nightstand, had been told about them by her aunt — constituted the only first-person testimony of what that marriage had actually been.

Roy switched tools. He set the pick down and selected a thin, hooked implement that he inserted with the delicacy of a surgeon. Mara watched the lock and tried not to think about the fact that the lock was not what she'd expected. She had imagined a Master Lock, brass, the kind you buy at the hardware store in a blister pack. The kind that signals: I'm satisfying a requirement, nothing more. The Abloy said something different. It said: what is inside this unit matters to the person who locked it.

She set the thought aside. It did not fit the model, so she set it aside.

Minutes passed. Roy muttered to himself, withdrew the tool, reinserted it at a different angle. The silence of the corridor magnified every small sound — the click of metal on metal, the creak of Roy's knees, Dani's gum. Mara became aware of her own breathing, her own heartbeat. She had planned this for eleven weeks. She had located the unit. She had identified the cleanout date. She had hired Roy. She had rehearsed the route. She had done everything right, and now she was standing in a corridor inside a crime, and the lock was resisting, and in the resistance she felt the first faint pressure of something she refused to name.

It was not doubt. Doubt implied uncertainty about the justice of what she was doing, and she was not uncertain. She was retrieving her dead mother's journals from a man who had stolen them. The restraining order had been real. The reason for the restraining order had been real. She was twelve years old the night the police came, and she remembered the specific weight of the officer's hand on her shoulder as he walked her to the cruiser, and the way her mother's face looked in the porch light — not crying, not screaming, just *absent*, as if she had left her own body behind as a placeholder while the real her went somewhere safer. That was real. That had happened. And whatever this lock was or wasn't, whatever it cost or didn't cost, it did not change the foundational facts of her life.

Roy grunted softly. Something inside the lock gave way — a faint, dense click, more felt than heard, the sound of precision components yielding to superior precision. He turned the cylinder. The shackle released.

He pulled the lock free and held it up, almost admiring it, then placed it on the ground beside his bag.

"There you go," he said.

Mara stepped forward. She gripped the handle at the bottom of the rolling door. The metal was cold, and the cold traveled up through her hand and into her wrist, and she thought, absurdly, of the phrase *point of no return*, and how it was an aviation term — the point in a flight after which you no longer have enough fuel to go back to your origin — and how the metaphor had always been wrong for human decisions, because humans always had enough fuel to go back, they simply chose not to.

She pulled the door up.

## III.

The first thing was the smell.

Not rot, not mildew, not the stale breath of a sealed room. It smelled clean. Faintly cedar. The particulate-free stillness of climate-controlled air, like the inside of a museum archive or a temperature-regulated wine cellar. She reached inside and found a light switch where a light switch would be in a room that someone used regularly, and when she flipped it, two overhead LED panels came on — not the fluorescent tubes of the corridor but newer fixtures, warmer, intentionally installed.

The unit was ten by fifteen. She had expected chaos — boxes piled haphazardly, furniture draped in moving blankets, the compressed geological layers of a life's accumulated junk, unsorted, decaying, forgotten. The narrative had always been: he threw things in here and stopped caring.

The unit was not that.

Along the left wall, a set of steel shelving units — the good kind, powder-coated, adjustable, the kind you buy from a restaurant supply company, not the kind that comes flat-packed from a big-box store. On the shelving, arranged with a precision that made Mara's stomach tighten before her mind could explain why, were packages. Wrapped. In paper. Each one a different size, each one bearing a small white tag affixed with string.

She stepped inside. Dani came in behind her and stopped.

"What the hell," Dani said.

Mara moved toward the shelving. The packages were wrapped neatly — not beautifully, not with the practiced flair of someone who enjoyed wrapping, but with the careful, slightly overlapping folds of someone who had watched a tutorial and followed it faithfully. Some were in solid-colored paper: blue, green, dark red. Others in patterned paper that changed over the years — she could see the difference between wrapping paper from the early nineties and wrapping paper from last year in the way you can date wallpaper by its confidence. The tags were handwritten. The handwriting was her father's — she recognized it instantly, the way you recognize a voice you haven't heard in decades, the recognition arriving in the body before the mind, a spike of something electric and nauseating in the base of her throat.

The first tag read: **Mara — Age 1. 1984.**

The second: **Mara — Age 2. 1985.**
