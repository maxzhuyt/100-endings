# The Screening

## I.

The first donor of the morning was a liar, which was not unusual.

His name was Gerald Kemper, forty-six, and his intake form was immaculate. No recent travel outside the continental United States. No new sexual partners in the past twelve months. No history of intravenous drug use. His handwriting was small and careful, the letters pressed hard into the form as though permanence might make the answers more true.

"Looks good, Mr. Kemper," she said, scanning the page without reading it. "Just a few follow-up questions."

She didn't need the follow-up questions. She'd known what he was the moment he sat down, the way you know what's in a room when someone opens the door and a smell hits you. His mind opened the same way — involuntarily, indifferently, the way all minds opened to her. Not like a book. Books were organized. More like a bag spilled on a floor.

Gerald Kemper had been seeing sex workers for eleven years. Not sporadically, not in the shamefaced and episodic way of men who considered it a vice. Routinely. Budgeted. There was a woman he visited on the second and fourth Thursdays in a row house on Delancey whose front door stuck in humid weather. He was thinking, right now, as he sat in the blue vinyl chair with his sleeve already rolled, about the particular shade of the wallpaper in her upstairs room — a faded floral print, roses gone the color of old tea — and the way the window unit dripped condensation onto the sill in a rhythm he found soothing. He associated the sound with uncomplicated release. His wife believed he played in a poker league on those nights. He had fabricated the other players over the years until they'd become almost real to him: a Danny who bluffed too much, a Saul who drank Maker's Mark and talked about his boat. He was proud of the details. He'd given Danny a limp. Gerald Kemper organized his lies the way a careful man organizes his finances, and in his own estimation, he had never hurt anyone.

The sex had been unprotected eight of the last eleven times. He knew this was reckless but had constructed a logic — she was careful, she was clean, you could tell by looking — that allowed him to sustain the risk as a managed one. He did not actually believe this logic. Beneath it was the flat, familiar resignation of a man who had decided his own comfort was worth more than the math. He did not think about the recipients of his blood. He did not, in any practical sense, think about other people as entities who continued to exist when he left the room.

"Mr. Kemper, I'm seeing a discrepancy on line fourteen. You've marked 'no' for new partners, but the definition includes any contact in the past three months. Could you double-check that for me?"

He double-checked. He confirmed. His pen hovered, and inside his skull, the wallpaper roses bloomed and the window unit dripped. She watched him lie to her face with his expression composed in the mild, cooperative openness of a man at a routine appointment, and she marked the form.

"We're going to have to defer you today, Mr. Kemper. Just a procedural hold — you'll get a letter explaining the timeline for reapplication."

He protested. They always protested. Not vigorously — that would imply there was something to hide. Mildly, with the practiced confusion of someone who understood that a certain quantity of pushback was itself a performance of innocence. She gave him the number for donor services. He took the card. He left. In the hallway, his mind was already shifting to something else — a sandwich, parking validation, the slight ache in his left knee that he kept meaning to have looked at. She was gone from his thoughts before he reached the elevator.

She dry-swallowed two ibuprofen and called the next name.

Rhonda Patton, twenty-nine. Track marks on her left arm concealed beneath a long-sleeved thermal shirt in late June. Her form said no to intravenous drug use, and her mind said she'd shot up three days ago in a bathroom at the Greyhound station with a needle she'd shared with a man whose name she either didn't know or had decided not to retain. Her thoughts were fast and scratchy, a scoured quality to them, like a radio caught between stations. She was here because she needed the fifteen-dollar gift card the blood bank offered as a donor incentive. She was already spending it — her mind flicked to a bodega cooler, a shrink-wrapped sandwich, a specific brand of menthol cigarettes. She was not thinking about the needle or the bathroom or the man. She was thinking about lunch.

Discrepancy on line nine. Deferral. Donor services number. Next.

---

Tyler Oshiro, twenty, a sophomore at the university, walk-in, first-time donor. His form was honest. His mind confirmed it: no drugs, no risk contacts, one sexual partner — a girlfriend, condoms every time, he was almost pedantic about it. A careful kid. Good kid. She almost waved him through.

But. There it was, lodged in the undifferentiated murk below his active thoughts, in the place where memories go when they're not important enough to organize but too specific to dissolve entirely. Spring break, senior year of high school. A beach house. Alcohol. A girl — not the girlfriend, someone before — and a condom that broke, or was never used, depending on which layer of the memory she read. Tyler Oshiro didn't remember this clearly. He'd been drunk enough that the event existed in his mind not as a narrative but as a series of disconnected sensory fragments — sand, the smell of sunscreen and warm beer, a sliding glass door that someone had left open so the ocean was audible. He had no reason to believe anything had come of it. Statistically, probably nothing had. But the exposure window was there, and the antibody panel wouldn't catch what the antibody panel wasn't designed to catch, and she'd learned a long time ago that "probably nothing" was a phrase she couldn't afford.

Line six. Deferral. Card. Next.

---

Margaret Huang, fifty-three, repeat donor, ninth visit. Amlodipine for blood pressure, undisclosed. Deferral.

Next.

She ate a granola bar from the bottom of her bag. It was stale. The wrapper crinkled under fluorescent light that had been buzzing at a specific, persistent frequency for three months. She'd put in a maintenance request twice. The second time, someone had come, looked at the fixture, and said it was functioning within normal parameters.

She'd been doing this for eight years.

Eight years of bag-spill minds, of petty deceptions and large ones, of people who lied fluently and people who lied badly and people — rarer, but they existed — who told the complete truth and sat in the chair with their veins open and their histories clean and nothing in their skulls but vague goodwill and a mild curiosity about whether the needle would hurt. Eight years of reading the inside of the human animal and finding, with remarkable consistency, the same residue: fear, self-interest, compartmentalization, the small masticated lies people swallow so continuously they forget they're chewing.

She didn't hate people. That was a question she'd been asked, once, by the only person who knew what she could do — Dr. Kessler, the hematologist who'd hired her, who'd set up this arrangement, who checked in quarterly with the same careful, evaluative look he gave specimens under magnification. *Does it make you hate them?* No. Hate required a kind of emotional investment she'd burned through in the first eighteen months. What she felt was closer to what a sewer inspector might feel about effluent: a professional dispassion rooted in overfamiliarity. People were not good or bad. People were *porous*. Full of contaminants they didn't disclose because they'd stopped recognizing them as contaminants. She was a filter. Filters didn't hate what they caught.

This was the story she told herself, anyway, and she had told it enough times that it had acquired the functional weight of truth, the way Gerald Kemper's poker friends had acquired limps and bourbon preferences.

The telepathy had come when she was nine. She knew some people would have called it a gift — that there were entire narrative genres built on the premise that reading minds was a superpower, a blessing, a cosmic selection. She'd thought that herself, briefly, in the early months, when the fever broke and the world came back wrong and she could hear her mother's thoughts like a second voice layered under the first. She'd been sick — very sick, something the doctors couldn't fully explain, a viral encephalitis that should have killed her or at least left deficits, and instead left *this*. She didn't think about it anymore. It had happened. She'd adapted. The way you adapt to a bad knee or a missing finger — you adjust your grip on things and after enough time you forget there was ever a different way to hold them.

Her pager buzzed. She ignored it. The granola bar tasted like cardboard and flax.

Her pager buzzed again.

## II.

The bus had been a 34 crosstown, the driver a twenty-six-year veteran of the transit authority who had, in the relevant fraction of a second, glanced at a notification on his phone. The bus had drifted into the oncoming lane at the Ninth Street intersection and struck a delivery van head-on, which had then spun into a line of parked cars, which had then — in the cascading mechanical logic of urban disaster — created a chain of impacts involving a total of nine vehicles, a city fire hydrant, and the plate-glass storefront of a dry cleaner whose owner would later be found dead of a cardiac event that may or may not have been caused by the fright of a Buick LeSabre entering his shop at forty miles an hour. Forty-one people were injured. Eleven critically. The call went out at 10:47 a.m.

She heard the ER shift before she understood it. The sound came first — a change in the building's ambient frequency, the way a hive changes pitch when the queen moves. Then the minds. They hit her in layers, a thermal wall of cognition expanding through the hospital's corridors as staff received pages, checked phones, began to move. Urgency had a specific psychic texture, different from panic, different from fear. It was *narrowing*. Dozens of minds simultaneously contracting to the same point of focus, all the background noise of personal worry and boredom and hunger dropping away, replaced by a single operational question: *what do I do right now.*

She was already standing when Dr. Werner appeared in the doorway of her screening partition. Werner was the charge nurse coordinator, a woman whose mind ran like a logistics program — clean, sequential, devoid of sentiment during working hours.

"MCI, Ninth and Broad. We're at capacity in seven minutes. I need you downstairs."

"I'm mid-intake."

"Intake's suspended. We need bodies."

Werner was gone. In the hallway, an orderly was running. Running in a hospital. She'd been here long enough to know what that meant about the severity.

She took the stairs because the elevator would be commandeered for gurneys.

The ER was already transforming when she reached it. The ordinary architecture of the department — the partitioned bays, the triage desk, the curtained areas where people waited with their mundane emergencies — was being overwritten by the logic of mass casualty. Gurneys were being repositioned. An anesthesiologist she recognized only by his walk was cutting through the waiting room, barking orders to a resident who was carrying four units of something in a cooler and trying not to collide with a woman in a neck brace who was sitting on the floor because there were no chairs left. The PA system was producing a continuous stream of names, codes, and room assignments that layered over each other until the words became tonal rather than semantic — just *sound*, authoritative and relentless, the hospital speaking in tongues.

She opened herself up wider than she normally allowed, and the minds poured in.

— *femoral bleed, clamp's not holding, where's the —*
*— my daughter was on that bus, my daughter, she's eleven, she has a blue —*
*— type and cross, type and cross, who ordered the type and cross on bay —*
*— can't feel my legs, I can't feel my, please, I can't —*
*— bag three's expired, pull it, pull it now —*
*— oh god oh god oh god oh —*

She walked through it. She had learned, years ago, to walk through it. You didn't fight the current. You didn't try to shut it out, because shutting it out required effort and effort cost focus and focus was the only thing that kept you from drowning. You let it wash through you and you grabbed the pieces you needed and you let the rest run off, like standing in a river and catching specific fish with your hands while the water just kept coming.

But this was not a river. This was a flood.

She caught the name Amara Williams the way you catch a ball thrown at your face — reflexively, painfully. Six years old. Thirty-eight pounds. Seat 14B on the 34 crosstown, window side, because her grandmother had let her pick and she liked to watch. The window had become shrapnel. The shrapnel had found her abdomen. She'd been extracted by a firefighter who, in his own mind, was replaying the moment he'd cut the seatbelt and the child had made a sound — not a scream, a sound *lower* than a scream, as though the pain were too large for a six-year-old's vocal register and had been transposed down into something almost adult — and he would hear that sound in three weeks, in four months, in the particular silence of 3 a.m. for the rest of his career.

Amara Williams. AB-negative. The phrase hit the air of the blood bank like a stone hitting water: *AB-negative.* Less than one percent of the population. The hospital's reserve was two units. She needed at least four. They'd used one already. Compatible donors in the system were being paged but the nearest was forty minutes out and one of them wasn't answering and the situation was — the situation was that a six-year-old girl was losing blood faster than they could give it and the math was narrowing to a point from which it would not widen again.

"We've got a walk-in board," someone said. "Anyone who matches, screen them and send them through."

She was the screen.

Werner found her again in the hallway outside the blood bank. "You're clearing donors. Anyone who's AB-neg or compatible, you process them. Fast."

"How fast?"

"The child has maybe twenty minutes."

Werner's mind, behind the logistics, held a single image she was trying not to look at: her own daughter, age four, in a bathtub, laughing. It was involuntary. It was the kind of association that happens when the professional mind briefly fails to outrun the personal one. Werner caught herself, buried it, moved on. But the image lingered in the space between them like the afterimage of a camera flash, and the protagonist — whose name was June, though she rarely thought of herself by name during working hours, having reduced herself through long practice to a function — June felt it land and let it pass through her and did not hold it. Holding things was how you broke.

The walk-in donors were already arriving. Word had gone out through the usual channels — social media, local news, the strange civic reflex that compelled people to drive to hospitals after disasters with a vague intention of helping. Most of them would be the wrong type. Some would be deferred for the usual reasons. She set up in an alcove near the blood bank entrance with a stack of intake forms and a box of gloves and began processing.

The first three were wrong — O-positive, A-positive, O-positive. The fourth was on beta-blockers she could read but he chose not to disclose. The fifth was clean but B-positive. The sixth was a woman whose thoughts were so full of images from the accident scene she'd driven past — the bus canted on its side like a beached thing, the spray from the hydrant catching light — that June had to push through the noise to find the medical history underneath.
