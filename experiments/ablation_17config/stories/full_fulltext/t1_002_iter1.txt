**AUDIO DIARY — ENTRY 001**
**DATE:** September 4, 1987
**RECORDED BY:** Margaret Calloway, CSR #4217

Okay. Testing, testing. This is—well, I guess this is an audio diary, which feels ridiculous to say out loud, but Dr. Rosen said it would help with the tension headaches if I "externalized the day's residue" instead of carrying it in my jaw. Her words. Forty dollars an hour for the phrase "externalized the day's residue." I told her I already spend eight hours a day converting other people's words into written record, and she said that was exactly the problem, that the words I transcribe aren't *mine,* and I need a space where the language belongs to me. So. Here we are.

My name is Margaret Calloway. Peggy to most people. Certified shorthand reporter, State of Texas, license renewed 2/86, next renewal 2/88. Typing speed 260 words per minute on the Stenograph™ Stentura 8000, which I purchased used from Dolores Wicker when she retired in '84 and which has a sticky S-key that I've learned to compensate for with a half-beat hesitation that no one has ever noticed in thirteen years of certified transcripts. I'm forty-one. I have a daughter, Claire, who is nine and plays left wing on the Sutton County Gators U-10 girls' soccer team, and a cat named Biscuit who takes half a thyroid pill crushed into wet food every morning and will not eat it if the pill fragments are larger than a lentil, which means I spend the first four minutes of every day with a butter knife and a cutting board performing what is essentially pharmaceutical surgery on a tablet the size of a peppercorn.

I'm telling you all of this, tape recorder, because Dr. Rosen said to start with the facts of my life. Ground yourself, she said. Okay. I'm grounded.

Now: the case.

I've been assigned to *State of Texas v. Eleanor Vance,* Case No. 87-CR-4401, Judge Harold R. Montrose presiding, Courtroom B, Sutton County Courthouse. Eleanor Vance is charged with two counts of aggravated assault on a minor, one count of criminal endangerment, and one count of what the indictment calls "ritualistic abuse of a child," which is a charge I have typed exactly four times in my career, all in the last three years, and all of which have resulted in convictions based on testimony that would not pass muster in a middle school debate club.

I'm editorializing. I shouldn't editorialize. That's not what I do. I am the record. The record does not have opinions.

But off the record—this record, the one that's mine—it's another satanic panic mess. I read the pretrial motions over my lunch break. Turkey on rye, no tomato, from the sandwich cart that parks outside the courthouse on Tuesdays and Thursdays. The state's theory is that Eleanor Vance, a fifty-three-year-old piano teacher who has lived in Sutton County her entire life, performed "occult rituals" on two children in her studio during lessons. The children's testimony was obtained through interview techniques that—

No. I'm not going to do this. This is not my deposition. This is my audio diary. Dr. Rosen said to note how I feel.

I feel annoyed. I feel like I'll be spending the next three to five weeks in Courtroom B typing nonsense about pentagrams and black candles while the prosecution's expert witness, who has a mail-order degree in "ritual crime analysis," explains to twelve people who have never left this county that Satan is real and he lives in a piano teacher's house.

The parking lot had a pothole the size of a dinner plate near the south entrance. I hit it pulling in this morning and spilled half my coffee on my steno case. That seems like a more relevant event than anything else I've described.

Claire has a game Saturday. I need to wash her jersey.

End of entry.

**AUDIO DIARY — ENTRY 002**
**DATE:** September 8, 1987

Well, that was Day 1.

Eleanor Vance looks like someone's librarian. That's the first thing I'd say, if anyone were asking me, which no one is, because the record does not describe. The record captures. But she looks like someone's librarian. White hair, neat, in a bun. Thin wrists. She was wearing a blue cardigan that looked hand-knitted. She sat with her hands folded for the first seven minutes of proceedings, perfectly composed, and I thought, this is going to be one of the quiet ones. Some of these defendants barely speak at all. They just sit there absorbing it.

Then she stood up.

The outburst itself was—I've heard them before. You get defendants in criminal cases who can't contain it, who break under the pressure of hearing their own life converted into legal argument. It's not unusual. I've typed "defendant becomes agitated" probably two hundred times. I've typed "unintelligible" more times than I've typed "sustained."

But I noted it on the record as "vocalizing, unintelligible, duration approximately forty seconds" because that's what it was. Technically. It wasn't screaming. It wasn't English. It was a sustained vocalization on what sounded like three or four repeating phonetic units, guttural, with a rhythmic structure that made it sound more like chanting than—

No. It sounded like a woman having a psychotic episode. That's what it sounded like. That's what it was. She was worked up, she broke, she made noises that a distressed person makes, and the bailiff took her out.

I did my job. I recorded it. I noted the duration. Judge Montrose called a thirty-minute recess and we resumed with the defendant sedated and Mr. Greer looking like a man who knows his case is already over.

Home now. Claire's at her father's until Wednesday. Biscuit ate his pill without a fight. Small victories.

I'm going to watch *Wheel of Fortune* and go to bed.

End of entry.

**AUDIO DIARY — ENTRY 003**
**DATE:** September 11, 1987

Okay, I need to preface this by saying I know how it sounds.

I review my own tape recordings after every session. That's standard practice—you run the audio against your steno notes to catch any discrepancies before producing the final transcript. I've been doing this for thirteen years. It's the most tedious part of the job and I usually do it while folding laundry or feeding Biscuit or standing at the kitchen counter eating crackers directly from the sleeve, which is what I was doing tonight.

I was reviewing the tape from Day 1. Eleanor's outburst. I had my headphones on and I was running through the forty-second segment at normal speed, checking my time-stamp notation, and something caught my ear. One of the guttural strings—the third repetition, about eighteen seconds in—had a tonal drop that didn't match the others. Like a different word embedded in the sequence. I rewound it.

This is—okay, I know about auditory pareidolia. I know the brain imposes pattern on noise. I know that if you tell someone they'll hear a word in static, they'll hear a word in static. I know this.

But I rewound it, and I played it again, and at the tail end of the tonal drop, there's a sibilant followed by what sounds like a long O followed by a hard B or P stop. And because I am a person who has spent her entire professional life discriminating between phonetic units, I recognized it. Not as English. As Latin.

*Introibo.*

I rewound it four more times. My hands were doing it before my brain had decided to do it. It was there each time. *Introibo.* Clear as anything else on that tape. Cleaner, actually—the rest of the vocalization is muddy and variable, but that particular three-syllable string is consistent across every replay.

*Introibo.* "I will enter." It's from the old Catholic Mass. The Tridentine rite. *Introibo ad altare Dei.* I will go unto the altar of God. I know this because I was raised Catholic and I spent seventeen years sitting in a pew listening to Father Cunningham drone through the Latin responses while my mother elbowed me to stop fidgeting.

So: a woman having a psychotic break in a courtroom produced, in a string of unintelligible vocalization, a phonetic sequence that happens to match a common Latin liturgical word. This is the auditory equivalent of seeing the Virgin Mary in a water stain. It's pareidolia. It is exactly pareidolia and nothing else.

I wrote it down.

I wrote it down in the margin of my steno notes, right next to the time code, in pencil. And then I rewound the tape and played the string one more time, and this time I played it at half speed, and—

It's late. I'm going to bed.

I'm not writing down what I heard at half speed because it doesn't matter because it was pareidolia.

End of entry.

*(Cross-examination of Defendant by Mr. Hutchins, resumed.)*

MR. HUTCHINS: Ms. Vance, you testified that on the afternoon of April 14th, you were teaching Tyler Baskin a Chopin étude. Is that correct?

THE DEFENDANT: That is correct.

MR. HUTCHINS: And this was the same lesson during which Tyler's mother alleges she heard chanting from the studio upon her arrival?

MR. GREER: Objection—characterization.

THE COURT: Sustained. Rephrase.

MR. HUTCHINS: Ms. Vance, what was occurring in the studio when Mrs. Baskin arrived?

THE DEFENDANT: Tyler was playing the étude. Opus 10, Number 4. The left hand passage in the development. He was struggling with the—

*(Defendant pauses. Defendant's head turns from counsel to the direction of the court reporter's station.)*

THE DEFENDANT: She's writing it down.

MR. HUTCHINS: Ms. Vance, please direct your answers to me or to the jury.

THE DEFENDANT: She writes it all down. Do you see her? The one who writes. She takes it into herself letter by letter.

THE COURT: Ms. Vance—

THE DEFENDANT: *(addressing court reporter directly)* You carry it. You don't know yet but you carry it. The record is the vessel and the vessel is the record. It goes into your machine and through the machine it goes into you, letter by letter, letter by letter.

MR. GREER: Your Honor, if we could take a brief—

THE DEFENDANT: *(volume increasing)* The vessel of record. The vessel of record. She holds the proceeding in her body. Every word committed every word—*(unintelligible, .03 duration)*—the mouth speaks and the hand writes and the hand writes and the word is made—*(unintelligible, .04 duration)*—I SEE YOU. I SEE THE CHAIN OF YOU.

*(Defendant stands. Defendant physically oriented toward court reporter's station. Bailiff approaches.)*

THE DEFENDANT: *(unintelligible vocalizing, sustained, .06 duration, overlapping with verbal commands from bailiff and Court)*

THE COURT: Remove the defendant immediately. We are in recess.

*(Defendant removed from courtroom at 2:47 p.m. Jury excused and admonished.)*

Okay.

I need to be precise. I need to be precise here because precision is what I do and if I lose that—I need this to be in my own words, clearly stated, in order. Like a deposition. Like I'm deposing myself. Okay.

Today at approximately 2:44 p.m. during cross-examination, the defendant, Eleanor Vance, turned away from prosecution counsel and addressed me directly for a period of approximately ninety seconds before she was removed from the courtroom. The content of her statements is on the official record the same way it's on every official record, which is in my handwriting, in my steno, processed through my machine, which means—

No. In order. Be in order.

I got home at 6:15. I fed Biscuit. I heated a can of soup and did not eat it. I sat down at the kitchen table with my tape recorder and my headphones and the steno notes from today and I played back Eleanor's monologue.

The first sustained unintelligible segment—.03 duration on the official record, which is approximately three seconds—contains phonetic content.

The second sustained unintelligible segment—.04 duration—contains phonetic content.

The final segment—.06 duration, which I recorded as overlapping with verbal commands from the bailiff—contains phonetic content.

I have been doing this for two hours. I have rewound each segment between fifteen and twenty times per segment. I have played them at normal speed, half speed, quarter speed. I have run them on the kitchen tape deck, which has better speakers than my portable unit. And I am going to describe what I found, clearly and without editorializing, because this is my record and I am the only person who is going to take it.
