# Part the First: Prolegomena, or, The Problem of Living Sources

I should like to begin with a confession, which is an unusual rhetorical posture for a work of historical scholarship but which, given the circumstances, is the only honest one available to me.

I have been working on what I initially titled "Perpetual Union: The Greenbriar-Chen Marriage as a Case Study in Parahuman Longevity and Domestic Dissolution" for the better part of eleven years. In that time I have consulted fourteen municipal archives, the National Registry of Specials (both the federal repository in College Park, Maryland, and the older, disorganized Massachusetts state collection, which still smells of whale oil and filing-cabinet rust), the Margaret Greenbriar Personal Papers at the Schlesinger Library,¹ three private collections held by descendants of James Chen's brother William, and the probate records of no fewer than forty-one false identities maintained by my subjects across a period spanning from the Polk administration to the second Obama term.

I hold a Ph.D. in Parahuman Historical Studies from Columbia University, where my dissertation, "The Bureaucracy of the Extraordinary: Registration Law and Civic Identity Among Specials in Antebellum New England," was awarded the Bancroft Prize in 2015. I mention this not to assert authority but to establish that I am, by training and by temperament, a scholar accustomed to the careful handling of primary sources, the adjudication of competing accounts, and the maintenance of professional distance from my subjects.

I have failed at the last of these so completely that it has become a structural feature of this work.

The difficulty — and I suspect the reader has already intuited this, but precision demands I state it plainly — is that my two primary subjects are alive. Not alive in the sense that a historian's subject occasionally is: elderly, fading, available for a brief oral-history interview conducted in a nursing home while a grandchild hovers anxiously by the door. Alive in the sense that Margaret Greenbriar, born March 7, 1831, in Boston, Massachusetts, Registered Special (Class: Cognitive, Subclass: Total Eidetic Recall, Manifestation: 1844), looks approximately thirty-five years old, possesses a memory that has not dropped a single sensory detail in one hundred and ninety-three years of continuous operation, and has *very strong opinions* about my scholarship.²

James Chen, born November 19, 1829, in San Francisco, California, Registered Special (Class: Somatic, Subclass: Biological Unkillability, Manifestation: 1843),³ also looks approximately thirty-five and has opinions about my scholarship, though his tend to be more amiable and less footnoted than his wife's.

His wife. I use the term advisedly. They have never divorced. This fact alone might constitute the central finding of this study, though its interpretation — whether the endurance of the union represents love, habit, spite, legal complication, or some combinatory phenomenon that resists categorization — is, I will confess at the outset, a question I am no longer confident I can answer with the dispassion my training demands.

What I can do — what this study attempts to do — is lay the documentary record before the reader with as much transparency as the format allows. This is a work of history, not of advocacy. I have endeavored to let the sources speak. The fact that certain of those sources are capable of *speaking back*, of telephoning me at my office and at my home and, on one occasion, at a restaurant in the West Village where I was trying to have a quiet dinner with my wife,⁴ does not alter the fundamental obligation of the historian, which is to assemble, contextualize, and interpret.

It does, however, complicate it.

What the reader will find in these pages is a documentary history of the longest verified marriage between two Registered Specials in the United States — a union spanning one hundred and seventy-seven years, fourteen cities, forty-one assumed identities, at least ninety-three documented separations (ranging in duration from forty-five minutes to eleven years), and one incident in Buenos Aires in 1936 that both parties have independently asked me not to describe, albeit for different reasons.

I will present primary sources in their original form wherever possible: journal entries, personal correspondence, legal filings, registration documents, interview transcripts, and, in certain cases, verbatim recollections provided by Mrs. Greenbriar-Chen, whose total eidetic recall renders her, in effect, a living archive — albeit one with editorial preferences.

The daguerreotype reproduced in Figure 1 [not included in this edition; see Appendix A] was taken on the occasion of the Greenbriar-Chen wedding, October 11, 1847, at the First Unitarian Church of Boston. Margaret is seated. James is standing. Both are very young. They look, if I may be permitted a moment of non-scholarly observation, like two people who have been handed a gift that neither of them yet understands is heavy.

Margaret disputes this characterization. She disputes most of my characterizations. In a 2023 interview she told me: "You keep looking at us like we're a cautionary tale, Dr. Thornton. Like we're what happens when love doesn't have the good sense to die. Has it occurred to you that you might simply be wrong?"

It has occurred to me.

I remain, for the purposes of this study, unconvinced.⁵

A note on terminology. Throughout this work, individuals possessing abilities that manifested at or around puberty are referred to as "Specials," per the standard usage established by the Federal Registration Act of 1838. The term "parahuman," deployed in certain activist contexts, is preferred by some Specials, including James Chen, and rejected by others, including Margaret Greenbriar, who has described it as "a word that tells you you're not quite human in a tone that wants credit for trying." I have employed "Special" throughout, except in direct quotation. I have attempted to avoid the colloquial "gifted," which, as Mrs. Greenbriar-Chen pointed out during one of our interviews, "presumes someone is doing the giving, and further presumes they meant well."

The marriage certificate is reproduced in Figure 2 [see Appendix A]. It is a standard Massachusetts civil document of the period, printed on cream stock, slightly foxed at the edges. In the space reserved for "Special Exemptions, Riders, or Ability-Related Covenants (per Massachusetts Registration Compliance Act of 1840, §14)," someone — almost certainly the presiding minister, the Reverend Josiah Whitfield — has written, in a tidy, somewhat reluctant hand: "Bride: eidetic recall (cognitive). Groom: unkillable (somatic). No spousal liability waiver requested. Standard vow."

Standard vow. "Till death do us part."

I once described this, in the 2019 conference paper, as "the most unintentionally ironic sentence in the American documentary record." Margaret Greenbriar, during her visit to my office hours, looked at me across my desk and said: "You think 'till death do us part' is the ironic part. It isn't. The ironic part is 'for better or for worse.' Death, we could have handled. It was the 'or for worse' that we weren't ready for."

**From the Personal Record of Margaret Greenbriar, Entry of October 12, 1847:**

*I am a married woman today and I find the condition peculiar. James sleeps beside me as I write this and he looks in sleep the way he looks at most things, which is to say open and unsuspecting and half in love with whatever happens to be in front of him. I could describe the exact way the moonlight sits on his collarbone. I will always be able to describe it. I do not yet know if this is a blessing or a sentence.*

*He married me knowing full well what I am. He said, and I remember the exact pitch of his voice because I will always remember the exact pitch of his voice, "Margaret, I don't care if you remember every foolish thing I say, as long as you also remember the good ones." He was smiling. The candle was guttering. There was a chip in the saucer under his teacup, left side, shaped like the state of Virginia if Virginia were slightly drunk.*

*I wanted to tell him: that is not how it works. I do not get to choose. The good ones and the foolish ones come in at the same fidelity. But he was smiling. And I loved him. And I was twenty-two years old and still foolish enough to believe love was the same thing as a compass or an answer rather than an open question you spend your whole life failing to resolve.*

*I did not tell him. I kissed him instead. I remember the exact taste. I will not write it here because some things should be allowed to remain in only one archive and that archive is me.*⁶

⁶ This passage was provided directly by Mrs. Greenbriar-Chen during our interview of March 4, 2022. # Part the Second: The Courtship, or, Something Worth Remembering

The meeting of Margaret Greenbriar and James Chen in the autumn of 1846 can be reconstructed with unusual specificity, owing to Margaret's recall and to two independent contemporaneous accounts: a letter from James to his elder brother William, dated November 3, 1846, and a brief mention in the diary of one Eliza Stanton Pratt, a neighbor of the Greenbriar family who attended the same event and who, with the discreet horror of her class and era, noted the presence of "a Celestial young man, evidently a Special of some somatic category, who conversed with young Miss Greenbriar at quite improper length."⁷

The event in question was a public lecture on phrenology, delivered by a Dr. Horace Penniman at the Boston Athenæum on the evening of October 29, 1846. Dr. Penniman's lecture, "The Cranial Geography of the Exceptional: What the Skulls of Specials Reveal About Divine Intent," was by all accounts a poorly reasoned exercise in pseudoscience — even by the standards of 1846, which were not, it must be said, exacting on this point. He argued that the abilities of Specials could be mapped to specific cranial protuberances, and invited several Registered Specials in the audience to submit to public examination. Neither Margaret nor James volunteered. They have given different reasons for their presence at the lecture in the first place: James claimed, in the 2023 interview, that he wanted to see what the man would say about Chinese skulls ("I was young and angry and I wanted to be angry at someone specific"); Margaret said she went because it was free and the alternative was an evening at home with her mother, who had recently taken to asking Margaret whether she remembered the precise wording of various Bible verses about the duties of unmarried daughters, "which of course I did, which was exactly the problem."
