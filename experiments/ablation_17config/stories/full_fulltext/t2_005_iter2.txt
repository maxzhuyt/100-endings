# Form 27-B: Application for Continued Existence

## I.

Every civilization that achieves interstellar capability is processed. This is not a policy. It is not a philosophy. It is a procedure, which is worse than either, because a policy can be debated, a philosophy can be rejected, but a procedure simply *is*, the way gravity simply is, the way aeli­gibility requirement on line 4 of an intake form simply is, and if you have ever tried to argue with line 4 of an intake form, you will understand the fix that humanity was in.

The Processing had come to Earth on a Tuesday, which was, by the general consensus of those who survived it, the most appropriate day of the week for the end of a civilization. It came not as fire, not as flood, not as a great voice from the heavens announcing judgment—it came as a notification. A form, transmitted simultaneously to every receiving device on the planet, in every language, including three that had been dead for centuries and one that would not be invented for another fifty years. The form was eleven pages long. It was printed in 9-point font. The relevant passage was on page eight, paragraph four, subclause (c)(ii): *Your species has been classified under Provision 7.1 of the Interstellar Capacity Statute as an emergent Type-C civilization. Per standard processing protocol, your homeworld will be archived and your biological presence redistributed. You are entitled to file one (1) appeal within thirty (30) standard galactic days. Sincerely, the Office of Auditory and Existential Compliance.*

There was a phone number at the bottom. It was forty-seven digits long and no one could get through.

What "archived" meant was this: the oceans were catalogued and compressed. The forests were indexed and stored. The mountains were given reference numbers. Everything alive that was not on the ship—every dog, every oak tree, every cluster of barnacles on every pier in every coastal town from Gloucester to Galway to the curve of the Amalfi coast where, once, on an evening in September, a man had proposed to a woman by saying nothing at all and handing her an anchovy—all of it was processed. Processed. The word is important. It is the Auditors' word, and it means exactly what it means when a slaughterhouse uses it, and exactly what it means when a computer uses it, and exactly what it means when a human-resources department uses it, and the three meanings have always been more similar than anyone would like to admit.

Three thousand people survived, because three thousand people happened to be aboard the *Tikvah*, a generation ship that had been under construction in high orbit for eleven years and was six months from completion. It was not ready. Its water recyclers were untested, its port-side agricultural bay was an empty hall of bare aluminum and exposed wiring, and its filing system—the digital repository that the ship's Records Division would use to submit humanity's appeals, continuity petitions, habitat requests, settlement applications, cultural-preservation claims, biological-diversity waivers, atmospheric-preference surveys, and requests for information regarding the appeals process for the denial of requests for information—was built, in the chaos and horror of those thirty days, by a twenty-four-year-old database engineer named Marcus Yeh who worked without sleeping for nine of them and who made, on the eighth day, at 3:47 a.m. ship-time, a single error.

He selected Form 27-B.

He should have selected Form 27-B/6.

The difference between these two forms was a stroke, a single oblique line, a fraction of a character, a hairsbreadth of typographical distinction that, in the Auditors' filing architecture, represented the difference between *Application for Continued Existence (General, Non-Specific, Preliminary Review)* and *Application for Continued Existence (Provisional, Carbon-Based, Emotional)*. Form 27-B, the general version, had been deprecated nine cosmic fiscal cycles ago. It routed to a processing queue that no Auditor had checked since an epoch when the dominant lifeforms in the galaxy communicated through subsonic vibration and reproduced by fission and had no particular use for the word "emotional." Everything filed under Form 27-B went into this queue and sat there, silent and unread, like letters to a saint whose shrine has been bricked over.

For seventeen years, the Records Division of the *Tikvah* filed Form 27-B. They filed it with diligence. They filed it with care. They filed it with the tender, painstaking attention of monks illuminating manuscripts, because filing was, by the seventeenth year of the voyage, no longer an administrative act. It was a liturgy. When the ship's children asked what the adults did in the Records Division—and the Records Division had grown, by then, to occupy a full deck, with thirty-two volunteer staff and a waiting list—the adults said, "We are making sure they know we are here." They meaning the Auditors. They meaning the universe. They meaning anyone, anything, any process or system or celestial clerk who might, upon receiving the proper form, look up from their desk and say: *Ah. Yes. You. You are still here. Let me see what I can do.*

The forms were filed in triplicate. They were formatted precisely. They were, by any objective measure, beautiful—the digital equivalent of hand-calligraphed petitions, every field populated, every appendix cross-referenced, every page footer bearing the ship's registration number and the phrase, *submitted in good faith on behalf of 3,000 human souls.* They were the most careful, most loving, most meticulous documents ever produced by a species whose history of careful documentation included the Library of Alexandria, the Domesday Book, and Mrs. Eleanor Tisch's handwritten inventory of every item in her Brooklyn apartment, which she had maintained weekly for forty years and which had been archived, along with Eleanor and Brooklyn and the concept of apartments, during the Processing.

On the morning of Year Seventeen, Day 114, a junior clerk in the Records Division named Priya Osei was conducting a routine cross-check of submission codes when she noticed the discrepancy. She checked it twice. She checked it four times. Then she sat very still for eleven minutes, which Priya's coworkers would later describe as the loudest silence they had ever heard, and then she walked to the office of the Director of Records, and she placed a single printed page on his desk, and she said:

"None of it went through."

She meant: none of the 4,312 appeals. None of the 891 continuity petitions. None of the 267 habitat requests, the 45 cultural-preservation claims, the 12 atmospheric-preference surveys, or the single, extraordinary, fourteen-page document titled *Application for Reclassification of Species Status from "Archived" to "Extant," with Supplementary Evidence of Continued Biological and Emotional Viability Including but Not Limited to: One Birth (Emi Vasquez-Okafor, 3.2 kg, healthy, Year Three), Fourteen Marriages, the Maintenance of a Functional Library, and the Ongoing Cultivation of Tomatoes in Hydroponic Bay 7.*

None of it. Seventeen years. 3,000 people. A dead phone.

The Director of Records, a man named Yohannes, who had held the position for eleven years and who had believed, had *needed* to believe, with the particular ferocity of a man who had lost a continent and a language and a mother and a sky and had replaced them all with the conviction that the correct form, properly submitted, was a kind of prayer, sat down, and pressed his palms flat against his desk, and breathed, and did not cry, because there was a form for grief and he had filed it years ago and it, too, had gone nowhere.

## II.

The emergency assembly was held in the ship's central atrium, which had been designed as a park and had become, over seventeen years, the closest thing the *Tikvah* had to a public square, a cathedral, and a living room. There were trees here—small ones, labored over, each one named. There was a bench that a man named Hector had carved from reclaimed bulkhead paneling as a gift for his wife on their anniversary, their first anniversary in space, and the bench bore an inscription that read, in Hector's slightly uneven hand, *Sit here with me and pretend it's the Malecón.* Hector was dead now. His wife, Liliana, sat on the bench every evening.

Captain Anastasia Kouri stood at the front and did what captains do when the situation is unthinkable: she thought about it in military terms.

"We have been filing into a void," she said. She was a tall woman with a shaved head and the bearing of someone who had once commanded a frigate and now commanded a village in a can. "For seventeen years, we have been politely requesting the universe's permission to exist, and the universe has not received our request. Our position is therefore unchanged from Day One: we are, as far as the Auditors' system is concerned, already dead."

She let this land. It landed.

"I am done," she said, and her voice had the cold clarity of someone crossing a line, "asking politely. We are three thousand human beings. We are not a form. We are not a petition. We are not a *filing error.* I am proposing that we—"

"Excuse me."

The voice came from the middle section, Row 22, Seat 9—a seat that was, the omniscient record can confirm, the same seat Gloria Alvarez-Kimathi had occupied at every ship-wide assembly for seventeen years, because Gloria believed in assigned seating the way other people believed in God, which is to say, with full awareness that the system was arbitrary and the unshakable conviction that it was necessary.

Gloria stood. She was fifty-nine years old. She was five foot three. She wore her hair in a bun that she set each morning with two pins that she had brought aboard the *Tikvah* during the evacuation, choosing them, in the ninety seconds she had been given to select personal items, over photographs, over jewelry, over the collected poems of Wisława Szymborska, because she had memorized the poems and she could not memorize the pins and a woman's hair should be out of her face when she is working.

"I'll handle the re-filing," she said.

Captain Kouri looked at her. Three thousand people looked at her.

"I'm sorry," the captain said. "Who are you?"

"Gloria Alvarez-Kimathi. Records volunteer, Deck Four, Tuesday and Thursday shifts." She paused. "Before that, California Department of Motor Vehicles, Office 34-C, Bakersfield. Twenty-eight years."

There was a silence, which Gloria did not rush to fill. She had never rushed to fill a silence. Silences, in Gloria's experience, were where people sorted themselves out, and the worst thing you could do was interfere with someone in the middle of sorting themselves out.

"Ms. Alvarez-Kimathi," the captain said, with the strained patience of a woman who had been about to propose something involving the ship's limited weapons array and was now being interrupted by a DMV clerk, "I appreciate the offer, but this is not a *clerical* problem. This is—"

"It is, though," Gloria said. Not rudely. Not defiantly. With the calm, immovable certainty of a person stating that water is wet. "It's a clerical problem. It has always been a clerical problem. The Processing was a clerical act. Our survival is a clerical matter. The Auditors are clerks. The system is a filing system. You can be angry at it—I've seen a lot of people be angry at it, I once watched a man in Bakersfield throw a chair through a plate-glass window because his registration renewal was rejected for a missing middle initial—but being angry at a filing system is like being angry at weather. You can do it. It won't change the weather."

"What will change it?"

"Filing correctly."

Gloria said this the way she said everything: as though the answer were obvious, which to her it was. She had known, from the age of twenty-two, when she had started at the DMV—had been assigned to Window 7, the window that everyone avoided because it handled the complicated cases, the expired licenses that had been expired for decades, the immigrants with documents in languages the system didn't recognize, the elderly who had lost everything and couldn't prove they were themselves—that the world was divided into two kinds of work. There was the work of building systems, and there was the work of translating between people and systems, and the second kind was harder and less respected and more important. The system did not care about you. That was not a flaw in the system. That was what a system *was*: a process designed to treat unlike things alike, to replace the chaos of individual need with the architecture of category. And someone had to stand in the gap.

She had stood in the gap for twenty-eight years. She had held the hand of a ninety-one-year-old woman who wept because she could not remember her Social Security number, and Gloria had said, "That's all right, we'll find it, let's start with your name," and they had found it, and the woman had gotten her ID, and she had squeezed Gloria's hand and said, "You're an angel," and Gloria had said, "I'm a GS-7. But thank you." She had once spent four hours on the phone with Sacramento to get an error code reversed for a man whose license had been flagged as belonging to a dead person, and the man was not dead, and Sacramento's position was that the system said he was dead and the system was the system, and Gloria's position was that the man was standing in front of her and breathing and his name was Gerald and he wanted to drive to his daughter's wedding, and she had won, not through charm or eloquence or the invocation of principle, but through the patient, relentless, filing of the correct countervailing form, three times, until the system yielded. She understood something that captains and generals and diplomats did not: that arbitrary systems are not defeated by force or by reason. They are defeated by *someone who knows the procedure better than the system knows itself.*

The captain stared at her.

"I've seen worse queues," Gloria said.

She went home—Cabin 1142, Deck Four, a room the size of a parking space that she had made habitable through the judicious application of a shelf, a reading lamp, and a small framed photograph of her daughter, Marisol, who had been in Buenos Aires during the Processing and who was not on the ship and who Gloria did not talk about except once, to Yohannes, late at night in the Records office, when she had said, simply, "I file because Marisol can't"—and she began to read.

She read the Auditor Procedural Manual.

It was 4,211 pages long in its translated form. The translation was, the Records Division estimated, approximately 40 percent accurate, which meant that 60 percent of the manual was gibberish, or poetry, or procedural instruction so alien as to be indistinguishable from either. Gloria read every page. She read the way she had always read bureaucratic documents: not for meaning, not for logic, but for *pattern*. Every system had a pattern. Every system had been designed by someone, and that someone had made choices, and those choices had a rhythm, and if you could feel the rhythm, you could predict the next beat, and if you could predict the next beat, you could file the right form before the system knew it needed one.

She read for six days. She took notes in a small notebook in handwriting so precise it looked typeset. On the seventh day, she presented herself at the captain's office and said:

"I need a shuttle, a delegation of three, and permission to visit coordinates given in footnote 1,742 on page 3,008 of the procedural manual, subsection 'Physical Submission of Hardship-Class Appeals, Facilities and Access.'"

"What's at those coordinates?"

"A waiting room," Gloria said.
