**BEFORE THE HONORABLE WILLIAM T. ALDISS, JUDGE PRESIDING**

\[PROCEEDINGS RESUMED AT 9:07 A.M.\]

MR. HALLIDAY: Ladies and gentlemen. I want to return you, briefly, to the morning of March the eleventh, 1986. I know it is difficult. I know some of you have children of your own, and that this is painful. But I ask you to stay with me, because I want to talk about what was found in that house on Barrow Mill Road, and I want to talk about what it means.

On March the eleventh, deputies responding to a welfare check entered the home of Eleanor Mae Vance. In the back bedroom, they found the bodies of Tyler James Hollowell, age four. Brianna Sue Hollowell, age six. And Marcus DeShawn Watts, age seven. Three children placed in the defendant's care by the Sutton County Department of Social Services.

The medical examiner will tell you—has already told you—that these children did not die of accident or illness. The medical examiner will tell you that the injuries found on these small bodies were systematic. Deliberate. Ritualized.

And the evidence will show—has already shown—that the defendant, Eleanor Mae Vance, participated in a network of individuals engaging in organized satanic ritual abuse, and that the deaths of Tyler, Brianna, and Marcus were the culmination of practices that had been ongoing in that house for months.

THE COURT: Mr. Halliday, the jury has heard opening statements. If you're revisiting foundations, please connect them to today's witness.

MR. HALLIDAY: Yes, Your Honor. I raise this context because the defense has signaled its intention to present the defendant as herself a victim—confused, manipulated, incompetent to have formed the intent—

MR. DARRAGH: Objection. Characterization of defense strategy.

THE COURT: Sustained. Mr. Halliday, call your witness or sit down.

MR. HALLIDAY: The State calls Dr. Patricia Loomis.

\[WITNESS SWORN AT 9:14 A.M.\]

MR. HALLIDAY: Dr. Loomis, you conducted a psychological evaluation of the defendant at the request of the State?

DR. LOOMIS: I did. Over the course of three sessions in June and July of this year.

MR. HALLIDAY: And your conclusion?

DR. LOOMIS: That Ms. Vance is competent to stand trial. She understands the charges against her. She understands court proceedings. She is capable of assisting in her own defense, though she has frequently declined to do so.

MR. HALLIDAY: In your professional opinion, does the defendant suffer from any psychotic disorder that would impair her understanding of right and wrong?

DR. LOOMIS: No. I found no evidence of psychotic ideation. Ms. Vance is oriented, lucid, and—

\[DEFENDANT RISES FROM COUNSEL TABLE, 9:18 A.M.\]

\[UNINTELLIGIBLE—DEFENDANT REMOVED FROM COURTROOM\]

THE COURT: Let the record show the defendant has been removed by the bailiff. Mr. Darragh, I trust you'll speak to your client about courtroom conduct before the afternoon session.

MR. DARRAGH: Yes, Your Honor.

THE COURT: We'll recess until two o'clock.

\[PROCEEDINGS RECESSED AT 9:22 A.M.\]

---

**MARGINALIA — RUTH EISLER**
**Appended to official transcript, Vol. III, p. 47**
**Personal notation, not entered into the record**

At 9:18:31 a.m. the defendant stood and vocalized for approximately nineteen seconds before the bailiff reached her.

The official transcript designates this as UNINTELLIGIBLE. I am entering here, for my own reference, a phonetic transcription of the full vocalization as I captured it in simultaneous shorthand and confirmed against my audio backup:

/ɑːʃ.tɛ.ɾɪ.mɑ.ɾuː.θɛs.kɑ.nɛ.ðɑ.ɹiː.ɛl.kə.vɑ.siː.ɑn.tɛ.mɪ.ɾuː.θɑ.ɾɛ.sɪ.pɪ.ɛn.dɑː/

Duration: 19 seconds (9:18:31–9:18:50).

Volume: consistent. Not rising or cracking. Steady exhalation.

Judge Aldiss ruled it unintelligible and I have entered it as such in the record. The designation stands. But I want to note here, because it is my professional observation and I have been doing this work for seventeen years, that the vocalization was continuous, unbroken, produced at consistent volume and pitch, and segmented into what I can only describe as regular syllabic intervals. There was no gasping. No repetition of syllables as might occur in glossolalic episodes, which I have encountered twice before in my career (State v. Pruitt, 1974; State v. Ballard-Hernandez, 1981). This did not sound like the way people sound when they scream in court. I've heard a lot of people scream in court. It didn't sound like language, either, exactly. It sounded composed.

I am marking this notation PERSONAL and will not include it in any filed materials. This is stenographic observation only.

—R.E.

This is October fourteenth. I'm going to try something I haven't done before, which is keep an audio record of my working notes separate from the case marginalia. I don't know if this is useful. I think it might be, just for my own processing.

The Vance trial had its first real disruption today. Day seven. Defendant had an outburst during the State's psych witness and was removed.

\[Pause, four seconds. Drink.\]

I've marked it unintelligible in the official transcript and that's correct—it doesn't correspond to any English utterance I can identify, and Judge Aldiss made the ruling, so that's that. But I've been doing this a long time and I want to describe what bothered me about it, just for my own record.

When people have outbursts—when they scream in the courtroom—there are patterns. I've seen dozens. You get repetition. You get escalation. The voice goes up, it cracks, it breaks. It's chaos. Emotional chaos. What came out of Eleanor Vance at 9:18 this morning was not chaos.

I've got it transcribed in IPA in my marginalia. Three stress groups, roughly equal in duration, with a rhythmic regularity that I'd estimate—I don't have the tools to be precise about this—at something like five or six syllables per group, with the stress falling regularly. It's. I don't know what it is. Metered. Like verse.

\[Clock ticking.\]

This is probably nothing. There's a psychiatric explanation, I'm sure. Patricia Loomis was literally in the middle of giving testimony about the defendant's mental state. I'm not a psychologist. I'm a stenographer and I'm very good at my job, and my job is to capture what's said in a courtroom, and when something I captured doesn't match the box it was put in, that irritates me.

\[Ice settling in glass.\]

I'm going to set this aside and get back to prep for tomorrow. Dr. Loomis resumes at nine.

\[Tape click.\]

**MARGINALIA — RUTH EISLER**
**Appended to official transcript, Vol. V, p. 112**
**Personal notation, not entered into the record**

Second vocalization, 2:41:07 p.m., duration approximately twenty-one seconds (two seconds longer than the first).

Phonetic transcription:

/ɑːʃ.tɛ.ɾɪ.mɑ.ɾuː.θɛs.kɑ.nɛ.ðɑ.ɹiː.ɛl.kə.vɑ.siː.ɑn.tɛ.mɪ.ɾuː.θɑ.ɾɛ.sɪ.pɪ.ɛn.dɑː.mɛ.ɑː/

Core sequence is functionally identical to the October 14 vocalization. The first eighteen seconds overlay precisely, syllable for syllable, stress for stress. At the nineteen-second mark, rather than stopping as in the first outburst, the defendant added two syllables — /mɛ.ɑː/ — before the bailiff intervened.

This is the same utterance. Not similar. The same. Reproduced from the same position in the sequence, at the same tempo, with the same stress pattern, after a gap of three days, under different evidentiary circumstances (psych witness vs. crime scene testimony), in a manner I have never encountered in any courtroom vocalization in seventeen years of practice.

I am now certain this is not glossolalia and not psychotic speech. It is a recitation.

—R.E.

October seventeenth. Second outburst from Eleanor Vance today. I've now transcribed both vocalizations phonetically and compared them side by side, and I want to walk through what I've found, because it's—it is not what I expected.

The core syllable sequence is identical. I mean *identical.* Seventeen years of doing this and I've never seen a person reproduce an unscripted vocalization exactly over a three-day gap. There is no drift. No variation. The stress pattern is—hold on.

\[Sound of pages. Ruth clears her throat.\]

The stress pattern is consistent with trochaic tetrameter, which I only know because I looked it up. Four groups of two syllables, stressed-unstressed, repeated in what I'm going to call stanzas for lack of a better word. The whole thing runs to approximately four stanzas in the complete version from today, plus the extra two syllables at the end where she continued past where the first outburst cut off.

So. I did something this evening that is probably silly and I want to acknowledge that up front. I was sitting here going over my shorthand notes and thinking about the syllable structure, and—I don't know why, it was an instinct—I read the phonetic transcription backward. Out loud. And it didn't…I'm going to play it back.

\[Click. Brief hiss. Then Ruth's voice, reading slowly, from a different recording or the same tape rewound:\]

"Ad. nep. ip. is. er. ath. oor. im. et. na. is. av. ak. le. eer. ath. en. aks. eth. oor. am. ir. et. sha."

\[Click.\]

\[Silence, five seconds.\]

So that's me reading the IPA in reverse, syllable by syllable. And what I heard when I played it back—this is subjective, I understand—was something that sounded like words. Specifically it sounded like Latin. Or something adjacent to Latin.

I dug out my old Cassell's from college—the binding cracked, I haven't opened it since probably 1967—and I started looking things up.

*Designare.* To mark out, to designate. *Recipiendam.* From *recipio*—to receive, to take back. Specifically the gerundive form, indicating something that *must be* received. The feminine form. A feminine recipient.

*Vas.* Vessel.

\[Pause.\]

So if I'm not hearing ghosts in the tape hiss—and I might be, I'm fully aware I might be—what I'm getting, very roughly, is something like: "The vessel designated for receiving." Or "She who must receive." Something in that neighborhood.

\[Ruth laughs, short and bright. Almost a bark.\]

I took two years of Latin at West Mecklenburg and I was not very good at it, so I am probably mangling this beyond all recognition. This is what happens when you take your work home.

\[Five-second silence.\]

\[Quieter.\]

It is unsettling, though. I'll say that out loud. It is unsettling. Because even if my Latin is wrong, the fact that the reversed sequence generates anything resembling morphological units—even the *appearance* of language—is. I mean, that's unusual. Reversed babble shouldn't sound like words.

\[Clock ticking. Eight seconds.\]

I'm going to set this aside. I need to focus on accuracy, not puzzles. The Vance trial resumes Monday and there's a hearing on whether the defendant stays in the room, and I need to be prepared for that, not sitting here playing stenographic parlor games.

The Latin is wrong. Or the reversal is coincidence. Or I'm pattern-matching because I've been staring at these syllables for two hours. Probably that.

\[Drink.\]

I need to focus on accuracy, not puzzles. I already said that.

\[Tape click.\]
