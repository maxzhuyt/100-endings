# The HOA Board Will Now Come to Order

## I.

The notice was laminated. That was the detail Meredith Chen would remember later, when people asked her how it started — not the fine itself, not the bylaw citation printed in a serif font she didn't recognize, not even the zip ties fastening the notice to the railing of the ramp that Kevin Muñoz and his crew had finished seventy-two hours earlier. The lamination. Someone had fed this piece of paper through a machine to make it weatherproof. Someone had thought about rain.

She stood on the porch in her slippers and read every word.

> **NOTICE OF VIOLATION — CREEKWOOD COMMONS HOMEOWNERS ASSOCIATION**
>
> Property: 414 Briarwood Lane, Unit N/A (single-family detached)
>
> Nature of Violation: Unauthorized exterior structural modification (wheelchair access ramp, front elevation) in contravention of CC&R Article IX, Section 3(b) and Architectural Standards Guideline 2017-04, Paragraph 12.
>
> A fine of $50.00 per day will be assessed beginning on the date of this notice and continuing until (a) the structure is removed, or (b) retroactive approval is obtained through the Architectural Review Committee.

There was a signature at the bottom, printed in that same careful serif. Douglas R. Whitmore, President, Creekwood Commons HOA Board of Directors. Below his name, a phone number and an email address and the hours during which the management office — which was Doug's kitchen table, but the notice did not say this — accepted inquiries.

Meredith removed the zip ties with a pair of kitchen scissors. She rolled the laminated notice into a loose tube and carried it inside.

The hospital bed was in what used to be the dining room. They had moved the mahogany table into the garage in October, when Roger's oncologist had used the phrase "home-based palliative" and Meredith had understood, with the fluency of a woman who had spent thirty-one years modeling mortality tables for Pacific Mutual, exactly what trajectory that phrase described. The bed had a motorized frame and a pressure-redistribution mattress and a side table crowded with pill bottles organized by time of day. Roger was asleep. His breathing had the whittled quality it took on in the afternoons now, thin and concentrating, as though breath were a resource he was learning to ration.

She did not tell him about the notice.

She went to the kitchen, sat at the table where her laptop was open to a solitaire game she'd been losing, and took the calculator from the drawer. It was a Texas Instruments financial calculator, the same BA II Plus she'd used for her last fifteen years at Pacific Mutual, its keys worn to a shine that no longer corresponded to the letters printed on them. She knew the keys by feel.

Fifty dollars per day.

Thirty days: $1,500.

She keyed it in anyway, though she didn't need to. The act of pressing the buttons was a kind of thinking, the way some people needed to move their lips when they read.

Ninety days: $4,500.

Six months: $9,125. She added the leap-year half-day out of habit. Precision was not a choice for Meredith; it was a resting state.

One year: $18,250.

She set the calculator down. From the dining room she could hear Roger's breathing, that careful metering, that economy. She thought about the ramp. Kevin Muñoz had built it to ADA specifications even though the ADA did not technically apply to private residences — he'd done it because Meredith had asked and because Kevin was the kind of contractor who could not bring himself to build a thing worse than he knew how to build it. The ramp was thirty-two feet long, because that was what a 1:12 slope ratio required for their porch height of thirty-two inches. It was forty-eight inches wide. The handrails were thirty-four inches high on both sides, with returns that curved back to the posts so a sleeve or a pocket couldn't catch. The surface was pressure-treated Southern yellow pine with a non-slip grit coating, and Kevin had counter-sunk every screw so Roger's wheels wouldn't bump.

It was not an eyesore. It was beautiful in the way that correctly solved problems are beautiful, all the math resolved into wood and gradient and the quiet conviction that a man in a wheelchair should be able to leave his own house.

It was unauthorized only in the sense that she had not filed a form.

Meredith picked up the calculator again. She was not computing anything now. She was holding it the way another person might hold a rosary or a fist — as a shape for the hand while the mind did something that had no shape.

Eighteen thousand, two hundred and fifty dollars. Per year. Applied against a form she had not filed because her husband was dying and the ramp was correct and she had not known — had not imagined, had not modeled into her assumptions — that someone would laminate a fine and zip-tie it to a medical device.

She put the calculator down. She closed the solitaire game. She opened a browser and typed: *Creekwood Commons HOA bylaws PDF*.

She began to read.

## II.

Vivian Coe lived in unit 7C of the Willowbrook apartments on the east side of the development, in a one-bedroom whose most prominent feature was the court-reporting machine on a tripod stand in the living room, facing the television. She practiced on the news. She had been practicing on the news for six years, since the licensing board had revoked her certification, and her transcripts of CNN's evening programming were flawless — more accurate, in fact, than anything the network's own closed-captioning system produced. This was not a comfort. The transcripts sat in binders on a shelf next to the window. No one had asked for them.

Meredith had found her through a freelance transcription service that contracted with local law firms for deposition work. Vivian's work was the most accurate in the service's stable, and also the cheapest, because Vivian could not advertise a credential she no longer held and therefore could not negotiate.

"I need a stenographic record of a public meeting," Meredith said. She was standing in Vivian's doorway. She had not called ahead. She had driven here with a manila folder on the passenger seat and the precise, directed energy of a woman who had spent three days reading bylaws and was now moving through the world with a plan so cold and complete it could have been a spreadsheet. "HOA annual meeting. One hundred twelve homeowners eligible to attend. Historically, between twenty and thirty-five show up. The meeting will last approximately ninety minutes. I need a verbatim transcript — speaker-attributed, time-stamped, formatted to legal deposition standards."

Vivian looked at her. She was fifty-seven. She had short gray hair and reading glasses on a chain and the bearing of a person who had been formally apologized to so many times that the word *sorry* had lost its molecular structure in her ears.

"An HOA meeting," Vivian said.

"Yes."

"You want court-reporter-grade transcription of an HOA meeting."

"I want a record," Meredith said, "that cannot be misremembered, summarized, or selectively omitted. I want a record that would hold up if someone challenged it. A record that is better than the official minutes. Which, based on what I've read, would not be difficult."

And there it was — the hook, set so precisely it was almost inaudible. Not a compliment. Not an appeal to sympathy. A professional assessment that the existing record was substandard, delivered to a woman whose entire life had been destroyed by a substandard record.

The case had been Clark v. Clark, a custody dispute in the Thirty-Seventh District Court. Vivian had been the court reporter for seven years, the best in the circuit by any metric anyone applied. On the fourth day of testimony, a pediatric psychologist had said — or Vivian had heard, or Vivian's fingers had rendered — "The father has shown *no* consistent pattern of engagement" when the psychologist had actually said "The father has shown *a* consistent pattern of engagement." Two letters. One word. A negation that reversed the meaning of an expert's testimony. The attorneys caught it in the read-back the next morning, but the damage had three-dimensional consequences she had not been able to predict: a continuance, a motion to strike, a father's attorney who argued that the entire transcript was unreliable, a judge who agreed. The case unraveled. The custody arrangement that resulted was not the one the evidence supported, and Vivian knew this because she had been in the room for every word and her transcript — except for those two letters — was perfect.

The licensing board had called it "a pattern of negligence," which meant they had gone back through her previous transcripts and found three other errors across seven years, all of them minor, none of them consequential, but enough to justify the word *pattern*. She had not fought it. She had understood, in the way that people who work inside systems understand, that the decision had already been made and the hearing was the lamination — the process that made the outcome weatherproof.

"When is this meeting?" Vivian said.

"October fourteenth. Seven p.m. Creekwood Community Church, Fellowship Hall B."

Vivian looked at the binders on the shelf. Six years of perfect transcripts of Anderson Cooper, of congressional hearings, of pharmaceutical commercials whose fine-print disclaimers she had captured at speeds that would have made her old colleagues nauseous. Six years of proving, to no one who was asking, that her hands did not make the mistake that her hands had made.

"I'll need a sight line to the podium," Vivian said. "And I'll need the names of every board member in advance. Speaker attribution is only as good as the identifier key."

Meredith handed her the manila folder.

Gerald Koh was sixty-nine years old, and he owned three editions of *Robert's Rules of Order Newly Revised*: the 11th edition (standard), the 12th edition (current), and a 1970 seventh edition that he had found in a used bookstore in Portland that still bore the marginal annotations of someone named "R. Dietrich," whose cramped pencil notes Gerald had come to regard, over years of study, as a kind of private correspondence with a dead friend. He also owned the *In Brief* edition, the authors' pocket guide, and a CD-ROM of the 10th edition that he could no longer play on any computer he owned but kept in its jewel case on the shelf between the 11th and 12th editions like a relic.

He taught a continuing-education course called "Order in the Room: Parliamentary Procedure for Civic Life" at the community college. Enrollment fluctuated between four and nine. The college had threatened to cancel the course twice. Gerald had argued, both times, that parliamentary procedure was the immune system of democratic governance, that without it every meeting was merely a room full of people being loud at each other, and that the college's own faculty senate, which could not get through an agenda without a shouting match about parking, was proof of what happened when no one in the room understood the difference between a motion and an amendment. The college had relented, both times, less because Gerald was persuasive than because the paperwork to cancel a course was more burdensome than the cost of letting him continue to teach it.

Meredith had taken the course two years ago, when Pacific Mutual had asked her to serve on an internal governance committee and she had wanted to understand the procedural framework. She had been the best student Gerald had ever had, not because she was the most interested — Gerald had had students who shared his passion, or at least performed a reasonable facsimile — but because she was the most precise. She had understood immediately that parliamentary procedure was not about rules. It was about the architecture of collective decision-making: who could speak, when, for how long, about what, and — critically — who decided when the deciding was done. She had understood, in other words, that it was a system of power, and she had treated it with the same respect she brought to actuarial tables: not love, exactly, but the acknowledgment that the thing had teeth.

She met him at a coffee shop. She brought a copy of the Creekwood Commons bylaws, annotated in three colors of ink. She laid it on the table and opened to Article IV, Section 7, Subsection (c).

"The annual meeting requires a quorum of twenty percent of eligible members, present or by proxy," she said. "One hundred twelve eligible members. Quorum is twenty-three. Average attendance over the last six years is twenty-eight, based on the sign-in sheets I obtained through a records request. Doug Whitmore has, in four of those six years, collected enough proxies from non-attending members to control the margin."

Gerald picked up the bylaws. He was already reading.

"The proxy form," Meredith continued, "is a single sentence on the back of the annual meeting notice. It says: 'I hereby authorize the Board President to vote on my behalf on all matters before the membership.' It does not specify which matters. It does not expire. And it has been, in at least two instances that I can document, collected *before* the agenda was published."

Gerald looked up. His eyes had the particular brightness of a man who has spent decades studying a discipline that the world considers trivial and has just been shown, for the first time, a place where it matters.

"That proxy language is non-compliant," he said.

"I know."

"Under Chapter XX of the 12th edition, a proxy must specify the meeting and the scope of authority delegated. A blanket proxy that predates the agenda is — " He paused, not searching for the word but savoring it. "Void."

"I know," Meredith said again. "What I need is someone who can raise the challenge correctly. In the room. Using the right words, in the right order, at the right time."

Gerald Koh had attended hundreds of meetings in his life. City council sessions. Planning commission hearings. He had once sat through a seven-hour school board meeting about a mascot change. He had watched, in every one of these rooms, as procedure was mangled, ignored, overridden by whoever spoke loudest or held the gavel. He had wanted, every time, to stand and say: *Point of order.* He had wanted to say it in the specific, technical sense that meant *the chair has made a procedural error and I am formally requiring that it be corrected.* He had never once been permitted to say it in a context where it would change anything.

"What time should I arrive?" Gerald said.

The book club met on Thursday nights in rotating living rooms. They had been meeting for eleven years. The current membership was six, but one of them — Dottie Parrish, eighty-one, the eldest — had moved to an assisted-living facility in May and attended now by speakerphone propped against a stack of that month's book. They called themselves, with the serene self-awareness of women who have survived enough decades to find their own pretensions amusing, the Briarwood Bibliothèque.

The members: Anita Gerhardt, seventy-four, former high school principal, read everything with a red pen and wrote "STAKES?" in the margins when a novel lost its nerve. Linda Park, sixty-three, who had run the reference desk at the county library for thirty years and could destroy any argument by asking for its source. Claudette Simmons, sixty-one, an actuary like Meredith — they had met at a conference in 2006 — who had left the field five years earlier and now read novels the way she once read mortality tables, looking for the point where the math underneath the story broke down. Fran Okoro, sixty-seven, retired nurse practitioner, who read slowly, spoke rarely, and, when she did speak, made everyone else in the room feel that they had been playing at analysis while she had been doing surgery. And Dottie, on speakerphone, who had been a family court judge for twenty-two years and who had once said, during a discussion of *Anna Karenina*, "Tolstoy understood custody law better than most lawyers I've known," and no one had been sure she was joking.

Meredith came to the Thursday meeting with a bottle of wine and twelve photocopied pages: a summary of the fine, the ramp specifications, the bylaw citations, the meeting minutes analysis, and the mulch fund numbers, which she had not yet shared with anyone.
