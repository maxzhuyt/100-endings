# The Thirty-Seven Letters of Monk Hideo, As Compiled by His Opponent

*Edited, with annotations, by Ōnishi Tadamune, Holder of the Tenrai-ha Line,*
*Winner of the Eleventh All-Province Invitational Tournament*

---

## Editor's Preface

The letters collected in this volume were received by me between November of 1962 and March of 2003. I have arranged them chronologically, preserving original dates where legible and estimating where they are not. The condition of the earliest letters is poor; letters one through four were written on monastery paper of low quality, and the ink has foxed badly along the folds. From the fifth letter onward Hideo appears to have secured a supply of better paper, possibly through the abbot at Eiheiji, and these have survived well. All letters are reproduced in full. I have made no excisions.

A note on calligraphy. In the first letters Hideo's hand is recognizable as the hand that signed tournament rosters in 1959, 1961, and 1962—upright, severe, with the brush held high. By the middle letters (roughly 1974–1986) the hand has loosened considerably, the strokes grown broader and more deliberate, suggesting a shift to a seated writing posture and possibly the onset of the joint stiffness he mentions in Letter Twenty-Two. The final letters are written in a hand I would not have attributed to the same man. Whether this represents deterioration or refinement, I leave to those more qualified in such matters than a retired swordsman.

I should state the relevant public facts. The Eleventh All-Province Invitational Tournament was held in October of 1962 at the Prefectural Civic Hall in Kōchi, under the auspices of the All Japan Kendo Federation. The final match was between myself and Hideo, then ranked fourth dan, representing Ehime Prefecture. The match ended at six minutes and fourteen seconds of the regulation period by forfeit, when Hideo removed his *men*, placed it on the floor of the court, bowed to the *shinpan*, and left the hall. He had entered no monasteries by that date but did so within the month. He never competed again. I was awarded the championship by decision of the tournament committee.

I have appended annotations to the letters where I believed clarification of references, dates, or context would be useful to the reader. I have tried to keep these brief and limited to matters of record. Where I have not succeeded in this, I ask the reader's patience.

The question of why I am publishing these letters now admits of a simple answer: I am seventy-nine, I have been diagnosed, and there is no one to whom I could entrust the task who would understand what they held. My students would see letters from a monk. Scholars would see a curiosity. I am not certain I see more than that myself. But the correspondence spans forty years. It was not invited and could not, for reasons I have never satisfactorily explained to myself, be refused. If there is a debt in this, I discharge it here.

A final technical note. I have transliterated all kendo terminology according to the conventions of the All Japan Kendo Federation's English-language publications. Where Hideo uses archaic or idiosyncratic terms from the Jigen-ryū or Ittō-ryū traditions, I have provided the standard modern equivalents in brackets. Page numbers refer to the manuscript notebook in which I transcribed the letters in 1998, when the originals had become too fragile for regular handling. The notebook will be deposited with the Kōchi Prefectural Archive upon my death.

Ōnishi Tadamune
Kōchi, 2003

## Letter One

*Received November 1962. Written on unheaded monastery paper. Undated. Postmarked Eiheiji, Fukui Prefecture.*

---

Ōnishi,

You will be surprised to receive this, and I apologize. There is likely a protocol for these situations and I am certain I am violating it. I have been here two weeks or three weeks—the days are not easy to distinguish from one another, which I gather is the purpose.

I wanted to ask about your elbow. In the semifinal against Watanabe you took a *kote* strike on the outside of the right forearm that looked incorrect—his angle was too steep and the shinai flexed badly. I saw you rotate the arm between points. At the time I meant to ask after the match whether you had been hurt. Then other considerations intervened. It has bothered me, in the way that small derelictions bother one when the larger derelictions are too vast to contemplate.

The food here is adequate. The sitting is difficult. I had not appreciated how much noise a body makes until I was asked to be silent.

I remember the light in the hall coming through those high western windows, falling across the court in bars. Very dramatic. Like fighting inside a cage made of light. But that may be memory editing itself again. Memory is a shameless revisionist.

I will not write again unless you wish it.

Hideo

---

**Annotation to Letter One** (Ō.T.): The Prefectural Civic Hall in Kōchi faces east-northeast. There are no western windows. The light in the hall entered through a bank of frosted glass panels on the eastern wall and was, by the afternoon final, indirect and flat. The bars of light Hideo describes did not exist. I state this not to impugn his memory but to be precise. I have had occasion to think about the light in that hall many times, and the light was grey, even, and without drama.

Regarding the elbow: it was bruised but not damaged. It did not affect my readiness for the final.

## Letter Two

*Received January 1963. Monastery paper. Dated by Hideo: "The first week of the new year."*

---

Ōnishi,

Your silence is appropriate and I do not read it as unkindness. I said I would not write again without invitation, and I find I have lied. Put it down to monastic boredom, if you're feeling generous, or to a deficiency of character, if you're not.

I have begun studying with Abbot Genshō. He is an unimpressive-looking person, very small, with ears that are too large for his head. He asked why I left competition and I told him the truth, or a version of it, which is that I had reached a point where continuing was impossible. He said: "Impossible is a word people use when they mean 'too expensive.'" I don't know if that's wise or merely clever, but it has kept me up at night, which may be the same thing.

I should tell you that I have not touched a shinai since arriving. They do not practice here. There is a bamboo grove behind the kitchen garden that makes a sound when the wind moves through it that is close to the sound of practice—close enough to be a problem.

I hope the new year finds you well and that the elbow has healed fully.

Hideo

---

**Annotation to Letter Two** (Ō.T.): Hideo's claim that his departure was prompted by having reached "a point where continuing was impossible" appears also in a 1967 interview with *Kendo Nippon* magazine, in which a reporter tracked him to Eiheiji and was granted ten minutes. The article quotes Hideo as saying: "I saw something on the court that ended my ability to compete." The article treats this as a spiritual experience. It was not, I believe, a spiritual experience.

## Letter Three

*Received March 1963. Monastery paper. Undated.*

---

Ōnishi,

Thank you for answering. Your letter was the first personal mail I have received here and the monk who distributes post looked at me with what I think was alarm, as though I had been discovered to have an outside life. I read it four times. Your handwriting is worse than mine, which is a remarkable achievement for someone who has not taken up monastery life.

You ask, plainly, why I forfeited. I owe you an answer and I will try to give one, though I suspect it will not arrive in this letter. Some things can only be said after sufficient runway—years of preamble, maybe, the way a pilot needs a long approach to land a heavy aircraft. Forgive the metaphor. I have been reading whatever the monastery library has, and it is an eccentric collection.

What I can say now: my forfeit was not a judgment on you, nor a commentary on the tournament's legitimacy, nor a protest of any kind. You would have been within your rights to interpret it as an insult, and I'm grateful you did not. Or if you did, you have been gracious enough to write back anyway.

I will say this much more and then stop: in the moment before a *men* strike, if you have practiced long enough, there is a quality of seeing that is not ordinary seeing. It is faster than language. The eyes take in the whole opponent—not the target, the *whole*—and in that fraction of a second you know things you have no right to know. I experienced this in our match. What I saw persuaded me to put down my *men*. I am not yet able to say more than this. I may never be.

Please write again.

Hideo

## Letter Four

*Received September 1963. Monastery paper. Dated: "Early autumn."*

---

Ōnishi,

Nine months of silence. I've earned it, I suppose. A man tells you he saw something nameless in your body during a match and then demurs, and you're meant to simply absorb this. I would be angry too, though you would of course never use that word.

The monastery persists. I have been assigned to the kitchen garden, which suits me. There is a satisfaction in vegetables that swordsmanship never provided: they don't argue, they submit to the blade willingly, and the result of cutting them is soup rather than tragedy. That was an attempt at levity. I'm told I should practice those.

Let me try a different approach. Let me tell you about Yamaoka Tesshū. You know the story—everyone in kendo knows the story—but let me tell it the way Abbot Genshō told it to me last week, because his version differs in an interesting way.

Tesshū, as a young man, was unable to defeat his teacher, Asari Gimei. The master was simply beyond him. For years Tesshū trained and challenged and lost. Then one morning, in meditation, Tesshū experienced what Zen calls *kenshō*—a moment of seeing into one's true nature—and when he next stood before Asari, the master bowed and said: "You have arrived." They never fought. Asari could see it in him. The contest was over before a blow was struck.

This is the standard version, the one that appears in the books. Genshō's version adds a detail I have not encountered elsewhere. He says that what Tesshū saw in his *kenshō* was not his own true nature. It was Asari's. He saw into the master so completely that striking became unnecessary because he could see, in the architecture of the master's body and breath, the single place where the master could be struck. And seeing this—seeing the master's mortality and humanness where before he had seen only an impenetrable wall—dissolved the need to prove anything.

Genshō says: "Tesshū did not surpass Asari. He simply saw him. And to truly see another person is to forfeit the competition, because competition requires that the other remain opaque."

I have been thinking about this for days. I suspect you will see why.

Hideo

---

**Annotation to Letter Four** (Ō.T.): The anecdote about Tesshū and Asari is well-documented in multiple sources, including Tesshū's own writings and Stevens' biography (*The Sword of No-Sword*, 1984, though of course this postdates Hideo's letter). The detail about Tesshū seeing Asari's vulnerability rather than his own nature is not attested in any source I have been able to locate. It may be Abbot Genshō's invention, or Hideo's.

The distinction matters.

## Letter Five

*Received April 1964. First letter on improved paper. Dated: "8 April, the second year of my residency."*

---

Ōnishi,

You write back and you do not ask. I don't know what to make of this. Perhaps you are employing the strategist's patience: give the opponent time and he will reveal his position. Perhaps you simply don't care—you have the championship, a teaching career presumably, and the unsolicited letters of a monastery dropout are a minor irritation you tolerate out of decency. Or perhaps—and this is the possibility that keeps me awake on my thin mattress in the cold—you already know, or suspect, and the knowing is something you have decided to carry silently, the way one carries any other wound that cannot be treated.

I have been re-reading Musashi. *The Book of Five Rings.* I last read it at sixteen and found it thrilling; I read it now at twenty-eight and find it terrifying. Not because of the violence but because of the seeing. Musashi says, in the Water Book: "The gaze should be large and broad. There are two kinds of sight: perception and seeing. Perception is strong; seeing is weak. To perceive the distant as near and the near as distant—this is essential." He is talking about combat distance, about *ma-ai*, about spatial judgment. But read it again. "To perceive the distant as near": to understand a faraway thing as though it were inside your own body. "And the near as distant": to hold at analytical remove the thing that is closest to you. This is how I experienced the final minute of our match. You were very near, two meters, in *chūdan*, and I saw you as though from a great distance, the way one sees a landscape from a mountain, every feature clear and legible and utterly beyond intervention. And simultaneously I understood something about you with the intimacy of something happening inside my own chest.

I know this sounds mystical. It is not. It was technical. What I saw was technical. That's the unbearable part.

I enclose pressed chrysanthemums from the garden. The autumn here was extraordinary.

Hideo

---

**Annotation to Letter Five** (Ō.T.): The chrysanthemums did not survive. Fragments of dried petals remained with the letter when I transcribed it in 1998. They were brown by then and had stained the bottom of the page.

Hideo's quotation from Musashi is accurate. I have used the Watanabe translation.

## Letter Seven

*Received February 1966. Dated: "2 February."*

What remains suggests it concerned principally the daily schedule at Eiheiji and Hideo's appointment as assistant garden-keeper. One legible passage reads: "…the body remembers what the mind prohibits. I was pulling radishes last week and my right hand assumed a grip I recognized only after several seconds as the…" The rest is lost. —Ō.T.]**

---
