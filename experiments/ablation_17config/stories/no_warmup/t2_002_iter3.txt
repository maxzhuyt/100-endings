# THE HOA BOARD WILL NOW COME TO ORDER

## I.

The ramp went up on a Saturday in October, the kind of day where the light comes through the maples at an angle that makes everything look the way you'll remember it later.

Their son-in-law, Craig, arrived at seven in the morning with a truckload of pine from Home Depot and a YouTube tutorial queued on his phone. Meredith watched from the kitchen window while he measured twice and cut once and then measured again because the first measurement didn't account for the lip of the porch. Leonard watched from his wheelchair on the driveway, offering commentary that Craig bore with the particular patience of a man who genuinely loved his wife's father and also genuinely wished his wife's father would stop talking.

"You've got a two-degree cant to the left," Leonard said.

"It's fine, Dad."

"I'm not saying it's not fine. I'm saying it's two degrees."

"How can you even—"

"Forty years of calculating risk for a living. You develop an eye."

The ramp took most of the day. It was unpainted pine. It was slightly too steep—ADA recommended a 1:12 slope ratio; Craig had built something closer to 1:10, which didn't sound like much unless you were the one in the chair. There was a place on the left side where a knot in the wood made the railing rough, and Meredith knew she'd need to sand it before Leonard absentmindedly dragged his palm across it and came away bleeding.

When it was done, Craig pushed Leonard up for the inaugural run, and Leonard raised both arms over his head like he was on a roller coaster, and Craig's daughters—nine and six—clapped from the lawn, and Meredith clapped too, and for one afternoon the house felt almost normal.

"Little steep," Leonard said at the top, breathing harder than he should have been.

"It's fine," Craig said.

"Feels like a ski jump."

"Do you want me to rebuild—"

"I want you to hand me a beer and sit down. You've been working all day."

They sat on the porch, the four adults, while the girls played in the yard. Meredith brought out a plate of sandwiches and the last of the good pickles, the ones she put up in August when things were still merely bad and not yet what they'd become. Leonard ate half a sandwich and drank most of a beer, and Meredith noted both of these things with the part of her brain that now tracked his intake the way he used to track actuarial tables—automatically, dispassionately, with full awareness of what the numbers meant.

They didn't talk about what the ramp was for. They didn't talk about the hospice paperwork on the desk in the study, or the fact that Dr. Petersen had used the phrase "making him comfortable" three times at the last appointment, which was two times more than necessary for information and exactly the right number of times for emphasis. They talked about Craig's job, and the girls' school, and whether the Phillies had a shot. They talked around the center of things, the way they'd been talking for months, orbiting the truth like it was something hot.

This was not avoidance. Meredith wanted to be clear about that, at least to herself. This was the vocabulary of a marriage that had lasted forty-one years—long enough to know that some things, once spoken, rearranged the air in a room permanently. Leonard knew his prognosis. She knew his prognosis. To say it aloud would not make it more true. It would only make it more present, and it was present enough already, thank you, sitting there in the wheelchair he'd been in for six weeks, rolling up the ramp their son-in-law built because Leonard's legs had decided to go first, as if his body were a building being vacated floor by floor.

After Craig and the girls left, Meredith wheeled Leonard back inside—down the ramp, which was indeed too steep, and through the garage because the front door still had the step they hadn't solved yet—and helped him into bed. The helping was its own careful negotiation. She held the transfer belt. He gripped the bed rail. They'd developed a system that preserved the fiction of his independence, a choreography in which she provided support while he provided the appearance of not needing it. They were good at this. They'd been practicing for months.

"Thank you," he said, when he was settled.

"For what?"

"I don't know. Everything. The pickles."

"Go to sleep, Leonard."

He was asleep in minutes. She stood in the doorway and watched him and did not cry, because she had made a deal with herself early on that she would not cry where he could hear, and the house was small, and his hearing was still perfect—his ears, like his mind, stubbornly untouched by whatever was dismantling the rest of him.

She went out on the porch that evening and stood at the top of Craig's ramp. It glowed pale in the porch light, new wood against the dark yard. It smelled like pine and possibility. It was not pretty. It was not up to code. Craig had missed a screw on the third board, and she could see the head poking up slightly, a tripping hazard she'd deal with tomorrow.

But it was there. And tomorrow Leonard could come out to the porch under his own power, could sit in the morning light with his coffee, could feel the October air without asking anyone's permission. That was the ramp. Not a concession. Not a white flag. A door.

Meredith went to bed.

## II.

The notice appeared on Tuesday.

It was laminated, which struck Meredith as both thorough and aggressive, as if the complaint might face inclement weather on its journey from the front door to the recycling bin. It was printed on cheerful yellow paper. There was a clip-art house in the upper right corner—a cartoon colonial with a chimney and a curlicue of smoke, the Platonic ideal of a home as imagined by someone who had never had to install a wheelchair ramp in one.

> **CEDARBROOK COMMONS HOMEOWNERS ASSOCIATION**
> **NOTICE OF VIOLATION**
>
> Dear Homeowner,
>
> This notice is to inform you that the property at **14 Briarwood Lane** has been found in violation of the Cedarbrook Commons CC&Rs, specifically:
>
> **Section 4.11.2 — Exterior Modifications**
> *"No exterior structural modification, addition, or alteration shall be made to any lot without prior written approval from the Architectural Review Committee."*
>
> **Nature of Violation:** Unauthorized exterior modification (wooden ramp structure, front porch)
>
> **Fine:** $50.00/day beginning from the date of this notice until the structure is (a) submitted for architectural review and approved, or (b) removed.
>
> We value our community standards and appreciate your prompt attention to this matter!
>
> Sincerely,
> *Doug Whitmore*
> President, Cedarbrook Commons Homeowners Association

Meredith read it twice. The exclamation point at the end—*we appreciate your prompt attention to this matter!*—seemed like it belonged to a different document, something about a bake sale or a neighborhood cleanup. She looked at the porch, at the ramp, at the yellow paper in her hand. Then she went inside, found the HOA directory, and called the management company.

"You've reached Pinnacle Community Management, serving Cedarbrook Commons Homeowners Association. Our office hours are Monday through Thursday, nine a.m. to four p.m. To report a maintenance issue, press one. For architectural review applications, press two. For all other inquiries, please leave a message after the tone, and a member of our team will return your call within three to five business days."

The tone.

"Hello, this is Meredith Hale at 14 Briarwood Lane. I received a violation notice today regarding a wheelchair ramp we installed for my husband, who is—who has a medical need for it. I think this must be a mistake, or maybe there's an exemption process I'm not aware of. Could someone please give me a call? My number is 610-555-0147. Thank you very much."

She hung up. She put the notice on the kitchen counter, next to the fruit bowl, where it sat like a piece of mail she hadn't quite decided whether to open. She did not tell Leonard because there was nothing to tell yet. It was a mistake. Someone had seen the ramp and filed a complaint without knowing the circumstances, and once the circumstances were known, the matter would resolve itself, because that was how things worked. You explained the situation. People understood. The system—whatever system—had room in it for common sense.

Three to five business days.

On day six, she called again.

## III.

The Cedarbrook Commons HOA held its monthly board meetings on the second Wednesday of every month at 7:00 p.m. in the community clubhouse, a squat brick building near the pool that smelled like chlorine in summer and damp carpet all year round. Attendance typically ranged from eight to fifteen residents, depending on whether anything controversial was on the agenda. Tonight, it was fourteen.

Doug Whitmore called the meeting to order at 7:02, which was itself a small performance—he believed in starting on time but not exactly on time, because exactly on time felt rigid, and Doug was not rigid. Doug was reasonable. Doug was a guy in a golf shirt, late fifties, with a full head of silver hair and the build of someone who had been athletic in college and kept just enough of it to look vital. He had a broad face, good teeth, and the handshake of a man who had read an article about handshakes.

"All right, let's get started. Welcome, everyone. I see some familiar faces and—Tom, is that you back there? Haven't seen you since the Fourth of July cookout. How's the knee?"

"Better," Tom said.

"Glad to hear it. Gail, did you do something different with your hair? Looks great."

Gail, who had done nothing different with her hair, touched it anyway and smiled.

"Okay. Before we get into the agenda, I brought a little something." Doug reached under the folding table and produced a foil-wrapped loaf. "Banana bread. Homemade. My grandmother's recipe—she was from Kentucky, used to say the secret was overripe bananas and a shot of bourbon. I leave the bourbon out, but don't tell her." He placed it on the table and unwrapped the foil. "Walnuts this time. I know some of you are Team No Walnuts, and I respect that, but I stand by my choices."

Gentle laughter. He cut the loaf with a plastic knife and passed slices around on napkins. People ate. It was good.

The agenda moved through the standard items: treasurer's report (the reserve fund was healthy, the landscaping contract had been renewed), maintenance updates (the pool pump was being replaced, the front entrance sign needed repainting), and a brief discussion about holiday decorations (lights were permitted November 15 through January 15; inflatable decorations remained prohibited per Section 6.4, "because, folks, I think we all remember the twelve-foot Santa incident of 2019").

Then the barking dog.

The dispute was between the Nguyens, on Cedarwood Court, and their neighbors, the Fiedlers. The Nguyens had recently adopted a rescue dog, a hound mix named Captain, who barked. Specifically, he barked when left alone, when the mail arrived, when squirrels existed, and—according to the Fiedlers—between the hours of 6:00 a.m. and 11:00 p.m., with strategic breaks for meals and naps.

Both parties were present. Mrs. Nguyen had brought a folder of documentation. Mr. Fiedler had brought audio recordings on his phone. The air in the clubhouse tightened the way air does when neighbors are about to stop being neighbors and start being litigants.

Doug held up one hand.

"Okay. I hear both of you. And I want to say—Tom, Karen—" He looked at the Nguyens. "What you did, adopting Captain, that's a good thing. Rescue dogs need homes, and it sounds like you've got a lot of love to give. And Dave—" He turned to Fiedler. "Your concerns are valid. Everyone deserves peace in their own home. That's not an unreasonable thing to want."

He leaned forward, both palms on the table.

"Here's what I'd like to propose. Karen, Tom—have you looked into bark training? There's a woman over in Devonshire, Linda Ferris, she does incredible work with rescues. I'll get you her number. Dave—I'm going to ask you to give it thirty days. Thirty days, and we revisit. If Captain's still causing a disruption, we'll figure out a next step together. But I think we owe it to our neighbors to give them a chance to solve this. Can everyone live with that?"

The Nguyens nodded. Fiedler hesitated, then nodded.

"Beautiful. Karen, I'll text you Linda's number tonight. She's a miracle worker. Dave, thank you for your patience. This is what Cedarbrook is about."

A new resident—young, early thirties, attending her first meeting—leaned to the woman beside her and whispered, "He seems great."

The woman beside her, who had lived in Cedarbrook Commons for twenty-two years and had once been fined $200 for leaving her garbage cans visible from the street on a Wednesday, said nothing.

Doug moved to close the meeting. "Before we adjourn—I just want to say, these meetings, this turnout, this is what makes Cedarbrook special. I've been on this board for fifteen years, and I've watched this community grow, and face challenges, and come together every time. This—" He gestured at the room, the people, the damp carpet, the banana bread. "This is what we're protecting. Not the rules for the sake of rules. The community. The thing we built together."

He smiled. It reached his eyes. It absolutely reached his eyes.

"Meeting adjourned. Don't forget banana bread."

## IV.

The next monthly meeting was on a Wednesday. The ramp had been up for twenty-five days. The fines had been accruing for twenty-two.

Meredith had never attended an HOA meeting in thirteen years of living in Cedarbrook Commons. She had paid her dues, kept her lawn within acceptable parameters, and regarded the HOA the way most people regard the water treatment plant—essential, invisible, and someone else's problem. She was, at sixty-three, a person whose life had been organized around competence and routine: thirty-five years as a data analyst for an insurance company, followed by a retirement she'd planned to spend gardening, reading, and growing gently old with Leonard on the porch he could no longer reach without a ramp some young man on a board wanted her to tear down.

She arrived at the clubhouse at 6:45, fifteen minutes early. She wore a blue cardigan and reading glasses on a chain around her neck. She carried a manila folder containing, in order: Leonard's diagnosis letter from Dr. Petersen, three photographs of the ramp printed on their home printer (slightly magenta, because the cyan cartridge was low), a handwritten summary of the ADA's requirements for residential accessibility (researched at the public library), and a copy of the violation notice.

She sat in the third row and waited.

Doug noticed her when he arrived at 6:55. Of course he noticed her. He noticed everything.

"Meredith! It's good to see you out. How's Leonard doing?"

He knew about Leonard. Everyone knew about Leonard. Cedarbrook was that kind of place—news traveled through dog walkers and mailbox encounters and the gentle surveillance of neighbors whose concern was real, even if their understanding was approximate.

"He has good days and bad days. Thank you for asking, Doug."

"You tell him we're thinking of him. The whole community. If there's anything we can do—"

"Actually, that's why I'm here. I'd like to address the board about the violation notice."

"Of course, of course. We have a public comment period right at the start. I'll make sure you get your chance."

He touched her shoulder as he moved past. It was warm. It was sincere. She hated that it was sincere.
