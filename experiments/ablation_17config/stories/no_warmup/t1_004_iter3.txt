# The Night Language

## I.

The mop was a pendulum, and the night was a clock, and Eamon was the mechanism between them.

He started on the third floor at 10 PM because Mrs. Ashbury in 304 fell asleep first and slept deepest and would not be disturbed by the wet hush of cotton fibers on linoleum. He worked east to west because the eastern corridor caught the runoff from a leaking HVAC unit and if he didn't get to it early the water would creep under the baseboard and into 311, where Mr. Pak kept his slippers on the floor beside his bed and would, in the morning, put his feet into damp cloth and say nothing about it because Mr. Pak had stopped complaining about anything months ago.

Eamon knew which floorboards announced him. There was a seam between the third-floor corridor and the alcove near the supply closet that popped like a knuckle if you stepped on its left edge. He stepped on its right edge. There was a threshold outside 202 that groaned under any weight at all, so he lifted the mop bucket and carried it across, the dirty water tilting but never spilling, his arms making the small adjustment they'd learned to make over eleven years in this building.

Eleven years. The same hallways. The same thirteen-watt fluorescents that made everything look like the inside of an eyelid. The same linoleum, cracked in a map of fissures he could have drawn from memory, each crack a tributary he'd traced a thousand times with the mop head, working the grime out of the channels because no one else would and because grime, left long enough, became permanent.

He did not think of himself as careful. Careful was a word for people who had to override a first impulse. Eamon had no first impulse that wasn't this. The building was his body's extension, and he moved through it the way blood moves through a limb—without announcement, without deliberation, because the limb requires it.

The patients knew him in their own ways. Some didn't know him at all, which was fine and right and as it should be. Some knew him as a shape that came when the lights were lowest, a shape that left behind a clean floor and an adjusted thermostat and, once, a hallway lamp re-angled so that its glare no longer fell across the face of a woman who slept on her back with her eyes not fully closed. The day nurse had moved the lamp back the next morning. Eamon had moved it again that night. This went on for a week until the day nurse forgot about it and the lamp stayed where Eamon put it and the woman slept without flinching, and nobody noted this as a victory because nobody noted it at all.

His muteness was not new. He had been born with vocal cords that did not do what vocal cords were supposed to do, or rather, they did it partially—enough to produce a hum, a grunt, a sound that a doctor had once described to his mother as "effortful phonation unlikely to yield functional speech." He was four when this was explained. He remembered the doctor's office, the thin paper on the examination table crinkling under him. He did not remember feeling that anything had been taken from him. You do not mourn a limb you never had. You simply learn that the world is built for people who have it, and you find the passages built for no one, and you move through those.

The hospice had hired him because he was cheap and because he showed up. He stayed because the dying did not require him to speak. This was not poetry; it was logistics. In a hospice, the technically living came and asked their questions—*Is she comfortable? Did he eat? How long?*—and the staff answered with varying calibrations of truth and mercy, and all of this required language, required the specific human machinery of vocal cords and syntax and the agreed-upon lie that words meant what they appeared to mean. Eamon was exempt from this economy. He cleaned. He maintained. He noticed. And because he could not participate in the building's spoken narrative, he perceived the one that ran below it—the narrative told by a water cup moved two inches closer to a bedside, by the particular rhythm of a ventilator that had shifted from its normal cadence, by the smell of a room in which something had changed.

He could not have explained this. He would not have wanted to. The mop was a pendulum. The night was a clock. He moved through it.

At shift change on a Tuesday, he came in through the service entrance as he always did and passed the front desk as he always did and nodded at Carmen, the evening RN who was gathering her things, as he always did. Carmen nodded back. Carmen was the only staff member who consistently acknowledged his arrival, not out of particular warmth but out of a habit of decency that had survived twelve years in end-of-life care.

She was talking to someone—Ronda from administration, who was never here at this hour.

"They just left it in the east dayroom," Carmen said. "I don't even know if the charging station is compatible with our outlets."

"It's compatible. I checked."

"You checked the outlets?"

"I checked the spec sheet. Three-prong, standard."

"Ronda, I don't have time to—"

"You don't have to do anything. It's autonomous. It does its own orientation. Walk it through the floor plan once and it maps the layout. After that, it self-directs."

"And medications?"

"It doesn't dispense. It monitors, repositions, does comfort checks. Basically a CNA minus the attitude." Ronda laughed at her own joke. Carmen did not. "Look, St. Anne's got two of them last year and their satisfaction scores went up eleven percent."

"St. Anne's has a budget."

"This one's free. Donated. Some tech company decommissioned a batch from a memory care facility in Scottsdale. Wiped and refurbished. The director signed off. It's already here, Carmen."

"It's better than nothing, I guess."

"That's the spirit."

Eamon set down his bucket and opened the supply closet. Behind him, the conversation continued in the precise bureaucratic dialect of people deciding things about other people's lives. He took out the mop, the floor cleaner, the caddy of rags and disinfectant he restocked each night. He closed the closet. He began his route.

On his first pass through the east dayroom, he saw it.

It stood beside the window. Not in the way a person stands beside a window—shifting weight, gazing out, drawn by some thought or restlessness—but in the way a floor lamp stands beside a window. It was there because someone had put it there and it had not yet been given a reason to be elsewhere. It was humanoid in the way these things usually were: close enough to trigger the brain's social instincts, distant enough to trigger its alarm system. It had a face—or rather, a facial region—composed of a matte polymer surface with slight contours suggesting cheekbones and a brow. Its eyes were dark and steady and did nothing that eyes were supposed to do. It did not blink. It did not track Eamon as he entered and crossed and left the room. It simply stood, powered on, waiting.

It was tall in the way that its designers had probably thought of as "nonthreatening"—just under average male height—and its frame was narrower than a person's would be, more like a suggestion of a body than a body itself. Its hands, though. Its hands were startlingly good. Eamon noticed this because he noticed hands the way some people noticed faces. The android's hands had fingertips with visible dermal texture. Its joints articulated smoothly. Its wrists moved with the kind of fluidity that cost money, the kind of engineering that someone had lavished attention on. These were hands built to touch people, and they had been built by someone who understood what that required.

He mopped around the dayroom. The android stood by the window. The floor under its feet was clean, because it had not moved, because it had no weight to track in dirt, because it existed in this building without leaving evidence of itself.

Later, near the end of the overlap before the night shift settled fully into its quiet, he saw it being walked through the second-floor corridor by a maintenance tech who'd come in for the express purpose of "onboarding" it—a word the tech used three times in the five minutes Eamon was within earshot. The tech walked ahead. The android followed. Its gait was fluid, nearly silent, a smooth locomotion that belonged to a building with polished hardwood and recessed lighting, not to this one with its buckling floor tiles and its fluorescent tubes that buzzed at a frequency only Eamon and the roaches seemed to hear.

It moved too well for here. That was his first thought—not a thought exactly, but a physical awareness, the way you feel a new piece of furniture in a room before you consciously register it. The building had a rhythm. Everything in it was slightly broken, slightly lagging, slightly off-true. The patients. The equipment. The staff. Eamon himself. This building held what was wearing down, and it held it patiently, and nothing in it glided.

The android glided.

He watched it round the corner with the tech. Then he wrung out the mop and moved on.

---

The first full night.

He became aware of it the way he became aware of most things in the building: not by confrontation but by accretion. A presence in his periphery. A door that was slightly more ajar than he'd left it. A room that, when he entered to empty a wastebasket, carried the faint trace of movement—a curtain still settling, a chair displaced by inches.

The android was making its rounds.

He worked his route. Third floor, east to west. Second floor, west to east. First floor, common areas and the intake office that always smelled like burned coffee and copier toner. Back up to the third floor for the 1 AM check—his unofficial, unasked-for pass through the patient rooms, the one that wasn't in his job description and never had been but that he did because if he didn't, the gap between Carmen's last round at midnight and the night nurse's first check at 2:30 was a long time for someone to lie in the dark with a dry mouth or a twisted sheet or a pain that had crept in from a new direction.

On this pass, he found the android in room 308.

The door was open. The room was occupied by June Tessaro, eighty-one, pancreatic, the kind of dying that was slow and then suddenly not. She was asleep, her body making the small involuntary adjustments of shallow rest—a foot twitching under the blanket, a hand opening and closing against the mattress. The heart monitor beeped its iambic reassurance. The oxygen concentrator hummed its lower, steadier note. The room had the sound of a body being mechanically witnessed.

The android stood beside the bed. Its hands—those exquisitely engineered hands—were lifting the edge of June Tessaro's pillow.

Eamon stopped in the doorway.

The android lifted the pillow a quarter inch on the left side and slid its palm beneath it, adjusting something—the angle, the loft, the distribution of fill—and then withdrew. The motion took perhaps three seconds. It was so smooth and so contained that it appeared rehearsed, but it did not appear mechanical, which was a distinction Eamon could feel but could not have articulated. A mechanical gesture repeated itself identically each time. This gesture had specificity. It was calibrated to *this* pillow, *this* neck, *this* degree of compression.

And it had happened *before* June moved.

Her head had been in the earliest phase of a shift—the muscular prelude to a change of position that hadn't yet reached consciousness—and the android had adjusted the pillow to receive the new angle before the head arrived there. As if it had heard the discomfort before the body produced it. As if it were listening to a frequency below the audible.

June's head settled into the new position. She did not wake. Her breathing eased by a degree so slight that only Eamon, who had listened to breathing in dark rooms for eleven years, would have registered it.

The android moved to the side table. A water cup, plastic, half full, with a bent straw. June's right hand was at her side, fingers slightly curled, nowhere near the cup. The android relocated the cup two inches closer to the bed's edge and angled the straw toward where a reaching hand would expect to find it. June had not reached for it. June was asleep. But when she woke—at 2 or 3 or 4 AM, with the thick-throated dryness that woke most of them—the straw would be where her fingers went before her eyes opened.

The android moved to the foot of the bed. It lifted the folded blanket that lay across the bed's lower rail—the second blanket, the thin thermal one that the hospice stocked in bulk—and opened it with a single lateral motion that made no sound and disturbed no air and settled it over June's legs and feet with the evenness of a snowfall. This was at 1:07 AM. At 1:09 AM, June's foot, the one that had been twitching, stilled under the new warmth. A shiver that had been building in her calves—a shiver Eamon had not even noticed—resolved before it fully arrived.

The android stood for a moment. Its dark, unblinking eyes rested on June's face, or seemed to. It was impossible to know what it perceived, or if *perceived* was even the right word. Then it turned and moved toward the door.

It passed Eamon in the doorway.

It did not acknowledge him. It did not adjust its path to go around him. It simply moved through the space it was going to move through, and that space happened to have no one in it, because Eamon had already stepped aside—not out of deference, but in the way he stepped aside for gurneys and medication carts and all the other objects that moved through this building on their own logic. The android passed, and the air it displaced was neither warm nor cold, and it carried no scent, and it made no sound on the linoleum, and it was gone.

Eamon stood in the doorway of 308 and looked at June Tessaro sleeping. The angled pillow. The positioned straw. The blanket his own hands had not thought to unfold.

Something in his chest that was not a word and not a feeling but some third thing—a recognition, perhaps, or the space where a recognition would go when it was ready—shifted, and settled, and went quiet.

He picked up his mop. He moved on.

## II.

Four nights in.

He was on the second floor, 2:40 AM, emptying the small wastebasket in room 217. The android was already in the room.
