### AUDIO DIARY — PERSONAL ARCHIVE OF M. KOPEK
### ENTRY 001 / CASSETTE A-SIDE / DATED MARCH 2, 1987

*(Sound of a chair adjusting. A small cough. When the voice begins, it is measured, unhurried, and precisely enunciated—the voice of a woman who has spent her professional life listening to the exact shape of every syllable.)*

This is Maren Kopek, C.S.R. number 4179, licensed in the state of Ohio. Today is Monday, March the second, 1987. I'm beginning a personal audio log to run concurrent with my official duties on a new assignment. I want to be clear about the purpose of this recording, mostly for my own benefit: this is a professional exercise. I have kept similar logs for three of my last four major proceedings, and I find that verbal self-review improves my accuracy rate. My accuracy rate is already 99.6 percent. I would like it to be higher.

*(Pause. The sound of a tea kettle being lifted from a burner.)*

I've been reassigned to Division Three of the Sutton County Court of Common Pleas, effective this morning. Criminal docket. The assignment is one case: *State of Ohio v. Eleanor Ruth Vance*, case number 87-CR-4421. I'll summarize the case in a moment. First I want to note the circumstances of the assignment itself, because I believe in thorough documentation.

I requested this posting. That's not typical for me—I generally go where the office sends me without complaint. I process the docket, I produce the record, I go home. But the position came open suddenly in January when Denise Hartwell vacated it. Denise was the regular Division Three reporter, had been for nearly two years. We weren't friends, exactly—Denise was social in a way I've never managed to be—but we were collegial. We sat near each other at the holiday party in December and she seemed fine. Content. She'd mentioned the Vance case was coming and that it would be significant. She seemed, if anything, energized by it.

Then in January she simply … wasn't there. The office said she'd left the position. No forwarding information. No goodbye. In this profession, burnout is not uncommon. Court reporters hear difficult things. We absorb the worst of what people do to each other and we render it into clean, neutral text, and some people find that the neutrality is what undoes them—that the act of stripping horror of its emotional register and presenting it as typography is its own kind of violence. I've never felt that way. To me, the record is a public good. The record is the only thing that stands between a citizen and the arbitrary exercise of state power. The record is sacred, and I am its instrument. I don't editorialize. I don't react. I am not a participant in proceedings. I am a mechanism of preservation—a very accurate mechanism—and I have made my peace with that.

*(The kettle is set down. Tea being poured.)*

Which is a long way of saying that I asked for the Vance case because it will be high-profile and a high-profile case handled with precision is good for a career. The chief reporter approved the transfer without fuss. I begin tomorrow.

Let me note the time: this entry was recorded at 9:14 PM.

I'll describe the case in a separate entry after I've reviewed the full docket sheet. For now, I'm going to proofread tomorrow's exhibits list and go to bed.

*(Click.)*

**SUMMARY OF FACTS AS ALLEGED BY THE STATE:**

The defendant, Eleanor Ruth Vance (d.o.b. 11/09/1951), has been employed as a licensed social worker with the Sutton County Department of Child Protective Services since 1979. In this capacity, she served as the assigned caseworker for the above-named children, all of whom were in the foster care system under the jurisdiction of the Sutton County Juvenile Court.

Between January 1985 and March 1986, the three deceased children died in separate foster placements. In each case, death was attributed initially to natural causes—specifically, to a cascade of multi-organ failure of indeterminate etiology. The children had no prior history of chronic illness. They were between the ages of 4 and 7 at the time of death.

Investigation was initiated in June 1986, following the hospitalization of Miles David Welles (age 5), who presented with symptoms consistent with the early-stage organ failure observed in the three deceased children. Miles survived. His blood work revealed traces of an unidentified compound that the State's forensic toxicologist has characterized as consistent with a plant-derived alkaloid poison.

Subsequent investigation established that the defendant was the sole individual with unsupervised access to all four children. The State will present evidence that the defendant used her professional position to secure private visitation hours, during which she administered the substance to the children under the guise of nutritional supplements.

**Re: Allegations of Ritualistic Elements:**

The investigation was complicated in its early stages by community allegations that the children's deaths were connected to a "satanic ritual abuse" network operating in Sutton County. These allegations originated from interviews with surviving foster children conducted by Dr. Annabel Portnow, a child psychologist retained by the Department of Child Protective Services, whose interview methodology has since been challenged as leading and contaminating. Dr. Portnow's recorded sessions with the children include allegations of robed figures, animal sacrifice, underground chambers, and "magic words that make you go to sleep."

**The State's position** is that these ritual abuse allegations are unfounded and are the product of contaminated interview techniques amplified by media coverage and community hysteria. The State notes the national context: the McMartin Preschool case in California (1983–present), the Jordan, Minnesota cases (1983–84), the Kern County cases (1982–86), and numerous other proceedings in which allegations of organized satanic abuse have been advanced on the basis of unreliable child testimony and subsequently collapsed.

The State will argue that the defendant either (a) deliberately cultivated an atmosphere of satanic panic as a smokescreen for straightforward acts of poisoning motivated by Munchausen syndrome by proxy, or (b) suffered from delusional beliefs about satanic practices but was nonetheless legally competent and capable of forming the specific intent to kill.

**The Defense** has entered a plea of not guilty. Defense counsel has indicated an intent to pursue a diminished capacity defense, arguing that the defendant experienced a psychotic break precipitated by professional burnout and that she came to sincerely believe, under the influence of the same community hysteria, that the children were in danger from supernatural forces—and that her actions, however misguided, were intended to *protect* them. Defense counsel has further indicated an intent to challenge the forensic toxicology evidence and to present alternative medical explanations for the children's deaths.

**The Court notes** that this case has attracted significant media attention and that jury selection will require particular care.

THE COURT: Thank you. Juror number twenty-two, please state your name for the record.

PROSPECTIVE JUROR #22: James Delafield.

THE COURT: Thank you, Mr. Delafield. Please remain standing. Counsel for the State may proceed.

MR. GARRETT: Good morning, Mr. Delafield. You heard me describe the general nature of this case during our initial remarks. I'd like to ask you a few questions about your ability to serve fairly. Do you have any personal connection to the Sutton County Department of Child Protective Services?

PROSPECTIVE JUROR #22: No, sir.

MR. GARRETT: Have you or anyone in your immediate family ever been involved in the foster care system, either as a foster parent, a foster child, or in a professional capacity?

PROSPECTIVE JUROR #22: No.

MR. GARRETT: Thank you. I need to ask you about a sensitive subject. In my opening remarks, I mentioned that this case involves allegations — allegations that the State believes to be unfounded — of satanic ritual abuse. Have you followed any of the news coverage about similar allegations in other parts of the country? The McMartin case, for instance?

PROSPECTIVE JUROR #22: I've seen things on the news. Sure.

MR. GARRETT: And have you formed any opinions about those cases?

PROSPECTIVE JUROR #22: Not really. I figure that's for the courts to decide.

MR. GARRETT: That's a fair answer. Let me ask you this: if the evidence in this trial were to include testimony about rituals, about the occult, about things that might sound outlandish — would you be able to evaluate that testimony based on the evidence presented in this courtroom, rather than on any preconceptions you might hold?

PROSPECTIVE JUROR #22: Yes, sir. I believe so.

MR. GARRETT: The State has no objection to this juror, Your Honor.

THE COURT: Mr. Ostrander?

MR. OSTRANDER: Thank you, Your Honor. Mr. Delafield, good morning.

PROSPECTIVE JUROR #22: Morning.

MR. OSTRANDER: My client, Ms. Vance, is a woman who has been accused of terrible things. If you were selected for this jury, would you be able to look at her and presume her innocence until the State proves otherwise beyond a reasonable doubt?

PROSPECTIVE JUROR #22: I would. That's how it's supposed to work.

MR. OSTRANDER: I appreciate that. Now, you said you've seen news coverage about ritual abuse cases. Did any of that coverage cause you to form an opinion about whether satanic cults actually exist and operate in American communities?

PROSPECTIVE JUROR #22: I think some of those cases have been blown out of proportion, to be honest. But I don't know enough to say for certain one way or another.

MR. OSTRANDER: That's very candid. No further questions. The defense accepts this juror, Your Honor.
