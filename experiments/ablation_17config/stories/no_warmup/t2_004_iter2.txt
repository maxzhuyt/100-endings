# THE HATEFUL IMMORTALS

## A Documentary Account by Dr. Amelia Thornton, Department of Extranormal Studies, Columbia University

---

## ACT ONE

### "IN THE BEGINNING, THERE WAS LOVE (AND THEN APPROXIMATELY 63,000 ARGUMENTS)"

---

I want to be careful about what I call this.

I have spent eleven years researching the marriage of Margaret Greenbriar and James Chen — eleven years of archival work, oral histories, legal documents obtained under the Freedom of Information Act and, in three notable instances, through less sanctioned channels. I have read their letters. I have sat in their homes. I have listened to them speak about each other with a degree of candor that I suspect they have afforded no one else, partly because I earned their trust and partly because, after 157 years of marriage, they have each accumulated such a vast treasury of grievances that the prospect of a sympathetic listener with a recording device proved irresistible to both of them.

I am not a marriage counselor. I am not a therapist. I am a historian of extranormal phenomena, and what I have attempted to compile here is a scholarly account of the longest continuously documented human pair-bond in recorded history. I have tried to be objective. I have tried to treat Margaret and James as subjects rather than characters, as people rather than archetypes.

I have not always succeeded.

What follows is, I believe, the most complete account of their shared life that exists outside their own memories — though "memories," in Margaret's case, is a word that requires considerable qualification. I have organized it chronologically, supplemented by what I've come to think of as exhibits: the letters, the legal filings, the interviews with historians and neighbors and one deeply unfortunate couples therapist from Topeka. I have also included direct interviews with Margaret and James themselves, conducted separately at their mutual and vehement insistence, and once — exactly once — conducted together, at my insistence.

That joint session lasted eleven minutes. The transcript is included in Appendix F. I do not recommend reading it on a full stomach.

I will let the reader decide what this is. A love story. A tragedy. A case study in the outer limits of human stubbornness. What I can say, with the full weight of my academic credentials behind me, is that it is a *phenomenon* — one that has outlasted governments, wars, the invention of flight, the splitting of the atom, and, as of last Tuesday, Margaret's patience with James's continued refusal to learn how a dishwasher works.

Let us begin at the beginning. Or rather, at two beginnings, because Margaret Greenbriar and James Chen were fully formed disasters long before they found each other.

---

For the uninitiated — though at this point in history, I would hope the uninitiated are few — a brief contextualization is warranted.

Extranormal abilities have been documented in the human population for as long as there have been humans to document them. They manifest most commonly at or near puberty, they are genetically unpredictable, and they range in scope from the profoundly consequential to the aggressively mundane. The reader will be familiar with the broad categories codified by the Department of Extranormal Affairs in 1934: somatic, cognitive, environmental, and anomalous. The reader will also be familiar with the DEA itself, in the same way one is familiar with the Department of Motor Vehicles — a vast, underfunded bureaucracy staffed by people who are, by and large, doing their best under circumstances that would drive anyone to drink.

The relevant point for our purposes is this: by the mid-nineteenth century, "specials" — the colloquial term that Margaret detests and James uses with cheerful indifference — were a known quantity, woven into the social fabric with all the grace and consistency that human societies typically bring to things they do not fully understand. Which is to say: poorly, unevenly, and with a great deal of paperwork.

Margaret Greenbriar and James Chen each manifested abilities that were, individually, remarkable. In combination, they produced a marriage that I can only describe as *structurally adversarial at the molecular level* while also being, against all evidence and reason, genuine.

### I. MARGARET, BEFORE

Margaret Greenbriar was born in Boston, Massachusetts, in 1847, the second daughter of Edmund and Clara Greenbriar. Edmund was a printer by trade and an abolitionist by conviction — not the polite, parlor-room variety, but the kind who hid fugitives in his cellar and kept a loaded pistol in his typesetter's drawer. Clara was a teacher of mathematics at a small women's academy, a woman of ferocious organizational skill who could, family letters suggest, silence a room by adjusting her spectacles.

Margaret inherited her father's moral certainty and her mother's spectacles trick. She was, by all surviving accounts, a difficult child in the way that intelligent girls in constrictive eras are often difficult: she asked too many questions, she argued with her teachers, she read books that weren't assigned and then argued about those, too. A letter from her elder sister, Ruth, to a cousin in 1858 — Margaret would have been eleven — contains the following observation: *"Meg has taken to correcting Father at supper on matters of historical fact, and while she is invariably right, I do wish she would learn to be right more quietly."*

She would not learn to be right more quietly. This remained true at the time of our most recent interview, 166 years later.

What matters about Margaret before her power is this: the architecture of her personality was already in place. The precision, the moral seriousness, the incapacity for letting things go — these were not symptoms of her ability. They were Margaret. Her ability simply made them *permanent*.

It manifested in the autumn of 1860. She was thirteen.

The accounts are secondhand — Margaret's own recollection of the event is, of course, perfect, but she has always been reluctant to describe the initial onset in detail. What we know, primarily from Clara Greenbriar's diary, is this: Margaret woke one morning and could recall, with absolute fidelity, every moment of a dream she had had the night before. Not the hazy residue of dream-memory that fades with breakfast, but the complete sensory record — the colors, the sounds, the temperature of dreamed air, the precise emotional texture of each moment. She told her mother. Clara, who was a practical woman, tested her: read her a page of Latin text Margaret had never seen before, and asked her to repeat it an hour later. Margaret recited it forward, backward, and with annotations on the typeface.

Within a week, the family understood the scope. Margaret could not merely *choose* to remember. She remembered *everything*. Every sound, every smell, every shifting quality of light, every word spoken in her presence, every emotion she felt — preserved in full fidelity, accessible instantly, immutable. Her mind had become a recording device of perfect resolution and infinite storage, and it had no off switch.

Clara's diary records the early wonder: Margaret helping her father typeset from memory, reciting poetry she'd heard once as if reading from an open book, delighting her family with perfect recall of happy moments — *Do you remember when we went to the harbor and the seagull stole Father's hat? I can still feel the wind. I can still hear him shout.* The family marveled. Margaret marveled.

The marvel curdled within months.

There exists a letter from Margaret to Ruth, written in early 1861, that I consider one of the most important documents in the Greenbriar-Chen archive. Margaret was fourteen. She wrote:

> *Ruth, I must tell someone and I choose you because you are far enough away that your pity cannot reach me in person. Father and I quarreled last Thursday over a matter too small to recount. He was disappointed in me. I could see it in his face — a particular arrangement of his features that I have catalogued without meaning to, the way his left eye narrows slightly more than his right when he is not angry but something worse. He forgave me within the hour. I know he forgave me because he said so and because I could see the forgiveness in that same face. But the other face has not gone. It is there when I close my eyes. It is there now, as I write this, as clear as the paper before me. It has not faded one degree. I am beginning to understand that it will not.*

She was fourteen years old, and she already knew. She could not forget. Not selectively. Not gradually. Not at all. Every wound would remain fresh, every slight preserved in amber, every unkind word stored at its original volume.

Her mother taught her the only survival skill that mattered: performance. Clara Greenbriar, with the pragmatism of a woman who had survived decades of New England social maneuvering, taught her youngest daughter to *act* as though she had forgiven, to *perform* forgetting like a parlor trick, to smile and say "I don't recall" with the facility of a trained actress. It was a kindness. It was also, in a sense, a deformation. Margaret learned to pretend she carried nothing, and people believed her, and the weight accumulated in silence.

In the context of her era, the family's caution was justified. "Specials" in the 1860s occupied an uneasy position: objects of scientific fascination, targets of religious suspicion, occasionally useful, frequently feared. Edmund and Clara kept Margaret's ability quiet — not secret, precisely, but contained. She was a bright girl with a good memory. Leave it at that.

She was not a bright girl with a good memory. She was a girl who could hear her dead grandmother's voice with the clarity of a living woman standing beside her, who could feel the precise texture of her childhood blanket decades after it had been given away, who could access any moment of joy or pain she had ever experienced with full emotional fidelity at will or, more often, without it.

She was, in short, a person for whom the past was never past. Remember this. It is the hinge on which everything turns.

### II. JAMES, BEFORE

James Chen was born in Boston in 1849, the son of Wei Chen and Lihua Chen, née Fang. Wei ran a modest import business serving Boston's small but growing Chinese community — teas, fabrics, sundries from Guangdong province, and occasionally, when tariffs permitted, a very good chili oil that James later described to me, with considerable emotion, as "the only thing my father made that outlasted me, because someone in Quincy still makes it from his recipe, and it's wrong, they use too much sesame, and don't write that down."

I wrote it down.

James was the third of five children and by all accounts the most exhausting. Where Margaret was difficult in the manner of a loaded crossbow — precise, potentially dangerous, aimed — James was difficult in the manner of a golden retriever that has been given coffee. He charmed people. He talked too much. He befriended everyone on his street and half the people on the next street and a dog named President that he would mention to me, 170 years later, with uncomplicated fondness. He was warm and reckless and stubborn in a way he didn't recognize as stubbornness because he'd papered it over so thoroughly with good cheer.

His father's trading business placed the family in a precarious position: prosperous enough to be visible, Chinese enough to be targeted, dependent on goodwill that could evaporate with a shift in politics or weather. James grew up understanding, as children of immigrants often do, that charm was a survival tool — that the difference between being tolerated and being attacked could be as narrow as a smile.

He was also, in a way that becomes important later, an optimist of almost pathological dimensions. Not because he hadn't seen cruelty — he had, from the age of five, regularly and without apology — but because something in his constitution oriented him toward the next thing rather than the last thing. The insult happened; the insult was over; what's for dinner? This was, at the time, a coping mechanism. Over the next century and a half, it would calcify into something more complicated.

His power manifested in 1863. He was fourteen.

The documentary record for this event is, unlike Margaret's quiet awakening, sharply and violently clear, because it happened in public and it involved a significant quantity of blood.

Boston in 1863 was a city in the convulsions of war, and the racial violence that had always simmered beneath its abolitionist reputation had found new pretexts to surface. What we know, from a police report I obtained from the Massachusetts State Archives and from three corroborating witness accounts collected by a journalist named Edwin Pace, is this: James was set upon by a group of men — the report identifies four; Pace's sources say six — on the evening of September 12, outside his father's shop. The inciting cause was, in the language of the police report, "the Celestial youth's presence." They beat him. Thoroughly. One of them, per Pace, used a dockworker's hook.

James died.

This is not speculation. Two of the witnesses — a grocer across the street and a woman in a second-floor window — described injuries incompatible with survival. The grocer, a man named Alton Reed, gave a statement to Pace that reads in part: *"The boy was opened up like a fish. I have been to war and seen men die of less. I crossed myself and looked away, and when I looked back, the boy was standing."*

The men fled. James stood in the street, covered in his own blood, and watched his body close itself back together. He told me he remembers looking down at his shirt — his favorite shirt, he noted, with the aggrieved tone of a man who has nursed this particular sartorial grudge across three centuries — and seeing the fabric ruined but the skin beneath it whole and new.

He was terrified. Briefly.

"I was scared for about ten minutes," James told me during our first interview, leaning back in a kitchen chair with the studied ease of a man who has been leaning back in kitchen chairs and alarming his wife for over a century. "And then I was something else. I don't have a good word for it. *Free* isn't right, but it's close. I was fourteen years old and I was a Chinese kid in Boston and people had just tried to kill me and I got up. I *got up.* Do you understand what that meant? Do you understand what it means to a kid like me, in that city, in that year, to know that they can't? That no matter what they do, you'll get up?"

I noted that his hands were not entirely steady as he said this. He noticed me noticing and put them in his pockets.

"Margaret would say I'm being reductive," he added. "She'd say I'm skipping the parts that mattered. The fear, the pain. She'd say I always skip those."

He paused.

"She'd be right. She usually is. Don't write that down, either."

The aftermath was immediate and predictable. An unkillable Chinese boy was news — dangerous news. The DEA did not yet exist; the federal government's apparatus for managing extranormal individuals was embryonic and largely confined to military applications. But attention was attention, and the Chen family understood that the kind of attention James had attracted was the kind that ended with men in uniforms at one's door, asking polite questions with impolite implications.

The family sent James away. To relatives in San Francisco, initially. Then further. The specifics are murky — James's own recollection of the period is suspiciously vague, and I suspect there are chapters he has chosen not to share with me. What is clear is that he spent the next two years in a peripatetic state, moving between Chinese communities up and down the Pacific coast, learning to be the kind of person who can leave a place without looking back.

This is a skill he became extraordinarily good at. It is, not coincidentally, the skill that Margaret finds most unforgivable.

The relevant psychological architecture, for our purposes, is this: James emerged from his adolescence as a person who oriented toward the future. What happened before was *over*. What mattered was what came next. He could not be killed, which meant (in the logic of a fourteen-year-old boy, which is to say, in the logic he would maintain with remarkable consistency for 170 years) that nothing was permanent except himself. Pain was temporary. Loss was temporary. He would outlive everything that hurt him, so why carry it?
