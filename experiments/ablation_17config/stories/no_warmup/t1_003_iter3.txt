# THE COLLECTORS

## I.

The storage unit was fourteen feet by twenty, climate-controlled, and Maren Alcott visited it the way other people visited church—dutifully, shamefully, with the desperate hope that this time the ritual might quiet whatever howled inside her.

She knelt on the concrete floor at 11:47 p.m., still wearing the silk blouse and pressed trousers she'd worn to the Henderson closing that afternoon, and she sorted. That was the word she used internally. *Sorting.* Not hoarding. Hoarding was what broken people did in broken houses with newspapers stacked to the waterline and dead cats mummifying under drifts of QVC merchandise. Maren was not broken. Maren sold four-point-two million dollars in residential real estate last year. Maren's own home was a study in Scandinavian minimalism—white walls, blond wood, a single orchid on the kitchen island—because Maren understood, better than anyone, the seductive lie of an empty room.

The storage unit was where the truth lived.

She had organized it beautifully, which she felt ought to count for something. Clear plastic bins, labeled. Shelving units from The Container Store, which she recognized as a specific form of irony but purchased anyway. But organization was just the scaffolding. Underneath, the accumulation was pathological and she knew it: fourteen identical white bath towels still in their packaging. A box of hotel soaps—not from hotels she'd stayed at, but collected from estate sales and thrift stores and, on one nauseating occasion, from a Marriott housekeeping cart she'd rifled while pretending to look for the ice machine. Seven door handles, brass, Victorian. A child's bicycle helmet, pink, size small, which she'd bought at a garage sale in Tacoma three years ago and which had nothing to do with her own daughter, who was nine and preferred purple and had never been to Tacoma. A Camp Alder Lake T-shirt, small, sun-faded to the color of a bruise, which she did not remember acquiring and could not bring herself to throw away.

She was holding the T-shirt now, not looking at it, running her thumb across the cracked screenprint of a pine tree while she rearranged a shelf of porcelain figurines with her other hand, when her phone buzzed.

The shift was instantaneous. She dropped the shirt into a bin, stood, brushed dust from her knees, and answered with the voice she used for clients: warm, competent, unbothered.

"Hey, baby."

"Mom." Her daughter Bex's voice was groggy and accusatory, the special register reserved for mothers caught doing something suspicious. "It's almost midnight. Where are you?"

"Just finishing up some staging at the Ecklund property. Big open house Saturday."

"You said you'd be home by ten."

"I know. I'm sorry. I'm leaving right now."

"You always say that."

Something seized in Maren's chest. The thing her therapist—her real therapist, not the app-based one she told her ex-husband about—called *the clutch.* That sensation of a hand closing around her sternum, squeezing. The feeling that if she left this space, something essential would be lost. That the next time she came back, the unit would be empty, everything gone, and she would have nothing, she would *be* nothing—

"Five minutes, Bex. I promise. Is Dad asleep?"

"He's watching that documentary about mushrooms again."

"Perfect. Don't tell him I called late. I'm walking to my car right now."

She walked to her car. She locked the unit. She drove home.

On the passenger seat, where she would not notice it until the next morning and would not be able to explain how it had gotten there, sat the Camp Alder Lake T-shirt, folded into a neat square, smelling of mildew and pine and something older and worse.

---

The letter was in the mailbox.

Not the email. The physical mailbox, the black metal one at the end of the driveway that mostly received campaign flyers and Pottery Barn catalogs addressed to the previous owner. Maren found it at seven-fifteen a.m., after dropping Bex at school, and she stood in the driveway in the thin October light and read it twice before her hands began to shake.

It was typed on plain white paper. No return address. No signature. It read:

> *Dear Maren,*
>
> *I know about the summer of 2003. I know what happened at the boathouse. I know what you four carried into the woods that night, and I know where you buried it.*
>
> *The ground at Camp Alder Lake is being sold to a development company. Excavation begins tomorrow morning at dawn.*
>
> *You should come tonight. All four of you. Before the machines do.*
>
> *You know the place.*

At the bottom, in handwriting—cramped, slanted, weirdly precise—was a single line:

> *You kept everything else. Did you really think you could throw this away?*

Maren's knees did not buckle. Her vision did not swim. She had spent twenty years practicing the art of continued functioning in the presence of unbearable information, and so she folded the letter neatly, placed it inside her purse, walked into the house, locked the door, entered the downstairs bathroom, ran the faucet, and sat on the tile floor shaking until her fitbit buzzed with a reminder to drink water.

Then she called Theo.

---

Theo Voss answered on the seventh ring, which meant he'd been debating whether to pick up, which meant he already knew.

"Maren."

"You got one too."

A long silence. Behind him, she could hear the specific acoustic signature of a large, expensive, mostly empty room. Theo was an architect. He designed museums and performance centers and corporate headquarters that appeared in *Dezeen* and *Architectural Digest,* and he lived alone in a loft in Portland that he'd renovated so obsessively that every surface was a manifesto about light and negative space. He was enormously successful and very thin and he had not had a long-term relationship in twelve years, a fact he attributed to his work schedule and which had nothing whatsoever to do with the fact that he stole things.

Not from stores. That was important to Theo, or had been, before the distinction collapsed. He stole from job sites. He stole from clients. He stole from friends' houses—a specific spoon, a particular book, a child's drawing off a refrigerator—with a deftness that was genuinely astonishing, an almost surgical ability to identify the one object in a room that someone treasured most and to make it vanish. He had been caught only once, at a dinner party, slipping the host's dead mother's reading glasses into his coat pocket. He told them he'd mistaken them for his own. They believed him because he was Theodore Voss, and Theodore Voss would never.

He would. He did. He couldn't stop.

"Mine was waiting at my office," he said. "Slid under the door. Maren, the wording—"

"I know."

"Someone was *there.*"
