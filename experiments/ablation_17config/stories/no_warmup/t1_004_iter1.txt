# The Divided Caretaker

## I.

The mop moved in long, deliberate arcs across the linoleum, each stroke overlapping the last by exactly the width of two fingers. George Tamlin had not measured this. His body had measured it for him, over seven years of nights, until the motion lived in the fascia of his shoulders and the rolling bones of his wrists and did not require thought. Thought was, in any case, something George conducted differently than most. It moved through him in images and pressures and the specific weight of objects in his hands. The mop handle: thirty-two ounces. The bucket, full: a dull ache in the left shoulder that worsened in November. The ring of keys on his belt: a percussion instrument that marked his passage down every corridor, the only announcement he would ever make.

He worked the east wing first. He had always worked the east wing first. The dying were housed there in their final weeks, and their rooms accumulated a particular residue that was not, strictly speaking, dirt. It was the film of process—the body's slow, laborious dismantling of itself, conducted through sweat and exhalation and the shedding of skin that had given up on regeneration. The smell was sweet and alkaline, and George knew it the way a sailor knew weather: not as information but as atmosphere, a thing that pressed against the skin and told you where you were.

Room 14: Mrs. Oleander, whose lungs sounded like a paper bag crumpled slowly, over and over, all night. Room 16: Mr. Devereaux, who talked to his dead wife in the hours between two and four with such conversational ease that George sometimes paused outside the door, not to eavesdrop but to stand in the presence of a voice that still believed it was being heard. Room 18: empty since Tuesday. George cleaned it anyway. The room held its former occupant the way a glass holds the ghost of water—the ring, the trace, the faint suggestion of volume.

He emptied the sharps containers and the general waste. He polished the stainless steel rails that lined every hallway, running his cloth along their lengths with a focus that an observer might have mistaken for devotion. He checked the floor beneath the vending machine in the staff break room, where a slow leak in the refrigeration unit left a puddle that no one from maintenance had ever addressed. He mopped it. He had mopped it every night for three years. The puddle did not trouble him. Recurrence was simply the grammar of his work.

The day staff did not know his name, or if they had known it, the knowledge had not adhered. He was a shape in peripheral vision, a cart in a hallway, a set of hands operating a buffer at five in the morning. His employee badge said GEORGE TAMLIN, CUSTODIAL, but it faced inward half the time, and no one asked him to correct it. He was not shy. He was not sullen. He had suffered a stroke at fifty-one—a clot the size of a peppercorn lodging in precisely the wrong bend of precisely the wrong artery—and it had taken his speech with the casual thoroughness of a wave pulling a sandcastle out to sea. Everything else remained: his cognition, his memory, his capacity for sequential reasoning, his hands. The hands were large, scarred across the knuckles from decades of mechanical work that predated the hospice, and they were eloquent in a language that required a willing reader. No one here read them. The day staff communicated with him through notes left on the custodial closet door, printed in block letters, as though he were also without literacy. GEORGE—SPILL IN ROOM 22. GEORGE—FAMILY VISITATION TMRW, PLS EXTRA ATTENTION LOBBY. He read them. He performed them. He left no notes in return.

What the day staff could not know, because they were not present for it, was the nature of George's passage through the building at night. He knew which rooms held patients close to death by the sound of the air inside them—a quality of silence that was not silence at all but the room's held breath, its surfaces absorbing the incremental withdrawal of a person from the world. He knew which nurses were compassionate and which were simply competent, because he saw what they did when no family member was watching. He knew that the supply closet on the second floor had a ventilation gap that let you hear everything said at the nurses' station, and that the head administrator had approved a reduction in overnight staffing for the third consecutive quarter, and that the remaining night nurse, a woman named Patrice, covered the entire building alone and sometimes wept in the medication room at three a.m. with her hand over her mouth.

George knew these things because he was the unregarded infrastructure of the building. The walls knew what he knew. The floors knew. He moved among the dying with the privilege of the invisible, which was no privilege at all except that it was total and unearned and could not be revoked because it had never been granted.

His hands pushed the mop. The water pulled the day off the floor.

---

The android arrived on a Thursday, in a box that was not large enough for what it contained.

George was in the custodial closet, refilling his spray bottles from the industrial concentrates, when he heard the intake door buzz and the wheels of a dolly across the lobby floor. He did not emerge immediately. He finished his task, because his tasks had a sequence and the sequence was a form of sanity. When he stepped into the corridor with his cart, rolling it toward the east wing, he passed the intake office and saw, through the reinforced glass window, a shape on the floor.

It was not yet a figure. It was a humanoid form in a molded transport cradle, knees bent to its chest, arms folded inward, head bowed—a posture of compression, like an infant before birth. Its casing was a matte gray-white, the color of surgical tables. Its surface was seamless where George expected seams. There were no visible joints at the wrists or elbows, only a smooth gradation of form that suggested movement without demonstrating it.

A man in a logistics vest was scanning a barcode on the cradle. Beside him, Carol Fenn, the night intake administrator, held a clipboard and a look of profound inconvenience. George could not hear them through the glass, but he could hear Carol. He could always hear Carol. Her voice was pitched to carry grievance across institutional distances.

"—donated, so there's no service contract. The family didn't want it after the—whatever it was. Memory issue. They wiped it, or they say they wiped it. Serial number matches the decom paperwork, but the cognitive suite might still have residual—"

The logistics man shrugged. He had other deliveries. Carol signed something. The logistics man left through the intake door, which buzzed again.

Carol looked at the android the way she looked at everything that created paperwork without contributing to the patient census. She thumbed through the documents on her clipboard, pulled one out, and paper-clipped it to the cradle. Then she turned off the intake office light and left through the staff corridor.

The android remained on the floor, in its cradle, in the dark.

George pushed his cart to the east wing and cleaned for an hour. He emptied Mrs. Oleander's waste bin and changed the liner. He mopped Mr. Devereaux's floor while the old man conducted a gentle argument with his dead wife about whether the garage door had been left open. He restocked the hand sanitizer dispensers and replaced a roll of paper towels in the shared bathroom. Then he returned to the intake office.

The android was still there. Someone—Carol, presumably, before leaving—had plugged a charging cable into a port at the base of its neck. A small light pulsed there, blue-white, slow as respiration.

George stood in the doorway and watched it. He had no particular opinion about machines. He had been a mechanic before the hospice, working on diesel engines in a shop that smelled of solvent and coffee, and he understood machines as systems of intention: they did what they were built to do, and when they stopped doing it, you found the part that had failed and you replaced it or you didn't. He had no romance about them and no fear. They were honest in a way that required no trust, because they had no capacity for deception.

He looked at the pulsing light. He went back to work.

Sometime after two a.m., he heard it.

Not a sound, exactly. An absence of sound that was new—the particular silence of something moving through the building that had not been there before. George was in the west wing, scrubbing a stain from the corridor floor, and he felt the hair on his forearms lift the way it did before a weather change. He stood, slowly, and looked down the long hallway toward the junction with the east wing.

The android was walking.

It had assembled itself from its compressed posture into full uprightness, and it moved with a fluency that George's mechanic's eye rejected and then, reluctantly, accepted. There was no stiffness. There was no mimicry. It walked the way water moved through a pipe—inevitably, without apparent effort, filling the space its design intended it to fill. It was perhaps five foot nine. Its face was not quite human, which was better, George thought, than if it had been trying to be. The features were simplified: smooth planes where cheekbones would be, a mouth that could shape expressions without attempting the full catastrophe of human nuance. Its eyes were dark, steady, and directed forward with an attention that seemed to precede it down the hall, as though its gaze arrived at each doorway a half-second before its body.

It turned into Room 14. Mrs. Oleander's room.

George did not follow. He stood at the corridor junction with his scrub brush in his hand and watched the doorway. The android was inside for perhaps ninety seconds. When it emerged, it moved to Room 16, then Room 20, then down the far hall toward the rooms George had not yet reached. It did not pause between rooms. It did not consult a chart or a device. It moved with the certainty of someone who already knew where it was going, though it had never been in this building before.

On its second pass through the east wing—George had resumed scrubbing, but slowly, the brush moving in absent circles while his attention tracked the android's trajectory—it passed within six feet of him. It looked at him. Not through him, not past him, but *at* him, with the brief, complete assessment of something determining whether he was relevant to its operations. He was not a patient. He registered, was cataloged, and was set aside. The android continued past him and entered Room 14 again.

George followed this time. Not into the room—he stood in the corridor, just outside the door, which the android had left open. Mrs. Oleander was asleep, her breathing the slow crumple of paper that was her signature. The room was lit only by the blue glow of the monitoring equipment and the pale wash from the corridor. The android stood beside her bed, motionless, and then—George saw it—tilted its head a fraction of a degree, as though listening to something below the threshold of human hearing. Then it reached out and adjusted her pillow. Not dramatically. A shift of an inch, perhaps less. It changed the angle of her head, opened her airway incrementally, and Mrs. Oleander's next breath came without the crumple. Smooth. Unobstructed. The android's hand withdrew. Mrs. Oleander shifted in her sleep, a movement that arrived two seconds after the adjustment that had anticipated it, and settled into the new position as though she had chosen it herself.

George watched from the hallway. His scrub brush dripped onto the floor, and he did not notice.

The android remained beside the bed for a moment longer. Then it turned, passed George without acknowledgment, and glided toward the next room. Its face, as it passed, was calm in the way that still water was calm—not suppressing turbulence but genuinely, constitutionally, without it.

George looked at Mrs. Oleander, who was breathing easily now, and then at the empty corridor where the android had been. He realized his mouth was slightly open. He closed it. He retrieved his scrub brush and returned to the stain on the west wing floor, which had dried during his absence and now required twice the effort to remove.

He worked the stain. His hands knew what to do. His hands always knew what to do.

Behind him, in the east wing, the android moved from room to room in the blue half-dark, and the building held the two of them—the man and the machine—in the same silence, like two frequencies occupying the same wire.

## II.

Over the next several nights, the android became part of the building's nocturnal grammar, and George, who understood grammar as repetition rather than language, began to learn its syntax.

It operated on a circuit. East wing first: rooms 14 through 28, then the overflow beds in the converted sunroom, then the west wing, then a return to the east. The cycle took approximately one hundred forty minutes. George timed it against his own routine—his body had been a clock for seven years, and he could map his position in the building to within five minutes at any point in the night without consulting a watch. By the third night, he knew where the android would be at any given moment, and by the fourth, he began—not deliberately, not with any decision he could have articulated—to adjust his own route so that his path intersected with its circuit.

He told himself he was cleaning. He was cleaning. He was also watching.

The first thing he noticed was the water. Room 22 held a man named Aldiss, whose medication left him parched but who could not reliably operate the call button due to advanced motor degeneration. Three times, George watched the android enter Aldiss's room carrying a cup of water—not from the hallway dispenser but from a small pitcher it prepared in the utility sink, filled to a level that suggested it knew precisely how much the man could safely swallow in his semi-recumbent position. It arrived before Aldiss woke. It was there, at the bedside, when the man's eyes opened and his mouth began its dry, searching motion. The android raised the cup to his lips with a steadiness that George, whose left hand still tremored on bad nights, could not have matched. Aldiss drank. The android waited. Aldiss drank again. Then the android set the cup on the bedside table, within reach but outside the radius of the man's involuntary arm movements, and left.

The timing was what unsettled George. Not the act—the act was kindness, or the mechanical reproduction of kindness, which, from the receiving end, might be indistinguishable. It was the *precedence*. The android arrived before the need manifested. As though it were reading a script written in the patient's physiology—respiratory rate, skin conductivity, the micro-expressions of a body moving toward thirst—and responding not to the current state but to the state that was coming. George had seen good nurses do something similar, the ones who had been on the floor long enough that their intuition operated below conscious thought. But the android did it without intuition. Or perhaps with something that resembled intuition so closely that the distinction was academic.

The second thing he noticed was the temperature. Room 16, Mr. Devereaux. The old man's conversations with his dead wife followed a pattern that George had long since memorized: the arguments peaked between two and three, then softened, then dissolved into a murmuring that might have been prayer or might have been the involuntary vocalization of a brain losing its grip on the boundary between interior and exterior. During the murmuring phase, Devereaux's breathing shallowed and his body temperature dropped—George had seen the nurses note it on his chart, a nightly dip that was simply the landscape of his dying. The android, George observed, entered the room at the precise onset of this phase and adjusted the thermostat by two degrees. Not the blankets—the thermostat. A systemic change rather than a local one, warming the air itself so that the old man's body did not have to fight for heat while it was busy with the work of letting go. George watched from the corridor and felt something move in his chest that was not his heart and was not the residual nerve damage from his stroke but was some third thing, unnamed, that had to do with witnessing care so precise that it felt like a form of listening.

The third thing was the music.

In Room 24, a woman named Dora Kitts, who had been a piano teacher and who was now a body that had forgotten how to swallow, lay in the particular stillness of the deeply sedated. The android stood beside her bed and hummed.

George heard it from the hallway—a low, tonal vibration that emerged from the android's vocal apparatus (he did not know what else to call it) with the quality of a cello heard through a wall. It was not a song he recognized. It was not, as far as he could tell, a song at all—it had no repeating structure, no verse or chorus. It moved in response to something.
