# The Thirty-Seven Letters of Monk Hideo

## Being a Complete Collection of the Correspondence of Hideo Tachibana, Formerly of the Hokushin Ittō-ryū, to an Unnamed Recipient, with Annotations, Contextual Essays, and Unsent Replies

### Compiled and Edited for Posthumous Publication by the Recipient

---

## Editor's Preface

I have no right to publish these letters. This should be stated at the outset, plainly, so that no reader mistakes what follows for an act sanctioned by its author. Hideo Tachibana wrote to me over a period of thirty-eight years. He did not write for the public. He did not write for posterity. I believe—though I cannot be certain, and certainty is not a luxury this collection affords—that he wrote for me alone.

I am publishing them anyway.

I will not give my name. Those who follow the art will know my name, and those who do not need not learn it. I am identified sufficiently by my function: I am Hideo's opponent. I was his opponent on the eighteenth day of October, in the forty-third year of Shōwa, in the final round of the All Japan Championship of that year. I have been his opponent every day since, though we have not crossed swords again.

It has taken me eleven months to prepare this manuscript. My physician tells me I have perhaps four remaining. The mathematics are inelegant but adequate: I will finish what I have begun, and then I will not begin anything else.

There are those who will call this publication a service to the art—a window into the mind of perhaps the finest swordsman of the twentieth century, a man who vanished from competitive life at the apex of his powers and whose silence has been a source of relentless, often foolish, speculation for four decades. Those people are welcome to that reading. I will not correct them. But I will say this: a man does not spend eleven months of his remaining life on *service*. A man spends it on what he cannot die without doing.

I have annotated the letters where context is necessary or where my memory, failing as it is, still offers something a bare text cannot. I have attempted editorial restraint. Where I have failed, the reader will judge the failure on its own terms.

Hideo wrote to me thirty-seven times. I replied six times in total, always briefly, and those replies are lost—I did not keep copies, and if Hideo kept them, they are behind the walls of a monastery I will not visit. The asymmetry should be noted. Much of what follows will resemble a monologue. This is misleading. Every silence on my end was a reply. Every year I did not write was a sentence.

I ask no one's forgiveness for what I am doing. Forgiveness is a subject I will leave to Hideo, who understood it better than I, and also not at all.

—The Opponent
Kamakura, March

## PART ONE

## The Early Letters

---

### Letter One

*Received November, 1970. Two years and one month after the tournament. Postmarked from Eiheiji, Fukui Prefecture. Written on monastery stationery of good quality, the characters small, neat, and without flourish. The envelope was addressed formally, to my dojo.*

Esteemed Sir,

I write to inquire after the health of Yamashiro Sensei, who was, at our last meeting, suffering from a complaint of the lungs. I understand that you may have occasion to visit him, as your dojo is not far from his home, and I wonder whether you might convey my regards. A direct letter from me would, I think, be unwelcome to him, given the circumstances of my departure from competitive life. I trust your judgment in this matter.

The monastery is quiet. I have been assigned duties in the kitchen and find the work agreeable. There is a small garden here that the abbot permits me to tend.

I hope this letter finds you well.

With respect,
Hideo Tachibana

---

**Annotation:** The postmark was dated twelve days before I received the letter. Eiheiji is one of the two head temples of the Sōtō school—an unusual choice for a man of Hideo's lineage, which had always inclined toward Rinzai discipline. I noted this at the time. I note it again now.

Yamashiro Sensei had died four months before this letter was sent. I do not know if Hideo was unaware of this or if the inquiry was a pretext. Both possibilities disturb me equally.

I replied with a single line informing him of Yamashiro Sensei's passing. I did not convey regards. I did not add personal remarks.

I did not expect to hear from him. I did not expect the relief.

---

### Letter Two

*Received April, 1971. Postmarked Eiheiji.*

I am sorry to hear of Yamashiro Sensei's passing. Please accept my deep condolences.

Spring arrives slowly in the mountains. The monks here observe the season with a particular attention I have not seen elsewhere—there is a tradition of noting the first appearance of each returning bird, and the records go back nearly three hundred years. I have been permitted to examine these records. The precision is remarkable. Generations of monks recording the first song of the bush warbler, year after year, in the same column of the same ledger. The bird does not know it is being observed. The observation changes nothing about its song. And yet the record exists, and it is, I think, the most sincere document I have ever read.

I train alone in the early mornings, before the others wake. The abbot tolerates this. He says nothing about it, which is its own form of permission.

With respect,
Hideo

---

**Annotation:** He has dropped the family name. I should not have noticed this. I noticed it immediately.

---

### Letter Three

*Received January, 1972. Postmarked Eiheiji.*

The new year is cold. I am writing to no particular purpose.

There is a stream that runs behind the meditation hall which freezes in a pattern I have been studying. Each year the ice forms differently, yet there is a consistency to the underlying structure—the stream finds the same paths of least resistance, and the ice merely reveals them. What appears random from the surface is, from beneath, entirely determined by the shape of the streambed.

I wonder sometimes whether it is the same with men. Whether what appears to be choice is merely the ice forming along channels cut long before the moment of decision.

I do not mean this in the Buddhist sense. I mean it in the martial sense.

The abbot has given me a small room of my own, which is a kindness but also, I suspect, a recognition that I am not entirely suited to communal life. I am grateful and ashamed in equal measure.

I hope your training continues well.

Hideo

---

**Annotation:** No annotation necessary. I include this letter for completeness.

I include it for completeness.

### Letter Four

*Received September, 1973. Postmarked Eiheiji. A gap of twenty months between this letter and the previous one.*

It has been some time since I wrote. I offer no explanation, because I have none that would satisfy either of us.

A thing happened today which I will describe because I do not know what to do with it. A praying mantis appeared on the wooden rail outside my window. I have seen many mantises, but this one arrested me. It had found a moth—large, brown, still alive—and had it fixed between its forelegs. The grip was precise. I have trained with men who could not achieve such economy of force.

But here is what stopped me: the mantis held the moth for a very long time without killing it. Not from hesitation, I think—there was nothing tentative in the grip. The mantis was simply still. The moth was still. The two of them occupied the same silence. I watched for perhaps ten minutes, though time in such moments is difficult to account for.

Then the mantis released the moth. It flew away, damaged but alive. The mantis remained on the rail, forelegs still extended in the attitude of holding, though it held nothing now.

I do not know why it released the moth. I do not know if "why" is a question that applies.

I find I am writing to you about insects when there are other things I might say. I trust you understand the nature of the difficulty.

Hideo

---

**Annotation:** *I trust you understand the nature of the difficulty.* This is the first moment in the correspondence where Hideo directly acknowledges the existence of a shared, unnamed subject. I read this sentence many times. I read it now as I prepare this manuscript, and I find it has not diminished.

I did not reply.

---

### Letter Five

*Received March, 1974. Postmarked Eiheiji.*

Spring again. The bush warbler has returned. I have made my entry in the ledger, my first—the monks have granted me this task, which I take seriously, perhaps more seriously than is warranted.

I have been reading. The library here is not extensive, but there is a collection of Chinese poetry that rewards patience. I have spent several weeks with a single poem by Wang Wei, the one about the empty mountain after rain. You may know it. The final image has stayed with me: the moonlight shining through pine trees, the stream flowing over stones. The poem does nothing. It resolves nothing. It merely presents a landscape after something has happened—the rain—and the landscape is unchanged and entirely changed.

I think about landscapes after events. I think about what the stream does when the rain stops.

There is a young monk here who reminds me of someone. I will not say who. He has a quality of attention that is unusual—he watches things completely, without the restlessness that watching usually contains. I wonder if he knows what a gift this is. I suspect not. Gifts of that kind are invisible to their holders.

Hideo

---

**Annotation:** I believe the young monk reminded him of me, though I hold this belief loosely. I was known, in our youth, for a certain stillness in observation. Yamashiro Sensei once said of me that I could watch a candle without making it flicker. This was not entirely a compliment.

### Letter Six

*Received December, 1975. Postmarked Eiheiji. Written on thinner paper than the previous letters—monastery supplies, not personal stock.*

Winter.

I have been ill, nothing serious, a persistent cough that the cold worsens. The doctor in the village tells me I should descend to a lower altitude for the season, but I find I cannot. I do not mean that circumstances prevent me. I mean that I cannot.

A thing I have noticed about rivers in this region: when a large stone sits in the current, the water does not break against it. Not here, not with these particular rivers, which are older and more practiced than rivers elsewhere. The water divides, passes around the stone on both sides, and reunites downstream as though the obstacle had never existed. The stone remains. The water is unchanged. But there is a place just behind the stone—the lee, where the current is disrupted—where the water is confused, eddying, turning back on itself. It is the calmest place in the river and also the most turbulent. Both things at once.

I have been that water for some time now.

Hideo

---

**Annotation:** I received this letter on the morning of the anniversary. I do not know if Hideo was aware of the date when he sent it. It does not matter. Some things arrive when they arrive, and the timing is the message, whether intended or not.

On that same morning, I was teaching an advanced class at my dojo. We were practicing *debana-waza*—techniques of interception, where one strikes at the precise moment the opponent initiates. I have taught this class a thousand times. On that morning, I stopped midsentence and dismissed the students early. I sat alone in the dojo for an hour.

I did not reply to this letter. I did not reply to any letter during this period. My silence was not indifference. I want the reader to understand this, though I am not certain I can explain what it was instead.

---

### Letter Seven

*Received June, 1976. Postmarked Eiheiji. This letter is significantly longer than the previous six—eleven pages, written in a smaller hand, with few corrections. It appears to have been composed in a single session, though the ink density shifts midway, suggesting a pause to re-grind the inkstick.*

I have been thinking about the life-giving sword.

I use the old terminology—*katsujinken*—because the concept resists translation and I find that even between two people who share a language, certain ideas can only be approached in their original costume. You will know the concept. You will have taught it. I wonder if you have believed it.

The tradition gives us a clean opposition: *katsujinken*, the sword that preserves life, and *satsujinken*, the sword that takes it. Yagyū Munenori spends considerable effort distinguishing between them. The death-dealing sword kills in the obvious sense. The life-giving sword kills the impulse to kill—it destroys the aggression in the opponent without destroying the opponent himself. This is presented as the higher art. The pinnacle. The great achievement of a mature swordsman.

I accepted this for many years. I taught it. I believed it the way one believes a mathematical proof—completely, and at a great distance from the body.

But there is a case the tradition does not address, or addresses only in whispers, and I find I can no longer avoid it:

What does the life-giving sword do when it encounters a vulnerability it did not seek?

I will be precise. Imagine a swordsman—let us say he is skilled, let us say he has spent his life in disciplined study—who enters a match with a worthy opponent. The match is honest. Both men have trained fully. Neither conceals his art. The confrontation is, let us say, the confrontation both men were born for. The culmination.

And in the course of this confrontation—not before, not in preparation, but *in the living moment of exchange*—one swordsman perceives in the other a structural flaw. Not a lapse in concentration. Not a momentary weakness. A flaw in the architecture of the man's training. A line of entry that, if struck with a particular force at a particular angle, would not merely score a point but collapse the structure entirely. The body would fail along this line. The damage would be severe. Possibly irreparable.

The swordsman who sees this flaw did not look for it. He was not studying his opponent for weakness—he was engaged, fully, in the honest exchange. The flaw revealed itself the way a crack in porcelain reveals itself only when light strikes at a certain angle. He could not have seen it from the outside. He could only see it from within the match, from within the specific geometry of their two bodies in motion.

He sees it. His body is already in motion. The rhythm of the exchange is carrying him toward the precise position from which the flaw is exploitable. He has perhaps three heartbeats.

*Katsujinken* says: preserve life. Do not kill. The sword that gives life is superior to the sword that takes it.

But what does the life-giving sword *do*? What is the action? The tradition speaks of a sword that "kills the opponent's killing intent," but this opponent has no killing intent—he is merely fighting honestly, with a flaw he does not know he possesses. There is no aggression to subdue. There is only a man who is open, in a way he has never been open before, to a destruction he cannot see.

Does the swordsman strike elsewhere—deliberately miss the flaw, target a lesser opening, win the match without exploiting the vulnerability? This is possible, but it is a lie. It is a contaminated victory. Both men will know, even if only one of them knows *what* he knows, that the match was shaped by concealment.

Does the swordsman reveal the flaw? Does he stop the match, lower his weapon, and say: *here, this is what I have seen, this is where you are open, this is where you could be destroyed?* The tradition has a word for this kind of revelation between warriors of equal rank. The word is not kind.

Or does the swordsman withdraw altogether? Does he remove himself from the equation—take the knowledge and leave, so that the flaw persists but is never exploited, and the opponent continues in ignorance, which is also a kind of safety?

I have been thinking about this for some years now.

I do not have an answer. I am not certain an answer exists in the form I keep looking for it—a clean resolution, a correct action, a move that satisfies all obligations simultaneously.
