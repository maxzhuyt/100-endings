# The Commissar's Confession

---

The seal is mine. The red wax, the impression of the Republic one and indivisible, the careful cursive of my name beneath the printed apparatus of the decree — mine. I have pressed this seal four hundred and eleven times since Ventôse. I have not miscounted. A man in my position does not miscount. A man in my position counts everything: the seals, the signatures, the doors he passes through each morning in the Tuileries, the number of steps from his desk to the Committee chamber, the guards who nod to him (seven, always seven between the east corridor and the stairs, and if someday there are eight, he will know). Four hundred and eleven warrants of transfer. Not execution orders, you understand — the Tribunal does that, the Tribunal is sovereign, the Committee merely arranges, facilitates, identifies the enemies of the people and delivers them to the people's justice. The language is precise. I helped make it precise. I sat in the third row of the drafting session when Saint-Just presented the Law of 22 Prairial, and I noted in the margin of my copy that the elimination of defense counsel would increase processing capacity by a factor of three, and I handed this note not to Saint-Just but to the clerk beside me because a man in my position does not address Saint-Just directly, a man in my position is the mechanism, not the hand. The mechanism does not speak to the hand. The mechanism turns.

I have been turning for three years.

The corridor is cold. It is Messidor and the corridor is still cold, the stones of the Conciergerie keeping their winter like a grudge, and my boots echo in a way they should not echo because there should be other boots, there should be the escort, but I am early. I am early because I could not remain in the room where I slept, which is not my room, which is Citizen Daubigny's room in the section office near the Pont au Change, because Citizen Daubigny is in Lyon overseeing requisitions and offered me the use of his quarters when he saw me working past midnight, and I said *thank you, citizen, the Republic is served by your generosity*, and I lay on his bed and I did not close my eyes. Not once. The ceiling had a crack in it shaped like the Rhône and I followed the Rhône from its source to its mouth again and again until the crack was no longer the Rhône but a fissure in everything and I rose and dressed and walked here in the dark.

Because today I oversee the transfer.

Today is mine.

---

How it began. No — how it began is not the correct question, a man trained by the Committee frames the correct question first and then — the correct question is: when did the second set of books open? When did the count split? Because there has always been one count, the official count, warrants signed, names processed, suspects identified — and then at some point there was a second count running underneath, silent, a shadow ledger, and in that ledger the entries are: a name whispered to a man in a brown coat outside the Café Procope, a route marked on a map that was burned within the hour, a shipment of blank passports diverted to an address on the Rue des Mauvais Garçons that I have never visited and will never visit because a man in my position does not visit, he arranges, a man in my position is the mechanism —

The mechanism turns in two directions now. Has turned. For three years.

Duval recruited me. I should say his name because he no longer has a name, not in any record that matters, he is a body in a ditch outside Arras or perhaps outside Nantes — the batches are not always carefully documented, this is one of the inefficiencies I have noted in my official capacity, this failure to properly record the disposal of enemies of the Republic, and I drafted a memorandum on the subject in Pluviôse that was well received, that was *praised*, Citizen Inspector your attention to procedural rigor is noted, and I sat in the third row and received this praise knowing that my memorandum on the proper documentation of mass executions was, in its secret function, an attempt to determine whether Duval was dead or alive, because if the Committee kept better records I might have found his name, I might have known —

He is dead. He has been dead since Germinal. Probably. I have no confirmation. A man in my position does not receive confirmation of such things; he reads the absence. Duval stopped appearing at the agreed locations. The chalk mark on the wall near the Marché des Innocents was never refreshed. The dead drop on the Île Saint-Louis held the same message for nine days, which was seven days past the protocol for retrieval, and on the tenth day I removed it myself, violating every procedure Duval had established, because the message was in his hand, and his hand was the last —

I am walking too quickly. The escort will not arrive for another quarter-hour. I am standing now in the antechamber of the women's holding cells and the turnkey is looking at me with the expression that turnkeys in the Conciergerie reserve for officials who arrive too early, which is suspicion tempered by deference, because I carry the Committee's seal and the Committee's seal is the highest authority in the Republic one and indivisible and no turnkey questions the seal, but the turnkey *notices*, the turnkey files the irregularity in whatever crude ledger operates behind his eyes, and I must not be irregular. I must not be early or late or too attentive or too casual. I must be the mechanism.

"Citizen Inspector. They are not yet prepared."

"I will wait."

I will wait. I am good at waiting. I have been waiting for three years.

---

Three still hidden. This is the count that matters now, the count beneath the count. Duval is dead. Mervaud was taken in Prairial, tried by the Tribunal in a batch of fourteen, executed on the same day — I processed his warrant of transfer, my seal, my signature, my hand steady because a man in my position cannot tremble, a man in my position processes the warrant of his co-conspirator with the same bureaucratic care he processes every other warrant because the mechanism does not distinguish, the mechanism turns. Mervaud in the morning. Eleven others before noon. Three after. I ate my lunch between the batches. Bread and a thin soup. I remember the soup was too hot and I burned the roof of my mouth and for an hour afterward there was this rawness, this stupid physical fact, my burned mouth, while Mervaud's head —

Three still hidden. I will not name them, not even here, not even in this space that is only thought and therefore should be safe but is not safe, because I have seen what the Committee extracts from minds that believed themselves safe, I have sat in the room while the questions were asked, not the crude room, not the room with the implements, but the quiet room, the room with the desk and the lamp and the patient voice that says *Citizen, the Republic asks only for truth, the Republic is your mother and wishes only to understand*, and I have watched men who believed their minds were fortresses hand over the keys because the patient voice knows where the doors are.

Three.

In a house in the Faubourg Saint-Marcel. With papers I prepared. Under names I invented from cemetery records — a small innovation of mine, using the names of dead children, because dead children are not investigated, dead children have no suspicious associations, dead children are the perfect citizens of an invisible republic. I am proud of this technique. I am ashamed of this pride. The dead children whose names I stole were real children who died of real diseases in real parishes and I have used their interrupted lives as wallpaper for my —

Three people are alive because of me. This is the second ledger's sum. This is what I have to show for three years of turning in two directions. Three. Against four hundred and eleven. The mathematics of it. The obscene mathematics.

---

She smelled of ink. This is the first thing — no, not the first thing, the first thing was the door, Duval's hand on my shoulder, *There is someone you must meet, she continues her husband's work* — but what I remember first, what the mind offers first when the mind is allowed to stop counting, is the smell of ink. Printer's ink. A specific compound: lampblack, linseed oil, something acrid underneath that caught in the throat. Her hands were stained with it. The crescents of her fingernails were black with it. She did not try to wash it away. She wore it the way other women wore their cockades — as a declaration.

Citizeness Vernet. Marie-Anne Vernet. The printer's widow.

Her husband had been arrested in Frimaire of the previous year for publishing a pamphlet critical of the Maximum — not of the Republic, not of the Revolution, of a pricing regulation, a technical complaint about bread and flour that any baker in Paris would have endorsed, and for this he was brought before the Tribunal and sentenced within the hour because the Tribunal does not distinguish between criticism and treason, the Tribunal does not distinguish at all, the Tribunal is a machine and the machine —

She continued his work. Not the pamphlet — she was not foolish — but the press. She printed other things. Notices for the sections, official communications, the mundane paperwork of revolution, and underneath, in the gaps between official jobs, she printed what needed printing. Lists of the arrested that the Committee preferred to keep unrecorded. Instructions for families: how to petition, where to send food, which officials could be approached and which could not. A practical literature of survival. No philosophy. No grand manifestos about the rights of man. Addresses. Names. Quantities of bread that could be passed through the grate of a particular cell in the Conciergerie if the turnkey on the Thursday shift was given fourteen sous. That level of specificity. That level of *use*.

Duval brought me to her because the network needed her press, and I was the one who could provide the paper — official paper, watermarked, requisitioned through channels I had access to, paper that could make a false passport look authentic because it was authentic, it was real Committee stock diverted at the source. I brought her paper and she gave me —

What did she give me?

It was not an argument. She never argued. I was so accustomed to argument — the Committee argued constantly, Saint-Just argued, Robespierre argued, the entire Revolution was an argument, a syllogism that had learned to kill — and she did not argue. She made soup. This is not a metaphor. She stood in her shop on the Rue de la Huchette with ink on her hands and she made soup for the families of the condemned, and when I arrived with my paper and my careful anonymity and my sense that I was doing something important, something ideologically significant, she handed me a bowl and said —

*Eat first. The Republic can wait. Your hands are shaking.*

My hands were shaking. I had not known my hands were shaking.

She saw it. She saw it before I did.

---

There is noise now in the corridor. The escort arriving. Four soldiers of the National Guard, their boots on the stones making the sound that boots are supposed to make in the Conciergerie: regular, martial, indifferent. The turnkey is unlocking the cells. I hear the locks — each one distinctive, a different pitch of iron, and I have learned to read the locks of this building the way a musician reads a score, and the lock now turning is the women's ward, third door —

I straighten my coat. I check my papers. The warrant of transfer is in my left breast pocket, signed by Fouquier-Tinville himself, the Public Prosecutor, whose signature I have studied so carefully that I could forge it in my sleep, and in fact I have forged it, twice, on documents that redirected two prisoners to a holding facility that did not exist, from which they were collected by people I will never meet and taken to places I will never know. Two of the three still hidden reached safety through Fouquier-Tinville's own hand — my hand performing his hand, the mechanism turning within the mechanism.

But this warrant is genuine. This warrant is real. This warrant bears the names of eleven prisoners to be transferred to the Place de la Révolution for the execution of their sentences, and the seventh name on the list is Vernet, Marie-Anne, widow, printer, enemy of the people, and the signature at the bottom of the page, beneath Fouquier-Tinville's, authorizing the supervision of transfer, is mine.

---

The order came three days ago.

No — the order came from a clerk. This is important. The order did not come from Robespierre's hand. Robespierre does not issue orders to inspectors of my rank; Robespierre issues principles, and the principles flow downward through Saint-Just and Couthon and the Committee and the sub-committees and the clerks and finally the principles arrive at my desk as specific instructions: *Citizen Inspector, you are assigned to supervise the transfer of the following condemned on 19 Messidor.*

A routine assignment. I have supervised transfers before. Thirty-one times. It is part of my function. It is within the normal rotation of duties. There is no reason — none that I can identify, and I have identified every reason for everything in this building for three years — there is no reason to believe this assignment is anything other than routine.

And yet.

The list included her name. The list was prepared by the Revolutionary Tribunal based on sentences handed down over the previous week. The Tribunal does not coordinate with my duty rotation. The Tribunal does not know or care which inspector supervises which transfer. The intersection of her name and my assignment is coincidence.

Or.

Or the Committee knows. The Committee has known for some time — since Duval's arrest, perhaps, since whatever Duval said or did not say under questioning — and the Committee, which is patient, which has the patience of a mechanism, which can wait as long as mechanisms wait, has allowed me to continue turning because a mechanism that is watched is more useful than a mechanism that is stopped, and now the Committee wishes to see what the mechanism does when it is fed the name of the woman who —

I do not know. I cannot know. This is the nature of the Committee's power: it does not need to act to be felt. The possibility of its knowledge is sufficient. I sit at my desk and I am watched; I walk through the corridors and I am watched; I lie on Citizen Daubigny's bed and I am watched — not by any specific observer, not by any specific eye, but by the *principle* of observation, which Robespierre has made as pervasive as air.

Does he know?

The question is unanswerable. The question is therefore permanent. It moves into the foundations of every thought I have and it will not leave.

---

They are bringing the prisoners out now. I hear them before I see them — the shuffle, the chains (some are chained, the dangerous ones, the ones who fought or spoke or refused, and some are not, the ones who have passed into the stillness that comes after terror, the stillness I have seen so many times that I have given it a taxonomy: there is the stillness of shock, the stillness of resignation, the stillness of a soul that has already departed and left the body to manage things, and the worst, the stillness of understanding, which is calm and open-eyed and looks directly at you) —

The first prisoner is a man I do not know. Old. A ci-devant something, an aristo or a pretend-aristo, someone whose name was on a list because a neighbor denounced him for hoarding candles. Candles. The Republic will survive because this man's candles have been identified and his head will be taken. The second is a woman, young, weeping, I do not look at her long enough to note her features because I cannot spend my looking, I must ration my looking the way the Republic rations bread, because the seventh prisoner is —
