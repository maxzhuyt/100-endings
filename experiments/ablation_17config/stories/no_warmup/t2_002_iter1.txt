# THE HOA BOARD WILL NOW COME TO ORDER

## I.

The banana bread was still warm.

Doug Whitmore set it on the folding table beside the sugar packets and the coffee urn that hadn't been properly cleaned since the Ford administration, and he smiled the way he always smiled—with his whole face, so you felt it was just for you. He had a gift for that. Thirty people sat in stackable chairs in the community room of the Cascade Terrace Homeowners Association, and when Doug smiled, each of them felt individually noticed.

"Before we move on to old business," Doug said, settling behind the pressboard table at the front of the room, "I want to thank Linda Petrosian for the beautiful job she did with the planters along the west entrance. Linda, you out there? Stand up."

Linda Petrosian stood up. She was sixty-one and her cheeks turned the color of the geraniums she'd planted. People clapped. Doug clapped the loudest.

"Now," Doug said, and the warmth in his voice didn't go away but it tilted, just slightly, the way a level tilts when the ground beneath it isn't quite right, "we do have a compliance matter. Gary, I'm sorry to do this to you, bud."

Gary Feinman, three rows back, stopped chewing his banana bread.

"The architectural committee conducted its quarterly walkthrough last Tuesday, and Gary, it looks like you've got a garden gnome situation on the south side of your front bed."

"It's one gnome," Gary said.

"It's a wonderful gnome," Doug said. "Sheila and I walked by it the other day and she said, 'Doug, that is a gnome with real personality.' And I agree. But per Article III, Section 12, ornamental statuary in front-facing beds is limited to natural stone or approved ceramic, and your little guy is—what is he, resin?"

"He was my mother's."

"And I respect that. I do. Gary, I respect that deeply. But if I let resin gnomes go, next week I've got a plastic flamingo on Birchwood Lane and a concrete donkey on the corner of Maple and I can't put that genie back in the bottle. You understand."

Gary Feinman understood. Everyone understood. The fine was $35 and Gary paid it at the table after the meeting, and Doug squeezed his shoulder and said, "You're a good man, Gary," and Gary said, "No, you're right, Doug, rules are rules."

This was how it worked. It always worked. Because Doug was never angry. Doug was never petty. Doug was *sorry.* Doug was on your side but his hands were tied. The bylaws were the bylaws. He didn't write them—well, he had written some of them, and revised more of them, and interpreted all of them, but that was beside the point. The point was that the rules existed for a reason, and the reason was Cascade Terrace, and Cascade Terrace was all of them, together, protecting what they'd built.

And what they'd built was fine. The houses were fine. The lawns were fine. The property values had appreciated at a steady 3.2 percent annually for the past decade, which Doug mentioned at every meeting the way a cardiologist mentions your resting heart rate—not bragging, just noting that the patient was alive and well and you were welcome.

Nobody ran against Doug anymore. There had been challengers early on—an aggressive IT consultant in 2014, a retired principal in 2017 who'd campaigned on "fiscal transparency" until Doug invited her to dinner and she withdrew the next day, citing "family obligations." Now the ballot had one name, and the vote was a formality, and the formality was Doug's favorite part. He loved the vote. He loved being chosen.

The bylaws sat in a white three-ring binder on the table in front of him, and when he referenced them, he didn't open the binder. He didn't need to. He quoted from memory, or from something adjacent to memory—a territory where the actual text blurred into Doug's understanding of the text, which was always more convenient. He touched the binder the way some men touch a Bible: not to read it, but to remind you it was there, and that he was its keeper.

"Any new business?" Doug asked. He asked this at every meeting. Nobody ever had new business.

"All right, then. Meeting adjourned. Grab some banana bread on the way out."

They did. They always did.

## II.

Meredith Chen did not eat banana bread. She did not eat most things that could not be described with numerical precision. A banana bread was an approximation—a pinch of this, a handful of that—and Meredith had built her life on the elimination of "approximately."

Thirty-two years at Pacific Mutual, pricing catastrophic risk. Earthquakes, floods, the probability of a 747 engine failing at 35,000 feet. She could tell you, not in metaphor but in six decimal places, how likely your house was to burn down. She had never once been wrong by a statistically significant margin. She had retired three years ago and the world had become, briefly, a place without enough numbers in it.

Then Leonard got sick, and the numbers came back. Different numbers. Worse ones.

The pill organizer on the kitchen counter had seven columns and four rows. She filled it every Sunday night: the Aricept at breakfast, the lisinopril at lunch, the trazodone at bedtime, and the rotating cast of supplements that his neurologist endorsed and Meredith categorized as "insufficient evidence but negligible downside risk." A spreadsheet on her laptop tracked his weight, his blood pressure, his good days and bad days, coded green and yellow and red in a simple stoplight system that she had never shown him because Leonard would have found it either hilarious or heartbreaking and she wasn't prepared for either.

He was in the living room now, in the recliner, reading a biography of Ulysses S. Grant. He was on page 214 and had been on page 214 for three days, but he kept the book open on his lap because he liked the weight of it, and Meredith understood this without being told, the same way she understood to put his coffee in the blue mug with the chipped handle because it was the one his hand remembered.

"Mer," he said.

She was in the kitchen, adjusting his afternoon medications. She didn't answer immediately because she was counting pills.

"Mer, come look at this cardinal."

She finished counting. She went to the window. The cardinal was sitting on the fence, red as an emergency.

"That's the same one from Tuesday," Leonard said. "I'm sure of it."

"You can't identify individual cardinals, Leonard."

"I can. This one has a judgmental expression. Like your mother."

"My mother has been dead for eleven years."

"And yet that cardinal has her exact energy. Look at it."

Meredith looked at the cardinal. It did, in fact, appear to be evaluating them.

"I'll allow it," she said, and went back to the kitchen.

This was their marriage. It had been their marriage for fifty-one years—Leonard finding the absurd and Meredith finding the precise and both of them meeting somewhere in the middle where those things were, it turned out, not so far apart. He had been an English professor. She had been an actuary. At a dinner party in 1972, he'd told her that actuaries were the only people who made death boring, and she'd told him that English professors were the only people who made life boring, and he'd laughed so hard he'd spilled wine on his shirt, and she'd calculated the probability of the stain coming out (low, given the tannin content of a 1968 Cabernet), and he'd said, "Marry me," and she'd said, "The statistical likelihood of a successful marriage based on a single dinner party interaction is—" and he'd said, "Marry me anyway," and she had.

Now he couldn't walk to the mailbox. Now the left side of his body was retreating from him in slow, cruel increments—first the foot, then the leg, then the hand that used to grade papers in a cramped cursive she'd spent decades decoding. The diagnosis was a alphabet soup of syllables that Meredith had researched until she'd reached the limits of what research could do, which was to tell you exactly how bad things were with beautiful specificity.

He needed a wheelchair most days now. He could still transfer himself—from bed to chair, chair to toilet—but the three steps from the front door down to the walkway had become an event requiring planning, assistance, and a level of physical indignity that Leonard bore with the same wry resignation he brought to everything.

She adjusted his pillow. She did this multiple times a day, a small recalibration, two inches to the left, a compression check on the lumbar support. She never said why and he never asked. An observer might have called it tenderness. Meredith would have called it "postural maintenance." The result was the same.

## III.

The ramp was built on a Wednesday by a contractor named Eddie Salazar who charged $2,400 and did clean work. It was pressure-treated pine, thirty-six inches wide, with a gentle 1:12 slope and a simple railing on both sides. It ran from the front door to the walkway, eliminating the three steps. It was not beautiful. It was the color of lumber. It had the aesthetic appeal of a loading dock.

It was also the difference between Leonard leaving his house and Leonard being trapped in it.

The first violation notice arrived on Friday.

It was on Cascade Terrace letterhead—a tasteful sage green with a watermark of the community's decorative entrance gate. Meredith opened it at the kitchen table while Leonard ate oatmeal.

**NOTICE OF VIOLATION — NON-COMPLIANCE WITH ARCHITECTURAL STANDARDS**

*Property: 14 Birchwood Lane*

*Violation: Unauthorized exterior structural modification (front-facing wheelchair access ramp) not submitted for nor granted approval per Article III, Section 8, of the Cascade Terrace HOA Bylaws & Covenants.*

*Action Required: Remove unauthorized structure within 14 days or submit Variance Request Form (Form 117-B) to the architectural review committee.*

*Daily fine of $50 will begin accruing on the 15th day of non-compliance.*

*Sincerely,*
*Douglas R. Whitmore, President*
*Cascade Terrace Homeowners Association*

"What is it?" Leonard asked.

"The ramp."

"What about the ramp?"

"It's unauthorized."

Leonard put his spoon down. "It's a wheelchair ramp."

"It's an unauthorized exterior structural modification."

"Meredith, it's a wheelchair ramp. For my wheelchair. Which I use because I can't walk."

"I'm aware of your ambulatory status, Leonard."

She filed the notice in the folder she kept for HOA correspondence, which until now had contained only the annual assessment invoices and a flyer about the community yard sale. She located Form 117-B on the HOA website—a PDF that required hand-completion and physical delivery to the management office, which was open Tuesdays and Thursdays from 10 a.m. to 2 p.m. She filled it out that afternoon with the mechanical precision of someone who had spent three decades filling out government risk-disclosure forms. She delivered it on Tuesday at 10:07 a.m.

It was denied on Thursday. The denial letter cited "aesthetic inconsistency with the community's approved exterior material and color palette" and noted that any structural addition to a front-facing elevation required review by the full architectural committee, which met quarterly. The next meeting was in eleven weeks.

Meredith called the HOA management office. A woman named Tammy told her she could appeal the denial at the next quarterly meeting of the architectural review committee, or at the next annual general meeting, whichever came first. Meredith asked what happened to the fines in the interim. Tammy said the fines continued to accrue during the appeals process per Article V, Section 4.

"So the fine accrues while I wait for the process you're telling me to use."

"That's correct, Mrs. Chen."

"That's a penalty for compliance."

"I'm sorry?"

"You're fining me for following your process. The process takes eleven weeks. At fifty dollars a day, that's $3,850 in fines before I'm even allowed to appeal."

Tammy said she understood the frustration and would Meredith like to leave a message for Mr. Whitmore.

Meredith left a message. Doug called back the next day. He was very sorry. He was so, so sorry about Leonard, and he wanted Meredith to know that this was not personal, that he had enormous respect for her and for Leonard, and that he remembered when Leonard used to lead the community's Fourth of July trivia contest, what a guy, what a sharp mind.

"The thing is, Meredith," Doug said, and his voice had that tilt in it, that gentle slope toward inevitability, "the bylaws are the bylaws. I didn't write them."

He had written some of them.

"If I make an exception for you, I have to make an exception for everyone. And then what do we have? Anarchy. I know that sounds dramatic, but I've seen it happen. Over in Pinewood Estates, they let their architectural standards slide, and you know what happened? Someone put up a six-foot metal rooster in their front yard. Property values dropped four percent in one quarter."

"My husband can't leave the house, Doug."

"I know. And I am *heartsick* about that. I really am. Tell you what—I'm going to bring over some banana bread this weekend. Sheila's recipe. It's Leonard's favorite."

The banana bread arrived on Saturday. It was, in fact, very good. Leonard ate two slices. The fines were $300 by then.

Meredith sat at the kitchen table that night after Leonard was asleep. She had the violation notice, the denial letter, and the folded document Doug had handed her when he'd dropped off the banana bread—a copy of the Cascade Terrace bylaws, the full governing document, sixty-three pages in 10-point font, printed double-sided. He'd given it to her with a paternal smile and said, "Everything you need to know is in here."

Meredith looked at the document. She looked at the compounding fines—$50 a day, every day, a geometric cruelty that she could project out on a timeline and watch consume their savings. She looked at the hallway where Leonard's wheelchair was parked, the one that couldn't get down three steps without the ramp that was costing them $350 a week to keep.

She opened the bylaws to page one.

She did not skim. She did not scan for the relevant section and stop. She read from the first "WHEREAS" to the last signature on the final amendment, and she read the way she had once read actuarial tables—not for meaning but for *structure.* For the load-bearing walls. For the places where the weight shifted. For the seams.

## IV.

People who did not know Meredith might have imagined this as a montage: the small woman at the dining room table, papers spread before her, pen in hand, discovering truth by lamplight. It was not a montage. It was nineteen days.

She made a chart. Then she made a second chart because the first chart revealed the need for a third chart, and by the time the system of charts stabilized, her dining room table looked like a field office for a forensic accounting investigation, which, she supposed, it was.

The bylaws of the Cascade Terrace Homeowners Association had been originally drafted in 1987 by a real estate attorney named Morton Quill, who had done an adequate job. The problem was everything that had happened since. Thirty-seven years of amendments, revisions, addenda, and special resolutions, authored by a rotating cast of board members with varying levels of literacy and no legal training, had produced a document that contradicted itself in at least fourteen places.

Article III, Section 8 (architectural modifications) said all exterior changes required review by the architectural committee. Article III, Section 15 (accessibility) said the board "shall make reasonable accommodations for residents with documented medical needs." These two sections had never been reconciled. They simply coexisted, like two laws of physics that couldn't both be true.

The quorum definition: Article II, Section 4 stated that a quorum for general meetings required "a majority of member units." In 1987, there had been 96 units. There were now 147. The bylaws had never been amended to reflect this. Doug had been conducting business under the assumption that quorum required 49 households—a majority of the original count. In fact, it required 74.
