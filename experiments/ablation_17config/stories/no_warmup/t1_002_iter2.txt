# THE SUTTON COUNTY TRANSCRIPTS

---

**COMPILER'S NOTE**

The following materials were recovered from a commercial storage facility at 1140 Industrial Parkway, Unit 37-C, Sutton County, California, in March 1994, following a default action initiated by Pacific Storage Solutions, Inc. against the account of MAREN E. KIMBLE. The unit's rental agreement had been prepaid via automatic withdrawal from Ms. Kimble's personal checking account for a period of eighteen (18) months, terminating upon exhaustion of designated funds. Ms. Kimble, formerly employed as a court stenographer for Sutton County Superior Court, was separated from county employment in September 1992 under circumstances documented in Personnel Action File #92-0347, which remains sealed pursuant to county administrative policy.

Ms. Kimble's current whereabouts are unknown. The Sutton County Sheriff's Office declined to open a missing persons investigation, citing the absence of evidence of foul play and the voluntary nature of Ms. Kimble's departure from the area.

The materials consist of: seven (7) audiocassette recordings made on a hand-held dictation device; one (1) partial set of trial transcripts bearing handwritten annotations; and miscellaneous photocopied documents from county administrative files. They are presented here in the order most consistent with their apparent chronology.

Questions regarding the disposition of these materials should be directed to the Sutton County Clerk's Office, Attn: Records Division.

## MOVEMENT ONE

**AUDIO DIARY — TAPE 1**
*Recorded February 8, 1991. Microcassette, Olympus Pearlcorder model. Duration: 11 minutes, 43 seconds. Partial transcript follows.*

This is Maren Kimble, February eighth, 1991, personal case notes. Just for my own records—the same as always.

I should say at the top that I started doing this, God, must be twelve years ago now? After the Delacroix trial, which was one of those boundary disputes that metastasized into attempted murder, and I realized after four weeks of testimony that I could not, for the life of me, remember a single thing anyone had said that wasn't on my tape. Which is profoundly normal—that's the job, your hands move, the words pass through you, you do not *retain*—but it rattled me. So I started keeping these. Five, ten minutes after a session, just talking through what happened, what I noticed, how the room felt. Professional development. A tool for staying present.

[Sound of a cabinet opening, liquid poured into a ceramic vessel.]

Harold, get off the—thank you.

[Pause.]

Right. So. New assignment. *People v. Vance*, Department 4, Judge Moreland presiding. The satanic daycare thing.

I should be more precise than that. Eleanor Vance is not accused of running a daycare, and the charges are not, technically, satanic anything. She was a licensed social worker and foster parent, and she is accused of the ritualistic murders of three children who were in her care. Jason Pruitt, age seven. Amelia Sounders, age five. Thomas Bell, age nine. Over a fourteen-month period.

[Long pause.]

The word "ritualistic" is doing an enormous amount of work in that sentence, and I'll be interested to see how the prosecution defines it. My understanding from the pretrial filings is that they found books, some copied Latin texts, some sort of ledger in her house, and they've built a narrative around that. Eleanor believed in the occult, Eleanor believed the murders were spiritually necessary, Eleanor is mentally ill in a way that gave her delusions a liturgical structure. That's the theory.

And look. I've been doing this for a long time. I was working in San Bernardino when the McMartin preschool case was in the news every single day. I watched otherwise intelligent people talk about secret tunnels and hot-air balloon rides and Chuck Norris as though any of it survived five seconds of critical thought. The satanic panic is a mass delusion. I don't say that lightly. It has a body count of ruined lives, and it is a *mass delusion*.

So I'll be honest: I'm going into this one with a prior. I think the prosecution is going to have to work very hard to convince me that whatever happened to those children—and something obviously happened to those children—had anything to do with the devil.

[Sound of a teaspoon against ceramic.]

I need to call Beth on Sunday. I keep forgetting until Monday and then I feel guilty all week. I should set an alarm. That's a normal thing people do, setting alarms to remember to call their sister. That's not pathological. That's just—it's a busy time.

Trial starts Monday. I'll be running my Olympus as backup, same as usual.

[Recording ends.]

**TRANSCRIPT — SUPERIOR COURT OF CALIFORNIA, COUNTY OF SUTTON**
*Case No. 90-CF-0412*
*THE PEOPLE OF THE STATE OF CALIFORNIA v. ELEANOR MARIE VANCE*
*Department 4 — Hon. Richard L. Moreland, presiding*
*Court Reporter: Maren E. Kimble, CSR #4187*

**TRIAL DAY ONE — February 11, 1991**
**PROSECUTION OPENING STATEMENT**

---

MR. HARWICK: Thank you, Your Honor. Good morning, ladies and gentlemen of the jury.

What I'm going to describe for you this morning is not easy to hear. I want to acknowledge that at the outset. The facts in this case involve the deaths of three children, and the details of those deaths are disturbing, and there will be moments during this trial when you will want to look away. I am going to ask you not to look away. I'm going to ask you to stay with the evidence, because these three children—Jason Pruitt, Amelia Sounders, Thomas Bell—cannot speak for themselves, and the evidence is the only voice they have left.

Eleanor Vance was a licensed social worker employed by Sutton County Child Protective Services from 1974 until her termination in 2016—excuse me, 1986.

[Handwritten marginal note in blue ink: *"Stumble on the date. Nerves? He recovered fine. Speaking at ~160 wpm, a little fast for the jury."*]

She was also a licensed foster parent. Between March of 1988 and May of 1989, three children were placed in her home in sequence. Not simultaneously—in sequence. Jason Pruitt was placed first. He died on June 14, 1988, of what was initially classified as a grand mal seizure of unknown etiology. He was seven years old. Amelia Sounders was placed second, three months after Jason's death. She died on November 2, 1988—All Souls' Day, members of the jury, and that date is not incidental—of sudden cardiac arrest. She was five. Thomas Bell was placed third. He died on May 1, 1989—Beltane, a date of significance in occult practice—of acute respiratory failure. He was nine.

Three children. Three "natural" causes. Three dates that map onto a ritual calendar. One home.

Now, I want to be very clear about something, because I suspect the defense will suggest otherwise: the State is not asking you to believe in the devil. The State is not presenting a case about the supernatural. The State's position is this: Eleanor Vance believed. She believed with the absolute conviction of someone suffering from a severe delusional disorder, and she acted on that belief. She acquired occult texts—we will show you those texts. She copied passages in her own hand—we will show you those copies. She kept what we will demonstrate is a log, a record of names and dates organized around a structure that Dr. Perry and Dr. Thwaite will help you understand. And she was placed, by the systems that were supposed to protect these children, in a position of absolute authority over their lives.

The autopsies conducted on all three children revealed anomalous patterns of bruising on the torso and inner forearms consistent with physical restraint during the dying process. These children did not die peacefully. They did not die of natural causes. They died because Eleanor Vance restrained them and subjected them to an experience so physiologically traumatic that their small bodies simply stopped.

[Handwritten marginal note: *"Jury foreperson, seat 1, looked away during this. Seat 7 (retired teacher, per voir dire notes) did not. Watching defendant."*]

The defense will tell you this is satanic panic. The defense will invoke McMartin. The defense will tell you that this community, this county, has been caught in a wave of national hysteria, and that Eleanor Vance is just another innocent person swept up in the tide. Listen carefully when they do. Then look at the evidence. Listen to the evidence. And ask yourselves whether three dead children in one home—on those dates, with those injuries, with those books on the shelf—is hysteria.

Or whether it's murder.

THE COURT: Thank you, Mr. Harwick. Ms. Alcott, the defense may make its opening statement.

**DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT**

---

MS. ALCOTT: Thank you, Your Honor.

Members of the jury, good morning. I'm Catherine Alcott, and I represent Eleanor Vance, and before I say anything else I want to ask you to do something that Mr. Harwick very carefully did not ask you to do. I want you to look at my client.

[Pause.]

Look at her. Sitting right there. A fifty-one-year-old woman who spent twenty-two years in social work, who took in other people's children because she could not have her own, who is sitting in this courtroom right now with her hands folded because that is what she does when she is frightened, and she is more frightened than she has ever been in her life.

Now, Mr. Harwick is a skilled prosecutor, and he just told you a story. It was a compelling story. It had a villain and it had victims and it had a calendar of evil dates. The only thing it didn't have was evidence that Eleanor Vance killed anyone.

Because here, members of the jury, is what actually happened. Three children in Eleanor Vance's care died. That is a fact. That is a terrible, devastating fact, and Eleanor Vance has lived with it every day since May of 1989, and she will live with it every day for the rest of her life. But children in foster care die at a rate that should stagger every person in this room. They come from backgrounds of abuse and neglect, they carry genetic predispositions that are often unknown because their medical histories are incomplete, and they die of seizures and cardiac events and respiratory failure far more often than children in stable homes. That is not murder. That is a systemic failure that this country refuses to address, and Eleanor Vance is not its scapegoat.

Mr. Harwick mentioned the bruising. Dr. Louise Perry, the prosecution's own forensic witness, will tell you under cross-examination—and I beg you to listen carefully—that the bruising she observed is *consistent with* restraint but is also *consistent with* the self-inflicted trauma of seizure activity. Consistent with. The prosecution's own medical evidence cannot distinguish between murder and natural death. Think about that.

He mentioned occult materials. You will see those materials. They are books you can purchase at any Waldenbooks in this mall or any mall in America. They are Latin texts that Eleanor copied from the public collection at Cal State Sutton's library while she was researching a continuing education paper. They are her professional case log—her work files, members of the jury—that the prosecution has reinterpreted by squinting at the dates through a lens of superstition.

In 1983, in Kern County, California—three hours from where we sit—thirty-six people were charged with ritual satanic abuse of children. Thirty-six. Most were convicted. Most of those convictions were later overturned, because they were built on coerced testimony, moral panic, and the same kind of narrative Mr. Harwick just presented to you: a good story, with a villain, and a terrified community that needed someone to blame.

Do not let Sutton County become the next Kern County.

[Handwritten marginal note: *"Strong. Jury's listening."*]

**AUDIO DIARY — TAPE 2**
*Recorded February 11, 1991. Duration: 8 minutes, 22 seconds.*

Maren Kimble, February eleventh, post-session notes.

First day. Impressions.

Harwick is competent. Not spectacular, but he's organized and he's not overplaying it, which is smart given how easy it would be to overplay it. He's framing this as mental illness, not as "the devil made her do it," which is the only way to prosecute something like this without losing the college-educated jurors.

Alcott is better than I expected. She's got that controlled-anger thing that plays well—she's genuinely outraged on her client's behalf, or she's good enough for it not to matter. The McMartin comparison is her strongest card an she played it early. That's a choice. We'll see if it holds.

[Pause.]

Eleanor.

I've been watching people in courtrooms for twenty years. I know what guilty looks like. The ones who did it—there's usually something performative, either too much or too little. Too calm, too upset, too engaged with the jury, too studiously indifferent. It's theater either way. And innocent people—they look scared. Scared and baffled and sometimes angry, like *how is this possible, how is this real.*

Eleanor doesn't fit either. She sits with her hands folded. She watches the proceedings like someone at a lecture—attentive, patient, but...removed? No, that's not it. She watches like someone who already knows how it ends. Not resigned. Something else. The way you'd watch a movie you've already seen, and you know the sad part is coming, and you're bracing.

[Pause.]

I don't know. First days are unreliable. You form an impression and then spend the rest of the trial trying to confirm it, which is exactly the cognitive bias I'm supposed to be guarding against. There's a thing I've noticed over the years—I call it courtroom static. Cases involving children's deaths generate this emotional distortion field where everyone in the room, the jurors especially, stops reasoning from evidence and starts reasoning from the gut. The gut says *those children are dead and someone must pay* or the gut says *this poor woman is being railroaded*, and from that point on, every piece of evidence just becomes ammunition for whichever conclusion the gut already reached. I have to be especially precise in cases like this.

That's the job. The words pass through me. I get them right. That's all.

[Sound of a cat meowing.]

Yes, Harold. I know. Dinner.

[Recording ends.]

**TRANSCRIPT — TRIAL DAY THREE — February 13, 1991**
**PROSECUTION WITNESS: DR. LOUISE PERRY, M.D.**
*Medical Examiner, Sutton County Coroner's Office*

---

MR. HARWICK: Dr. Perry, can you describe for the jury the findings of your autopsy of Jason Pruitt?

DR. PERRY: Jason Pruitt, age seven, was brought to my office on June 15, 1988, having been pronounced dead at Sutton County General the previous evening. External examination revealed a well-nourished male child, no signs of chronic malnutrition, no signs of prior fracture, no external wounds. Internal examination revealed cerebral edema consistent with prolonged seizure activity. The proximate cause of death was status epilepticus—a continuous seizure state—of unknown etiology. Jason had no prior seizure history in his available medical records.

MR. HARWICK: Was there anything else of note on external examination?

DR. PERRY: Yes. Jason had bruising on the anterior torso—the chest and stomach—and on the medial aspect of both forearms. The bruising was perimortem, meaning it occurred at or very near the time of death.

MR. HARWICK: Can you describe the pattern of that bruising?

DR. PERRY: The pattern was bilateral and roughly symmetrical. The forearm bruises were elongated, approximately three to four centimeters in width, running along the inner arm from wrist to elbow. The torso bruises were more diffuse but concentrated in a band across the upper abdomen and lower chest.

MR. HARWICK: In your professional opinion, is this pattern of bruising consistent with the child having been physically restrained—held down—during the seizure event?

DR. PERRY: It is consistent with that, yes.
