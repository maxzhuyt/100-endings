# THE COLLECTORS

---

## ACT ONE: THE LETTERS

---

### 1.

Mara Chen was not stealing the candle.

She was *holding* the candle. She was *considering* the candle. She was standing in the home goods section of a T.J. Maxx in Bethesda, Maryland, at 11:47 on a Tuesday morning—a Tuesday morning when she should have been on a conference call about the Meridian merger—and she was conducting a perfectly normal olfactory evaluation of a bergamot-and-tobacco candle that retailed for $7.99 and that she could afford approximately ten thousand of.

She set it down.

She picked it up again.

Her coat had been tailored with interior pockets deeper than the originals. She'd commissioned them from a woman on Etsy who specialized in concealed-carry modifications for outdoor jackets. Mara did not own a gun. She owned many candles.

The thing about the moment before she took something was that it was the only moment in her life when all the noise stopped. The conference call, the billing targets, the texts from her husband about preschool tuition and whether they should refinish the deck—all of it fell away, and there was only the object and the question of whether she would take it, and the question wasn't really a question at all because she always took it, and the knowing that she would take it was itself a kind of peace, the way the last seconds before sleep are peaceful because the decision to remain conscious has already been lost.

She took the candle.

Her hand didn't tremble. It hadn't trembled since she was twenty-two, in a Walgreens in Cambridge, when she'd pocketed a tube of Burt's Bees lip balm worth $3.49 and nearly fainted in the parking lot afterward. She was thirty-three now. Her hands were as steady as a surgeon's. Steadier. Surgeons, she imagined, occasionally questioned their choices.

She walked past the registers, through the security gates, into the bright suburban afternoon. Nothing beeped. Nothing ever beeped. She had a sense for which items were tagged that bordered on the clairvoyant.

In her car, she placed the candle in the center console alongside a receipt from the dry cleaner's and a protein bar wrapper. She would not light the candle. She would not display the candle. Tonight, after her husband fell asleep and the house was dark, she would carry the candle downstairs to the basement, to the storage closet she'd installed a keyed deadbolt on—*for important documents*, she'd told Marcus—and she would place it on the shelf beside approximately three hundred other candles, and four hundred tubes of lip balm, and sixteen Bluetooth speakers, and an entire drawer of reading glasses she did not need and would never wear, and she would look at them, all of them, and for thirty seconds or maybe a full minute she would feel something that functioned almost identically to being okay.

Then she would lock the door and go back to bed.

Dean Ostrowski hadn't seen the surface of his kitchen table since the previous administration.

Not Trump—Obama. The first term. He'd set a newspaper down on it in 2011 and that newspaper had become a geological stratum, a foundation layer upon which thirteen years of accumulated matter had accreted: junk mail and takeout menus, expired coupons and unmatched batteries, three broken phones he intended to repair, a salad spinner still in its box, eleven twist-ties gathered into a small nervous bundle, a cat toy for a cat he'd never owned, and, at the current apex, a stack of *National Geographic* magazines dating from 1974 to 1981 that he'd rescued from a neighbor's recycling bin at 2:00 a.m. the previous Thursday while wearing, for reasons that even he recognized as clinically significant, a headlamp.

He ate standing at the counter. He navigated his apartment through a system of paths—narrow defiles carved between the mountains of *things*—that he privately referred to as the Corridor and the Canyon and, when he was feeling particularly honest, the Shame Hallway. The Shame Hallway led from the bathroom to the bedroom and was flanked on both sides by stacked plastic bins, their contents labeled in his precise architect's hand: *CABLES MAYBE USEFUL, FABRIC SCRAPS (COTTON), MANUALS UNKNOWN DEVICES, TO SORT (URGENT), TO SORT (LESS URGENT), TO SORT.*

He'd been a genuine architect once. He'd had an office in a glass building in Portland and a drafting table and colleagues who admired his eye for space and light. This was the kind of irony that, if he thought about it directly, might kill him, so he didn't think about it directly. He thought about it obliquely, the way you'd glance at an eclipse: there was a man who could design airy, soaring spaces to house the human body, and that man now lived in a one-bedroom apartment where he had to turn sideways to reach the toilet.

He was currently standing in the Canyon, holding a ceramic soap dish in the shape of a duck that he'd found in the alley behind his building. The soap dish was chipped. It was ugly. It matched nothing he owned. He cradled it in both hands like a wounded bird, and the feeling was so familiar it was almost boring: if he threw it away, something terrible would happen. He couldn't articulate what. He'd tried, once, in therapy—the six sessions he'd attended before the therapist's office building had a yard sale and he'd carried home two boxes of strangers' books and been too ashamed to go back. The terrible thing wasn't specific. It was more like a weather system, a low-pressure front of dread that moved in whenever he tried to let an object go. As though every object were a finger plugging a hole in a dam. As though the dam were the only thing between him and a flood whose exact nature and content he could not name but knew, with the conviction of a man who has touched a live wire and survived, would destroy him.

He placed the duck on a shelf he'd built above the microwave. The shelf was already full. He placed it on top of what was already there. He'd build another shelf this weekend.

He always built another shelf.

Sylvie Tran sat in the dark of her parked car in a Wendy's drive-through lane, engine running, and ate.

She'd ordered two Baconators, a large fry, a ten-piece nugget, a Frosty, and a side salad she would not touch. The side salad was for the narrative—it was the story she told the part of her brain that watched and judged and kept meticulous accounts. *See? A salad. This is a normal meal that a normal person is eating in a normal way.* The Baconators told a different story, but the Baconators were disappearing fast, and what is consumed does not testify.

She ate with the efficiency of someone defusing a bomb. There was no pleasure in it, or rather, the pleasure was so enormous and so annihilating that it had lapped back around into something indistinguishable from pain. Each bite was a door slamming shut on a room she couldn't bear to be in, and the next bite was the next door, and the one after that, and the project was to get every door in the hallway closed before the thing at the end of the hallway reached her, which meant she needed to eat faster.

She was a pediatric nurse. She spent her days monitoring children's vitals and explaining to frightened parents that fevers were the body's way of fighting and that the human organism was, in general, astonishingly good at healing itself. She was authoritative and calm. She was good at her job. None of the parents at CHOP would have recognized the woman in the Wendy's parking lot—jaw working, eyes fixed on the middle distance, a spot of ketchup on the lanyard she'd forgotten to take off. This was the other Sylvie. This Sylvie came out at specific times: after a bad shift, after a fight with her mother, after any interaction that left her with an unprocessed feeling she couldn't name. The other Sylvie was fast and silent and mechanical and always, always hungry in a way that food could not fix but that food could postpone, and postponement was the only technology Sylvie had ever reliably mastered.

She finished the second Baconator. She started on the nuggets.

In thirty minutes she would go home. She would brush her teeth twice, bag the trash, and take it to the dumpster behind her apartment complex rather than the kitchen bin, because the kitchen bin was in the kitchen and the kitchen was where her roommate might see, and her roommate was a CrossFit instructor who ate sprouted things and who once, with no malice at all, had said the words *nothing tastes as good as strong feels* and Sylvie had smiled and agreed while internally adding this sentence to the long list of sentences that were true for other people.

In the car, alone, she peeled the wrapper off the last nugget. The feeling was already fading. The doors were closing, but they were cheap doors, and the thing at the end of the hallway was patient.

Theo Barker was going to lose fourteen thousand dollars in the next ninety seconds and he felt, for the first time all week, genuinely alive.

He was in a private back room at the Horseshoe in downtown Cleveland, playing no-limit hold 'em with five men who had more money than sense and one woman who had more sense than everyone else at the table combined and who was, at this moment, sitting on a nut flush that Theo could read in the microexpressions of her left hand but was choosing to ignore because the size of the pot was producing in his circulatory system a sensation functionally identical to what he imagined skydiving felt like, or possibly heroin.

He had a pair of sevens. A pair of sevens was nothing. A pair of sevens against a probable flush was a bonfire into which you fed your rent and your car payment and the $400 you'd promised your sister you'd pay back and the last shreds of the story you told yourself about being a guy who'd gotten his shit together.

He pushed his chips forward.

Theo had gotten his shit together multiple times. He'd gotten it together in 2016 and then un-together'd it at a riverboat casino in Indiana. He'd gotten it together again in 2019, attended GA meetings for nine months, and then blown it on a weekend trip to Reno that he'd told his employer was a sales conference. He'd gotten it most recently together eight months ago, when he'd started the landscaping business and things had been going well enough that his sister had stopped looking at him with that specific expression—pity braided with exhaustion—that he hated more than anything else on earth.

The woman with the flush called.

Theo lost.

Fourteen thousand, two hundred dollars. The number was so clean and terrible that it was almost beautiful. He felt the loss in his chest like a physical cavity—a hole punched through the sternum, cool air rushing in. And the thing was: the hole felt good. The hole was *information*. The hole told him he was still capable of feeling something at a magnitude that matched the magnitude of the thing inside him that needed to be felt over. The everyday emotions—contentment, mild frustration, the gentle warmth of a Sunday afternoon—were like trying to drown out a fire alarm with a whisper. He needed the loss. He needed the fourteen thousand dollars to vanish so that the panic would come and the panic would be large enough, loud enough, catastrophic enough to cover the other sound, the older sound, the one that came from the basement of his memory where something was locked behind a door that got thinner every year.

He shook the woman's hand. He smiled. He walked to the parking lot and sat in his truck for a long time.

His phone showed two missed calls from his sister, one from a client about a lawn renovation, and a voicemail from his GA sponsor that he would not listen to.

He started the engine. He would drive home. He would lie in bed doing mental arithmetic—the terrible, compulsive math of how to cover the loss, which invoices he could accelerate, which bills he could defer—and the arithmetic would keep him busy, would keep his surface mind occupied, and underneath, in the dark, the relief of the loss would do its work, muffling the thing that could not be muffled any other way.

---

Four people. Four cities. Four versions of the same gesture: the hand reaching for the thing that will ruin you, the moment of sweet blankness when it closes around it, the silence afterward that passes for peace.

It was a Tuesday in October. The leaves were turning. None of them knew yet that the letters were already in the mail.

### 2.

Mara found hers on Thursday, tucked between a credit card offer and a flyer for a roofing company. A plain white envelope, her full legal name—*Mara Leigh Chen*—typed across the front in a serif font that looked almost institutional. No return address. Postmarked from a town in upstate New York she hadn't thought about in twenty years until she read the postmark and felt the blood leave her face so fast she had to put a hand on the kitchen counter.

She opened it standing up, with a paring knife, the way she opened everything—precisely, along the seam. The paper inside was heavy stock, the kind that costs more than it should. The message was typed in the same serif font:

---

*Dear Mara,*

*You've been collecting things that don't belong to you for a long time. I wonder if you remember the first thing you buried that wasn't yours.*

*Camp Aldersgate closed in 2009. The property has been in probate since 2017. No one goes there. No one has gone there.*

*But something you left behind is still in the ground.*

*I think it's time you come collect it.*

*You have until 6:00 a.m., Sunday, October 20th. Come to the camp. Bring the others. Find what you buried.*

*If you don't: I have documentation. I have photographs. I have a letter to the Garrett County Sheriff's Department written and stamped and waiting.*

*You were children then. You're not children now. What you did doesn't go away because you grew up. What you did doesn't go away because you stopped talking about it.*

*I know what it did to each of you. I've been watching. I know about the things you take, Mara. I know about all of it.*

*Sunday. 6:00 a.m. Remember the way.*

---
