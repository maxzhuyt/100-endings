# The Last Watch of the *Mara Celeste*

---

You want to know about the boat.

Everyone wants to know about the boat. The adjusters, the coast guard, the man from the maritime commission with his little recorder and his little questions. They all want to know about the boat. They ask about the storm. They ask about the heading. They ask why we didn't run for Hamilton when the weather turned, and I tell them what I know, which is what I saw, which is not the same as what they're looking for.

I was the cook. I want to be clear about that. I was the cook, and I saw what I saw from where cooks see things—through the galley hatch, from the companionway stairs, across the card table at six in the morning when the coffee was still hot and the day hadn't yet decided what it was going to do to us.

They want sequence. They want the story to go in order. But that's not how I have it. I have it in pieces, and some of the pieces are louder than others, and some I can hold up to the light and turn, and some I can't pick up at all.

So I'll tell you what I can pick up.

---

The *Mara Celeste* was a forty-six-foot ketch, wooden-hulled, built in 1958 by a man named James Wen at a boatyard in Mystic that doesn't exist anymore. I know this because his granddaughter told me, not all at once, not in any kind of a speech, but in the way you learn anything real about a person—a sentence here, a correction there, a hand on a particular plank with a look that meant something she didn't say.

The boat was old. I want to be honest about that too. The brightwork had gone milky in places and the port-side winch had a trick to it—you had to lean your hip into the base while you cranked or the whole thing would bind. There was a patch on the helm seat, canvas over canvas, sewn in a stitch that wasn't Wen's. Bigger hands. Heavier thread. She never re-did it. She could have. She was good with her hands, better than good, she could fix anything on that boat because she'd grown up fixing it, but she left the patch the way it was. The name on the transom was painted in block letters that had been gone over once, maybe twice, in a blue that didn't quite match the original blue, and if you looked closely you could see the shadow of the first coat underneath, the way you can see old roads under new pavement from the air.

I knew the galley better than any room I've ever paid rent on. The knife rack was mounted wrong—too close to the stove—so if you were rolling in any kind of sea you'd put your arm across the handles reaching for a pot. I burned myself on the first day and cut myself on the third and after that my body knew the space and I didn't think about it anymore. The cutting board was built into the counter. Someone had routed a groove around its edge to catch the juice from fish or fruit, and the groove had gone dark with years of use. I'd run my thumbnail along it while I was thinking. I did this so many times the groove became my groove too, my thumbnail in the same channel as whoever had cut limes there before me.

Wen's cabin was forward. I slept aft, above the engine compartment, where the heat and the noise either kept you up or put you under, depending on how tired you were. Between us, the galley. Everything that mattered between us happened in that galley.

I shipped on in April, Bridgetown. She needed someone cheap and I needed a bunk. That's the whole of it. She was running cargo between the islands—nothing glamorous, nothing illegal, just goods that needed to get from one place to another on a schedule that was slower and less reliable than the ferry but also less expensive. She had a reputation for being difficult, which as far as I could tell meant she didn't talk much and didn't apologize for it.

I didn't need her to talk. I needed a galley and a paycheck and somewhere to be that was moving, and she gave me all three.

---

The card game.

I should tell you about the card game because everyone asks me about the end, about the last day, about the cards on the table, and they don't understand what they're asking about unless they know what it was before.

It was rummy. Not gin rummy—the other kind, the long kind, the kind with melds and runs that build over sessions if you let them. We didn't keep a running score, or we did, but loosely, on the back of a provisions list that got turned over every week. She'd mark her points in pencil. I'd mark mine in pen. Neither of us acknowledged this difference and it meant nothing but it was consistent and now I can't stop thinking about it.

Six in the morning, every morning. I'd put the coffee on and she'd come down from her bunk or up from the chart table, depending on whether she'd slept, and she'd sit across from me in the galley and I'd deal because she said I shuffled better, which wasn't true, but I liked doing it so I didn't argue.

She held her cards close to her chest, fanned tight, and she'd look at them a long time before she drew. She was not a good bluffer but she thought she was, which is a different thing. She had a tell—when she had a good hand she'd go still, and when she had nothing she'd move too much, rearranging cards that didn't need rearranging. I never told her this. Telling her would have ended something I didn't want to end.

Some mornings we played a full hand. Some mornings she'd draw two cards and then go topside because the wind shifted or a line needed tending and I'd leave the hands face-down on the table and when she came back, sometimes an hour later, she'd sit down and pick up her cards as if no time had passed. We never discussed this either. It was just our way.

---

The letters.

The first one I saw was in May. I didn't read it. I saw it on the chart table, half-folded, and I saw enough of the letterhead to know what it was—one of those institutional fonts, a bank or a lender, the kind of paper that exists to make you feel small. She'd left it out, which wasn't like her. Wen was private. She kept her documents in a waterproof case under her bunk and she didn't leave things where people could see them.

But she left that one out, and I think now that it was because she'd been reading it for a long time and her hands had gotten tired.

After that, they came regularly. Bridgetown, St. George's, Antigua, every port had one waiting, forwarded through the harbormaster's office or general delivery, and each time Wen would take the envelope and go into her cabin and the door would close and later she'd come out and she wouldn't mention it and neither would I.

What I noticed was this: she stopped smiling in June.

I don't mean she became grim or sullen. She was still herself. She joked with the dockworkers in Castries and haggled over fuel and told me a long, improbable story about her grandfather racing a nor'easter off Nantucket that I'm still not sure was true. But the smile—the real one, the one that came up slow and started in her eyes and took its time getting to her mouth—that one stopped. What replaced it was a performance of a smile, competent, adequate, and I could tell the difference because I'd been watching her face across a card table for two months by then and a card table is a very honest distance. Close enough to see. Far enough that you're both pretending not to look.

I found out about the mortgage from Danny Reeves, who ran fuel at the marina in Martinique and who talked too much and knew too much about everyone else's business. He told me the bank had called in the loan on the *Mara Celeste.* Wen's grandfather had taken out the mortgage in 1979. Wen had inherited the debt with the boat. She'd been servicing it for eleven years on cargo fees that were getting thinner every season as the bigger outfits undercut her. She'd missed four payments. The bank had sold the note to a collections agency. The collections agency had contracted a marine recovery firm.

They were going to take the boat.

Danny told me this while I was buying onions and I stood there in his fuel office holding a mesh bag of onions and I understood everything that had happened to Wen's face over the last two months—every tightened jaw, every silence at the table, every night she stayed up past midnight at the chart table plotting routes that would keep us at sea a little longer.

I went back to the *Mara Celeste* and I made dinner and I didn't say anything. She didn't say anything. We ate rice and fish in the cockpit and watched the harbor lights come on and she said, "Weather's turning south," and I said, "I heard," and that was the last night before the last passage.

---

We left Martinique on August 14th with a partial cargo of machine parts for Hamilton. The route would take us north-northwest through the Sargasso, that strange dead space in the middle of the Atlantic where the currents turn in on themselves. Sailors used to say the Sargasso could hold you—the weed would thicken around your keel and the wind would die and you'd sit there, caught, until your water ran out. That's mostly myth. Mostly. But there is something about the Sargasso that makes you feel like you've stepped off the map, like the ocean there is a different, older ocean that isn't entirely interested in letting you pass through.

The weed was thick that week. Great mats of sargassum, golden-brown, tangled with plastic and pumice and the occasional lost buoy, drifting in slow gyres that looked purposeful, almost animal. I'd watch it from the galley hatch while I cooked and think about how you could live your whole life in one place and never arrive anywhere.

We had a crew of three besides ourselves. Matteo, who was young and strong and afraid of nothing, which meant he was afraid of everything and didn't know it yet. Sam, who had been sailing since before I was born and who slept with a knife in a sheath duct-taped to the bulkhead beside her bunk. And Tomas, who was quiet and competent and who I think was in love with Wen in the resigned, uncomplaining way that people love someone who will never look at them that way.

Five of us. For what was coming, it might as well have been none.

---

The forecast changed on the 16th. A tropical system that had been meandering east of the Leewards turned north and then north-northwest and began to intensify. By the time the advisory reached us it was a Category 2, which doesn't sound like much if you've never been on a forty-six-foot ketch in open water. On a boat like the *Mara Celeste,* a Category 2 is the end of every argument you've ever had with the sea.

Sam brought the printout to Wen at the chart table. I was in the galley, close enough to hear.

"We can make Hamilton in thirty hours if we push," Sam said. "Twenty-four if the wind cooperates."

Wen looked at the chart a long time. Too long for someone who already knew these waters better than she knew rooms she'd rented.

"What's our heading now?" she asked. Which she already knew.

"Three-twenty," Sam said. "Wen. We need to come about."

Wen folded the advisory and put it in her back pocket and said, "Let me think on it."

I knew then. I knew the way you know something with your stomach before your head catches up. Hamilton, Bermuda was where the recovery firm had an office. Hamilton was where the repo men were waiting with their paperwork and their crane and their claim on a dead man's boat. Wen wasn't weighing options. Wen was looking at two doors and behind both of them was the same loss, and at least the storm would not come with a clipboard.

She didn't turn for Hamilton.

By midnight the barometer had dropped twelve millibars and the wind had shifted east and the argument on deck was loud enough that I could hear it over the stove.

---

I want to stop here and say something because the man from the maritime commission asked me this and I answered it badly and I want to answer it better.

He asked me if Captain Wen made a reckless decision. He used that word. *Reckless.* And I said no, and he wrote that down, and what I should have said was something harder and more true, which is that Wen made the only decision that let her keep the one thing she had left, which was not her life but her boat, not her boat but her grandfather's boat, not the boat itself but what the boat meant—that he had built it and she had kept it and it was *theirs* in a way that a bank's numbers on a ledger could claim but never make real.

She wasn't brave. I want to be clear about that too.

I have heard people call her brave and it makes me angry in a way I can't fully explain. Courage is when you're afraid and you act anyway. Wen was not afraid of the typhoon. She was afraid of driving into Hamilton harbor and watching men she'd never met step aboard her grandfather's boat with a lien and a bill of sale.

She chose drowning over dispossession.

Those are different things, and the difference matters, and if you walk away from this thinking she was some kind of hero in a slicker standing tall against the gale, then I've failed at telling you what actually happened to us.

---

## Day One

The storm arrived like weather. That's the thing people don't understand. It doesn't come all at once. It comes like a long sentence that keeps going past where you expected the period.

The wind built through the morning. The swells went from six feet to ten to fifteen over the course of hours, and for a while the *Mara Celeste* handled it the way she'd handled storms for thirty-three years—she rolled and pitched and found her feet and kept going. She was a good boat. She was a beautiful boat. Even in that wind, she had a steadiness that I think came from the way she'd been built, with an understanding of wood and water that doesn't exist in fiberglass, doesn't exist in molds and factories, only exists in the hands of a man who shaped every plank to meet every other plank the way you'd introduce members of a family.

The crew worked. Wen worked. I cooked. This sounds absurd—a woman making soup in a typhoon—but the body needs fuel and the hands need occupation and a galley is a place where you can still be useful when you can't do anything about what's happening outside.

At six in the morning, because it was six in the morning, I put the coffee on.

Wen came down. She was soaked through and her hair was plastered flat and there was a bruise forming on her forearm where the boom had caught her, and she sat down across from me at the galley table, which was gimbaled so it stayed level even when the world didn't, and she said, "Deal."

I dealt.

She picked up her cards. She held them close. She looked at them for a long time. Outside, something metallic broke free on deck and went clattering across the planking and over the side and was gone. Wen didn't look up.

"Draw or pass?" I said.

She drew. She studied the card. She placed it in her hand and rearranged two others and I knew from the rearranging that she had nothing, and I didn't say so.

"Your turn," she said.

I played my hand. She played hers. The boat rolled twenty degrees to port and held there for a long, sick moment before coming back. The coffee pot slid on its rail and caught. Wen laid down a meld—three sevens—and marked the points in pencil on the provisions list, which was Wednesday's list, which had flour, yeast, tinned tomatoes, salt written in my hand on the other side.

That was the last normal morning.

---

I brought her tea at noon. This was the other thing I did—coffee at six, tea at twelve. She drank the tea. She was standing at the helm and the spray was coming over in sheets and the tea was half salt water before she got it to her lips but she drank it and handed the cup back and said, "Thank you, Marla," which is my name, which she didn't often use.
