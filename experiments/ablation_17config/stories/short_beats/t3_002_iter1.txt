# The Last Watch of the Mara Celeste

People want to know about the storm, so I will tell you about the storm. But I have to tell it the way I know it, which is not the way you want to hear it.

Captain Wen died at the wheel of her grandfather's ship on the morning of November eleventh in a typhoon that the weather service later named Bettina, though we did not know its name while we were inside it. I was the cook. I had been the cook for six years. What I can tell you about Captain Wen's final hours I can tell you because I was bringing her tea, which is what I did every four hours regardless of conditions, and what I did that night and that morning and the morning after that, because she did not ask me to stop and I did not see a reason to.

You have already decided what kind of story this is. I can see it in the way you're sitting. The captain who would not abandon her vessel. You are thinking of duty, of some code she carried like a logbook in her chest, of the noble way certain people choose the sea over survival. I am telling you now so that we do not have to come back to it later: that is not why she stayed.

The bank had been writing letters since March. First to the company address in Surabaya, then to the registered office in Hamilton, then directly to the ship, which meant they had paid someone to find out our route. Wen read the first few at the galley table while I was cutting onions. She read them the way she read tide charts — silently, once, all the way through — and then she folded them back into their envelopes and put them in the drawer where I kept the cinnamon and the star anise. I did not ask why that drawer. It was not my business. By August, the letters did not come to the ship anymore. They came through attorneys. I know this because the envelopes changed — heavier paper, windows showing her full legal name, which I had not known until then was Wen Yijun. The attorneys were in Bermuda, which was our next scheduled port. The letters said the Mara Celeste was to be surrendered upon arrival to satisfy the outstanding debt on a loan Wen's father had taken eleven years prior against the vessel's assessed value. The loan had been refinanced twice. The interest alone was more than the ship earned in a year. Wen's father had been dead for three years. He had signed with a thumbprint and a guarantor who turned out to be a man from the bank.

So when Bettina formed to the east and the maritime advisory came through telling all vessels in our corridor to divert to the nearest safe harbor, and the nearest safe harbor was Hamilton, Wen looked at the barometer and then looked at the chart and then told the first mate — this was Oso, a decent man, slow to dress — that she would be holding course through the system rather than making port. Oso asked if she was sure. She said she was sure. He asked again, differently. She told him that anyone who wanted off could take the tender, that she would not hold it against them, and that there were flares and water enough for twelve hours. Oso looked at me. I was making rice. I did not look up. There was no decision for me to make because I had made it years before that, though I could not tell you exactly when. Seven people left on the tender. The four who stayed were Wen, myself, Oso, and the engineer, Pak Salim, who was sixty and had no family alive and said, when asked, that he would rather not ride a rubber boat in open ocean at his age.

Oso and Pak Salim are not part of this story in the way that you and I are discussing it. They did what they did and I honor them for it but I did not watch them. I watched her.

The wind came first as sound. If you have not been in a typhoon on a wooden ship — and the Mara Celeste was teak, built by F.H. Lau & Sons in 1952 to Wen's grandfather's specifications, sixty-eight feet, gaff-rigged, the last of seven sister ships and the only one still sailing — then I will tell you that the sound comes before anything else. It comes the way a headache comes, not from a direction but from inside the structure of things. The teak talked. It had always talked. On calm nights, if you pressed your ear to the galley wall, you could hear the wood settling into itself, adjusting to the temperature of the water, and it sounded like an old woman turning in bed. That was the ship I knew. The sound it made when Bettina's outer bands reached us was not that. It was the sound of wood remembering that it had once been a tree, and that trees can be broken.

I put the kettle on at six in the evening, which was when I always put it on for Wen's first night watch. Oolong, loose leaf, the tin she kept in her quarters that she bought in bulk from a shop in George Town. I used the same pot I always used, the white enamel one with the chip on the spout. The water took longer to boil because I had to brace myself against the stove with my hip, and the ship was already rolling in a way that made the flame lean. I poured. I carried it up. She was at the wheel. The rain had not started yet but you could smell it — a smell like rust, like the sky itself was corroding. I set the cup on the ledge beside the binnacle where I always set it. She said thank you. I went back down.

When I came to collect the cup an hour later, it was full and cold. This was not unusual in rough weather. I poured it out and made a new cup and brought it up. She was still at the wheel. The rain had started — not falling, arriving, is the better word, as if the air had simply become water and had been waiting to tell us. Wen's hair was plastered to her head and her jacket was dark with it but her hands on the wheel were steady and placed at ten and two, which was how her grandfather had taught her, she once told me, because he believed the old courtesies applied even when the sea did not reciprocate.

The second cup went cold too. I collected it, poured it out, and made a third. This was our night.

By midnight, the sea was in the galley. Not a flood — a visitation. It came through the seams of the companionway hatch in thin, exploratory streams, feeling its way along the floorboards like fingers. I mopped it with the towel I used for dishes. I mopped it again. The pumps were running — I could hear them beneath me, Pak Salim's territory, that shuddering rhythm — but the water came faster than the pumps could answer. I stopped mopping and started bailing with the stockpot, the big one, twenty liters, and I thought as I bailed that I had made dashi in this pot two days ago and that the sea was returning to collect its stock.

The pumps failed at four in the morning. I know the time because I had been watching the clock — not out of anxiety, but because the clock was how I organized everything I did, and when the pumps stopped, the silence they left was louder than the storm. Pak Salim came up from below, soaked to the waist, and said one word to Wen. She nodded. He went back down. I do not know what he tried to do in the hours after that. I think he was the kind of man who could not be in a room with a broken machine and not put his hands on it, and I think that is where he was at the end, below, with his hands on it.

Wen lashed herself to the mast before dawn. She used a bowline on a bight for her waist — the loop large enough that she could still turn her torso to reach the wheel — and a clove hitch to fix the trailing end to the mast's lower cleat. Then a round turn and two half hitches as a backup, because her grandfather had taught her that every knot deserves a witness, by which he meant a second knot that watches the first. I stood in the companionway and observed her tying them. Her fingers did not hesitate. She tied knots the way I diced garlic — with the kind of speed that is not fast but merely unburdened by thought, because the motion had been performed ten thousand times and the fingers had their own memory. The line was three-strand manila, old, soft with use. She pulled each knot tight with a sharp exhale through her nose, the same sound she made when she sat down to a plate I had cooked and found it right.

Oso was on the foredeck. I could see him through the rain, a shape, lashing down what could be lashed. A wave came over the bow and he went flat against the deck and for a moment he was part of the sea and then he was not. He stood up. He continued lashing. He was a decent man, as I said. Slow to dress.

The morning came gray, the way mornings come in a typhoon — not sunrise, but a slow, grudging dilution of the dark, as if the night resented leaving. The ship was listing. The Mara Celeste had a draft of eight feet in good trim and now she was favoring starboard by — I don't know the number. Enough that the galley table was no longer level. Enough that the cans I kept in the lower cabinet had rolled to one side and were pressing against the wall like passengers on a train taking a curve.

I set the table for two.

This is the part you need to understand, though I don't think I can make you understand it in the way I mean it. I set the table because it was morning and in the morning I set the table. The ship was dying. I could feel it — not in my mind, where you feel fear and decisions, but in my feet, which had known this deck for six years and could tell the difference between a ship that is struggling and a ship that has begun to grieve for itself. The Mara Celeste was grieving. And I set the table because the table was my province and Wen had the wheel and Pak Salim had the engine and Oso had the deck, and if I did not set the table, then I was just a person standing in a galley on a sinking ship, and I could not be that person. I did not have the constitution for it. I only had the constitution for the cook. So I set the cups and I set the saucers and I opened the drawer and I took out the cards.

We played every morning. Wen and I. Six years of mornings, minus the ones she spent ashore, which were not many, because Wen did not like the shore and the shore, with its banks and its attorneys and its letters, had given her no reason to revise that opinion. The game was cribbage. She had learned it from her grandfather. I had learned it from her, badly at first, and then less badly, and then well enough that she had to pay attention, which I think was when she began to like me, because Wen's affection was a thing you earned by making her concentrate.

I went up. The rain had thinned to a steady, driving sheet — not gentle, but no longer the hammering chaos of the night. The sea was still enormous. I could see it over the rail, walls of water that moved like architecture, like buildings falling slowly in every direction. But the wind had shifted and there were moments — three seconds, five seconds — when you could stand on deck without holding something. I brought the board, the pegs, and the deck of cards in a canvas bag I held against my chest.

Wen was still lashed to the mast. The wheel was cleated now — there was no point steering; we were going where the sea wanted us to go, and she must have understood this, because she had let go. Her hands were in her lap. The ropes held her upright. She looked at me when I came up, and her face — I will tell you what her face looked like because I remember it with the precision of someone who looked at it every day. Her face was calm. Not peaceful, which is what people say when they mean the person had given up. Calm in the way that a ledger is calm when the numbers have been tallied and the books are closed. She had done her accounting. The ship was hers and the sea was taking it and the bank was not, and the numbers balanced.

I sat on the deck across from her and put the board between us. The deck was wet. The board was wet. I dealt. The ship tilted and the cards slid and I caught them and held them and dealt again, pressing each card flat against the wood with my palm to keep it in place. She picked up her hand and fanned it. Six cards. I picked up mine.

She had the five of diamonds, the five of hearts, the jack of spades, the four of clubs, the seven of spades, and — I saw when she laid her crib cards down — she put the seven and the jack in the crib, which told me she was playing for the double run, which was how she always played when she had two fives. I had a pair of eights and nothing else worth naming. The starter card was the six of diamonds, which helped neither of us. We pegged. She led with the four. I played an eight. She played the five for seventeen. I played my other eight for twenty-five, and she said, "Go," which was the first word she had spoken to me since the tea.

I pegged one for the go and one for the last card and we counted our hands.

She won the hand by four. She was ahead by twelve on the board. She had been ahead on the board for most of six years, if I am being honest, and I am being honest with you because there is no reason left not to be. She was the better player. She saw the combinations faster. I saw them eventually, but she saw them the way she saw weather — whole, all at once, the pattern arriving in her mind fully formed.

We played a second hand. The ship groaned beneath us — a deep, structural sound, not the teak talking but the keel arguing with itself, the joints that held the Mara Celeste together debating whether to continue. A wave broke over the port side and water sheeted across the deck and wet the cards and I wiped them on my shirt and we continued. She won the second hand too. She was ahead by nineteen. During the play, she said "fifteen-two" and "thirty-one for two," and the sound of her voice counting — the absolute ordinary flatness of a woman counting pegging points in cribbage while her grandfather's ship took on water around her — was a sound I cannot make you hear. I am telling you the facts of it. I cannot give you the sound. The sound is mine.

Oso appeared at some point during the second hand. He stood at the edge of the cockpit, holding the boom gallows, and watched us play. His face was doing something I do not want to describe. He said something to Wen. I think it was about the tender, or what was left of it, or where the water was now. She looked at him and said, "Your deal, Mara," which was not his name. It was mine. She called me Mara. She had called me Mara since the second year, which was the year I made her the black sesame soup her grandmother used to make, and she said, "This ship is named wrong, it should be named after you," and after that I was Mara and the ship was the ship.

I dealt the third hand. I was dealt the five of clubs, the five of spades, the ten of hearts, the queen of diamonds, the three of clubs, and the nine of hearts. I put the nine and the queen in the crib. The starter card was the jack of clubs, and Wen said, "His nobs," because the jack matched the starter suit in my crib, which meant a point for me, and she said it the way she always said it, slightly clipped, as if granting me even a single point by luck offended her sense of order. I played the three. She played a two — I did not see where the two came from, she must have been holding it — and pegged for the run. I played the five. She paused.

She paused for a long time. The ship was leaning further now. The cribbage board was sliding slowly toward her on the tilted deck, the pegs trembling in their holes like small, frightened animals. I put my hand on the board to hold it. She looked at her cards. The wind had pulled a strand of hair loose from where it was plastered to her head, and it stood out from her temple like a wire. She played the ten. Twenty. I played my other five for twenty-five. She said, "Go." I pegged one.

I won the hand by two. She was still ahead on the board by fifteen. She did not offer a fourth hand and I did not ask for one.
