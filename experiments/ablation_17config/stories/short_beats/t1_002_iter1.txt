PROCEEDINGS COMMENCED AT 9:03 A.M.

THE COURT: Good morning. We are on the record in State versus Vance, Case Number 19-CF-00347. This is the time set for voir dire examination. Are the parties ready to proceed?

MR. HARBAUGH: The State is ready, Your Honor.

MS. CHEN: The defense is ready, Your Honor.

THE COURT: Thank you. Will the clerk please bring in the first panel.

(Jury panel entered the courtroom at 9:06 a.m.)

THE COURT: Good morning, ladies and gentlemen. My name is Judge Thomas Warfield and I want to thank you for your service here today. I know that jury duty can be an inconvenience, and I appreciate your willingness to participate in this important process. Now, this case involves charges of aggravated assault, unlawful restraint, and conspiracy to commit a felony against the defendant, Ms. Eleanor Vance. I want to be straightforward with you: some of the testimony you may hear in this trial will involve allegations of ritualistic practices. I want to ask each of you whether you have any familiarity with or strong opinions about such matters that might affect your ability to be impartial. We'll start with Seat Number One. Sir, could you please state your name for the record.

JUROR NO. 1: Uh, Daniel Huckaby.

THE COURT: Mr. Huckaby, do you have any familiarity with the subject matter I just described?

JUROR NO. 1: Not really. I mean, I think I saw something about this on the news?

THE COURT: That's fine. Do you believe that having seen news coverage would affect your ability to evaluate the evidence presented in this courtroom?

JUROR NO. 1: No, sir. I don't think so.

THE COURT: Thank you. Seat Number Two.

JUROR NO. 2: Margaret Fife.

THE COURT: Ms. Fife, same question.

JUROR NO. 2: I go to church, if that's what you're asking.

THE COURT: I'm asking whether you have any preexisting opinions about allegations of ritualistic abuse that might prevent you from—

JUROR NO. 2: I believe the devil is real, Judge. I don't think that makes me partial. I think that makes me honest.

THE COURT: Noted. Counsel, any objection to Juror Number Two at this time?

MR. HARBAUGH: No objection from the State.

MS. CHEN: No objection, Your Honor.

THE COURT: Seat Number Three.

JUROR NO. 3: Paul Whitmore.

THE COURT: Mr. Whitmore, any familiarity or strong opinions?

JUROR NO. 3: No, sir.

THE COURT: Seat Number Four.

JUROR NO. 4: Linda Sprouse.

THE COURT: Ms. Sprouse?

JUROR NO. 4: I just want to say I can be fair.

THE COURT: That's all we ask. Seat Number Five.

JUROR NO. 5: Robert Gaines.

THE COURT: Mr. Gaines?

JUROR NO. 5: I'll be honest, Your Honor, I've followed this case some. But I haven't made up my mind about anything.

MR. HARBAUGH: The State would like to question Mr. Gaines further, Your Honor.

THE COURT: Go ahead.

MR. HARBAUGH: Mr. Gaines, when you say you've followed this case, can you tell us what sources you've consulted?

JUROR NO. 5: Just the Register-Herald. And there was something on Channel 6 a while back.

MR. HARBAUGH: And based on what you've seen, have you formed any opinion about whether the defendant is guilty or not guilty?

JUROR NO. 5: No, sir. I'm pretty good about waiting for the facts.

MR. HARBAUGH: The State finds Mr. Gaines acceptable, Your Honor.

MS. CHEN: Defense has no objection at this time.

THE COURT: Very well. We'll continue. Seat Number Six.

(Voir dire continued through the morning session. Jury selection completed at 4:47 p.m. A panel of twelve jurors and two alternates was empaneled.)

THE COURT: Ladies and gentlemen of the jury, we will begin opening statements tomorrow morning at nine o'clock. Please do not discuss this case with anyone, including each other, and please avoid any media coverage. Have a good evening.

(Court adjourned at 4:52 p.m.)

EXAMINATION OF DR. NEIL PRESCOTT
BY MR. HARBAUGH:

Q. Dr. Prescott, could you state your credentials for the record?

A. I hold an M.D. from the West Virginia University School of Medicine and a board certification in forensic psychiatry. I've been in practice for nineteen years. I currently serve as a consulting psychiatrist for the Southwestern Regional Jail and have performed court-ordered competency evaluations in approximately two hundred cases across the state.

Q. Were you retained by the State to evaluate the defendant in this case?

A. I was.

Q. And when did that evaluation take place?

A. I conducted two sessions with Ms. Vance. The first was on April fourteenth of this year, the second on April twenty-first. Each session lasted approximately ninety minutes.

Q. Can you describe the defendant's presentation during those evaluations?

A. Ms. Vance was cooperative. She was oriented to time, place, and person. Her speech was organized and coherent for the vast majority of both sessions. She demonstrated an above-average vocabulary and an ability to follow complex questions.

Q. You said "the vast majority." Were there exceptions?

A. There were. During the second session, when I asked Ms. Vance to describe the events of the night in question in her own words, she became — I would describe it as dissociative. Her affect flattened. Her eye contact, which had been consistent, broke. And she began to vocalize in a manner that was not consistent with organized speech.

Q. What do you mean by that?

A. She produced a sustained vocalization — approximately thirty seconds — that did not correspond to English or, as far as I could determine, any recognizable language. The phonemic content was repetitive and rhythmic.

Q. Glossolalia?

A. That would be the clinical term, yes. Speaking in tongues, colloquially. It's a phenomenon observed in certain dissociative states, as well as in some religious and cultural contexts.

Q. In your professional opinion, was the defendant experiencing a genuine dissociative episode or was this a Performance?

MS. CHEN: Objection. Leading.

THE COURT: Sustained. Rephrase, Mr. Harbaugh.

Q. In your professional opinion, Dr. Prescott, what was the nature of the vocalization you observed?

A. In my opinion, Ms. Vance was experiencing a genuine dissociative episode consistent with a diagnosis of dissociative identity disorder, which I noted in my report. The vocalization appeared involuntary. Her body posture changed. Her breathing pattern changed. I do not believe it was a conscious performance.

Q. Does the presence of a dissociative disorder, in your opinion, affect the defendant's ability to distinguish between events she experienced and events she believes she experienced?

A. That is the central clinical question in this case, and my answer is yes, it does affect that ability. Dissociative identity disorder involves significant disruptions in memory, identity, and perception. A person experiencing DID may hold beliefs about past events with absolute conviction — beliefs that are subjectively real to them — without those events having occurred in objective, external reality.

Q. So Ms. Vance could sincerely believe she was the victim of ritualistic abuse without that abuse having taken place?

MS. CHEN: Objection. Leading, Your Honor. And it calls for speculation.

THE COURT: I'll allow it. The witness is qualified as an expert. You may answer, Dr. Prescott.

A. Yes. In my clinical opinion, that is not only possible but consistent with her presentation.

Q. Thank you, Doctor. I have no further questions at this time.

THE COURT: Ms. Chen, cross-examination?

MS. CHEN: Thank you, Your Honor.

CROSS-EXAMINATION OF DR. PRESCOTT
BY MS. CHEN:

Q. Dr. Prescott, you've testified that Ms. Vance's vocalizations were, in your opinion, consistent with a dissociative episode. Is that correct?

A. Yes.

Q. Have you ever, in your nineteen years of practice, encountered a patient whose dissociative vocalizations contained verifiable content?

A. I'm not sure I understand the question.

Q. I'll be more specific. Have you ever encountered a case in which a patient, during a dissociative episode, produced vocalizations that, upon analysis, contained identifiable linguistic content — words, phrases, syntax from an actual language?

A. Glossolalia, by definition, is characterized by the *absence* of identifiable linguistic structure. It resembles language without being language. That's what distinguishes it from, say, xenoglossy, which is —

Q. I'm familiar with the term, Dr. Prescott. Xenoglossy — the production of a language the speaker has never learned. Is that a recognized clinical phenomenon?

A. It is a *claimed* phenomenon. It is not generally accepted within the psychiatric community.

Q. But it has been documented?

MR. HARBAUGH: Objection. Relevance.

MS. CHEN: Your Honor, the State has introduced testimony characterizing the defendant's vocalizations as meaningless. I am entitled to challenge that characterization.

THE COURT: I'll allow it within limits. Continue, Ms. Chen.

Q. Dr. Prescott, did you make any recording of Ms. Vance's vocalizations during your evaluation?

A. I did not. That is not standard practice for a competency evaluation.

Q. So your characterization of her vocalizations as meaningless glossolalia is based on your real-time perception during the session, not on any subsequent linguistic analysis?

A. Correct. But I should note that I have extensive experience with —

Q. Thank you, Doctor. That answers my question. Let me ask you one more thing. You testified that Ms. Vance "may hold beliefs about past events with absolute conviction" without those events having occurred. In your clinical experience, is the reverse also possible — that events occurred which the patient cannot access or articulate through normal speech?

A. That is — yes, that is a feature of dissociative disorders. Traumatic memories may be encoded in ways that resist normal narrative recall.

Q. So the vocalizations could, in theory, represent an attempt to express something that actually happened to her but that she cannot articulate in ordinary language?

A.
