# The Commissar's Confession

## I.

The list is on good paper. That is the first thing. The Republic cannot feed its children but it can source linen rag for its death warrants and this is not an observation you are having, this is not the kind of thought a man in your position has, so: the list is on good paper and Citizen Fouquier-Tinville is speaking.

"—the Committee's particular confidence in your thoroughness, Citizen Inspector, which is why—"

Nod. You are nodding. Good. The face is performing. The face has been performing for three years, eleven months, and some number of days you stopped counting when Maret died because Maret was the one who counted, Maret kept his little journal with its little cipher and somewhere in a drainage ditch outside Nanterre that journal is rotting into the same mud as Maret's hands that wrote it and this is not—

The list. Read the list.

Fouquier-Tinville's office smells of ink and tallow and the particular sourness of a man who sleeps in his clothes. Behind him the window gives onto the Cour du Mai where three clerks are eating bread in the rain. One of them laughs. The sound enters the room like something from a painting of a world where people laugh for reasons.

Read the list.

*Desmoulins, Lucile — wife of, already processed, associate of*

No. That name is finished. She is finished. They are all finished before you read them, that is the nature of a list like this, the verdict precedes the names, the names are a formality the Republic performs for the Republic's sense of its own procedures.

Read.

*Bourdier, Gilles — printer, Rue Saint-Jacques—*

Dead already. Executed Ventôse. But the network remains and here is the proof because the next name is

*Bourdier, Marguerite — widow of the above*

and the room does not change. The tallow does not gutter. Fouquier-Tinville is still speaking. The face is still performing. Inside the performing face a man is falling through a floor that was never there and *this is not the time,* you do not have the luxury of interiority because Fouquier-Tinville has paused, which means Fouquier-Tinville has asked a question, which means the face must answer it.

"The tumbril schedule," the face says, because that is always a safe thing to discuss, logistics, the machinery of death has machinery and the machinery requires schedules and schedules are the language a reliable man speaks.

"Tomorrow. First light. The sections are restless and Saint-Just feels a dawn procession calms the faubourgs."

Saint-Just *feels.* As if Saint-Just has access to the verb. But yes. Nod. Good.

*Bourdier, Marguerite.*

Her kitchen. November. The candle guttering because she could not afford good wax. The documents spread across the table and her hands — printer's hands, she'd learned the trade from her husband though no guild would acknowledge it — her hands sorting the forged *laissez-passers* with a speed that made you ashamed of your own fumbling, and the way she said *This one will bleed in the rain, you've used too much gum arabic,* and the absolute practical love in that correction. Not love for you. Love for the work. Love for the three families who would cross the barrier at Saint-Denis carrying paper that would not bleed in the rain because Marguerite Bourdier understood that craft was a form of mercy.

The list continues.

*Herault, Claude — clerk, Tribunal—*

Your man. The one who "lost" transfer orders. Twice.

*Pichon, Anne — seamstress, Section des Gravilliers—*

Your woman. The one who carried messages sewn into the hem allowances of republican cockades. The coarseness of the joke delighted Maret. *They wear our secrets on their heads,* he said, and he laughed the way he laughed, which was silently, his shoulders shaking, because even in safe houses Maret understood that laughter was a sound and sounds carried.

Maret is in a ditch.

*Relevigny, Jacques — former priest—*

The one who heard confessions that were not confessions, who received in his little room above the tanner's on the Rue Mouffetard the desperate and the hunted and sorted them with the calm of a man who believed God was watching and God was patient and God would, in His time— but God's time and the Committee's time are different durations and the Committee's time has caught up and Relevigny is on this list and God has not intervened and this is not a theological crisis you are having, this is an operational one.

Six names. Six of the eleven remaining contacts in the Secteur Nord network. Six people who trusted you, or trusted Maret, or trusted the promise Maret made with your face and your credentials and your position inside the apparatus that is now, with immaculate bureaucratic efficiency, handing you the list of your own people and calling it a promotion.

"You will oversee the transfer personally," Fouquier-Tinville says. He is not looking at you. He is looking at his next piece of paper. You are already processed. "The Committee is aware that several of these names may be connected to a broader moderate conspiracy. Your report will determine whether further arrests are warranted."

Further arrests. The other five. The ones not on this list. The three who are still operational — Clary in the Faubourg Saint-Antoine, Guillaume at the grain depot, the woman known only as Suzette who controls the route to Calais. If you are writing the report, you control the scope. If you control the scope, the three survive. That is the arithmetic. That is always the arithmetic. Six die so three live. Three live so the network breathes. The network breathes so that someday, when the Terror exhausts itself — when — if—

"It will be thorough," the face says.

Fouquier-Tinville nods without looking up. You are dismissed. You are trusted. You are one of the Revolution's most reliable instruments and the Revolution is handing you a blade and asking you to cut your own tendons and call it surgery.

The door. The corridor. The stairs. The rain.

Marguerite Bourdier is in a cell below this building and above her head your boots are striking the cobblestones and she cannot hear you and if she could she would not call out because she too understands that survival is a performance and the performance requires silence and silence is all you have ever given her and silence is all you will give her tomorrow when you walk her to the blade.

---

## II.

Dawn is the color of a bruise and the tumbril has a broken wheel that catches every third revolution — you can hear it, a stutter in the rhythm, *clack clack CLACK, clack clack CLACK* — and Marguerite Bourdier is standing because there is no room to sit and she has not looked at you.

She knows.

She must know. She is looking at the houses, the shuttered windows, the city pretending to sleep while six people ride to their deaths on a cart with a broken wheel, and she is not looking at you because looking at you would be a communication and communication in the presence of the guards would be— or because she hates you. That is also possible. That is, if you are honest inside this skull where honesty costs nothing because no one can hear it, the more likely explanation. She is a woman of considerable intelligence and she has had all night in a cell to perform the calculation you performed in the time it took to read her name and she has arrived at the same conclusion you arrived at, which is: he will not save me. He cannot. The arithmetic forbids it.

The arithmetic.

Maret would have hated that word. Maret, who insisted that every evacuation plan include a line for *the ones we cannot move,* not because the line would save them but because acknowledging them was the minimum debt the living owed the doomed. Maret, who said: *The day we stop counting the ones we lose is the day we become what we're fighting.* Maret, whom you stopped counting when his body entered the lime and the lime did what lime does and there was no line for Maret in Maret's own ledger because Maret was dead when they found his journal and the journal was cipher and the cipher died with him and—

*Clack clack CLACK.*

The street is the Rue Saint-Honoré because the street is always the Rue Saint-Honoré, the Republic has a talent for scenic routes, the condemned must pass through the streets that made the Revolution so the streets can see what the Revolution makes of those who— but this is Hébert's rhetoric, or Desmoulins's, or Saint-Just's, the words are the same words rearranged, every faction shouts *liberty* the way a parrot shouts *liberty,* the sound is the same and the meaning is whatever meaning the throat requires—

Three agents. Focus. Clary in the Faubourg Saint-Antoine has the documents for the Rolland family, eight people, three of them children, the youngest not yet walking. Guillaume has the grain-cart route that bypasses the barrier at Charenton. Suzette has the Calais connection and past Calais the sea and past the sea a country that is not yet eating itself. If you save Marguerite Bourdier — if you arrange a diversion, a bribed guard, one of the old tricks Maret designed — the investigation into the escape will widen. Fouquier-Tinville will order a full audit. Your report, which you planned to write as a neatly cauterized narrative (*six conspirators, isolated cell, no further connections*), becomes instead an open wound that every investigator in the Tribunal will probe. Clary is exposed within days. Guillaume within the week. The Rolland children do not reach Calais.

The math says no.

The math has always said no. The math said no when Vignon was arrested and you let Vignon go to the blade because Vignon knew about the Rue Mouffetard safe house and the safe house was sheltering— the math said no when the Pryce brothers were taken at the barrier and you signed the transfer order yourself, your own handwriting on the paper that sent them to the Tribunal, because the Pryce brothers were known to the guard at Charenton and if the guard at Charenton was questioned— the math says no. The math always says no. The math is a machine that runs on people and produces *the network survives* as its output and the network is an abstraction that you have permitted to become more real than the woman standing six feet from you who is not looking at you because she knows you have done the math.

She pressed a cup of chicory into your hands in November. Real chicory. She had traded God knows what for it. You drank it in her kitchen while she corrected your forgeries and the steam rose between your faces and she said, *My husband believed the Revolution would be the last thing France needed to believe in,* and she was not crying but her voice had the particular flatness of a person who had decided that crying was a resource she could not afford, and you said nothing, because what do you say, because what could you — *the Revolution is not a monolith, Citizeness, there are those of us who—* those of us who what? Those of us who resist from within? Those of us who hold titles in the apparatus and use the apparatus's own machinery to save a fraction of the people the apparatus would otherwise consume? Those of us who, in the final accounting, have signed more death warrants than we have forged *laissez-passers?*

You said nothing. You drank the chicory. You left. You carried three families across the barrier at Saint-Denis on paper that did not bleed in the rain.

One of those families was later arrested in Rouen.

Maret told you. Then Maret told you not to calculate the ratio. Then Maret died and left you with nothing but the ratio.

*Clack clack CLACK.*

The Rue Saint-Honoré is waking. A woman opens a shutter and sees the tumbril and closes the shutter. It takes her less than a second. The economy of that gesture — the minimal expenditure of attention required to register *ah, another load* and return to whatever morning task the shutter interrupted — this is what the Terror has accomplished. Not fear. Not ideology. *Habituation.* The carts are furniture. The blade is weather. A woman opens a shutter and closes it and the six human beings in the cart below her window are less interesting to her than whatever is on her stove and you cannot blame her because *you are walking beside the cart* and you are less interested in the six human beings than you are in the three human beings who are not here, the three who survive if these six do not, and this is the same habituation wearing a different justification the way the sections wear different cockades and call themselves different names and—

There. That thought. The one you are not going to follow.

The one about the cockades.

Père Relevigny is praying. His lips are moving and no sound is emerging and the guards have not noticed or do not care. What does he pray for? Forgiveness? Deliverance? Does he pray for you? He knows what you are. He heard your confession — your real one, the one you made in his room above the tanner's not because you believed in God but because you needed one human being to know, to *hold* the knowledge of what you were doing and what it cost, and Relevigny listened with the particular patience of a man who believed that listening was itself an act of grace and then he said, *My son, the question is not whether God forgives you. The question is whether you will, when the time comes, be able to distinguish between what you have done for others and what you have done to yourself,* and you thought at the time that this was the kind of beautiful imprecision that priests specialize in and now, walking beside his moving lips on the Rue Saint-Honoré with the wheel going *clack clack CLACK,* you think perhaps he was being more precise than you understood.

What have you done for others.

Seventeen families extracted through the Calais route. Six through Charenton. The Rolland family pending. Four individuals sheltered at Rue Mouffetard for a total of eleven weeks across nine months. Documents forged: you stopped counting at two hundred because Maret said counting was vanity and then Maret died and the count stopped mattering.

What have you done to yourself.

Twelve death warrants signed. Four interrogation reports written. One cell inspection personally conducted during which you looked Claude Herault — your own man, now standing in this cart — in the face and said *The Republic demands your cooperation* and he gave you nothing because he was protecting you and you gave him nothing because you were — the arithmetic — because the guards were watching and the performance required—

*The performance.*

You have been performing for three years. The face in the Committee halls. The face at the tribunals. The face that reported to Maret in the dark and became, for twenty minutes at a time in safe houses that smelled of damp plaster and fear, something like a human face, the face of a man who was doing a terrible thing for a reason and the reason was—

What was the reason?

There was a meeting. Early. Before the blood. A room above a bookshop on the Left Bank and eight people talking about the Revolution as if it were a living thing that could be *guided,* as if the forces that had stormed the Bastille and dethroned a king could be persuaded to stop at the right moment, to build rather than destroy, to make a republic that did not require a blade to sustain itself. Maret was there. Marguerite's husband was there. You were there. The air smelled of pipe smoke and possibility and someone — you cannot remember who — said *We will need people inside the apparatus, when the time comes, to ensure the transition—*

The transition. To what? The moderate republic that Brissot envisioned before Brissot's head was held up to a crowd that cheered? The constitutional order that Danton demanded before Danton's tongue was cut out by the men he'd raised to power? Every destination the Revolution promised has been consumed by the machinery of arrival. The transition you were recruited to facilitate has transitioned, and transitioned, and transitioned, and each transition has required a new set of names on a new list and a new reliable man to walk the condemned to a new blade and—

Stop.

Marguerite Bourdier's hands are at her sides. Her fingers are not moving. A printer's hands at rest. When she sorted documents her fingers moved with the precision of a compositor setting type, each sheet lifted and placed and aligned with a speed that contained decades of practice, her husband's practice become hers, the craft outliving the craftsman the way the Revolution was supposed to outlive
