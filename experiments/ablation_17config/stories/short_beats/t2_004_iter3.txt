# The Hateful Immortals

### A Documentary Account by Dr. Amelia Thornton, PhD
### Department of Anomalous History, Columbia University

## Part One: The Vow That Doesn't Know What It Means

---

The daguerreotype is slightly overexposed, a problem common to plates processed in the smaller Boston studios of the late 1840s. The chemical haze lends the image an unearned softness, a glow that later commentators have been unable to resist interpreting as symbolic. It shows two people standing before a minister whose name survives only in a church ledger: Reverend Horace P. Whitfield, Second Congregationalist Church of Boston, who officiated eleven marriages in the autumn of 1847 and who, by all available evidence, found nothing remarkable about this one.

The bride is nineteen. She is small and sharp-featured, with dark hair parted strictly in the center, and she is not smiling, though this is not unusual for the medium — exposure times still demanded several seconds of absolute stillness, and the expression most sitters adopted was not severity but simply the face a person makes when told not to move. Her name is Margaret Greenbriar. She will not age past this face for the next 177 years.

The groom is twenty-two. He is tall, broad through the shoulders in the way of men who have done physical labor since adolescence, and he is half-Chinese in an era when this fact determines the geography of his daily life — which streets feel safe, which lecture halls will seat him, which landlords will rent to him and his new wife. His name is James Chen. Four years before this photograph was taken, he was buried under nine tons of collapsed factory masonry for fourteen hours and emerged, according to the *Boston Evening Transcript*'s account, "without so much as a bruise or a shortness of wind, though his coat was quite ruined." He has not been seriously injured before or since. He will not be.

They met on the evening of March 14, 1847, at a public lecture on phrenology delivered by a Dr. Allston Pierce at Tremont Temple. The event is documented in Margaret's diary, which is the first of approximately ninety-seven volumes held in the Greenbriar-Chen Collection at the Smithsonian's sealed archive. The entry, in her small, startlingly legible hand, reads:

> *March 14th. Attended Dr. Pierce's lecture on the cranial sciences with Cousin Harriet. The hall was cold and the doctor was a bore. However, I was introduced afterward to a Mr. James Chen, who works at the Lowell mills and who survived the Pemberton collapse last year — the very incident I read about in the Transcript. He is tall and has a serious manner of speaking but laughed when I told him that Dr. Pierce's own skull, by his own system, suggested a deficiency of intellect. He asked if he might call on me. I said that he might. He was carrying a green umbrella, which seemed optimistic given the weather, which was overcast and 54 degrees.*

This is the entry that launched a thousand dissertations. Not because of its content, which is ordinary and charming, but because of what it reveals about its author's mind. She records the temperature. She records the umbrella. She records the exact phrasing of her own joke and his reaction. She was nineteen and had been doing this since she was thirteen — recording everything, not because she needed the written reminder but because the written reminder was the only way she had found to make other people believe her when she told them what she remembered. Her memory had first manifested as a parlor trick. At a family gathering in 1841, young Margaret had recited, word for word, a sermon she had heard once, six months prior. Then a second sermon. Then a legal document her father had read aloud at dinner. By the time she was fifteen, she could recall every conversation she had ever had, every page she had ever read, every face, every meal, every weather pattern, every cruelty and every kindness, with a fidelity that left no room for softening, revision, or mercy.

It was, in 1847, considered a delightful gift. "Miss Greenbriar's Remarkable Memory" had been the subject of a brief notice in the *Salem Register* the year before, written in the tone reserved for eccentric talents — sword swallowers, calculating prodigies, dogs who could count. Her family regarded it as useful. She could recall recipes without consulting a book. She could reproduce the exact wording of a disputed contract. She could settle arguments about who had said what. She was, in the language of the era, "a living ledger."

No one, including Margaret, appears to have considered what it would mean to possess such a memory for an entire lifetime. But this is not a failure of imagination particular to Margaret or her circle. It is the universal failure of all people at the beginning of anything — the inability to project a present condition across the full arc of its consequences. In 1847, Margaret Greenbriar had been alive for nineteen years. Her memory held nineteen years of material. This was already unusual, already isolating in ways her diary occasionally hints at ("I find that others are always revising what has happened until the past they carry bears no resemblance to the past I witnessed, and I do not know whose version is the more bearable"), but it was manageable. She could not yet conceive of herself at forty, at eighty, at a hundred and ninety-six, carrying every unrevised moment like a stone in an ever-expanding sack.

James Chen's gift was, if anything, even more easily domesticated by the assumptions of his era. He could not be killed. He could not, apparently, be seriously injured. He had survived the Pemberton Mill collapse — an industrial disaster that killed and maimed over a hundred workers — by simply existing under the rubble until rescuers removed it, his body having declined to be crushed. Before that, according to a letter from his older brother William (held in the Chen family collection at the Bancroft Library), James had come through childhood scarlet fever, a fall from a second-story window at age nine, and a horse kick to the chest at twelve with identical results: nothing. William Chen writes to a cousin in Canton in 1845:

> *James is either very lucky or very strange. Mother says God protects him. Father says he is too stubborn to die. I think it is something else but I do not know the word for it in English or in Chinese.*

There was no word for it. Not in 1845, not precisely. The concept of "anomalous persistence" — the term this archive uses — would not be codified for another century. In the 1840s, James was simply understood to be extraordinarily hardy, a quality admired in young men and especially useful in one who worked in the kind of industrial settings that regularly killed people who were not James Chen. His unkillability was an asset, like strength or good eyesight. It made him employable. It made him, in the logic of the era, a good prospect.

The courtship lasted four months. It is well-documented, primarily through Margaret's diary, which records every meeting, every conversation, every fluctuation of feeling with the granularity of a seismograph. The entries are, by any honest assessment, beautiful. They have the specificity that only perfect memory can produce, but they also have something that perfect memory cannot explain — a warmth, a quality of attention that is not merely optical but emotional. She notices him. Not just the facts of him (height: six feet and one quarter inch; habitual coat: dark wool, patched at the left elbow; typical greeting: a slight bow, then a smile that began in the left corner of his mouth before reaching the right) but the *experience* of him. An entry from April 1847:

> *He told me today about the collapse. Not the public version — the miracle, the survival, the reporters — but what it was actually like to lie under the rubble for fourteen hours knowing he would not die but not knowing if anyone would come. He said the worst part was not the weight or the darkness but the sound of other people dying nearby and being unable to reach them. He wept when he told me this. I do not think I have seen a man weep before, or if I have, I have not before now understood that this can be a form of courage rather than its absence. I wanted to put my hand on his but I did not. I am recording this so that I will be able to read it later and remind myself to be braver.*

The entry is frequently cited. It appears in Hwang's *Anomalous Love: Persistent Persons and the Problem of Attachment* (2019) and in the less scholarly but more widely read *Forever and Ever and Ever: The Greenbriar-Chen Marriage* by journalist Paul Cotter (2022). What is less frequently noted is its final line. "I am recording this so that I will be able to read it later and remind myself to be braver." She did not need to record it. She would remember every word James said, the timbre of his voice, the precise pattern of his tears. She was writing not for her memory but for her *courage* — leaving herself a note of permission, a bread crumb back to the woman who wanted to reach for his hand. Margaret's journals, across their first decade, are full of these — not records but exhortations. She is coaching herself into love. Not because the feeling is insufficient but because something in her temperament, or perhaps in the nature of a mind that forgets nothing, makes her cautious about surrendering to anything she cannot un-experience.

She surrendered anyway.

James's letters from this period survive in smaller numbers — he was not a compulsive documenter, and he wrote primarily to his brother William, whose descendants donated seventeen letters to the Bancroft Library in 1988. They are less detailed than Margaret's diary but no less sincere. A letter from May 1847:

> *Dear William — I believe I am going to marry Miss Greenbriar. I know what you will say. You will say I have known her for six weeks and that I cannot possibly know my own mind. But I tell you something, brother: she remembers everything. Everything I say to her, she keeps. I have never before had the feeling that my words mattered to someone in this way — that nothing is wasted or lost. I said something foolish to her on Tuesday about the color of the sky and she repeated it back to me today, word for word, with a look that made me think she had been turning it over and over in her mind like a stone in a stream. I have found a woman who will never forget how much I love her. Is this not the great wish of every man who has ever loved — to be remembered exactly?*

The dramatic irony is, of course, suffocating. Reading this letter in 2024 — knowing everything that follows — it takes on the quality of a document written in a language the author does not fully speak. James is right. Margaret will never forget how much he loves her. She will also never forget the first time he raises his voice to her — the date, the provocation, the exact volume and register of his tone, the way his mouth tightened on a particular syllable, the temperature and quality of the light in the room — and she will carry this memory, undiminished and undistorted, alongside every other memory, while James himself will have forgotten the incident by the following spring.

But that is later. In 1847, it is simply a love letter, and it is simply true.

They married on October 3rd. The ceremony, as noted, was performed by Reverend Whitfield. The daguerreotype was taken afterward by a studio on Washington Street; the receipt, in the amount of seventy-five cents, survives in Margaret's papers. Six guests attended, including William Chen, whose brief account to his wife confirms the emotional valence: "James looked happier than I have ever seen him. His bride has a sharp way about her that suits him. I believe this may be a genuine match."

The vows were standard for the era and the denomination. They included the phrase "till death do us part."

It is not necessary to dwell on this phrase. The reader, having been presented with the facts — a groom who cannot die, a bride who cannot forget — is capable of performing the arithmetic unaided.

Their first years in Boston occupy approximately three volumes of Margaret's diary and are documented in a handful of James's letters, a landlord's logbook from their apartment on Beacon Hill (they rented two rooms above a chandler's shop for six dollars per month), and scattered municipal records. By every available account, these years were happy. Not uniformly so — Margaret records the ordinary frictions of early marriage (disputes about household expenditure, a period of tension when James worked consecutive double shifts at a new mill, a sharp entry about his habit of leaving boot-mud on the staircase "as if the laws of cleanliness were a personal insult to his autonomy") — but fundamentally, structurally happy, in the way that the early years of a good marriage are happy: the discovery of another person's interior life as a place one can live.

James, for his part, wrote to William in 1849:

> *Married life agrees with me. Margaret has organized our accounts so thoroughly that I believe we could survive a siege. She is teaching herself French from a grammar she memorized in a single reading. She remembers every kindness and acknowledges them — she told me last Thursday that the way I mended her shawl two months ago "still makes her feel cared for," which is a strange gift, William, to be reminded of your own best self by someone who will never let it fade. I am trying to be worthy of being remembered so precisely.*

"I am trying to be worthy of being remembered so precisely." This sentence has haunted scholars for decades. Hwang reads it as an early expression of the anxiety that would later dominate the marriage — the pressure of performing for an audience who never stops recording. Cotter, more generously, reads it as aspiration: James understood that Margaret's memory conferred a kind of immortality on his daily actions, and he wanted that record to reflect his best intentions. Both readings are probably correct. They are certainly not mutually exclusive.

The first years produced other documents: receipts for books (Margaret consumed them voraciously, though she needed to read them only once), a membership card for a lending library, a brief notice in a neighborhood circular praising "Mrs. Chen's remarkable assistance in settling a disputed property boundary, she being able to recite from memory the exact terms of the original deed." They attended lectures. They hosted William and his wife for Sunday dinners. Margaret began and abandoned a novel. James experimented with carpentry and proved, according to Margaret's diary, "enthusiastic but not talented — the bookshelf he has constructed leans eleven degrees to the east, which he insists is intentional."

They were, in other words, a young married couple. The fact that one of them would never forget a moment of their life together and the other would never die was, in the context of 1840s daily existence, simply a background condition, like being tall or having a talent for arithmetic. Their anomalies had not yet had time to reveal themselves as what they actually were. A perfect memory across five years of marriage is a love poem. A perfect memory across fifty is something else. An unkillable husband at twenty-five is a comfort. At a hundred and twenty-five, he is a cellmate.

But this is the historian's curse: the tyranny of knowing what comes next. In 1847, in 1849, in 1852, Margaret and James Chen were simply in love. The documentary record — voluminous, precise, alive with small joys — supports no other conclusion.

The first crack appears in 1854, and it appears, as cracks often do, not in the private archive but in the public one — in the observations of people who had no reason to suspect what they were observing.

A letter from one Mrs. Eliza Hartwell to her sister in Philadelphia, dated August 12, 1854, reads:

> *You asked after the neighborhood. All is much the same except that old Mr. Briggs has died and the Fultons' youngest has the croup. Oh — and a curious thing about the Chens two doors down, whom I have mentioned before. They have lived here now seven years, and Katherine remarked to me last week that neither Mr. nor Mrs. Chen appears to have aged in the slightest since their arrival. I had not noticed it myself until she pointed it out, but now I cannot unnotice it — Mrs. Chen looks precisely as she did the year they moved in, not a line, not a gray hair, not the smallest change. Mr. Chen the same. Katherine says it is the Oriental constitution, which I think a stupid and probably wrong explanation, and anyway Mrs. Chen is not Oriental though Mr. Chen is. It has occasioned some comment. I imagine there is a boring explanation but I confess I do not have one.*

There is no record of what Margaret and James thought during this period, whether they had discussed the problem between themselves, whether they had noticed it before the neighbors did.
