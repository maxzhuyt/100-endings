# The Thirty-Seven Letters of Monk Hideo, As Compiled by His Opponent

---

## Editor's Preface

I have permitted myself to be called many things over the years. Master. Teacher. Champion of the Seventh Invitational. The Man Who Won by Forfeit. This last one is not a title anyone seeks.

The facts of the matter are well established and do not require my embellishment. On the fourteenth day of October, 1983, in the final round of the All-Japan Classical Martial Arts Invitational — the tournament that was then, and remains, the most prestigious gathering of traditional swordsmanship practitioners in the country — Hideo Takamura, holder of an unbroken competitive record spanning eleven years and forty-three bouts, stepped onto the floor for the championship match against me. The judges were seated. The hall was full. Both of us had bowed.

Fourteen seconds into the engagement, Hideo lowered his shinai, stepped back, and announced his forfeit to the head judge. He left the hall without speaking to anyone. Within three weeks, he had entered the monastic community at Eiheiji. Within a year, he had transferred to a smaller, more remote temple in the mountains of Niigata, where he remained for the rest of his life.

He never competed again. He never taught. He never, to my knowledge, held a sword of any kind after that day.

The speculation that followed was immediate and has endured for four decades. Injury. Illness. A spiritual crisis timed, with cruel precision, to the most public moment in our art. A calculated insult to the tournament's organizers, to me, to competitive swordsmanship itself. A protest whose object no one could identify. Every school has its preferred theory. None has produced evidence. Hideo himself never issued a public statement. He granted no interviews. The few journalists who made the journey to Niigata were turned away at the temple gate.

What was not known — until now — is that Hideo wrote to me.

The first letter arrived on the ninth of November, 1983, twenty-six days after the forfeit. The last arrived on the fourth of March, 2024, which was eleven days ago. Between these dates, I received thirty-seven letters in total. They are reproduced here in their entirety, in their original order of receipt, with annotations I have supplied for context and, occasionally, for clarification of technical terms that may be unfamiliar to readers outside our discipline.

I am publishing this collection because the historical record of classical swordsmanship in this country is incomplete without it, and because the community of practitioners who have debated the meaning of the forfeit for forty years deserves access to the primary evidence. I have instructed my son to deliver the manuscript to the publisher upon my death, which my physicians have indicated will occur within the season.

I should note one additional fact, as its omission would constitute a dishonesty I am not willing to carry into whatever comes next.

In forty years, across thirty-seven letters, I never wrote back.

I have my reasons. They are not among the things I intend to explain.

— K.
March 2024

## Letter One
### *Received 9 November 1983*

Esteemed Sir,

I will not insult you by writing as though we are friends. We are not friends. We have met only in the context of competition, and our exchanges, such as they were, took place in the formal language of the match — the bow, the stance, the crossing of swords. I have no claim on your attention and no reasonable expectation that you will read beyond this sentence.

I write because I find that I cannot stop composing letters to you in my mind, and the monks here tell me that a thought which circles without release becomes a sickness. So I release it here. I expect nothing in return.

The temple is cold. I arrived in October, and the older monks tell me that what I am experiencing is not yet cold — that I have not yet learned what the word means, and the mountains will instruct me through the winter. They say this with a gentleness that I recognize from the dojo: it is the gentleness of someone who knows what is coming and has decided that warning would be a form of cruelty.

I am learning to make ink. The proportions of water and soot must be exact, and the grinding must have a particular rhythm, and the stone must be of the correct grain. I am not good at it yet. The older monks grind ink the way the great swordsmen move — without visible effort, the technique so deeply embedded that it has ceased to be technique and become simply the body's knowledge of itself. I grind ink the way a beginner holds a sword: with too much force, too much attention, too much desire for the outcome to be correct.

I think you would find this amusing. I think you would see me crouched in the calligraphy hall, hunched over the stone, producing ink that is either too thin or too thick, and you would recognize it: a man who has mastered one discipline discovering that mastery does not transfer. The body must begin again. The body always begins again.

I do not know yet what I am writing to you about. Perhaps future letters will clarify this, or perhaps they will not. For now, accept only this: I am here. I am making ink. The mountains are not yet cold.

With respect,
Hideo

> **Editor's note:** *This letter arrived without a return address, though the postmark identified the region. The stationery is plain temple-issue paper, and the ink is indeed uneven — thicker in the opening lines, thinner toward the end, as though the mixture degraded during the writing. I note this for completeness, not for symbolism. The handwriting is unmistakably Hideo's. I had seen it only once before, on the registration form for the Invitational, which all competitors completed by hand. His penmanship at that time was economical and unremarkable. Here it is more careful, slower, as though each character is being considered individually. Whether this reflects a new discipline or a new uncertainty, I cannot say.*

## Letter Four
### *Received 22 April 1984*

I have sent three letters now and received no reply. I note this not as a reproach but as an observation: silence is also a stance.

Spring arrived in the mountains three weeks later than in the lowlands. The monks measure the season by the sound of meltwater, which begins as a trickle audible only at night and grows, over the course of weeks, into a roar that makes the wooden buildings hum. I am told that experienced monks can determine the date within a few days based solely on the pitch of the runoff. I cannot do this. But I have begun to hear what they hear, which is itself a kind of progress.

I have been given permission to assist in the vegetable garden. The abbot assigned me this duty with the explanation that a man who works with his hands in soil will eventually stop clenching his jaw. He did not explain how he knew about my jaw. Perhaps it is visible. Perhaps all men who arrive here from the world carry the same tension in the same places.

The garden is orderly. The rows are straight. The work is repetitive and requires a quality of attention that is familiar to me — not the explosive focus of the match but the sustained, low-grade awareness of practice, the ten-thousandth repetition of a form, where the mind must be simultaneously engaged and empty. I find this comforting and suspect I should be wary of comfort. The abbot would agree. He has told me several times that a monk who is comfortable is a monk who has stopped moving.

I have been thinking about the nature of repetition. In the dojo, we were taught that repetition is the mechanism by which technique is inscribed on the body. Ten thousand cuts to make one cut. But there is a secondary function of repetition that I did not understand until I came here, which is this: repetition is also the mechanism by which the mind is gradually worn smooth, the way water shapes stone. The stone does not choose its shape. The water does not intend a shape. The shape emerges from the sustained application of force over time, and neither the force nor the object has any say in the result. I suspect that is what the monks are doing when they sit in meditation for hours — they are being water, and the stone is whatever they carried with them when they arrived.

I do not know yet what shape is being worn into me. I know only that something is being worn.

With respect,
Hideo

> **Editor's note:** *Letters two and three, omitted here, are brief. The second describes the temple's daily schedule. The third describes the process of receiving a tonsure and his surprise at the weight of his own hair when it was collected for disposal. Neither approaches the subject that I believe these letters, taken as a whole, attempt to address. I include them in the complete manuscript appended to this volume but do not reproduce them in the main text. I have taken this editorial liberty because thirty-seven letters, presented without curation, would try the patience of even the most dedicated reader, and because certain letters serve primarily as connective tissue — they maintain the habit of correspondence without advancing its purpose. I am aware that this admission — that I believe the letters have a purpose — compromises my claim to editorial neutrality. I make it nonetheless. I am not interested, at this stage, in pretending I do not know what Hideo was trying to tell me.*

## Letter Seven
### *Received 3 January 1985*

The new year. The monks performed a ceremony at midnight that involved the striking of a bell one hundred and eight times, once for each of the worldly desires that Buddhist teaching identifies as the sources of suffering. I stood in the freezing courtyard and counted the strikes and tried to feel the corresponding desires dissolving, as the tradition promises. I felt cold. I felt the vibration of the bell in the bones of my chest. I did not feel one hundred and eight desires dissolving, but I did feel something — a loosening, perhaps, the way a joint loosens after injury when it is finally moved through its full range.

One hundred and eight desires. I wonder which number corresponds to the desire to be understood by a specific person. I wonder which corresponds to the desire to have made a different choice. I wonder whether these are, in fact, the same desire wearing different masks.

You will notice that I am approaching something. I notice it too. I have noticed it for several letters now. I begin with weather, or with temple life, or with some observation about the monks, and then the writing tilts, as though the page has an incline I cannot see, and I find myself sliding toward the thing I sat down to say. And then I stop. And then I write about something else.

I am told that this is a common pattern among new monks. The abbot calls it "circling the cushion" — the novice who walks around the meditation hall rather than sitting down, who arranges his robes, who adjusts his posture, who finds one more task to complete before facing the silence. Eventually, the abbot says, the circling itself becomes exhausting, and the monk sits not because he has found courage but because he has run out of alternatives.

I have not run out of alternatives yet. The mountains are vast, and the vocabulary for weather is inexhaustible, and I could fill a hundred letters with the quality of winter light on the temple roof without ever sitting down on the cushion that waits for me at the center of every sentence I write to you.

But I will tell you this: the circling is not random. It has a shape. And if you are patient — if you are reading these at all, which I do not assume — you may begin to see the shape, and the shape will tell you what the center is, even if I never arrive there.

With respect,
Hideo

> **Editor's note:** *I read this letter in my office at the dojo on an evening in early January. I had taught three classes that day. My right knee, which had troubled me since the semifinal at the 1979 Invitational, was aching in the cold. I remember these details because I remember placing the letter on my desk after reading it and sitting for a long time without moving, which is unusual for me — I am not a man given to stillness. I was aware that Hideo had just described, with considerable precision, the experience of reading his letters: the sense of something approaching, the sense of something withheld, the tilt of each page toward a gravity it refused to name. He had described my experience of his correspondence to me within the correspondence itself, and I could not determine whether this was manipulation, confession, or simply the inevitable result of a perceptive man observing his own patterns. I mention this not to interpret the letter but to establish that by the seventh letter, I had become aware that the correspondence was not incidental. He was building something. I did not yet know what.*

## Letter Eleven
### *Received 8 September 1986*

A visiting monk lectured today on the concept of *ahimsa* — non-harm — and its relationship to the Buddhist precept against taking life. The lecture was elementary, intended for novices, and covered ground that any practitioner of our art encounters in his first year of serious training: the paradox of disciplines designed to destroy the human body being practiced by men who profess an ethics of restraint. The visiting monk resolved the paradox in the usual way — the sword, he said, is a metaphor; the cut is a metaphor; the opponent is a metaphor; what we are truly cutting is the ego, and what we are truly defeating is the self. This is the standard formulation. You will have heard it. I have heard it a thousand times.

I have always found it insufficient.

Let me be precise. I do not mean that it is wrong. I mean that it is incomplete in a way that permits a comfortable dishonesty. The sword is not *only* a metaphor. The cut is not *only* a metaphor. The opponent is not *only* a metaphor. When I stood across from you — or from any man — in competition, the controlled, ritualized, regulated engagement we performed was built on a foundation of real technique, technique designed for the destruction of a human being, technique whose referent is not spiritual growth but death. We have chosen to formalize and constrain this technique. We have chosen to blunt the swords and pad the bodies and appoint judges who will intervene. But the knowledge underneath is the knowledge of how to end a life, and to pretend otherwise — to retreat entirely into metaphor — is to deny the gravity of what we carry.

I say this because I want you to understand that when I speak of what happened between us, I am not speaking in metaphors.

I am speaking of a real body. A real flaw. A real strike that was not metaphorical, whose consequence would not have been metaphorical.

The visiting monk left this afternoon. The regular monks served him tea and rice cakes at the gate. He was gracious. I stood at the back and said nothing. I wanted to ask him: when you say the opponent is a metaphor, do you mean that his life is a metaphor? Do you mean that the specific architecture of his body — the way he holds his weight, the way his left shoulder sits a degree lower than his right after an old injury, the way this creates a particular geometry — do you mean that this is metaphor? Because I have seen this architecture. I have read it as fluently as a scholar reads a text. And what I read was not metaphor. It was a sentence, written in the grammar of the body, that said: *here. Here is where it ends.*

I did not ask him this. I ate a rice cake and watched him leave.

With respect,
Hideo

> **Editor's note:** *The reference to a left shoulder sitting lower than the right is not a general observation. I sustained a fracture of the left clavicle in 1974, during a training accident at the Sendai seminar. The bone healed imperfectly. The asymmetry was slight — a matter of millimeters — but it was, I am told by my students, visible to a trained eye. I had not previously considered it a significant element of my competitive profile. My teachers, in reviewing my form, had noted the asymmetry but classified it as cosmetically trivial, biomechanically negligible. Hideo, it appears, classified it differently.*
