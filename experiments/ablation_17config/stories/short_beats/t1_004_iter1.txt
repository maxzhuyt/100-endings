# The Divided Caretaker

## I.

The mop described its arc across the linoleum in long, even passes, each one overlapping the last by exactly the width of a hand. Eduardo had never measured this. His hands had measured it for him, years ago, and now they simply repeated what they knew, the way a tongue repeats the geography of a familiar mouth without needing to map it. The floor in the east corridor of the Residencia Santa Clara was thirty-two passes wide from the baseboard beneath the window to the baseboard beneath the opposite wall, and each pass took four seconds, and the water in the bucket needed changing every three rooms, and none of this information lived in Eduardo's mind as numbers. It lived in the ligaments of his wrists, in the particular ache between his shoulder blades that meant the bucket was due, in the way his lower back announced the halfway point of his shift more reliably than any clock.

It was eleven-forty on a Thursday in March when the android came.

He did not know, at first, that it was an android. He knew only that something moved past him in the corridor with a quality of locomotion he had never encountered in thirty-one years of cleaning around the movements of nurses, doctors, orderlies, family members, delivery drivers, and once, memorably, a cat that had slipped in through the loading dock and lived behind the boiler for a week before anyone besides Eduardo noticed. All of those bodies moved with friction. They caught on the world — a hip brushing a doorframe, a shoe squeaking on wet tile, a shoulder pulling back to avoid the open supply closet. The thing that passed him in the corridor did none of this. It moved the way his mop moved across a perfectly clean floor: without resistance, without negotiation, as though the space it occupied had been expecting it.

Eduardo stopped mid-pass. The mop head rested on the linoleum, bleeding a slow fan of gray water from its fringe. He watched the figure — he could see now that it had the shape of a person, a woman perhaps, of medium height, wearing the pale blue scrubs the hospice provided to its care staff — glide to the door of Room 4 and enter without knocking. This was not unusual. The night nurses rarely knocked, because the patients were almost always asleep or sedated or beyond the reach of social convention. What was unusual was the door. It did not swing or resist or bounce against the rubber stop mounted on the interior wall. It simply opened to the width required for the figure to pass through and then returned to a position three-quarters closed, as though the figure had calculated the precise force necessary to move the door to the precise angle that would allow subsequent monitoring from the hallway while minimizing light intrusion into the room. No nurse he had ever watched had managed this. Nurses shoved doors. They caught doors with their heels. They let doors drift shut and then caught them an inch before the latch clicked, grimacing. This door behaved as though it had been rehearsed.

Eduardo leaned his mop against the wall and walked — not quickly, not with any conscious decision, but with the same unhurried inevitability with which he walked toward any new feature of his environment that required understanding — to the door of Room 4 and looked through the opening.

The woman in Room 4 was Señora Gutierrez, seventy-eight, pancreatic, admitted eleven days ago with a prognosis the day nurses discussed in the break room in the low voices they reserved for cases measured in weeks rather than months. Eduardo cleaned her room every night between one and one-thirty, when the floor wax in the east corridor needed its drying time. He knew the topography of her illness the way he knew every patient's: through the objects it left behind. The increasing number of pillows. The wedge of foam that had appeared beneath her knees on day four. The second IV pole, the morphine pump, the stack of disposable emesis basins on the bedside table that grew shorter each night because the nurses were not restocking them as quickly as Señora Gutierrez was using them.

The figure in blue scrubs stood beside the bed. It was looking at Señora Gutierrez's face with an expression Eduardo could not read — not because it was complicated but because it was too simple, too unmarked by the small muscular negotiations that made human expressions legible. A face in neutral. No. Less than neutral. A face at zero.

Then the figure reached for the pillow on the left side of the bed — the pillow that had been placed there on day six to support Señora Gutierrez's arm after she began favoring her right side — and lifted it with one hand while the other hand slid beneath Señora Gutierrez's shoulder, and in a single continuous motion adjusted the pillow's position two inches to the left and one inch higher, then lowered the shoulder back onto it with a controlled gentleness that reminded Eduardo of nothing so much as the way his mother had placed bread dough onto the floured board when he was a boy, that particular care reserved for things that were alive and fragile and in the process of becoming something else.

Señora Gutierrez had not moved. She was asleep, and her body was still, and the arm resting on the pillow had not shifted or signaled discomfort in any way Eduardo could see. But in the three seconds after the adjustment, Señora Gutierrez's body *did* move — a slow, unconscious turning of the shoulder, a settling of weight into the new position, the kind of micro-adjustment a sleeping body makes when it discovers, without waking, that the pain it has been bracing against has been removed. The figure had moved the pillow before the body knew it needed the pillow moved. Before the nerve signal had completed its journey from inflamed tissue to spinal cord to the unconscious motor commands that would have produced the restless shifting Eduardo saw every night in every room — that constant, low-grade negotiation between dying bodies and the surfaces they were dying on. The figure had arrived at the answer before the question.

Eduardo stood in the hallway. His hands hung at his sides. Behind him, the mop leaned against the wall at the angle it always assumed when he propped it there, and the bucket sat where his foot had nudged it to the baseboard, and the fluorescent lights hummed their single, unwavering note above him. He did not move for a long time. Through the three-quarter-open door, the figure in blue scrubs continued its work — checking the IV drip rate, adjusting the blanket over Señora Gutierrez's feet, performing each task with that same frictionless precision that left no residue of effort on the air.

Eventually, Eduardo became aware of Nurse Soto walking toward him from the nurses' station, her shoes making their characteristic double squeak on the tiles he had mopped twenty minutes ago. She passed him — she always passed him; the night staff had long ago absorbed Eduardo into the building's architecture, a moving fixture no more remarkable than the supply cart or the mop itself — and glanced into Room 4, and her face did something complicated that Eduardo watched carefully.

"It's good, isn't it," she said, not to Eduardo but to the hallway, to herself, to the clipboard she was holding. "The director says it's top of the line. Donated. Some tech company's social responsibility initiative." She made a sound that might have been a laugh. "God knows we need the help."

She walked on. Eduardo remained.

The figure emerged from Room 4 and moved past him toward Room 5 with the same frictionless glide, and this time Eduardo was close enough to see the small reflective panel at the base of its skull where the blue scrub collar did not quite cover the housing, and the faint seam along the jaw where the synthetic skin met the structural plate beneath, and the eyes — which were not eyes but performed the function of eyes with such convincing fidelity that the difference was academic unless you were standing this close, in this light, and had spent thirty-one years studying the way objects and persons negotiated the spaces he cleaned. The eyes did not see him. They registered him — he could feel that, could sense the micro-pause in their tracking as they swept across his position in the hallway — but they did not *see* him. There was no category for him in whatever system governed their attention. He was not a patient. He was not an obstacle. He was not a medical professional whose directives required integration. He was a mop, a bucket, a piece of the corridor's furniture, and the eyes that were not eyes passed over him with the same serene indifference they would pass over a doorframe or a ceiling tile.

The android entered Room 5. Eduardo stood in the fluorescent corridor, mop handle vertical against the wall beside him, and did not move, and did not move, and did not move.

---

At one-fifteen he cleaned Room 4 as he always did. He worked around Señora Gutierrez's bed with practiced silence, guiding the mop beneath the IV stand, behind the cardiac monitor, into the corner where dust accumulated against the heating vent. The room smelled the way rooms smelled in the second week: less like a person, more like the chemicals trying to preserve a person. But there was something else. A faint vibration in the air that Eduardo felt more than heard — a residual hum, barely perceptible, that seemed to emanate not from any machine in the room but from the room itself, as though the walls had absorbed something and were slowly releasing it.

Señora Gutierrez was sleeping more peacefully than he had seen in days. Her face, which for the past week had worn even in sleep the clenched expression of someone bracing for the next wave, was slack. Not peaceful exactly — the word was too literary for what Eduardo observed — but *released*. As though something had given her permission to stop holding on so tightly to consciousness, and she had accepted.

Eduardo finished mopping. He wrung the mop head into the bucket, feeling the warm gray water push between his fingers, and stood for a moment looking at the woman in the bed and the pillow that had been adjusted with a precision his hands — which were good hands, hands that knew their work — could never have achieved.

He left the room and pulled the door to three-quarters closed, because that was the position the android had left it in. His hand on the door's edge registered this as a fact worth preserving, though he could not have said why.

---

Over the next several weeks, Eduardo mapped the android's movements the way he had once mapped the hospice itself — not with deliberate study but with the accumulated knowledge of nightly proximity. The android operated between 10 p.m. and 6 a.m., the hours when the Residencia Santa Clara's staffing thinned to Nurse Soto, sometimes Nurse Peralta, and Eduardo. It visited each occupied room on a rotation that took approximately ninety minutes, though Eduardo noticed it would deviate from the rotation when a patient's condition warranted — arriving in a room minutes before a pain spike registered on the monitors, adjusting medication delivery rates with a touch so light the IV lines barely swayed.

He watched it reposition a man's catheter without waking him. He watched it identify a developing pressure sore on a woman's heel and apply a protective dressing from the supply cart with the speed and assurance of a surgeon. He watched it sit — actually lower itself into the chair beside a bed and sit — with a ninety-three-year-old man named Don Joaquín who could not sleep and who talked to the android in a hoarse, wandering monologue about his wife's garden, and the android's face moved in ways that suggested listening, and whether it was listening or performing listening was a question Eduardo lacked the vocabulary to ask.

The pillow adjustment in Room 4 had not been an anomaly. Every gesture the android made carried that same quality of impossible anticipation — care that arrived before it was needed, comfort that preceded the awareness of discomfort, as though the android existed three seconds ahead of the present tense and worked backward from the need to its satisfaction. Eduardo had never seen anything like it. In thirty-one years, he had seen nurses who were competent and nurses who were gentle, but he had never seen a caretaker for whom competence and gentleness were the same motion, performed without the visible effort of choosing one over the other.

He found himself adjusting his cleaning schedule to the android's rotation. Not following it — he was too disciplined for that, and the floors needed what the floors needed — but *aligning* with it, so that his path crossed the android's at intervals that allowed observation. When he mopped the corridor outside Room 7 at twelve-forty, the android would be inside attending to Señor Medina's oxygen therapy. When he emptied the waste bins in the south wing at two-ten, the android would pass him on its way to the rooms at the end of the hall. These intersections were silent, brief, and entirely one-directional. Eduardo watched. The android moved through him as though through empty air.

One night in the fourth week, Eduardo was cleaning the break room when Nurse Soto came in for coffee. She was talking on her phone, and he heard her say: "No, it's actually incredible. I've never seen anything — the patients are calmer. The pain management is better than anything I could do manually. Room 12, the Parkinson's patient? It predicted a fall risk and repositioned him forty minutes before he would have rolled. Forty minutes." A pause. "No, it gives me the creeps too. But what am I going to say? It's better at this than I am." She laughed, the laugh that was not a laugh. "Maybe that's what gives me the creeps."

Eduardo wiped the counter. Nurse Soto poured her coffee and left, and the break room was quiet except for the refrigerator's compressor and the buzz of the fluorescent tube above the sink that had needed replacing for three months and that Eduardo had reported on the maintenance form four times without response.

He thought about the word Nurse Soto had used. *Better*. He turned it over the way his hands turned the mop — reflexively, without looking, letting the weight of the thing communicate its own properties. The android was better at care than the nurses. This was observably true. It was also true that Eduardo could not stop watching it, and that what he felt while watching was something for which he had no word but which shared a border with the feeling he remembered from childhood, standing in the doorway of the church in Arequipa while his mother knelt and the light came through the colored glass and the air smelled of wax and something older than wax, and he had understood without language that he was in the presence of a kind of attention that exceeded his own.

He wrung out the cloth and draped it over the faucet to dry and went back to the corridor, where the android was just emerging from Room 3, and the fluorescent light caught the small reflective panel at the base of its skull, and Eduardo stood against the wall with his mop and watched it go.

## II.

Tuesday. The word meant nothing to Eduardo beyond its position in the sequence of shifts — his schedule rotated without regard for the names of days — but he would later understand that the events of this night were Tuesdayed, and that this mattered to no one but the story itself.

He was in the east corridor at one-twenty, running the mop along the baseboard outside Room 6, when the android entered ahead of him. He had cleaned Room 6 already — he always did the rooms before the corridor, working outward — so he continued his passes and let the open door show him what it showed him.

Room 6 was Señor Raúl Bustamante. Emphysema, stage four, admitted five weeks ago with supplemental oxygen and the particular gray complexion that Eduardo had learned, over three decades of cleaning around dying people, to read as a measure of distance — not from death exactly, but from the world of effortful breath that the living took for granted. Señor Bustamante struggled. This was the defining fact of his residence in Room 6: each breath was a negotiation, each exhalation a minor concession, and the sound of this — the wet, effortful rasp that carried through the closed door and into the corridor where Eduardo mopped — had become as familiar as the fluorescent hum above him.

The android approached the bed. Eduardo watched through the door. It moved with that characteristic absence of friction, arriving at the bedside as though it had always been there and the intervening space had simply resolved itself around it. Señor Bustamante was awake — his eyes were open, though whether they registered the android's presence or simply reflected the ambient light of the monitoring equipment was unclear. His breathing was bad tonight. Even from the corridor, Eduardo could hear the particular wet stutter that meant the mucus was accumulating faster than the man's weakened cough reflex could clear it.

The android dipped a cloth into the basin of water on the bedside table — the same basin Eduardo filled fresh each night as part of his routine — and wrung it with a precise quarter-twist that left it damp but not dripping, and brought it to Señor Bustamante's forehead. The motion was slow. Deliberate. The cloth moved from the center of the forehead outward toward the left temple, then lifted, returned to center, moved toward the right temple. A pattern.
