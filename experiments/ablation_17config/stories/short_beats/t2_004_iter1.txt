# The Hateful Immortals

**A Documentary History of the Greenbriar-Chen Marriage, 1847–2024**

*Directed and narrated by Dr. Elena Thornton*
*Adapted from her monograph* The Persistence of Vows: Immortality, Memory, and Domestic Irreconcilability *(Harvard University Press, 2025)*

---

## Part One: The Wedding and the Wager

---

**DR. THORNTON (V.O.):**

The known record begins in Boston, in the autumn of 1847, and it begins — as these things so often do — with a coincidence that would be rejected as implausible in fiction but which the archive stubbornly insists upon.

On September 14th of that year, a seventeen-year-old woman named Margaret Greenbriar walked into the Tremont Street offices of Dr. Josiah Alcott, a phrenologist of middling reputation, and submitted to an examination of her skull. The visit was her mother's idea. Mrs. Greenbriar the elder had become alarmed — not by her daughter's perfect recall of every conversation, every printed page, every overheard sermon and market-stall transaction since the age of thirteen — but by the social consequences of that recall. Margaret had, at a family dinner the previous week, corrected her uncle's account of a business dispute by quoting, verbatim and with attribution, eleven separate remarks he had made over a four-year period, arranged chronologically, each one contradicting the version he had just offered to the table. The uncle had left before dessert. Mrs. Greenbriar made the appointment the following morning.

Dr. Alcott's notes survive.[^1] They are brief. He found the temporal and parietal regions of Miss Greenbriar's cranium to be "remarkably developed, suggestive of an organ of Memory enlarged beyond all precedent in my experience." He recommended cold compresses and a reduction in the reading of novels. Beneath this prescription, in a different ink, he added a marginal annotation that appears to have been written later: "Patient recalled, without error, the entirety of my introductory remarks, including a false start I corrected mid-sentence. She also noted that my diploma, visible on the wall behind me, contained a misspelling of 'Philadelphia.' It does."

[^1]: Alcott, J. *Case Notes, 1845–1851.* Massachusetts Historical Society, Box 14, Folder 7. The phrenological examination cost seventy-five cents. Margaret would remember this amount — and the exact weight of the coins her mother placed on Alcott's desk, and the sound they made, and the quality of the light through his office window, which was west-facing and dirty — for the next 177 years.

Margaret did not have what modern clinicians would later term hyperthymesia, though that diagnosis was applied to her by no fewer than six researchers between 1979 and 2013, all of whom she eventually corrected.[^2] Hyperthymestic individuals possess superior autobiographical memory: they recall the events of their own lives with unusual completeness. Margaret Greenbriar recalled *everything*. Conversations conducted in her presence, whether she was attending to them or not. The precise chromatic value of every sky she'd glanced at. The weave count of fabrics she had touched decades ago. Musical performances reproduced note for note, including the errors. The complete text of every book she had read, every sign she had passed, every letter she had received — stored, indexed, and retrievable in full sensory fidelity, as though the original experience were being replayed rather than remembered.

[^2]: "They kept telling me I had a *condition*," Margaret said in a 2013 email to Dr. Yuki Sato of the University of Tokyo, one of several researchers with whom she maintained irregular correspondence. "As though perfect memory were a pathology and forgetting were the healthy baseline. I told Dr. Liebowitz in 1996 that the word 'hyperthymesia' comes from the Greek for 'excessive remembering,' and I asked him who got to decide what was excessive. He said the DSM. I said the DSM was written by people who couldn't remember what they had for breakfast. He did not find this charming. James would have found it charming. James finds all of my worst qualities charming, which is itself one of my worst qualities — or his. After 166 years I honestly cannot tell."

She was not a savant. She did not perform memory feats for audiences or submit to laboratory testing with any consistency. Her ability was not a parlor trick but a *condition of existence* — the water she swam in, invisible to her until she was old enough to recognize that other people's water was different. To be Margaret Greenbriar was to live without the mechanism that allows the rest of us to experience the present as primary. For Margaret, the present was simply the most recent layer of an infinitely accessible archive. Every moment she had ever lived was *right there*, every sensation at full volume, every slight and kindness preserved in amber, available to be activated by the smallest associative trigger — a smell, a phrase, a quality of afternoon light — and delivered to her consciousness with the same immediacy it had possessed when it first occurred.

She was, by all accounts, lonely.

James Chen was also lonely, though he did not know the reason until somewhat later.

The Chens had come to Boston from Canton in 1831. James's father, Chen Weiming, worked the Long Wharf — unloading cargo, mending nets, performing the thousand small acts of manual labor that Chinese immigrants in antebellum Boston exchanged for wages that were, by design, insufficient to accumulate into anything resembling stability. Weiming spoke functional English, married an Irish immigrant named Bridget Callahan in 1827 — a union so socially improbable that both communities treated it as a kind of mutual excommunication — and produced a son in 1828 whom they named James, after Bridget's father, with the Chinese name Jiaming, which means "excellent and bright," which he was, though the excellence and the brightness had limited outlets available to the mixed-race son of a dockworker in the 1840s.

James discovered his ability at the age of fourteen, when a crate of Sheffield steel fell from a crane arm and struck him directly on the head with sufficient force to kill, by the attending physician's later estimate, "any man alive, and most horses." James was unconscious for eleven minutes. He woke with a headache that lasted three days and a dent in his skull that un-dented itself over the following week, the bone re-knitting visibly beneath the skin, which several of his fellow dockworkers observed and none of them discussed, because the Long Wharf was a place where unusual things were best left unremarked upon if one wished to continue working there.

Over the next five years, James's unkillability was tested — mostly by accident, occasionally by the deliberate cruelty of men who found a half-Chinese dockworker's invulnerability to be a personal affront — with sufficient rigor to establish its parameters. He could not be killed by blunt force, by blade, by drowning, by fire, by any of the several epidemic diseases that swept the waterfront in the 1840s, or by a fall from the roof of a three-story warehouse, which he survived in 1846 with no injuries beyond a ruined pair of trousers and what he later described to Margaret as "a profound sense of embarrassment."[^3]

[^3]: Margaret's recollection of this phrasing, delivered in a 2019 interview, was accompanied by an involuntary smile — one of the few moments in our filmed conversations where her expression softened in a way the camera could register. "He said it exactly like that," she told me. "'A profound sense of embarrassment.' He was dangling his legs off the examining table and his trousers were torn at both knees and he looked — Dr. Thornton, I will tell you exactly what he looked like. He looked like a boy who had been caught doing something foolish and was trying to be dignified about it. He had a scratch on his chin that healed while I watched. That was the first time I saw it happen. I thought: oh. You too. You're impossible too."

He could be harmed. He felt pain. His body sustained injuries in the normal fashion and then repaired them at a rate that varied from minutes to weeks depending on severity. What he could not do was *die*. The mechanism — cellular, metaphysical, or otherwise — has never been identified, though not for lack of scholarly effort.[^4] For the purposes of this documentary, the operative fact is simpler: James Chen, as of the year of this writing, has lived for 196 years, has survived everything the world has devised to kill a human body, and is, by his own description, "very tired, but not in a way that sleeping fixes."

[^4]: See Nakamura, R. and Okafor, D. "Telomeric Integrity in Subjects Exhibiting Anomalous Cellular Regeneration: A Preliminary Framework." *Nature Genetics* 54, no. 3 (2022): 407–421. The paper, which studied both James and three other individuals exhibiting similar regenerative properties, concludes with the memorable admission that "the mechanism resists characterization by any framework available to the authors, which we note with professional humility and personal frustration."

They met on the Long Wharf on a Tuesday in March of 1847. The precise date is known because Margaret remembered it — the eleventh, a cold morning, the sky a specific shade of gray she once described to me as "the color of a headache" — and because the *Boston Daily Advertiser* recorded an altercation on the docks that day involving a warehouse fire. James had walked into the burning structure to retrieve cargo — not out of heroism, he would later insist, but because his foreman had told him to and he hadn't yet learned that his inability to die did not obligate him to demonstrate it on command. Margaret was passing beside the wharf with her mother. She saw a young man walk out of a burning doorway, his shirt on fire, patting at the flames on his sleeve with the irritated nonchalance of someone brushing away a fly.

Their courtship was short and, by the standards of 1840s Boston, transgressive along more axes than either of them could comfortably navigate. Margaret was the daughter of a respectable, if declining, Anglo-Protestant family. James was the mixed-race son of a dockworker. That they found each other at all was improbable. That they married was, in the social calculus of the time, something closer to an act of vandalism.

But the letters survive — seventeen from Margaret to James, twelve from James to Margaret, spanning April to October of 1847 and held in the private collection that Margaret maintained and eventually, reluctantly, allowed me to photograph.[^5]

[^5]: "I am showing you these," she said, sliding the first letter across the kitchen table with the care of someone handling unstable ordinance, "because you'll only pester me until I do, and because James said I should, and because — this is the actual reason, so write it down — I have carried these inside my head for 177 years and they have become so overlaid with subsequent context that I can no longer read them innocently. Maybe you can. Maybe someone should."

They are, even at this remove, remarkable documents. Not for their literary quality, which is modest, but for their directness. These were two people who had each been made singular by an ability they did not choose and could not explain, and who recognized in each other something that no one else in their lives had offered: a willingness to proceed without explanation.

Margaret's first letter to James, dated April 3, 1847, reads in part:

> *I must tell you a peculiar thing about myself, because Mother says I ought not to, and her judgment in these matters has been so reliably poor that I take her disapproval as a strong recommendation. I remember everything. I do not mean this in the way that people ordinarily mean it when they say they have a good memory. I mean that I remember everything. Every word you said to me on Tuesday is still available to me in its entirety, including the two occasions on which you began a sentence and then abandoned it, and the sound of your breathing when you paused to consider what I had said about the fire. I remember the exact color of the ash on your collar. I do not tell you this to alarm you. I tell you because you walked out of a burning building and were not burned, and so I think perhaps you will understand what it is to carry something impossible inside your body and have no one to tell.*

James's reply, dated April 9, is shorter. His handwriting is blunt, practical, and slopes markedly to the right:

> *Dear Miss Greenbriar — I have read your letter four times which I think you will agree is excessive for a man corresponding with a woman he has met once. You remember everything. I cannot die. I confess I do not know whether these facts recommend us to each other or ought to serve as a warning. But I have been warned about many things in my life and it has not yet made any difference as I am still here regardless. If you would like to walk with me on Saturday I will be at the north end of the Common at two o'clock. I will be the one who is not on fire. — J. Chen*

Margaret remembered reading this letter for the first time — the weight of the paper, the faint smell of salt and tar, the way her pulse changed at "I will be the one who is not on fire" — and she remembered it, she told me, in the same sensory resolution 177 years later, sitting across from me in a Portland kitchen while James was downstairs shelving books, the memory no dimmer and no less immediate than the coffee cooling between her hands. "That's the thing people don't understand about my memory," she said. "It isn't a filing cabinet. It isn't storage. It's *re-experience*. When I remember reading that letter, I am seventeen years old, standing in my mother's hallway, and my heart is doing something it has no business doing over a dockworker's son with ash on his collar. And I am *simultaneously* 194 years old and very tired and the coffee is getting cold. Both of those things are happening to me right now, at equal volume. Do you understand? Neither one is the echo. Neither one is the original."

They married on October 22, 1847, at a small church in Roxbury — one of the few congregations willing to perform the ceremony. The guest list was short. Margaret's mother attended and wept, though whether from sentiment or dismay is a matter on which the historical record is silent and Margaret's memory is, for once, tactfully ambiguous. James's parents attended. Weiming wore his best coat, which was not very good. Bridget brought a cake she had made herself from a recipe she could not read, because she had never learned, and which contained, according to Margaret's still-vivid recollection, "an almost heroic quantity of nutmeg."

There is a daguerreotype.[^6] Margaret and James, seated side by side, in the stiff, enduring-the-process posture that early photography demanded. She is thin-faced and serious and looks, in the way of all daguerreotypes, significantly older than seventeen. He is broad-shouldered and has a quality that even the daguerreotype's harsh chemistry cannot entirely suppress — a quality Margaret, in 2024, still described as "an energy about him, a sort of — he looked like he was about to say something funny and had just barely decided not to."

[^6]: Private collection of M. Greenbriar-Chen. The daguerreotype is remarkably well-preserved, owing partly to the chemistry of the process and partly to Margaret's obsessive archival care. "I remember everything," she told me, "but I do not trust my own memory. That's not a contradiction. I remember with perfect fidelity, but fidelity to *what*? To my experience. And my experience includes my feelings, my interpretations, the weather of my mood on the day of encoding. I keep the physical records because they are the only check I have against myself. The daguerreotype shows me what the camera saw. I remember what I saw. These are not always the same."
