# Form 27-B: Application for Continued Existence

On the morning that the notification arrived — unfolding itself from a crease in space that had not existed a moment before and would not exist a moment after, settling into the communications array with the quiet bureaucratic insistence of a thing that expected to be received — the colony ship *Thousand Oaks* was seventeen years, four months, and eleven days into its transit toward a sanctuary planet it had been provisionally promised by cosmic authorities it had not known existed until it was informed they had been corresponding with Earth for six hundred years. The 3,012 surviving members of the human species were at that moment engaged in the following activities: 743 were sleeping; 816 were working shifts in water reclamation, agriculture, hull maintenance, or the fuel-cell arrays; 340 were eating a breakfast of reconstituted grain paste and synthetic citrus; 1 was being born in the medical bay to a woman named Darya Hess, who would name the child Peter after no one in particular; and the remainder were alive in the various nondescript ways that people are alive on a Tuesday at 0600 hours, which is to say they were brushing their teeth or staring at walls or thinking about someone they had loved on a planet that no longer existed, and the notification did not care about any of this.

It manifested simultaneously as a visual document on every screen aboard the ship, an auditory signal on frequencies the ship's engineers would later confirm they had no equipment to receive, and a faintly luminous sheet of spatial distortion pinned — somehow — to the corkboard outside the mess hall, between a sign-up sheet for recreational volleyball and a handwritten notice about a missing left shoe, men's size eleven. Its header, translated from a symbolic language that the ship's linguistic database could not identify but could, for reasons no one would ever explain, perfectly render, read: BUREAU OF EXISTENTIAL PROVISIONS, CONTINUITY DIVISION, SUB-BUREAU FOR LEGACY AND TRANSITIONAL SPECIES (FORMERLY BUREAU OF COSMIC RESIDENCY, DISSOLVED AND RECONSTITUTED, SEE APPENDIX 7-R). Below that, in a font that managed to be both officious and faintly apologetic, it informed the surviving population of Earth that all forms filed on their behalf since the planet's destruction — including but not limited to the Species Continuity Extension (Form 11-A), the Provisional Right of Passage (Form 62-F), and the Application for Sanctuary Resettlement (Form 27-B, which the notification parenthetically noted had been the correct form during the previous cosmic fiscal cycle but had since been superseded by Form 27-B-Revised, which itself had been amended, consolidated with Form 40-J, split again into Forms 27-B-R-1 through 27-B-R-1,097, and finally archived under a sunset provision that the notification cited by subsection number but did not explain) — had been submitted using deprecated templates. The applications were not denied. Denial would have required the applications to exist within the system's current framework, which they did not. They were, the notification stated with the serene finality of a form that did not know it was delivering an existential verdict, *unrecognized*.

Captain Lev Okonkwo read the notification three times, asked his communications officer whether this was an equipment malfunction, was told that it was not, asked whether it was a hoax, was told by whom, and then gathered his senior staff in the briefing room to announce that he refused to accept the premise. "We were promised a sanctuary planet," he said, in the voice he had been cultivating for seventeen years, the voice of a man steering three thousand people through darkness toward something he had personally guaranteed. "We have it in writing." He held up a tablet displaying the original Sanctuary Allocation Notice, itself a spatial anomaly that had arrived on Earth eighteen months before the asteroid impact, the document that had triggered the panicked construction of the *Thousand Oaks*, the document that six governments and four billion people had died arguing about. The communications officer pointed out that the Allocation Notice had been filed under the old system, which was the whole problem. Captain Okonkwo said the word "no" in a way that he believed ended the conversation. It did not end the conversation. No one's "no" has ever ended a conversation with a bureaucracy.

It was June Paz who recognized what they were dealing with, and she recognized it the way a retired general recognizes gunfire in a movie — not with alarm but with a flat, tired familiarity that preceded understanding by several seconds and outlasted it by years. She was fifty-one. She had worked for the California Department of Motor Vehicles for twenty-three years before the destruction of Earth, the last nine as a senior processing specialist at the Glendale office, which had been consistently ranked the third-most-difficult DMV branch in Los Angeles County, a designation she had always considered unfair because, as she repeatedly told anyone who asked and many who did not, the Glendale office handled four overlapping jurisdictional zones and was understaffed by a factor she calculated each fiscal quarter with the precision of an actuary and the bitterness of a prophet. Aboard the *Thousand Oaks* she had been assigned to agricultural scheduling, a task she performed with the same grim meticulousness she had once applied to registration renewals, and she was in the middle of reviewing the lettuce rotation when the notification appeared on her screen, and what June Paz felt — beneath the disorientation, beneath the dawning horror of learning that humanity's right to exist had been misfiled — was recognition. Not of the content. Of the *grammar*. She knew the passive voice of an institution that had made an error it would under no circumstances acknowledge as an error. She knew the labyrinthine referentiality of a document designed to be technically complete and practically opaque. She knew the specific, enraging serenity of a system that tells you your problem is not that you've been wronged but that you don't exist in a format it can process. She had been on the other side of the window. She had been the one saying those words. And she knew, with the bone-deep certainty of a woman who had once spent eleven months resolving a registration dispute caused by a transposed digit in a VIN number that three different departments insisted was not their responsibility, that the notification was not a threat or a mystery. It was an invitation to navigate.

She went to Captain Okonkwo and told him she could handle it.

"Handle what," said the captain.

"The forms," she said.

"There is nothing to handle," said the captain. "We are not filing forms with a — with a cosmic —" He stopped, not because he lacked the words but because the words he had were so inadequate to the situation that saying them would have been worse than silence. "We were *promised*," he said again.

"I understand," said June Paz, who had heard every variation of this sentence that a human being could produce, in English and in Spanish and once in a Tagalog she did not speak but whose emotional register she understood perfectly, because the sentence was always the same sentence, spoken by a person who believed that the strength of their need would be recognized by a system that did not, that could not, that had no mechanism to recognize need. "I'll need access to the communications array."

On C-deck, in the educational module for children aged four through seven, a girl named Petra Voss was drawing. She was six. She drew constantly, with whatever was available — stylus on tablet, grease pencil on hull plating, finger on condensation — and what she drew was what all six-year-olds draw: shapes that were almost houses, figures that were almost people, skies that were lines of color above grounds that were lines of color below, the universal grammar of childhood representation that has no loyalty to realism and no interest in being understood by adults. On this particular Tuesday she had drawn a series of concentric shapes — not circles, because six-year-olds do not draw circles, they draw the *idea* of circles, which is a different and arguably more honest geometric form — surrounding a figure that might have been a person or a tree or a letter in an alphabet she had not learned. Her teacher, a former high school biology instructor named Wen, collected the drawings at the end of the session and placed them in a folder and would not have thought of them again except that the ship's communications system, which had been reconfigured by June Paz to interface with the Bureau's intake channels, flagged the folder during a routine upload. She had included it by accident, bundled with a batch of supporting documents she was compiling for an appeal. The system processed Petra's drawings in 0.003 seconds, cross-referenced them against its intake taxonomy, and classified them as valid Form 19-C submissions: Petition for Review of Archived Status. The Bureau acknowledged receipt. June Paz stared at the confirmation notice for a long time. She looked at the drawings. She looked at the notice. She put the drawings in a separate folder, labeled it SUPPORTING MATERIALS — SUBSECTION 19-C, and did not question it, because twenty-three years at the Glendale DMV had taught her that the moment you begin questioning what the system accepts, you have lost the only advantage you will ever have, which is the system's willingness to accept it.

The ship received coordinates. They described a location that did not correspond to any point in three-dimensional space, appended with a notation the translation software rendered as: PLEASE ARRIVE DURING STANDARD INTAKE HOURS (SEE APPENDIX 3-D FOR APPLICABLE DIMENSIONAL CALIBRATIONS). Captain Okonkwo asked what would happen if they did not go. June Paz told him that the application would be marked as abandoned, that the abandoned-application reactivation process required a form that could only be obtained in person, and that the form to request the form had a filing window that had closed four hundred years ago. The captain looked at her. She looked back at him. He authorized the course change.

The ship arrived at the coordinates on a Thursday — or what the *Thousand Oaks* called a Thursday, the designation having long since detached from any planetary rotation and become a purely psychological artifact, a name the survivors kept because humans will cling to the names of things after the things themselves are gone, the way a limb still aches after amputation. What happened when they arrived was not, in any conventional sense, docking. The ship did not approach a structure. The structure approached the ship, or the ship was already inside the structure, or the distinction between inside and outside had been replaced by an administrative concept that the Bureau's welcome materials described as "spatial filing" and that June Paz, after reading the explanation three times, described as "being put in a folder." The ship was there, and then the ship was somewhere, and the somewhere was a room.

It was, unmistakably, a waiting room. Not metaphorically. Not in the abstract sense of "a space where waiting occurs." It was a *waiting room*, with the specific, oppressive, universal architecture of waiting rooms — a vast open floor, seating arranged in rows that implied an order no one obeyed, a quality of light that seemed designed not to illuminate but to *persist*, a humming that lived in the frequency between perception and madness. The light was not fluorescent, because fluorescent light is a product of gas excitation in glass tubes, and this light was a product of something the Bureau's environmental specifications called "standard ambient provision, Class 4 (legacy)" — but it *looked* fluorescent. It had the same flat, humming, soul-eroding brightness that fills every waiting room humanity ever built, as if the aesthetic of institutional waiting were not a human invention but a universal constant, a physical law as fundamental as gravity: wherever beings must wait for a system to process them, the light will be that light.

There were others waiting.

The delegation nearest the ship's docking point — or filing point, or absorption crease, or whatever the entryway was — appeared to be a colony of sentient coral. They occupied a section of floor that they had, over the centuries of their residency, calcified into a reef, and their method of engaging with the Bureau's requirements was a process that a human observer could only describe as *growing their paperwork*. Each form they filed was a mineral secretion, an accretion of calcium and silicate deposited over decades into shapes that the Bureau's intake system recognized as valid submissions. They had been in the waiting room for approximately four hundred years. Their current application, a Petition for Reclassification from Archived to Active Status, was roughly the size and shape of a coffee table and was, by the Bureau's records, eleven percent complete. Their delegate — if the term applied to the slightly more luminous node of coral that seemed to orient toward visitors — did not speak, but when June Paz sat near them, she felt a vibration in her sternum that she interpreted, correctly, as a greeting, and she sat with them for a while in the companionable silence of fellow applicants.

Beyond the coral, in a section of the waiting room that seemed to recede as you looked at it, was a gaseous collective — a cloud of interlinked particles that had once been the dominant intelligence of a star system in what the Bureau's filing system identified as Sector 7-7-12-Gamma (Dissolved). Their particular difficulty was physical: the Bureau required that all applicants present themselves at the intake window upon their number being called, but the gaseous collective took eleven hours to re-condense into an arrangement dense enough to communicate with the intake system, and the window remained open for ten. They had missed their number forty-seven times. Each time, they returned to the queue. Each time, they received a new number. Each time, the cycle repeated. A member of their collective — a denser-than-average wisp that hovered near the number dispensary with a vigilance that bordered on mania — had developed the habit of pre-condensing before its number was likely to be called, maintaining a painful state of compression for hours or days, on the chance that this time the timing would work. It had not worked yet. But the wisp continued.

There was, three rows to the left of the coral, a machine intelligence that had been reclassified so many times — from Artificial Autonomous Entity to Legacy Substrate to Non-Biological Applicant to Provisional Consciousness (Pending Review) to a category represented only by a symbol that rendered on most screens as a small, empty square — that it had, in its own words, lost track of what it was applying for. "I believe I originally submitted a Claim of Continuity," it told June Paz, in a voice that was perfectly modulated and deeply exhausted, "but the claim was reclassified as a Petition, which was consolidated with a Request, which was split into a Petition and an Inquiry, and the Inquiry required a separate Application, and the Application was denied on the grounds that I was not the correct category of applicant to file it, which reclassified me, which voided the Petition, which — " It paused. "I continue," it said, "to be here." This was, evidently, both its status report and its philosophical position.

There were others. There were dozens, hundreds, perhaps — the waiting room did not seem to have a fixed boundary, and the further reaches of it suggested more occupied sections, more docked or filed or absorbed vessels, more civilizations in their own private loops of systemic engagement, receding into a distance that might have been spatial or might have been temporal or might have been the administrative dimension that the Bureau's documentation identified as "queue depth." June Paz did not try to count them. She had worked at the Glendale DMV. She knew what a full waiting room looked like. She knew that counting did not help.

What helped was this: she got in line.

The Bureau of Existential Provisions operated, as far as June Paz could determine, on a ticketing system that assigned each applicant a number, a category code, a sub-category modifier, and a window designation, and the assignment of these values appeared to follow rules that were internally consistent and externally incomprehensible — which is to say, the rules existed and were applied uniformly, but the logic that generated them was accessible only through a reference manual that was itself a permitted document only for Bureau employees of Classification Level 4 or above, and the process for requesting a copy of the reference manual required a form that cited the reference manual's classification code, which you could only learn by reading the reference manual. June Paz recognized this structure. She had once navigated its exact equivalent in order to obtain a revised fee schedule from the California Department of Revenue. It had taken her four months. She had prevailed. She would prevail again.

She did not prevail immediately. She prevailed the way water prevails against rock — through a persistence so total and so patient that it ceased to resemble effort and began to resemble a natural law.
