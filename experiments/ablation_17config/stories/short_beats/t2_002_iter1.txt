# The HOA Board Will Now Come to Order

## I.

The notice was printed on goldenrod paper and affixed to her front door with a single piece of painter's tape, the blue kind that peels cleanly and doesn't damage surfaces, because the Briarwood Estates Homeowners Association was, above all things, considerate.

**NOTICE OF VIOLATION — LOT 142**

Meredith Chen pulled the tape free, folded the paper in half without reading it, and carried it inside. She set it on the kitchen counter next to the fruit bowl, where it joined two prior notices she had also not yet read, and she poured herself a cup of coffee that had been sitting in the pot since six that morning. It was now quarter past four. She drank it cold, because reheating it would involve the microwave, and the microwave would beep, and the beeping would wake Leonard, and Leonard had only fallen asleep forty minutes ago after a coughing fit that had lasted, by Meredith's internal clock, somewhere between ten minutes and the rest of her natural life.

She unfolded the notice.

> **Property: 4712 Briarwood Lane, Lot 142**
> **Violation: Unauthorized structural modification to front elevation (wheelchair ramp, wood-frame construction, approx. 14 ft. length, non-compliant with Architectural Standards, Section 4.7.2(b))**
> **Fine: $50.00 per day, compounding, effective 30 days from initial notice (see Notice 1, dated March 3)**
> **Sincerely,**
> **Doug Whitmore, President**
> **Briarwood Estates HOA Board of Directors**

She went back and unfolded the first two notices. They were identical except for the dates. March 3. March 17. Today's was March 31. She sat down at the kitchen table and performed the mental arithmetic without thinking about it, the way a pianist's fingers find middle C: twenty-eight days of fines at fifty dollars, compounding daily. She did not finish the calculation. She knew what compounding meant. She had spent forty years at Midwestern Mutual explaining to people, in conference rooms with inadequate ventilation, exactly what compounding meant. Compounding meant that a small thing became a large thing while you were busy thinking it was still small.

From the back bedroom, the hospital bed's motor hummed, adjusting. Leonard shifting. The equipment had a vocabulary now — the oxygen concentrator's steady thrum, the bed's mechanical complaints, the particular creak that meant Leonard was trying to reach the remote on his nightstand. She listened until the sounds settled.

The ramp was visible through the kitchen window, running from the front porch to the driveway in a gentle slope. Their neighbor Bill Petersen had built it over two weekends in February, refusing payment, because his father had had emphysema too, and because Bill was the kind of man who saw a problem involving lumber and considered it fundamentally solved. The ramp was sturdy. It was sanded. Bill had even stained it to match the porch. It was also, per Section 4.7.2(b) of the Briarwood Estates Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions, a modification to the front elevation undertaken without prior architectural review, and therefore a violation, and therefore subject to fines, and therefore — in the bureaucratic grammar of the CC&Rs — an offense not against aesthetics or property values but against *order*, which was the only deity the HOA recognized.

Meredith called the HOA office the next morning. The office was a converted spare bedroom in Doug Whitmore's house on Briarwood Court, staffed by Doug's wife, Linda, who answered the phone with the warmth of a woman who genuinely believed she was helping.

"Oh, Meredith, yes, I see the file right here. You'll want to submit a Request for Variance — that's Form ARC-7, I can email it to you — and then you'll be scheduled for the next Architectural Review Committee hearing. They meet the second Tuesday. Doug says there's usually a slot."

"The fines are accruing while I wait for the hearing?"

"I know, it seems like a lot, doesn't it? But the bylaws are very specific. Once a violation is issued, it runs until the modification is approved or removed. But honestly? I think you'll do great at the hearing. Doug always says how much he respects you and Leonard."

Form ARC-7 was four pages. It required a site plan, a materials list, dimensions, photographs from three angles, a statement of purpose ("Please describe the modification and its intended use, including any impact on neighboring sight lines"), and two copies of everything, stapled, not paper-clipped. Meredith filled it out with the care she had once applied to actuarial tables. Under "Statement of Purpose" she wrote: *My husband has stage IV pulmonary fibrosis. He cannot walk down stairs. The ramp allows him to leave his house.* She stared at the sentence. She added: *He would like to leave his house.* Then she deleted the second sentence, because Form ARC-7 did not have a field for what people would like, and because the sentence made her eyes sting in a way she did not have time for.

The hearing was held on the second Tuesday of April in the Briarwood Estates clubhouse, a low-slung building between the pool and the tennis courts that smelled permanently of chlorine and committee meetings. The Architectural Review Committee consisted of Doug Whitmore, who was also HOA president, vice president, and — through a series of bylaw amendments Meredith only dimly understood — acting chair of every committee that mattered; Sheila Branford, a retired school principal who had never met a rule she didn't consider load-bearing; and Tom Pham, a mortgage broker who served on the committee because his wife had told him to get a hobby and he had misunderstood the assignment.

Doug was already seated when Meredith arrived. He stood, extended his hand, and held hers a beat longer than necessary.

"Meredith. How are you? How's Leonard doing?"

"He's — thank you, Doug. He has good days."

"Well, you tell him we're all praying for him. Jan Holloway's prayer circle has him on the list. Sheila, did you —" He turned. "Sheila, didn't you drop off a casserole last week?"

"Chicken divan," Sheila confirmed. "I left it with your neighbor."

"There it is," Doug said, as though the casserole resolved something. "Can I — before we start — I made banana bread this morning. Please." He slid a paper plate toward her across the folding table. The banana bread was dense, moist, studded with walnuts. It was, Meredith would later admit, very good banana bread.

The hearing lasted eleven minutes. Meredith presented her Form ARC-7. Doug read aloud from Section 4.7.2(b), which stated that all modifications to front elevations required prior committee approval and must conform to the "Briarwood Aesthetic Standard," a phrase that was not defined anywhere in the CC&Rs, which was precisely the point. Sheila asked about the ramp's dimensions. Tom Pham asked if there was a handrail, and when Meredith said yes, he nodded as though this changed something, and then Sheila said it didn't change anything. Doug listened to everything. He nodded thoughtfully. He made a note on his legal pad. And then he looked at Meredith with an expression of such genuine regret, such uncomplicated sympathy, that for one disorienting moment she believed what she saw in his face.

"Meredith, I want you to know that this was not an easy decision. I've thought about this a lot. We all have. And if it were up to me personally — just as a neighbor, as a friend — you know what I'd say." He paused. "But I have a responsibility to this community. The CC&Rs exist for a reason. If we make an exception here, however justified, we open a door that we can't close. I've seen it happen in other communities. It starts with one ramp, and then it's a shed, and then it's a boat in the driveway, and before you know it —" He spread his hands, gesturing at a future entropy only he could see. "The variance is denied. I'm sorry. I really am. And the existing fine schedule will remain in effect until the modification is brought into compliance."

"Brought into compliance means removed," Meredith said.

"The committee's recommendation would be to submit a new proposal for a rear-entrance modification, which is subject to a different standard under —"

"There is no rear entrance. We have a patio with a sliding glass door and a four-foot drop to the yard."

"Right, and so there might be options there that would —"

"My husband cannot be carried through the back of his own house."

Doug's face held its shape. The regret did not waver. That was the thing about Doug Whitmore's face — it was a face that had practiced regret until regret had become indistinguishable from contentment. He looked like a man at peace with the tragedy of doing what was necessary.

"I wish there were something I could do," he said.

Meredith drove home. She parked in the driveway next to the ramp. She sat in the car for two full minutes, engine off, watching a sparrow hop along the ramp's handrail. Then she went inside.

Leonard was awake, propped up in the hospital bed with the oxygen cannula in his nose and a crossword puzzle book open on his lap. He had not filled in any answers. His hands were not good today.

"How'd it go?" he asked. His voice was thin, aspirated, each word rationed like something expensive.

"They denied it. Fifty dollars a day."

Leonard closed his eyes. Then he opened them, and the corner of his mouth twitched. "Fifty dollars a day? Well." He breathed. "I'd better die faster. Save us some money."

"Don't," Meredith said, but she was already almost laughing, which was almost crying, which was the same muscle, and they had learned by now not to distinguish between the two. She sat on the edge of the bed and took the crossword from him and looked at it. Seven across: *Instrument of bureaucratic power, eight letters.* This was not actually the clue. The actual clue was about a river in Switzerland. But Meredith was, at that particular moment, not reading anything correctly.

That night, after Leonard's evening medications and the nightly negotiation with the oxygen levels and the ritual checking of things that could not be made better by checking, Meredith sat at the kitchen table and opened the Briarwood Estates governing documents.

She had read them before, the way everyone reads such documents: scanning for the parts that mattered, skimming the rest, absorbing just enough to comply. She had read them the way a passenger reads the emergency card in the seatback pocket — not to survive, but to feel responsible.

Now she read differently.

She read the way she had once read mortality tables at Midwestern Mutual — not for what they said, but for what they *assumed*. Every table, every risk model, every projection contained assumptions, and the assumptions were where the errors lived. You didn't find mistakes in the math. You found them in the premises. In the things the actuary had decided were true before the first number was written down.

She started with the bylaws. Then the CC&Rs. Then the articles of incorporation. Then the amendments, which were numbered and dated and cross-referenced and occasionally contradictory. She read with a yellow highlighter and a mechanical pencil and a legal pad. She read until the coffee was not just cold but the consistency of a grudge. She read until the sparrows started up outside, which meant it was nearly five, which meant she had been reading for six hours.

She did not go to bed. She read the quorum requirements. She read the proxy voting rules. She read the amendment adoption procedures, cross-referencing each amendment against the meeting minutes that were posted, by bylaw requirement, on the HOA website in unformatted PDF scans that would have been difficult for a normal person to parse but were, to a retired actuary, practically recreational.

By seven in the morning, she had filled nine pages of the legal pad with notes. Her handwriting, always precise, had gotten smaller as the night progressed, the letters compacted like something being pressurized.

Leonard was awake. She could hear the bed adjusting. She put the cap on the highlighter, squared the legal pad with the edge of the table, and went to help him sit up. She did not mention the bylaws. There was nothing to mention yet. She had found what she was looking for — not an answer, but the right shape of question. Certain assumptions. Certain premises that, if tested, would not hold.

She had spent forty years finding the fault lines in systems that believed themselves sound. The Briarwood Estates HOA was not a complex system. It was a simple system that believed itself complex, which was worse, because simple systems were more brittle and broke along cleaner lines.

From the bedroom, Leonard coughed.

Meredith washed her coffee mug, dried it, and set it upside down on the rack. Then she picked up her phone and called Patricia Huang.

## II.

Patricia Huang had been a court stenographer for twenty-three years in the Fourteenth Judicial District, which handles, among other things, land use and zoning disputes. She was, by all accounts, the best stenographer the district had ever employed: 260 words per minute, zero error rate across six consecutive annual reviews, a reputation for transcripts so accurate that attorneys would request her specifically, knowing that if a witness paused for two seconds, Patricia would note the pause, and if a witness coughed, Patricia would note the cough, and if an attorney said "um," Patricia would capture the "um" in all its vacuous splendor.

Her career ended on a Tuesday in October, three years before Meredith called her, when a developer named Craig Musselman took the stand in a zoning variance hearing concerning a proposed mixed-use development on the site of a former elementary school. Musselman testified that the community impact study had been "thorough and inclusive." Patricia, who had transcribed the depositions of fourteen community members who had never been contacted for the study, typed the sentence, then — without pausing, without looking up from her machine — added a parenthetical in the margin of the official transcript: *(this is a lie.)*

She was given the opportunity to delete it. The judge, who liked Patricia, told her the parenthetical had simply been a momentary lapse, a stray thought that wandered from brain to fingers, and that no one would hold it against her if she submitted a corrected transcript.

Patricia said she would be happy to submit a corrected transcript, and did, the next morning. The corrected transcript contained the same parenthetical, re-typeset in italic.

She was terminated. The union declined to grieve on her behalf, because the offense was unambiguous and because Patricia, in her exit interview, had described her action as "the only accurate thing I ever typed." She moved to Briarwood Estates, where she lived alone in a two-bedroom on Dogwood Circle, transcribed oral histories for the county museum on a freelance basis, and had not, as far as anyone knew, told a lie or permitted one in her presence since.

"You want me to take minutes," Patricia said, sitting across from Meredith at the kitchen table. She had arrived with her stenotype machine in a leather case and a legal pad of her own, which was already open.

"I want you to build a record."

"Of what?"

"Everything. Every meeting, every conversation, every procedural step. If it happens and isn't written down —"

"It didn't happen. I know how this works, Meredith."

"I know you do."

Patricia looked at the stack of governing documents on the table, the highlighted pages, the legal pad covered in Meredith's compressed handwriting. She picked up one of the PDFs Meredith had printed — minutes from the 2019 annual meeting — and read it with the specific, evaluative silence of a person who reads documents professionally.

"These minutes are garbage," she said.

"I know."

"They're not even paraphrases. They're summaries of paraphrases. Look at this: 'A motion was made and seconded to approve the budget.' By whom? What was the vote count? Was there discussion?" She set the page down with the careful distaste of a surgeon setting down a contaminated instrument. "Who took these?"

"Linda Whitmore."

"Doug's wife."

"Doug's wife."

Patricia opened her leather case and began assembling her stenotype machine. She did not say anything else. She did not need to. The machine was her answer.
