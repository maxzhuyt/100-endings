# The Collectors

## I.

The letter found Ruth on a Tuesday, which was the day the recycling was supposed to go out but never did.

She knew it was in the mailbox because she'd watched the carrier push it through the slot from behind the living room window, peering through the gap between a column of National Geographics (1974–1991, minus August '82, which she would never stop looking for) and a tower of shoeboxes containing other shoeboxes containing, at their nested center, nothing at all. The mailbox was eleven steps from where she stood. She'd counted before. Eleven steps on the path she maintained the way a city maintains a single cleared road after a blizzard: passable, barely, and only if you didn't make any sudden movements.

She started toward it and knocked a bag of twist ties off a shelf of paint cans, which nudged a bankers' box of phone chargers for devices she no longer owned, which slid into a column of egg cartons she'd been saving because you could start seeds in egg cartons and any year now she was going to start seeds. She caught the egg cartons. She pressed them back. She held very still until the architecture resettled.

The path to the mailbox threaded between the dining room table — visible only as a geological stratum, its oak surface a rumor beneath catalogues, coupons, broken umbrellas, a fish tank pump, bags of bags of bags — and the hall closet, which had not closed since 2019. She moved through it sideways, her back to the coats, which were not all her coats, some of which she'd pulled from donation bins because they still had life in them, because someone would want them, because waste was a kind of violence she could prevent.

The mailbox was a shoebox mounted on a post, the original metal one having rusted through years ago. Inside: an electric bill, a packet of coupons for a grocery store that had closed, a menu for a Chinese restaurant, and a cream-colored envelope with her full name — Ruth Anne Kimball — written in a hand she did not recognize.

She opened it standing in the eleven-step canyon.

*Dear Ruth,*

*I know what you buried at Camp Alder in August 1999. I have evidence — physical, specific, the kind that holds up. Within 48 hours it goes to your employer (I know about the code violations), your landlord (I know about the conditions), and the fire marshal, which we both know is the one that actually scares you.*

*Unless you come. Midnight, this Friday. Camp Alder, the main lodge. You, Marie, Nessa, and Theo.*

*It's time to dig it up.*

No signature. No return address. The postmark said Granton, New Hampshire, which was the town closest to the camp, and the recognition of that name moved through Ruth's body like a crack through ice.

She put the letter down on the nearest horizontal surface, which happened to be a box of broken clocks. Then she picked it up again. Then she put it down. Then she picked it up and put it in her pocket, and her hand stayed in her pocket, holding it, the way her hand always eventually closed around whatever it touched.

She thought about the last time she'd seen Marie, Nessa, Theo. She was twelve. They were twelve, eleven, twelve. It was dark, and they were digging, and someone was crying, and the dirt smelled like rust, and they promised, all four of them, hands dirty and shaking, that they would never speak to each other again.

She moved back toward the living room. Somewhere under the paint cans was her car key. She began to dig.

Marie DeLuca was three drinks into someone else's birthday party in a bar on North Milwaukee when she read the letter.

She hadn't planned to come out. She didn't know the birthday girl. She'd been walking past the bar, seen the cluster of people through the window, and gone in because bars were good and crowds were better because in crowds your hands could do their work and no one noticed. She'd been inside twenty minutes when she realized she'd acquired a lighter (silver, monogrammed, left on the bar next to an unattended drink), a book of matches from the hostess stand (already in her jacket, already forgotten), and the birthday girl's hair clip, which she'd plucked from the back of a chair so cleanly that her conscious mind only registered it now, in her pocket, as her fingers moved over its teeth.

The letter had been in her mailbox when she left her apartment. She'd stuck it in her jacket without opening it because the handwriting wasn't anyone she knew and she'd been focused on the walk, on the beautiful evening problem of what her hands would bring home.

She opened it now, at the bar, while her left hand moved a salt shaker in a slow arc from the bartop into the shadow below the counter and then into her bag, where it came to rest beside three sugar packets, a promotional pen, and a votive candle she'd lifted from a restaurant two weeks ago and kept meaning to put somewhere.

*Dear Marie,*

*I know what you buried at Camp Alder in August 1999. I have evidence — physical, specific, the kind that holds up. Within 48 hours it goes to your parole officer (I know about the violations), your employer at the gallery (I know what's been going missing from the stockroom), and Daniel, who I think you'd agree has been patient enough.*

*Unless you come. Midnight, this Friday. Camp Alder, the main lodge. You, Ruth, Nessa, and Theo.*

*It's time to dig it up.*

Marie read the letter twice. Her hand stopped moving. This was unusual enough that the bartender glanced at her — a woman gone suddenly still in a room full of motion, like a clock that skips a tick.

The names hit her in sequence. Ruth, who she hadn't spoken to since the summer they were twelve. Nessa, who she'd looked up once, at 3 a.m., seven years ago, finding a LinkedIn profile for a nutritionist in Dayton that she'd stared at for a long time before closing the laptop. Theo, who she did not allow herself to think about, whose face she had dismantled in her memory so thoroughly that what remained was not an image but a sensation: the particular weight of someone sitting next to you in the dark, saying nothing, while you both held something terrible.

She folded the letter and put it in her inside pocket, the same pocket where the hair clip rested, and she felt the two objects touch, and her fingers wanted to arrange them, to nest the letter around the clip, to hold and hold, and she pulled her hand out like she was pulling it from a fire.

She left the bar. On the walk to her apartment she pocketed a gas station attendant's pen and a pair of sunglasses from a rack outside a drugstore, both so reflexively that she was in her car and driving before she realized the sunglasses were on her face, tinted amber, turning the highway lights the color of an old photograph, the color of summer, the color of the dirt they'd packed down with their hands. She drove. The sunglasses stayed on.

In Dayton, Nessa Torres stood in her kitchen at 2:14 a.m. and surveyed her work.

The containers were arranged on the counter in the order she'd emptied them: a jar of peanut butter (scraped clean, a half-moon of nail marks in the residue), a sleeve of crackers (crushed wrappers stacked), a pint of ice cream (strawberry, the spoon still standing in the melt), a bag of shredded cheese (mostly gone, eaten by the fistful, the bag now folded into a neat square as if it had never been opened), and a box of cereal she didn't remember buying and didn't remember opening and didn't remember eating but which was, somehow, empty.

Her stomach was a planet. Dense, distended, exerting its own gravity. She stood very still and breathed through her nose and waited for the moment when the fullness crossed from discomfort into the specific nausea that meant it was time for the next part, and she hated the waiting but also relied on it because the waiting was a kind of order, and order was the thing she built and rebuilt every day, the only architecture that held.

The letter was on the table. She'd found it when she got home from the clinic — her clinic, the one with her name on the door, Dr. Vanessa Torres, MS, RD, LPC, the letters after her name a barricade she'd built so carefully — and she'd read it once and set it down and then the kitchen had happened, the way the kitchen always happened, as if the room itself reached out and pulled her in and closed the door.

*Dear Nessa,*

*I know what you buried at Camp Alder in August 1999. I have evidence — physical, specific, the kind that holds up. Within 48 hours it goes to the Ohio Board of Licensed Professional Counselors (I know what would happen to a nutritionist with your particular history), your clients (I have your mailing list, which was surprisingly easy to find), and your mother, who I understand still doesn't know.*

*Unless you come. Midnight, this Friday. Camp Alder, the main lodge. You, Ruth, Marie, and Theo.*

*It's time to dig it up.*

She'd read it and the floor had tilted, just slightly, just enough, and her hands had opened the peanut butter before her mind caught up, and now she stood among the wreckage of her pantry with a letter on the table that knew her name, her real name, the name underneath the degrees and the clinic and the meal plans she wrote for other people in her clean, confident handwriting — plans she sometimes stared at in the evenings and thought *I should try this, this looks like something a person could do.*

The bathroom was down the hall. The faucet turned easily. She ran the water. The sound was a white noise machine. The sound was a curtain. The sound was the oldest privacy she knew.

When it was over she sat on the bathroom floor with her back against the tub and held the letter in hands that smelled like soap — she always washed her hands immediately, three times, with soap, it was part of the order — and she read it again and this time what she felt was not surprise or fear but the blunt recognition of a road she'd always known was there, a road that went back to a place in New Hampshire where the lake was black at night and the trees were so tall they blocked the stars and four children knelt in the dirt and promised to become strangers.

She got up. She brushed her teeth. She packed a bag — toothbrush, change of clothes, her good jacket, the one that made her feel like someone with a working life. She did not pack food. She would not need food. She packed the letter and zipped the bag shut and the zipper sounded like a door closing and she thought: *Good. Close it. Go.*

Theo Harwick received the letter in a studio apartment in Montpelier, Vermont, that contained a mattress, a desk, a chair, and almost nothing else.

The apartment was temporary. They were all temporary. He'd had nine addresses in six years, not because he was running but because he kept arriving at the point in every lease, every job, every friendship, every cautious and promising acquaintance, when the thing in his chest opened and the words came out, and then he had to go.

He opened the letter standing at his desk. He read it slowly. His face did not change.

*Dear Theo,*

*I know what you buried at Camp Alder in August 1999. I have evidence — physical, specific, the kind that holds up. Within 48 hours it goes to — well. You and I both know there isn't much left to send it to. But I'll find something. I always find something.*

*Unless you come. Midnight, this Friday. Camp Alder, the main lodge. You, Ruth, Marie, and Nessa.*

*It's time to dig it up.*
