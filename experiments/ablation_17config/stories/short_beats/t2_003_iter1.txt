# Perdition Gulch

## I.

The town lay in a crease of the desert like a coin dropped between the cushions of an enormous and indifferent sofa. Forty-three buildings, if you counted the lean-tos. Fifty-one, if you counted the privies, which the territorial assessor did, because he was that kind of man. A single street of stamped earth ran north-south with the optimism of a spine in a body not long for this world, and along it the forgettable enterprises of marginal civilization had arranged themselves in the order of their necessity: the well, the general store, the boarding house, the saloon, the feed lot, and—set back from the road with the modesty of genuine authority—the house where the council met, which was just a house, which was not just a house at all.

Perdition Gulch worked. That was the thing that needed explaining, and nobody could quite explain it.

Other towns in the basin had dried up, burned down, or murdered themselves into footnotes. Caldera Springs lost half its population to typhus in '74. Santo Tomás survived the typhus but not the Apaches, or rather not the cavalry that came to address the Apaches and addressed everything else in the process. Brine Flats simply emptied, its residents departing one by one over the span of a single July, as though the town itself had exhaled and they were its last warm breath. But Perdition Gulch held. Held through drought. Held through the collapse of the silver market. Held through the two seasons when the railroad surveyors came and went and came again and finally chose a route forty miles to the north, a decision that should have been a death sentence and instead was merely a setback from which the town recovered with suspicious alacrity.

People who lived there felt it without naming it. A safety. An order. It was in the air the way humidity is in the air in other, luckier places—you couldn't see it, but your skin knew. Children played in the street past dusk. Doors went unbarred. When Hector Navarro's well went alkaline in the spring of '79, the new well was dug in the right place on the first try, as though someone had known exactly where the good water waited. When the diphtheria came through in '81, it took two people and stopped, which was not what diphtheria did, which was not what diphtheria had ever done in the recorded history of the territory.

The five were known the way load-bearing walls are known: structurally essential, visually ordinary, invisible until you imagined the house without them.

Silas Corwin ran the apothecary. He had the long, veined hands of a pianist and the disposition of a man perpetually embarrassed by his own competence. He compounded tinctures. He set bones. He did a brisk trade in willow bark and camphor. What he actually did—what his hands actually did when he laid them on the place where the illness lived—no one spoke of directly. You brought your child to Silas with the red rash climbing her throat, and you left with your child sleeping cool against your shoulder, and you did not ask questions because the answers would have cost you the safety of not knowing.

Alma Ruiz kept no shop and held no employment anyone could see. She lived in two rooms behind the Mexican cantina and was known to write in journals—always writing, always in those leatherbound books she ordered from a stationer in St. Louis at an expense that seemed impossible relative to any visible income. She was a small woman, dark and contained, with the posture of someone listening to a sound just below the threshold of other people's hearing. Which was almost exactly what she was doing, except the sound was time, and she heard all of it, all the time, without interruption or mercy. Alma Ruiz remembered everything. Not the way that phrase is used colloquially—not "a good memory," not "sharp as a tack." She remembered the way a daguerreotype remembers: fixed, total, permanent. Every face she had ever seen. Every word she had ever heard. The exact temperature of the air on the morning of April 14, 1848, when she was six years old and a goat bit her thumb in her grandmother's yard in Mesilla. The pitch, duration, and harmonic texture of every scream she had ever heard in her life, which was a catalog she would have given her eyes to lose, and could not.

The twins, Ruth and Esther Calloway, were thirty-nine years old and had never been more than nine miles apart. This was not sentiment. It was architecture. Ruth lived at the north end of town and Esther at the south, and between them they maintained a single continuous consciousness that operated like a map with two reading points—each twin saw what the other saw, knew what the other knew, felt the precise topography of the world from two positions simultaneously. When Ruth looked north from her porch toward the mining road at dawn, Esther looked south toward the wash, and between them the town was held in a stereoscope of attention that missed nothing. A stranger approaching from any direction was noted, measured, understood before he reached the first building. They had not discussed this with each other in twenty years. There was nothing to discuss. Discussion implies separation. They were one woman expressed as two, or two women who had surrendered the border between them so long ago that the question of which had ceased to be interesting even to themselves.

And Nettie.

Nettie Price was twenty-eight years old and had been hearing other people's thoughts since she was six. At six she had not understood what was happening, which was a mercy, because what six-year-old should know that her mother was thinking about the man at the feed lot while lying next to her father, or that the minister thought in phrases from Scripture not because he was devout but because the Scripture was a dam he'd built to hold back the other things, the things with teeth? By eight she had learned to manage the input. By ten she had learned to use it. By fifteen, when the council had already been operating for three years—the term "council" was Alma's, and none of them liked it, and all of them used it—Nettie was its most precise instrument: the girl who sat on the bench outside the general store looking blankly at nothing while inside her skull the town thought its thoughts and she sorted them like mail, setting aside the ones that mattered, the warnings, the intentions, the slow gathering of harm that could be addressed before it bloomed.

They had kept this town alive for twenty years.

They had kept it safe.

And now they were dying—not in their bodies, or not only in their bodies, but in the parts of themselves that mattered, the parts that were the reason for everything.

---

It started with Silas.

No—that was the story they told themselves later, because Silas was the most visible loss and the first one to leave. It started before Silas, in small wrongnesses that each of them experienced alone and, for a time, chose not to name. Alma lost a face. Not a face she cared about—a man she'd passed once in Tucson in 1869, a man buying rope in a hardware store, no one—but she had never lost a face before. She had never lost anything before. She noticed the void the way you'd notice a missing tooth: the tongue going to it involuntarily, again and again, finding the absence. Ruth felt a flutter of static in her connection to Esther, a half-second of snow, and Esther felt it too—felt Ruth feeling it—and they both decided it was fatigue. Nettie heard a thought from Clara Dunne at the boarding house and then couldn't hear Clara's next thought, as though someone had closed a door between two rooms and then opened it again. A stutter. A skip. Nothing, probably.

But Silas was the first one who could not pretend.

He laid his hands on Martin Olguin's boy, the one with the bone infection in his left leg, the green smell already coming off it, and he felt his hands do what they always did: he felt them reach into the hot architecture of the wound and find the place where the wrongness lived and he felt the pull, the draw, the current that traveled from the sick flesh through his palms and into whatever furnace inside him burned it clean. He felt all of this. And the boy screamed. And when Silas took his hands away the wound was the same—hot, tight, green-white at the edges—and the boy's mother was looking at Silas with an expression he had never seen directed at him before, an expression that contained the nascent seed of the understanding that he was just a man, just a man with long hands and camphor-smelling fingers who could not save her son.

He saved the boy anyway—with a bone saw, with catgut, with laudanum, with the brutal ordinary medicine he'd kept in practice for decades because some part of him had always known this day would come. The boy kept his leg. But something else was lost in that room, something Silas could feel like a phantom limb, and three days later he packed a single bag and was gone before dawn.

He left a note for Alma. It said: *I can still feel where it was.*

---

The morning after Silas left, Alma walked to the house where the council met—the house that was not just a house—and sat in his chair. She did not sit in her own chair. She sat in his. She needed to feel the particular shape of his absence, and she was a woman for whom the physical world was a mnemonic palace of terrifying fidelity.

She sat there and she remembered everything about Silas, which was no different from any other day, because she always remembered everything about everyone. But today she let herself remember one particular thing.

*Silas's hands, shaking. Silas's hands held out in front of him with the fingers spread, trembling, not with effort but with suppression. His hands in the dark, in the cold, on the ridge above the settlement, the night of 11 November 1863. Below them, the campfires of the settlers—fourteen families, forty-one people, including nine children under the age of twelve, and she knew this because she had counted them two days earlier on the road when they passed through Perdition Gulch asking about water. Below and to the east, not yet visible but audible to Nettie, to Nettie who was eight years old and already drowning in the noise of forty-one minds full of supper and fatigue and in the other noise, the harder noise, the noise of the minds approaching from the south, the minds that thought in the language of arithmetic: how many men, how many guns, how many horses to take, how many left alive to tell anyone, the answer to the last one being zero. Silas's hands shaking because his body knew. His body knew before his mind agreed. His body wanted to go down there and stand between the raiders and the settlers and put those hands on every wound before it was made, and his body was an idiot, his body did not understand the calculus, and so his hands shook and shook while the rest of him held still.*

Alma sat in Silas's chair and let the memory play in its full resolution—the cold, the smell of piñon smoke from below, the stars, the absolute clarity of the Milky Way that night, which she could still see when she closed her eyes and would always be able to see, she had thought, she had believed, until three days ago when she lost the face of the man buying rope in Tucson, and understood that "always" had become a word with a new and finite meaning.

She opened her eyes. She put her hands on the arms of the chair where Silas's hands had rested for twenty years.

She thought: *We are being unmade.*

But she did not say it aloud, because the twins were not there, and Nettie was not there, and there was no one to hear her except the narrator of this account, who heard everything, always, and did nothing about any of it.
