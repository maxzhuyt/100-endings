# The Last Watch of the Mara Celeste

I brought her tea at six every morning for four years. Oolong, loose leaf, one knuckle of ginger sliced thin enough to see through. Wen didn't take sugar. She said her grandfather hadn't taken sugar either and that this was the kind of thing you inherited whether you meant to or not, like a ship, like a name on a loan.

The morning I'm telling you about, the cup slid the length of the galley table and I caught it with my hip before it hit the deck. I set it back. It slid again. I wedged it into the corner where the table met the bulkhead, between a can of condensed milk and the stack of envelopes Wen had stopped opening in April. The tea sat there and went cold. I remember that clearly — watching the steam thin out and quit, the ginger slice turning slow circles on the surface like something searching for a way out of a small room. The ship leaned twelve degrees to port and stayed there as though deciding something.

The *Mara Celeste* was a forty-one-meter coastal freighter, steel-hulled, built in 1974 at a yard in Kaohsiung that no longer exists. Wen's grandfather Chen Jiaming bought her secondhand in 1986 with money he'd earned running refrigerated containers of milkfish between Penghu and Keelung. He renamed her. I never asked after what. By the time I came aboard she'd already been repainted twice and re-engined once and the second engine was twelve years past what any surveyor would call reasonable, and the surveyor had in fact called it that, in writing, in a report that was clipped to the inside of one of those envelopes between the condensed milk and the bulkhead.

Wen's full name was Chen Wen-hui. She was the third person in her family to captain the *Mara Celeste* and she would be the last, though not for the reason you'd think. Not for the typhoon. The typhoon was — I want to say this correctly — the typhoon was what happened. But it was not the reason.

The reason was a second mortgage taken out in 2019 on terms that the bank's representative later described, in a hearing Wen was not alive to attend, as "standard adjustable-rate." The reason was a fuel price spike and two cancelled contracts and a hull insurance premium that tripled in a single quarter because the underwriters had reclassified the *Mara Celeste* from "aging" to "derelict," a word that in English means both a ruined ship and a failure of duty, though the underwriters probably didn't intend the second meaning, or maybe they did. The reason was a man named David Lau from East Asia Maritime Recovery Ltd., who had twice flown to Bermuda to file papers and who had, on the second occasion, according to the harbormaster's deposition, actually stood on the pier with a clipboard and a lanyard around his neck and watched us leave port.

He waved. I remember that too. A small wave, not unfriendly, like seeing off someone whose house you'd already bought.

We put out in late August with a partial hold — machine parts bound for Nassau that no one in Nassau was particularly waiting for — because Wen had calculated that nine more days of operation would cover the month's minimum payment and if she covered the month's minimum payment the repossession proceedings would stall for another sixty days and if they stalled for sixty days she could find another contract and if she found another contract she could service the debt for one more quarter and if she could service the debt for one more quarter then the *Mara Celeste* would remain, for a little while longer, the *Mara Celeste*. I knew this because I did the books. I knew this because I stood in the galley every night after dinner and fed numbers into a calculator older than I was and watched the numbers come back wrong.

The storm was named Bailu. We had forecast. We had forty-eight hours of forecast telling us to shelter, to divert to Hamilton, to do anything other than what we did, which was to continue on our plotted course across the tongue of the Sargasso Sea because diverting to Hamilton meant berthing fees Wen could not pay and port inspections Wen could not pass and David Lau with his clipboard standing on another pier. So we sailed into it. I want you to understand: we did not sail into it because Wen was brave. We sailed into it because the alternatives had been repossessed along with everything else.

That first morning of the real weather, I watched my tea go cold in its wedged corner and I thought: this is the last time I will make this tea. And then I thought: but I will make it again tomorrow, because that is what I do at six. And I did. I made it three more times. The last cup I made, the water was not hot enough because the gas line to the range had cracked, and I heated it with a backpacking stove I kept in my cabin for emergencies, crouched on the galley floor with the ship rolling thirty-five degrees in each direction, shaving ginger with a paring knife while the sea tried to open us. I mention this not because it was difficult but because it was the obvious thing to do and I am tired of obvious things being made to sound remarkable. You make the tea. That's all. You make the tea because it is six and because she will want it and because the day you stop making it is the day you have agreed to something you are not ready to agree to, and so you make the tea.

She didn't drink that last cup either.

---

Wen and I played cards every morning after tea, before her watch. Tien Gow — Heaven Nine — which she'd learned from her grandfather and taught me my first week aboard with a patience I mistook, at the time, for friendliness. It was not friendliness. Or it was, but it was also evaluation. She told me later that you could tell everything about a person by whether they tried to win on the first hand or waited to understand the game. I asked what I had done. She said I had spent four hands studying her discards before playing a single tile aggressively. She said this was why she kept me.

By the third day of the storm we had lost the forward bilge pump and the aft one was cycling brown water that smelled of diesel and rot. The hold was taking a foot an hour. Wen had sent the distress call the night before — she was not suicidal, whatever the inquiry later implied; she called, she activated the EPIRB, she did what you do — but the sea state was beyond helicopter range and the nearest vessel was eleven hours east and not making good time in the swell. These were facts. Wen received them the way she received most facts, which was with a single nod and a recalculation.

That morning she came below as she always did and sat across from me at the galley table. The table was bolted to the deck but everything on it had long since been secured or surrendered to gravity. I had the tiles in a net bag cinched to a carabiner on my belt because otherwise they'd have scattered into the bilge. I spread them on the table between us. The ship groaned.

I should describe the sound. People say a ship groans and you think of wood, of old movies, of something almost musical. This was not that. This was the sound of steel plate flexing past its yield point, a deep harmonic shudder that you felt in your molars and your sternum before you heard it with your ears. It came in waves, synchronized not with the ocean swell but with some interior rhythm of the ship's own structural failure, as though the *Mara Celeste* were breathing and each breath cost her something she could not replace. Wen called it the death song. She said her grandfather had described it to her once — he had heard it on another vessel, years before she was born, and he told her that when a ship sings that song you do not have hours, you have the time the ship decides to give you, and you do not argue with what the ship decides.

Wen played the opening pair. Red sixes.

I remember the water on the table. Not a puddle — a film, a constant thin sheet that seeped from somewhere and crossed the surface in shifting patterns as the ship rolled. The tiles sat in it. Our fingers were wrinkled and white. Wen laid her tiles with the same deliberateness she always used, squaring them against the table's edge, and when the ship heeled and they slid she caught them and set them back and continued.

I should tell you about the lashing. At some point during the second day — I don't know the exact hour because the clock in the wheelhouse had shattered when a wave stove in the port window — Wen had tied herself to the helm station with a cargo strap, a ratcheted nylon strap rated to four thousand pounds, looped under her arms and around her chest and through the steel frame of the wheel mount. She did this the way a person puts on a coat. I came up to bring her dinner, which was cold rice and tinned mackerel because the stove situation was what it was, and she was already strapped in, adjusting the tension with one hand while she read the barometer with the other. I set the plate on the console. She said thank you. I went back below. I mention it because I am supposed to mention it — because it is the detail that the newspapers found, later, when they wrote about her, and it is the detail that makes her sound like something from a painting, some Lord Byron fantasy of the captain lashed to the mast. She was not a painting. She was a woman who knew the next wave might put her through the window and had solved the problem with the nearest available strap. It took her about fifteen seconds. She did not look heroic. She looked like someone who was busy.

We played three hands that last morning. She won two. I won one, and I believe she let me have it, though I could never prove this and she would have denied it with the particular flat expression she reserved for accusations she considered beneath response. The ship sang around us the entire time, that deep steel hymn, and the water on the table was deeper now — not a film but a pool, our tiles half-submerged, and we played them by feel as much as sight because the emergency lighting had dimmed to a brown flicker and the porthole showed nothing but white spray and the absence of sky.

She did not say: we are sinking. I did not say: I know.

She set her last tile down — a nine of myriads — and squared it against the table edge with her forefinger and looked at me across the flooded table and I looked at her and the ship groaned a sound I had never heard, lower than before, a sound like a door closing in a cathedral, and neither of us looked away. We sat with the tiles between us in their little sea.

Then she said, "Again tomorrow?"

And I said, "Six o'clock."

She went up to the wheelhouse. I heard the ratchet click of the cargo strap.

---

What I need to tell you is about her face. Not in the storm. Not at the wheel. At the end, which came not all at once but as a slow refusal of buoyancy — the bow going under first, and then the long shudder as the stern rose and the sea came in not through any single breach but through everything, through every seam and weld and rivet hole, as though the *Mara Celeste* had simply decided to let it in, the way you open a door for someone you've been expecting.

I was on the deck. I don't know why I was on the deck. I think because below was already sea and because the deck, for another few minutes, was still air, and because Wen was at the helm and I wanted to be where I could see her. The water was at my waist when I looked up through the broken wheelhouse window and saw her face.

She was looking out. Not at me. Not at the ship. At the horizon, which by then was not a line but a collapsing distinction — the water and the sky enacting their own foreclosure, the world repossessing its own edges. She had stopped fighting the wheel. Her hands were still on it but loosely, the way you hold something you're about to hand to someone else.

And she smiled.

I need you to understand the precision of what I'm telling you. I had seen Wen's face every day for four years. I had seen it in gales and in port and in the blue light of the calculator when the numbers came back wrong. I had catalogued, without meaning to and without being able to stop, every variation of her expression the way a navigator catalogs headlands — by what they reveal about where you are and what water lies ahead. I knew her face the way I knew the galley, by use and by repetition and by the kind of attention that accrues not because you decide to pay it but because you cannot find the place in yourself where it shuts off.

She had not smiled since March. I can be that specific. The last time was March eleventh, a Tuesday, in port in San Juan, when a boy on the quay threw a fish back into the harbor with a two-handed heave and a shout and it caught the light and she looked up from the bank letter she was holding and smiled at the arc of it — the fish, silver, catching sun, going home. March eleventh. After that, the second mortgage recalculated and the insurance tripled and David Lau flew to Bermuda for the first time and something in her face closed like a companionway hatch and did not reopen. I watched for it. Every morning over tiles, every evening over figures. It did not come back.

What I saw through the wheelhouse window in the last moments of the *Mara Celeste* was the same smile she had given that fish. Not a grimace. Not resignation. The sudden, unguarded, self-forgetting expression of a woman watching something she loved being returned to where it belonged.

The water was at my chest. Her hands rested on the wheel. The ship sang its last low note and the horizon took us both — and I say *took* but the word is wrong, every word for drowning is a word about force and what I saw in her face was not force but the absence of it, the first absence of it in months. She was not being swallowed. She was setting something down.

I have been told that I was recovered four miles from the debris field, unconscious, clinging to a section of decking that the Coast Guard divers later identified as part of the galley table. I have no memory of this. I have no memory of the water closing over me or of the helicopter or of the man who pulled me out. I am told I said her name. I am told I said it several times. I have no memory of that either.

But I remember the ginger slice turning circles in a cup of tea. I remember the nine of myriads placed with two fingers on a flooded table. I remember that she smiled for the first time since the bank letters started arriving, and that the horizon was the thing that gave it back to her.

I would have stayed for the tea alone. I want you to know that. I would have stayed for the cards. I stayed for four years of mornings and I would have stayed for the rest of them, and the rest of them came due on the same day, and I was there, and she smiled, and the ship went down, and I cannot tell you whether what I felt in that last moment before the water came was grief or recognition and I have come to believe the answer is that these are the same thing.

Her name was Chen Wen-hui. She was the captain of the *Mara Celeste*. The bank took the ship. The ocean took the debt. And I — I was the cook. I made the tea. I played the cards. I saw her face.

That is the whole of what I have.
