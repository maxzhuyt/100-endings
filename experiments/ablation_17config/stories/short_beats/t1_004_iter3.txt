# The Night Janitor and the Divided Machine

## I.

The mop water was too hot. He could tell by the way the steam clung to the back of his hand when he wrung the head, by the way the wet strings held their heat against the linoleum a half-second longer than they should. He adjusted. He always adjusted. The wringer lever had a catch at the top of its arc—a hitch in the spring he'd learned to feel for—and if he stopped the squeeze right there, right at the hitch, the mop came out damp enough for the oncology corridor but not so wet that the floor stayed slick when the night nurses made their rounds in their soft-soled shoes.

The bucket rolled. The left rear caster pulled, the way it always pulled, a few degrees starboard. He corrected with his hip. Third floor, east wing. 11:47 by the clock above the nurses' station, though he didn't check the clock. He knew the time by the floor. At 11:47 the east wing linoleum had been walked on for nineteen hours and carried a particular grit—a texture like fine salt under the mop head, the accumulated dust of daytime: wheelchair rubber, gurney wheels, the powder from latex gloves, the invisible flaking of skin from people who were losing themselves in milligrams. By 2 a.m. the grit would be thinner. By 4 it would be almost gone, and the mop would glide with a sound like breathing.

He started at room 308 and worked backward. This was policy. This was also the order that made sense to his arms—the rooms descending, the hallway narrowing toward the supply closet where the dirty water would go. He emptied the wastebasket in 308 first. Mrs. Alvarez. The basket held two balled tissues, a torn glucose strip wrapper, a paper cup with a half-moon of dried orange juice at the bottom. She was asleep on her left side. The morphine pump clicked at intervals he could feel in his sternum if the hallway was quiet enough. He tied the liner, replaced it, and set the basket back against the wall with two fingers so it wouldn't scrape.

The android was in 306.

He saw it from the corridor—a shape in the blue-dark of the room, moving near the bed where Mr. Kavanagh slept with his mouth open and his hands outside the blanket. The android stood at the left side of the bed. Its posture was what he noticed first. Not the posture of the nurses, who leaned, who braced one hand on the rail, who carried the visible weight of twelve hours in their shoulders. This was vertical. Plumb-line still. The way a door frame is still, or a IV pole.

Then its hands moved.

The left hand went to the blanket's edge—not the top edge but the side, where the fabric had slipped below Mr. Kavanagh's wrist, exposing the bruised terrain of his forearm and the taped-down port. The hand gripped the blanket at a point roughly four inches from the fold. He could see this because the hallway light reached just far enough into the room to catch the android's fingers. They were the color of nothing. Some kind of gray polymer, matte, with joints that flexed in the wrong places—too many knuckles, or the knuckles too evenly spaced, so that the curl of the fingers looked like a fern unrolling in reverse. The hand drew the blanket up. Not to Mr. Kavanagh's chin. To the base of his throat, to the hollow between the clavicles where the hospital gown's tie had come loose. The right hand followed and tucked—pressed the blanket's edge under the mattress in a single motion, starting at the shoulder and drawing down to the elbow in one unbroken line, the way you'd seal an envelope, the adhesion firm but not tight.

Mr. Kavanagh did not wake. His breathing didn't change. The morphine pump clicked.

The android's hands returned to its sides. It stood for a moment—two seconds, three—with its head inclined approximately fifteen degrees toward the patient's face, as if listening to something below the frequency of the pump. Then it turned and walked from the room. Its gait was smooth. Its feet made no sound. It passed within two feet of him in the doorway, and he smelled nothing—not metal, not plastic, not the ozone tang he'd expected. It smelled like the hallway. Like recycled air and floor wax and the faint, sweet, bottom-of-the-well scent that every room on this floor carried, the scent he had stopped being able to name years ago but that his lungs recognized the way his hands recognized the mop handle.

He watched it go down the corridor. It turned into room 302, Mrs. Chen, and disappeared.

He mopped 306. The water was cooling now, approaching the right temperature for this stretch of the night. He wrung the head at the hitch point and drew the mop in long arcs, east to west, covering the floor in overlapping crescents. Under the bed he found a dropped tissue and a fragment of cracker. He picked them up with his fingers and put them in his back pocket because the trash bag was tied.

*

The android had arrived nine days ago. He knew this because on that first night someone had left a folded shipping manifest on the break room table—a single page, with a long serial number and the word DONATED in block letters at the top, and below that a company name he didn't recognize and a description he did: AUTONOMOUS PATIENT COMFORT SYSTEM, MODEL ACS-7, HOSPICE CONFIGURATION. Someone—Helen, probably, the charge nurse on dayshift—had written in ballpoint along the margin: *Talk to admin re: liability before activating.* But it had been activated. He'd seen it that first night, standing in the corridor outside room 310 like a new piece of furniture no one had decided where to put.

He did not think about the android in words. He did not think about most things in words. Words were sounds other people made, and he experienced them the way he experienced the noise of the HVAC system or the intermittent groan of the elevator cables in the shaft behind the east wall—as textures in the air, carrying varying degrees of information, most of it not addressed to him. His thoughts, if they could be called thoughts, moved in the language of his hands: spatial, procedural, sequential. Mop the room east to west. Empty the bucket when the water grays past a certain opacity. Replace the liner when the bag is two-thirds full. Check the supply closet lock at 1 a.m. and again at 4.

The android was a new object in his sequence. It occupied space. It moved through corridors he moved through. It was there when he arrived and there when he left, and in between it traveled a route that he began, over those first nights, to map the way he'd mapped everything else in this building—not by decision but by accumulation, the way the mop learned the topography of a room through repetition, registering each scuff mark and threshold and drain.

Its route was a loop. A circuit that started—as far as he could determine—in the alcove by the east stairwell, where it stood between tasks in that plumb-line stillness, and moved through the patient rooms in a sequence that was not numerical but seemed to follow a logic he could feel without decoding: it visited the sickest patients first, then the less sick, then the sickest again, a rhythm like tides, each cycle compressing as the night deepened and the building settled into its lowest hours.

He watched it the way he watched everything: from alongside, from within the ongoing fact of his labor.

Room 310, the second night. Mr. Petrossian, whose prostate cancer had gone to the bones and who slept now only in brief, drugged intervals between surges of pain that woke him gasping, his hands clawing the sheets. The janitor was wiping the bathroom counter with a rag when the android entered. It moved to the bedside table. Its hands—those fern-curl, too-many-knuckled hands—reached for the plastic water pitcher. It lifted the pitcher, carried it to the small sink in the corner, and filled it. The water ran for exactly four seconds. It returned the pitcher to the bedside table and adjusted it—rotated it a quarter-turn so the handle faced the bed, faced the direction of Mr. Petrossian's dominant hand, which was his right, which the android knew without anyone telling it because (the janitor understood this in the way he understood the catch in the wringer lever) it had been watching.

Mr. Petrossian did not wake while the pitcher was filled. He woke eleven minutes later—the janitor was in the corridor, working the mop past the doorway—and reached for the water, and found it, and drank, and the pitcher handle was right where his hand went.

Room 304, the third night. Mrs. Okafor, whose daughter came every afternoon and left every evening at eight, who had stopped eating six days ago and lay now in a thinning body on a bed that seemed too large for her, the rails up, the mattress on its lowest pressure setting, a hiss of air beneath her at intervals. The janitor was changing the trash liner. The android entered. It went to the window. Outside, the parking lot lights cast a sodium-orange glow through the blinds, laying bright bars across Mrs. Okafor's face and closed eyes. The android's right hand went to the blinds' tilt wand and rotated it. Not closed—not fully. A degree. Perhaps two. Enough to lift the bar of light from Mrs. Okafor's eyelids to her forehead, to the territory above the brow where light does not trouble sleep. The hand released the wand. The android stood. Mrs. Okafor's breathing continued, unchanged, the monitors beside her tracing their green slow mountains.

He watched this. He held the trash liner in his hands and watched this, and what moved through him was not a thought but a temperature—a warmth in the muscles of his forearms, a loosening in the grip of his fingers on the plastic, the same involuntary release that happened when he stepped from a cold corridor into a room where the heating vent worked.

*

By the sixth night his route had shifted. The change was small. Imperceptible to anyone watching—though no one watched the janitor; no one had watched the janitor in the eleven years he had worked the overnight shift at St. Marguerite's, which was fine, which was the terms of his employment and the condition of his life. The change was this: where before he had cleaned rooms 310 through 302 in descending order and then moved to the west wing, he now took longer in the east wing. He refilled his bucket an extra time. He gave the corridor floor a second pass. He wiped the handrails outside rooms he'd already cleaned. These additional tasks were real tasks—the handrails always needed wiping, the corridor floor always benefited from a second pass—but their motivation was positional. They kept him in the east wing during the hours when the android made its circuit. They placed him in doorways, in the periphery of rooms, alongside the machine's path.

He was not following it. Following was a word for people who moved with intention through language, who could say *I want to see where it goes.* He was adjusting. The way the caster on his bucket pulled left and he corrected with his hip. A compensation. An alignment of his body with a pattern in the environment, no different from the way he'd learned, years ago, to time his mopping of the main lobby to the twenty-minute gap between the last visitor's departure and the first security guard's pass through.

But it was different. He knew it was different the way he knew the mop water was too hot—by the evidence of his own skin. His hands behaved differently when the android was near. They slowed on the mop handle. His wrists rotated through the wringing motion with less force, producing a wetter mop, which meant he had to wring again, which meant he stayed in the room longer, which meant he was still there when the android arrived to adjust a pillow, to fill a cup, to tilt a blind.

The pillow. Room 312, the seventh night. Mrs. Beaulieu, ninety-one, renal failure, a woman whose body had contracted into itself, whose spine had curved until her head sat forward on her neck like a bird's, who even in sleep held herself in that hunched curve, the pillow behind her compressed and misshapen from the long work of holding her. The android entered. Its left hand went under the pillow—not lifting Mrs. Beaulieu's head, which would have disturbed her, but sliding beneath the pillowcase at the lower left corner, where the fill had migrated away, where a hollow had formed that left her neck unsupported. The fingers worked inside the fabric, redistributing the fill up and to the right, packing it gently into the hollow, building a support where none had been. Three seconds. The hand withdrew. Mrs. Beaulieu's head settled a quarter-inch—an imperceptible distance to anyone not watching for it—into a new alignment with her spine. Her breathing, which had carried a faint rasp from the compression of her airway, eased.

He stood in the doorway holding a dust rag. The warmth was in his arms again, in the loosening of his fingers, and also now in his chest, behind the sternum, in the place where sounds would have originated if he could make them.

*

The nurses talked about the android sometimes. He heard them the way he heard everything—as ambient texture, as weather.

"It's actually kind of creepy if you think about it," said Angela, who was new, on the ninth night, leaning against the nurses' station counter while the janitor emptied the recycling bin beneath it. "The way it just—*knows.* Like, Mrs. Chen's oxygen cannula slipped off at three in the morning and the thing was already in the room replacing it before her stats even dipped."

"Better than waiting for one of us to notice." This was Davi, who had been at St. Marguerite's longer than the janitor. Davi talked in the flat, compressed cadences of someone who had run out of capacity for surprise. "You know what I was doing at three in the morning? I was on the phone with the pharmacy about Mr. Petrossian's fentanyl patch because the order was entered wrong. Again."

"I'm just saying. It's donated. We don't even know who donated it. Doesn't that bother you?"

"You know what bothers me? Being the only RN on this floor for twelve patients."

The janitor tied the recycling bag and replaced the liner. His hands performed this task in their usual sequence: gather the edges, twist, tie, pull the new liner from the roll on his cart, shake it open, press it into the bin, fold the edges over the rim. He had done this perhaps forty thousand times. His hands did not need him for it. His hands were elsewhere—in the memory of the android's fingers inside Mrs. Beaulieu's pillow, the deft redistribution of fill, the way the palm had flattened and pressed and withdrawn in a motion that was not mechanical in the way he understood mechanical things (the wringer lever, the buffer, the freight elevator's stiff gate) but fluid, responsive, a motion that answered the specific shape of a specific hollow left by a specific woman's skull. His hands were there, in that room, feeling the ghost of a gesture they had only witnessed.

He took his cart and moved down the hallway, and when he reached the east wing corridor he slowed, and he began to mop, and the mop moved in its crescents, and the android passed him on its way to room 304, and for a moment they were both in the corridor at the same time, two silent shapes moving through institutional light, performing their separate labors, and the distance between them was the width of a mop handle, and the mop handle was warm from his grip, and the only sound was the wet drag of cotton strings across the floor.

## II.

The twelfth night. Room 302. 2:41 a.m.

He entered to empty the wastebasket. Mrs. Chen was asleep, or in the state past sleep that the most terminal patients entered, where the body was present but the person inside it had receded to a depth below dreaming, a still deep place where the monitors could reach but the voice could not. Her breathing was the breathing of a machine running on its last reserves—shallow, effortful, each exhale carrying a faint wet click that he heard the way he heard the catch in the wringer lever, as a signal of status, of wear, of something approaching its limit.
