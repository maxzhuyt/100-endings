# The HOA Board Will Now Come to Order

## I.

The eighteenth letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Meredith noted because Tuesdays were when the visiting nurse came to change Richard's catheter, and there was something in the synchronization—the mundane machinery of dying and the mundane machinery of bureaucratic harassment arriving on the same morning, through the same front door—that struck her as structurally perfect. She stood in the kitchen holding the envelope while, in the living room, the nurse whose name was Danielle or Daniela made small sounds of professional competence.

The return address read: SYCAMORE RIDGE HOMEOWNERS ASSOCIATION, INC. — COMPLIANCE DEPARTMENT. The word *department* had always bothered her. It implied a suite of offices, a staff, fluorescent lighting. In reality, the compliance department was Doug Whitmore's home printer and a book of stamps.

She opened it with a butter knife.

*Dear Homeowner,*

*This letter serves as formal notice that the unauthorized exterior modification to 414 Sycamore Ridge Lane—specifically, a wooden access ramp affixed to the front-facing elevation of the property—remains in violation of Section 4.7(b) of the Sycamore Ridge Community Covenants, Conditions & Restrictions ("CC&Rs"), which states: "No exterior modification, addition, or structural alteration visible from any street, common area, or adjacent property shall be undertaken without prior written approval of the Architectural Review Committee."*

*As of this date, accumulated fines total $850.00. Fines will continue to accrue at a rate of $50.00 per day until compliance is achieved.*

*Sincerely,*
*Douglas P. Whitmore*
*President, Sycamore Ridge HOA Board of Directors*

Meredith set it on the kitchen table next to the previous seventeen. They made an unimpressive pile. She had paid none of them. She would pay none of them. She experienced, briefly, the white flash of fury that she had learned, over the past several months, to allow to pass through her without acting on it—the way you might allow a truck to pass on a two-lane road, dangerous, enormous, gone.

The ramp. The ramp was pine, treated and stained, with a thirty-two-inch rise and a one-to-twelve slope because that was what the ADA recommended and because her son-in-law, Kevin, who was not a carpenter but who owned a miter saw and approached all tasks with the earnest overconfidence of a man who had watched many YouTube videos, had insisted on doing it right if he was doing it at all. He'd built it over a long weekend in April while Richard sat in the wheelchair in the garage, watching, saying things like "That's not level" and "You'll want to countersink those." Kevin, to his credit, countersank them. The ramp had a railing on both sides. The railing was not beautiful. It was functional. It allowed a seventy-four-year-old man with metastatic pancreatic cancer to leave his house without being carried.

The first letter had arrived eleven days after the ramp was completed.

Meredith made a cup of tea. She sat at the table where she and Richard had eaten approximately 14,600 meals together—she had done the math once, idly, the way she did all math, because her mind was a device built for the computation of frequency and probability and she had never learned to fully turn it off. She looked at the pile of letters. She looked at the kitchen window, through which she could see the edge of Mrs. Nakamura's yard and, beyond it, the community mailbox station where Doug Whitmore sometimes lingered to inspect the landscaping around its base.

Then she got up and went to the filing cabinet in the second bedroom that had been Richard's study and was now the room where the oxygen concentrator lived when it wasn't in the living room. She pulled the HOA's governing documents from the folder marked HOUSE — LEGAL — MISC. She had filed them there twenty-two years ago when they closed on the property. She had never read them.

She carried the documents back to the kitchen table.

Forty-seven pages. Bylaws, Articles of Incorporation, CC&Rs, and three amendments ratified over the years. The paper had yellowed slightly. The font was the particular species of Times New Roman that communicated both authority and absolute indifference to the human eye. She uncapped a red pen—the same brand she'd used for thirty-one years at the firm, the little act of continuity she allowed herself.

Meredith read the way she had always read actuarial tables: not for narrative, not for meaning, but for *structure.* Every document was a system. Every system had load-bearing elements and decorative ones, joints that were welded and joints that were glued, places where the architecture was sound and places where some long-ago drafter had papered over an inconsistency with a vague subordinate clause and hoped no one would notice. Her profession had been the mathematics of catastrophe—calculating the probability that the unlikely event would occur and pricing it before it did. She had been, for three decades, a woman paid to notice what nobody else thought worth noticing.

She noticed.

Article III, Section 2: the board of directors served staggered two-year terms, with elections held at the annual meeting of the membership. The annual meeting was required—*required*, not optional—to occur within sixty days of the fiscal year's end. The fiscal year ended June 30. The meeting had to happen by August 29.

Article V, Section 1: a quorum for the annual meeting was defined as twenty percent of the membership, and the membership was defined as all record owners of lots within Sycamore Ridge, as determined by county property records.

She underlined *county property records* twice.

Article V, Section 4: any member in good standing could introduce new business at the annual meeting, provided said business was submitted in writing to the board secretary no fewer than ten days before the meeting. But Article IX, Section 3—buried in an amendment ratified in 2009, almost certainly at Doug's initiative, almost certainly for some other purpose entirely—stated that the agenda could be amended by a two-thirds vote of the members *present.* This was a hole. This was the kind of hole that a drafter leaves when he's thinking about one problem and doesn't notice that his solution creates another.

She read the financial provisions. She read the enforcement provisions. She read the amendment provisions. She read the dissolution clause—she was not planning to dissolve the HOA, but she wanted to know the parameters. She was building, in her mind, a topographical map of the organization's rules, the way she had once built probability models of natural disasters: here is where the earthquake lives, here is where the flood comes in, here is where the tiny crack in the foundation will, in the event of the right kind of pressure applied at the right angle, bring the whole thing down.

Six weeks until the annual meeting. It was enough.

She picked up the phone.

---

Lydia Fontaine-Moss lived four streets over in a house whose garden had once been the neighborhood's most photographed and was now, by Lydia's own description, "feral by intention." She was sixty-seven, Jamaican-born, British-educated, sherry-voiced, and she had been a certified court stenographer in the state of Maryland for twenty-three years until an incident in 2019 that she referred to only as "the situation with Judge Borkowski." When pressed—and people pressed, because the gap between Lydia's obvious competence and her voluntary unemployment begged for explanation—she would say, "I transcribed what was said. That was the problem." She would say no more. She still owned her stenotype machine. She kept it in its case on the hall table the way another woman might keep flowers.

Meredith called her at four in the afternoon.

"Are you busy?"

"I'm reading a deeply terrible novel for Thursday."

"I have something better."

Lydia arrived at Meredith's kitchen in twenty minutes. She read the fine letters. She read the bylaws. She drank her tea in silence while Meredith explained. When Meredith finished, Lydia set her cup down and said, "You want me to transcribe the meeting."

"I want you to transcribe every word anyone says, in real time, so that if Doug Whitmore contradicts himself at 7:42 p.m. about something he said at 7:15, we can read it back to him before the sentence is cold."

Lydia's expression did not change, but something in her eyes rearranged itself, the way a hunting dog's body rearranges at a sound in the brush.

"I'll need to practice," Lydia said. "I haven't worked a live proceeding in five years."

"We have six weeks."

"I'll also need a power strip. The community center's outlets are hopeless."

---

Gerald Park she found at a city council meeting in Laurel. He attended city council meetings the way some men attended baseball games—not because he had business before any particular council, but because he loved the game. He was seventy, Korean American, a retired civil engineer who had discovered Robert's Rules of Order in his early forties and had experienced what he described, without irony, as a conversion. He owned two editions of Robert's Rules—the standard and the *In Brief*—and he treated the standard edition the way a constitutional scholar treats the Federalist Papers: as a living document whose depths repaid infinite study.

She had met Gerald three years ago at a neighborhood potluck where he'd spent forty-five minutes explaining to her, with unfeigned passion, why the motion to table was the most misunderstood weapon in parliamentary procedure. She had listened, because Meredith's particular genius was that she always listened to people who loved the thing they were talking about, even—especially—when the thing was arcane to the point of absurdity. She had filed Gerald away the way she filed everything: as data. As a possible asset whose application was not yet clear.

She drove to the city council meeting. She found Gerald in the third row, taking notes in a composition book. On the current page she could see a tally of procedural errors the council chair had committed. The count was at nine, and it was only 7:30.

She sat beside him. She waited for a recess. Then she said, "Gerald, I need someone who understands parliamentary procedure better than anyone in the room. Possibly better than anyone in the state."

Gerald closed his composition book.

"Tell me about the room," he said.

---

The book club met on Thursdays, which was why it was called the Thursday Club, because they had exhausted their collective tolerance for clever names in the early years and settled on plain description. There were five of them, not counting Meredith and Lydia, and they had been meeting for eleven years, and the books were by this point almost incidental—a substrate over which the real business of the group was conducted, which was the maintenance of friendship through the ritual of shared opinion.

Bev Tarkanian, seventy-eight, a former school librarian whose husband had died in 2017. The HOA had sent her a violation notice for the black ribbon she'd hung on her mailbox during mourning, citing Section 4.2(a): "Mailboxes shall be maintained in a uniform appearance consistent with the community standard." She had removed the ribbon. She had not forgiven it.

Connie Wu, sixty-three, an active real estate agent who understood property values the way Meredith understood probability. The HOA had placed a lien on her property over a $300 dispute about a fence that was, by Connie's meticulous measurement, one and three-quarter inches past the setback line. The lien had appeared on her title. She had discovered it when she tried to refinance.

Pat Mooney, sixty-one, the youngest, a labor and delivery nurse who worked nights and slept days and had once, in a year of particular stress, let her lawn go brown for three weeks in August and received a letter threatening legal action under Section 6.1: "All lots shall be maintained in a condition consistent with the aesthetic standards of the community."

Ruth Chen—no relation to Meredith, despite the shared surname and the shared grievance—sixty-eight, retired from the Social Security Administration, who had tried to attend an HOA board meeting in 2021 to ask a question about the reserve fund and had been told, by Doug Whitmore, smiling, that the comment period had ended, and that she was welcome to submit her question in writing for consideration at a future meeting. She had submitted it. She had never received an answer.

And Dolores Fitch, seventy-two, an artist who made stained glass panels and who had placed one in her front window—a heron, blue and green, quite beautiful—and received a violation notice under Section 4.9: "No signage, artwork, or decorative displays visible from the exterior shall be placed in windows facing street-accessible elevations." Dolores had removed the heron. She had replaced it with a plain white curtain. She kept the heron in her garage. She sometimes mentioned it.

Meredith convened a special meeting of the Thursday Club on a Friday, which was itself a minor act of subversion. She served the good crackers. She waited until everyone had wine. Then she placed the HOA's governing documents on the coffee table alongside her annotated notes and the eighteen violation letters and said:

"I'm going to take the board."

She watched them process this. Bev leaned forward. Pat set down her glass. Dolores, who was seated on the floor because she preferred it, drew her knees up.

"How?" said Connie, because Connie always asked how.

"By the rules."

Meredith outlined it. The meeting. The quorum challenge. The procedural architecture Gerald was building. Lydia's transcription. The proxies they would need. The new business provisions. She laid each piece out the way she had once laid out risk assessments for insurance underwriting committees: clean, sequential, unassailable.

"What do you need from us?" said Ruth.

"Feet. I need you in the neighborhood, knocking on doors, talking to people. I need to know who's angry and about what. I need proxies from everyone who won't come in person. And I need anyone who *will* come in person to show up on August 28th and sit in a chair and vote."

"What are we running on?" said Pat.

Meredith paused. She had thought about this. She had rejected *fairness* as too abstract and *accountability* as too aggressive and *change* as too vague.

"Governance," she said. "We're running on actual governance. On the idea that the rules should be followed—*all* the rules, including the ones that protect residents from the board—and that the finances should be transparent and the meetings should be open and that no one should be fined fifty dollars a day for building their husband a wheelchair ramp."

The room was quiet. Dolores said, "I'm in."

They were all in. Meredith had known they would be. What she hadn't anticipated was the quality of their anger—not hot, not performative, but *settled,* like sediment at the bottom of a river, like something that had been waiting to be disturbed.

She did not say revenge.

She did not need to.

## II.

For the next five weeks, Meredith ran the operation from her kitchen table while Richard slept in the living room. The hospital bed faced the front window because Richard had asked for it to face the front window. He liked to see the street. He liked to see the mailman and the dog walkers and the kids on bicycles. Some mornings, when the pain was managed and the morphine had not yet thickened his thinking, he would narrate what he saw—"Mrs. Nakamura's out early, she's deadheading the roses, she's got the big gloves on"—and Meredith, passing through with her coffee, would stop and listen, because these narrations were a kind of proof that he was still here, still inside the day, still tethered to the world by the ordinary thread of noticing it.

He knew what she was doing. She had told him. He had said, "Don't bring me into it."

"You're already in it. You're the ramp."

"The ramp is the ramp. I'm the man in the bed. Don't use me, Mer."

She had not responded because he was right and she was going to do it anyway—not use him, exactly, but not pretend, either, that the ramp existed for any reason other than the man who needed it. There was a dishonesty she was unwilling to commit, and it was the dishonesty of abstracting Richard's illness into a cause. He was not a cause. He was her husband. He was dying. The ramp let him leave the house. Doug Whitmore wanted her to remove it. Everything else followed from this.
