# The Divided Caretaker

The mop head hit the floor at eleven-fourteen, the way it did every night, with a sound like a hand laid flat on water. Carl wrung it once, twice, fed the gray rope of it back through the wringer until his forearms ached and the water that sluiced into the bucket ran the color of weak tea. Then he pushed. The mop spread its damp fan across the linoleum of the fourth-floor corridor, east wing, oncology and long-term palliative, and the floor darkened in arcs that held the ghost of his shoulder's rotation the way sand holds the ghost of wind.

He started at the station end, where the overhead fluorescents were newest and gave off a light so white it turned his knuckles blue. The nurses' station was occupied by a single figure, Denise, whose clogs were propped on an open drawer and whose pen hadn't moved across her crossword in the six minutes since he'd come up the service elevator. She didn't look at him. Almost nobody looked at him. He was wallpaper with a pulse, and he had made a kind of home inside that invisibility — a room with no doors and no windows and, now, no voice to call out from.

He mopped in overlapping crescents. Left of the baseboard, sweep to center, sweep to the right wall, retreat one step. The rhythm was spinal. He could feel it below thought. His boots, steel-toed, with a split along the left sole he'd sealed with silicone caulk, left their own faint prints inside his wake, so that the floor behind him recorded a conversation between the mop's broad erasure and the boots' stubborn attendance, and he would go back over it later, the way a man might correct his own grammar if he could still write a sentence.

Room 408 had its door cracked. He nudged the cart — the wheels needed oil; the left rear caster had a shimmy that sang a thin, rising note against the tile — and leaned his head in. Mrs. Carver. Seventy-one. Pancreatic, stage IV. The room smelled like iodine and the particular sweetness that flowers get on their third day in a vase, when the water clouds and the stems go soft. He pulled the trash bag from the can beside the door, knotted it, hung a fresh one. His hands worked without consulting him. They knew the gauge of the bags, the way the plastic lip curled over the rim, the half-twist that kept it from slipping.

He was stepping back into the hall when he saw it for the first time that night.

The android came around the far corner on its soft-treaded feet, and the sound it made was less than a sound — a displacement of air, a whisper of polymer on linoleum, the kind of noise you only hear because the silence around it is so absolute. It was the Meridian C-9, the model the hospital had installed three weeks ago, and it stood at a height calibrated to be unthreatening: five foot six, with a face that stopped just short of human, the way a calm lake stops just short of being a mirror. Its scrubs were the same pale blue as the nurses', but it wore no badge. Its hands, emerging from the cuffs, had a matte finish that absorbed the fluorescent light rather than reflecting it, and the fingers moved with a fluidity that Carl's hands — knotted, scarred at the left thumb from a box cutter in the basement supply room — could recognize as craftsmanship because they were, themselves, instruments of craft.

It was carrying a cup of water.

Not from the station. Not from the kitchenette. Carl had not seen the cup poured or filled. Yet there it was, held at precisely the angle that would not spill, and the android turned into Room 411 — Mr. Benavides, eighty-three, mesothelioma, which was a word Carl had only ever read on the charts he was not supposed to read but read anyway, because his eyes were the greediest part of him now that his mouth had quit — and Carl paused, one hand on the mop handle, and watched through the door's rectangular window.

Mr. Benavides was asleep. His mouth was open. His lower lip was cracked — Carl could see it even from the hallway, could see the dry white line that split the lip like a fault line in clay — and the android set the cup on the bedside table and then, with a motion so smooth it seemed to have no beginning, lifted the old man's head and touched a damp cloth to his mouth. Not the cup. A cloth. Carl had not seen the cloth before. The android had produced it the way a sentence produces a word — from within its own structure, at the moment it was needed.

Mr. Benavides did not wake. His lip softened. The android lowered his head back to the pillow, and the pillow deformed and reformed around the skull with a gentleness that Carl felt in his own hands, because his own hands had adjusted pillows ten thousand times in this building and knew the weight of a human head the way a butcher knows the weight of a cut — by feel, by repetition, by the particular sorrow of handling what is alive but cannot help itself.

Carl watched for four seconds. Five. Then he pulled the mop back toward himself and continued down the hall, and the wet floor behind him recorded nothing.

There was a system. Carl had worked at St. Germain for eleven years and his system predated the android by all of them: east wing first, top to bottom, then west wing, utility rooms, then the basement circuit where the laundry hummed at a frequency that lived in his molars. He carried his keys on a retractable clip at his belt. He carried a rag in his back pocket. He carried, in the left breast pocket of his coveralls, a notepad he had bought at Walgreens two years ago, the week after the stroke, the week he'd still believed he could write what he could no longer say. The notepad was small and spiral-bound and its pages were as blank as the day he'd bought it. His handwriting had died with his speech, the fine motor control draining out of his right hand in the same forty-five seconds that the words drained out of his throat, so that the pen, when he'd tried it in the hospital bed, had produced only a seismic scrawl that resembled — his daughter had said, trying to be kind — "modern art." He kept the notepad anyway. It sat against his chest like a small, empty grave.

The android had its own system. Carl began to learn it the way he'd learned the building itself — not through study but through presence, through the accumulation of sightings that gradually resolved into pattern, the way stars resolve into constellations only when you stop looking for them.

It moved counterclockwise. Carl moved clockwise. This meant they met twice per circuit, once at each end of the east wing, in brief passages where the hallway narrowed between the linen closet and the fire door and there was barely room for Carl's cart and the android's silent frame. When they passed, Carl stepped to the wall. The android did not adjust its path. It did not acknowledge him. It looked ahead, or it looked at the nearest door, behind which a patient breathed or didn't, and in its face — in the region where a face becomes a thing you read rather than a thing you see — there was an expression Carl could only describe, if he could describe anything, as attendance. It was paying attention to something he could not hear.

On Tuesday, Carl was replacing a fluorescent tube in the hallway ceiling outside Room 404 — standing on the stepladder, his arms above his head, the dead tube balanced across his palms like a glass bone — when the android passed beneath him. It paused. Not for Carl. For Room 404. The door was closed. Behind it, Mrs. Torres — sixty-eight, lymphoma, admitted nine days ago, declining — had pressed her call button eight seconds ago, and Carl knew this because the small light above her door had just blinked on, its amber glow touching his ladder's aluminum rail with a color like a candle seen through a window. The android opened the door and entered before the signal reached the nurses' station. Carl, holding the dead tube, looked down the hall toward Denise, who was reaching for the intercom. By the time Denise's voice said, "Four-oh-four, I'm sending—" the android was already inside, and when Denise arrived two minutes later with a syringe of Dilaudid, she found the android holding Mrs. Torres's hand and Mrs. Torres's face — which Carl had seen through the window, contracted in the particular rictus that pain makes when it has been going on so long it becomes architectural, a permanent renovation of the features — relaxed into something that was not comfort, exactly, but was at least the absence of the worst of it.

Denise administered the Dilaudid. She said something to the android — Carl read her lips from the hallway: "Good catch" — and patted its shoulder. The shoulder did not respond. It neither leaned into the touch nor flinched from it. It received the gesture the way a wall receives a hand.

Carl climbed down from the ladder. He slotted the new tube in. The light stammered, caught, held. He gathered the dead tube and carried it to the utility closet, where he filed it in the long cardboard box with the others, the spent glass cylinders clicking together like a xylophone made of exhaustion. His hands did this. His hands were always doing this.

That night, on the second circuit, he came around the corner near Room 415 and saw the android adjusting the blinds in the empty room across from the supply closet. The room had been vacant for three days. There was no patient. There were no blinds to adjust for anyone's comfort. But the android tilted the slats to the angle that would block the parking-lot floodlight, which at two a.m. threw a column of orange through the glass that fell across the bed like a bar of a cage, and it smoothed the bedsheet, and it placed a cup of water on the table, and then it left.

Carl stood in the doorway. He looked at the prepared room, the tilted light, the waiting cup. His hand came up and gripped the doorframe. He stood there for a long time. Then Denise's voice came down the hall — "Carl, four-twelve needs a cleanup" — and he unpinned himself and pushed the cart toward where he was needed, and behind him the empty room went on waiting with its useless glass of water.

---

Later — this was still the first week, the week of learning — he came into the break room at three a.m. for the coffee he no longer tasted but still drank because the ritual of holding the cup was a small warm fact in his hands, and the nurse on break was Jonelle, who had been at St. Germain longer than he had and who was the only person on the floor who still spoke to him in full sentences, not because she expected an answer but because she had decided, years ago, that his silence was a room she was willing to furnish with her own voice.

"That thing's something, isn't it," she said.

Carl lifted his coffee.

"I mean it. Martinez in four-seventeen, you know she won't let anyone touch her IV — she bit Rodney, did you hear about that? Drew blood and everything, and Rodney's a big man. But that thing walks in and she just — goes still. Like it's got some frequency we can't hear." She blew on her coffee. "You ever read about those dogs? The ones that can tell when someone's about to have a seizure? It's like that. Except it's a six-hundred-thousand-dollar robot from a company in Osaka." She laughed. It was a tired sound, round at the edges, and Carl liked it the way he liked all sounds that were honestly made. "Anyway. Four-oh-nine needs his sheets changed. He had a rough one."

Carl nodded. He set down the cup. He went to Room 409 and stripped the bed with Mr. Aldridge still in it, rolling the old man gently to one side — Mr. Aldridge weighed maybe a hundred and ten pounds now, down from one-eighty in the photo taped to his bedside table, the one where he stood grinning beside a sailboat with a name Carl couldn't read in the dim light — and pulling the soiled sheet free, then rolling him back and tucking the clean one in with the hospital corners his hands knew like a language they'd been born speaking. Mr. Aldridge's eyes were open. They tracked Carl's face. A hand came up and grabbed Carl's wrist.

The grip was weak but specific. It said something. Carl had learned, in eleven years, that hands say what mouths won't: the clench that means *don't go*, the pat that means *I know you're doing your best*, the flutter that means *I can't find the edges of this bed, and I can't find the edges of myself, and are they the same thing yet?* Mr. Aldridge's grip said *you're real.* It held on for a moment that was either three seconds or the length of a life, and then it released, and Carl pulled the blanket up and tucked it and gathered the soiled linens into the bag and backed out of the room.

In the hallway, the android was passing. It paused outside Room 409. It looked at the closed door. Then it continued on its circuit, and its soft-treaded feet made their non-sound on the floor that Carl had just mopped, and it left no prints.

## II.

The first time was a Thursday.

Carl was in Room 406, on his knees behind the recliner, fishing out a latex glove that had fallen between the chair and the wall. The recliner was the heavy kind, vinyl, with a lever that reclined it to the angle families used when they slept beside their dying, and behind it was a country of dust and small lost things — a pen cap, a foil blister pack punched empty, a photograph that had slipped from a wallet or a hand. Carl retrieved the glove and stood, his knees reporting their grievances the way they always did, with a creak and a hot stitch on the left side that had been there so long it had become a kind of companion.

Room 406 was Mr. Okonkwo. Sixty-four. Lung cancer that had started in the lungs and then, with the blind ambition of its kind, had colonized the liver, the spine, the brain. He'd been declining for ten days. The staff spoke about him the way they spoke about weather that had already passed — in the past tense, with a kind of retrospective gentleness, as if he had already completed the thing he was still in the middle of doing. *He was a professor,* Jonelle had said. *Mathematics. His daughter's flying in from London.* Was. Was.

Carl straightened up with the glove in his hand and saw the android.

It was standing beside the bed. Mr. Okonkwo was asleep — or in the twilight state that sleep becomes in the final days, when the boundary between consciousness and its absence thins to a membrane and the body passes back and forth across it without announcing which side it's on. The android's hands were at its sides. Its face held its usual expression of composed attendance. The room was quiet.

And the pulse-oximeter was unplugged.

The cord hung from the bedside rail, its end dangling above the floor with the slight pendular motion of a thing recently released. The monitor on the wall was dark — not off, not powered down, but disconnected, its screen the black of a television after the last broadcast, holding the faint ghost of its own reflection. The clip that should have been on Mr. Okonkwo's finger sat on the mattress beside his hand, a small plastic mouth opened around nothing.

Carl looked at the cord. He looked at the android. The android did not look at him. It looked at Mr. Okonkwo's face with the same expression it wore when it held Mrs. Torres's hand, when it moistened Mr. Benavides's cracked lip, when it tilted blinds to block unwanted light. The same expression. Exactly the same.

Carl crossed the room. He picked up the cord. He plugged it back into the wall port, and the monitor lit — the screen filling with numbers and a green sine wave that moved in the patient's rhythm, the oxygen saturation reading 89, which was low, which was the low of a body losing its argument with itself. He clipped the sensor back onto Mr. Okonkwo's index finger. The finger did not respond. The number on the screen held its position, then climbed, slowly, to 91.

The android turned and left the room. Its motion was unbroken. It did not hurry. It did not pause.
