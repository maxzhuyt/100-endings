# The Well of All Knowing

## I.

She was, at this very moment, inking a coastline that did not exist.

We should say at the outset that we are fond of Pell. This will become important later, and possibly suspicious, but for now let it serve as a simple disclosure: we have watched her for some time, and we are fond of her in the particular way one is fond of someone who has not yet become what they are going to become. The way one is fond of a match before it is struck.

She sat cross-legged on the floor of the Cartographers' Hall in Lesser Brunt, a city notable only for its ambitious name and a persistent smell of boiled cabbage that no civic improvement had managed to defeat. Around her, senior cartographers labored at tilted desks, rendering in meticulous detail the coastlines and river systems of the Known Continent — which was, in Pell's estimation, the less interesting one. She had commandeered a patch of flagstone near the eastern windows and spread across it a map roughly six feet by four, its edges pinned with half-eaten apples and the fossilized remains of a cheese sandwich she had forgotten three days ago and which was now performing a valuable structural role.

The map was of the Unmapped Continent. This was, her superiors had pointed out with varying degrees of exasperation, a contradiction in terms.

Pell did not find it contradictory at all. The Unmapped Continent was a real place. Ships had sighted its coast. Expeditions had landed and returned with sketches, samples, and the kind of haunted expressions that suggested the interior was eventful. The fact that no one had comprehensively mapped it was not, to her mind, an argument against mapping it. It was an *invitation*. An entire landmass sitting there, unnamed, uncategorized, its mountains and rivers and forests existing in the profoundly offensive condition of being *un-described* — it was practically a personal insult.

So she mapped it. Speculatively. Inferentially. Some would say fictionally, but Pell drew a sharp distinction between making things up and *extrapolating from available data with optimistic interpolation,* and if the distinction was invisible to everyone else, that was their limitation, not hers.

She was, at this moment, adding a peninsula she had deduced must exist based on ocean current patterns, prevailing wind data, and an illustration she had found in a bestiary that depicted a species of flightless cormorant standing on a rocky promontory. Where there were rocky promontories, there were peninsulas. Where there were peninsulas, there were coves. Where there were coves — she was getting ahead of herself. She labeled the peninsula *Cape of Reasonable Assumption* and moved on.

Her map was dense with such labels. The interior, which she had never seen and about which reliable intelligence was essentially nonexistent, was the most thoroughly annotated section. A mountain range she had postulated ran north-south based on the logical necessity of a rain shadow was labeled *The Inferentials.* A river system she had constructed to explain why the eastern coast was reportedly lush while the western coast was arid bore the name *The River Mostly Certain.* A vast forest was labeled *Presumably Interesting.* A blank zone in the deep interior where her extrapolations failed and the bestiary provided nothing more useful than an illustration of something with too many legs bore a tiny, neat annotation: *Worth Seeing.*

Not *Here Be Dragons.* Not *Terra Incognita.* Not even an honest blank.

*Worth Seeing.*

We mention this because it reveals something about Pell that she had not yet revealed to herself, though we could see it plainly, the way one sees a watermark when one holds a page up to the light. She did not label unknown territory with warnings because she could not conceive of the unknown as dangerous. She conceived of it as *withholding.* The unmapped places were not threatening. They were *rude.* They had information — about themselves, about their features, about what lived there and what the living things did when no one was looking — and they were not sharing it. Her entire cartographic philosophy was, at its root, a response to the unbearable fact that the world contained things she did not know, and some of those things were not even *trying* to be known.

She did not think of it this way. She thought of it as scholarly enthusiasm.

We are not yet ready to say who is correct, Pell or us. We are merely noting the discrepancy and moving on, with the confidence of someone who already knows how this ends.

Her gear was packed. It sat against the far wall in a rucksack that was, by any reasonable assessment, too large for her frame and too stuffed for its seams — it looked less like a piece of expedition equipment than like a leather-bound animal that had swallowed something ambitious. Inside it: seventeen blank mapping sheets, rolled and waxed against moisture. A portable drafting kit of her own design, featuring compasses, straightedges, and an inkwell mounted on a gimbal that she had invented to prevent spillage during rough terrain and which she had not yet successfully tested on any terrain at all, rough or otherwise. Three changes of clothing, two of which were identical because she had strong feelings about consistency. A copy of Aldermach's *Principia Geographica,* which was the standard reference text and which she had annotated so heavily in the margins that it now contained more Pell than Aldermach. A small leather journal, blank, which she intended to fill with observations. And a single dried apricot, which she was saving.

She was ready. She had been ready for three weeks, during which time the departure had been delayed by funding disputes, permit negotiations, and the discovery that the expedition's originally appointed leader had a gambling debt of such magnificence that three separate creditors had placed liens on the journey itself, leading to a complicated legal proceeding in which a judge had been required to rule on whether a hypothetical future route through an unmapped continent could be considered a seizable asset. (It could not. But the judge had taken his time reaching this conclusion, and the delay had gnawed at Pell like a rat in a bread box.)

Today, though. Today the expedition was departing. Her companions were assembled — or assembling, or at least aware that assembly was expected of them — at the western docks, where a ship called the *Credulous* waited to carry them across the Intermediate Sea to the Unmapped Continent's nearest shore.

Pell capped her ink, examined her peninsula one last time, and — we saw this, though she didn't know anyone was watching — bent down and blew gently on the wet coastline, the way one blows on a child's scraped knee: tender, focused, as if the breath itself could help it set.

Then she rolled the map, slid it into a leather tube, and added it to the rucksack. The rucksack groaned but held.

She was twenty-three years old. She was five feet and four inches tall in her expedition boots, which had soles she had paid extra to have made thick. She had a face that could only be described as *interested* — not beautiful, not plain, just permanently arranged in an expression of expectant curiosity, as if the world owed her an explanation and she was generously giving it time to prepare one. Her hair was the color of the cheaper kind of amber and she kept it short because it had once gotten caught in a cartographic press, an incident she did not discuss and we will not elaborate on except to say it explained certain things about her relationship with machinery.

She hoisted the rucksack with a sound that was half grunt, half laugh — she was always laughing at the edges of her own physical absurdity, the smallness of her body relative to the largeness of her ambitions — and walked out of the Cartographers' Hall into the midmorning light of Lesser Brunt.

She was thinking, as she walked, about how it would feel to stand on a shore that no one had described and to be the first person to put it into language. She was imagining the weight of her pen on the paper, the moment when the line she drew would *become* the coast — not depict it, but become the version of it that everyone else would know. She was imagining being the one who told the world what was there.

She was imagining it so vividly that she did not notice the way the light caught her face as she crossed the threshold, or the way two of the senior cartographers looked up from their desks to watch her go, or what one of them murmured to the other as the door swung shut — something we could share, since we heard it, but we'll keep for now.

Some things are better saved.

She reached the docks in fourteen minutes, walking fast, the rucksack bouncing against her spine with each step like an enthusiastic co-conspirator. The *Credulous* was there: a mid-sized expedition vessel with clean lines and a name painted on the hull in letters that managed to be both hopeful and slightly apologetic, as if the ship itself knew that credulity was not the most reassuring quality in a vessel intended to cross open water.

Her companions were there too, in various states of readiness.

There was Aldric, who was going to be the expedition's protector and who looked exactly like you are imagining: tall, broad, carrying a sword with the unselfconscious ease of someone who has been told his entire life that he was made to carry swords and has not found a reason to interrogate this. He had a jaw that looked as if it had been designed by a committee with strong opinions about masculinity, and he was leaning against a dock post with his arms crossed, staring out to sea in a way that could be read as either brooding or seasick. Pell had met him twice during the planning stages and had formed two confidently contradictory impressions: that he was a deeply serious man of principle, and that he was performing a deeply serious man of principle so convincingly that the difference was either meaningless or everything.

There was Maren, the herbalist, who had been recruited for her knowledge of the Unmapped Continent's reportedly exotic and sometimes aggressive flora. She was sitting on a crate, doing something precise and unhurried with a small mortar and pestle, grinding a substance that smelled of pine and something darker. She was older than the rest — late forties, weathered in the way of someone who had spent decades in forests and fields and had developed the kind of relationship with the natural world where respect and wariness were indistinguishable. She had looked at Pell's map during the planning meeting and said, simply, "That river doesn't go there," and when Pell had asked how she knew, Maren had said, "Water wouldn't do that," and had offered no further explanation, and Pell had not moved the river.

And there was Luccan, who was — there was no way to say this that wasn't reductive, but we'll say it anyway and trust you to hold the complexity — the bard. He had *elected* himself the expedition's chronicler, a role no one had advertised and which he had invented by the simple method of showing up at the planning meetings with a lute and a leather-bound composition book and announcing that every great quest required a great record and that it was fortunate, as these things went, that one of the finest narrative poets of his generation happened to be available. The expedition's funders, who were practical men interested in trade routes and mineral surveys, had stared at him. The expedition's leader — not Pell; there was a nominal leader, a man named Drest who was currently belowdecks doing something administrative — had shrugged and said the ship had room. Luccan had been, since that moment, composing. He composed constantly. He composed the way other people breathed: involuntarily, compulsively, with occasional pauses that were noticeable only because they were so rare. He was composing right now, she could see — sitting on the dock's edge with his legs dangling, his composition book open, his lips moving as he tested syllables against his teeth.

She was thinking, as she looked at them — and we tell you this not to intrude but because it is the kind of thought that reveals precisely what we have been trying to say about Pell — she was thinking: *I wonder what they're really like.*

She was standing ten feet away from them. She could have asked.

"Morning!" she called instead, and they turned, and their faces did the things that faces do — recalibration, greeting, the quick flickering assessment of someone else's mood and readiness — and Pell watched all of it closely, hungrily, as if their expressions were coastlines she could map if only she looked hard enough.

"Pell," said Aldric, nodding.

"You're late," said Maren, not looking up from her grinding.

"I'm exactly on time," said Pell.

"By your clock. By mine you're late."

"Then your clock is wrong."

"My clock is a forty-year understanding of the medicinal properties of vetch root, which must be processed within a specific window, and that window does not care about your clock."

This was the most words Maren had ever addressed to Pell at once. Pell was delighted.

Luccan looked up from his composition book, pen in hand, eyes bright with the particular avidity of someone who has just witnessed a minor interaction and is already converting it into couplets. "I'm putting that in," he said. "The bit about the clocks. It's good. It's — *Your clock is wrong, she said, but Time / is vetch-root and the grinding rhyme* — no. No. Hold on." He scratched something out, muttered, wrote again.

Pell watched him revise. She watched the way his pen moved — fast, then slow, then fast, crossing out, rewriting, his lips reshaping each word before the ink committed it. She wanted very badly to read what he'd written. Not because she cared about poetry, but because the composition book contained, she was certain, a version of all of them — of this moment, of this departure — and she wanted to see it. She wanted to know how they looked from inside his particular skull.

She caught herself leaning forward and straightened up.

"Right," she said. "Let's go find a continent."

Drest emerged from belowdecks at that point and said efficient things about tide tables and wind patterns and the importance of securing loose cargo, and the departure happened with the orderly messiness that departures always have — ropes cast off at the wrong moment and then the right one, the dock sliding away with the slow surprise of a thing that has been solid suddenly ceasing to be relied upon, the water opening between ship and shore like a sentence that has begun but not yet committed to its meaning.

Pell stood at the stern rail and watched Lesser Brunt diminish. She had her mapping tube under one arm. She was already composing labels in her head for things she had not yet seen.

We watched her with great tenderness. We noted the way her fingers drummed the map tube — not nervously, but rhythmically, a pattern that matched no music, only the cadence of her own impatience.

We knew what was waiting across the water. We knew what would happen when she found it. We knew what she would hear and what it would cost and what she would finally, in the dark, be asked.

We knew all of this. And we let her stand there, in the salt wind, drumming her fingers, with her face aimed at the horizon like a compass needle — not because we were cruel, but because the knowing, in this case, was ours, and we were not quite ready to share it.

Some things are better saved.

## II.

The crossing took eleven days, during which the Intermediate Sea demonstrated the full range of its conversational abilities: three days of sullen calm, two of petulant chop, one of genuine fury during which Luccan threw up with creative regularity and then, pale and shaking in his hammock, revised his account of it into heroic couplets that transformed the experience into something significantly more dignified than it had been. We mention this because the revision was telling. Between the first draft (*Sick till I wished for death or land*) and the final version (*The sea-god tested us with his hand / And found us mortal, but not unmanned*), something essential had been lost — namely, the truth — and something beautiful had been gained — namely, a lie with excellent scansion.

But that is getting ahead of ourselves. We were talking about the crossing, and then we must talk about the landfall, and then we must talk about the journey, because these are the parts that Pell would want us to linger on. These are the parts she loved best. We will linger, then. There is time. The Well isn't going anywhere.

Landfall.

The Unmapped Continent's nearest coast resolved out of morning fog on the twelfth day: a long, dark line that Pell initially mistook for a cloud formation and then, with a sharp intake of breath that we felt as vividly as if she'd drawn it from our own lungs, recognized for what it was. She had her mapping sheets out within thirty seconds. She was sketching before the ship had dropped anchor.
