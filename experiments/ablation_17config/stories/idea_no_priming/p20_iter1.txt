# Summit Rules

## I.

Before she was buried, before the mountain ate her voice mid-syllable, Mara Vasquez was the most decorated relay alpinist in the Western Hemisphere, and perhaps you already knew that.

The numbers were public record. Six consecutive Cerro Ferro Ultra-Relay titles. Four route records, two of which she set in conditions that the Federación Argentina de Montañismo officially classified as "inadvisable" and that Mara's then-coach, the late Esteban Quiroga, privately classified as "stupid." A VO₂ max that a University of Colorado lab once measured at 74, re-measured at 76, and then measured a third time because a graduate student assumed the equipment was broken. It was not broken. Mara was simply operating at a metabolic level that suggested either extraordinary genetics or a clerical error by God.

The Cerro Ferro Ultra-Relay itself requires some explaining, though the explaining is part of the problem. The race occupied a peculiar niche in the ecology of extreme sport: too dangerous for mainstream coverage, too obscure for mainstream sponsorship, and yet irresistible to a small, committed population of athletes who regarded the Patagonian ice fields the way certain monks regard silence — as a medium for the revelation of character. Four-person teams. Thirty-one kilometers of mixed terrain across the Ferro massif, a geological formation that the Tehuelche people had named something that loosely translated to "the place that hates you back." Teams rotated lead position through five relay stages, each member required to take point for at least one leg. Technical ice. Exposed ridgelines. A summit push at 19,400 feet where the wind had been clocked at 130 kilometers per hour and the ambient temperature, on a calm day, could dissolve exposed skin in forty minutes. Seventeen teams had completed the relay in its twenty-two-year history. Three people had died. The race's official motto, printed on credentials and stamped into commemorative belt buckles, was *La montaña no negocia* — the mountain does not negotiate.

Mara negotiated anyway, and she won.

Her face was on the side of a Petzl headlamp box. Her silhouette — recognizable even in outline, that forward-pitched stance, the left arm slightly cocked like a broken wing from a shoulder surgery in 2016 — had been used, without meaningful compensation, by at least two Patagonian guiding companies as an unofficial logo. When she walked through El Chaltén in the week before race registration, restaurant owners comped her meals. Other climbers stepped aside on trails with a deference that had a nearly ecclesiastical quality, as though she were carrying something fragile and sanctified that they did not want to jostle. A route on the north face of the massif's secondary peak bore her name: the *Vasquez Direct*, a Grade V ice line that she had first-ascented at twenty-six and that no one had repeated since, partly because of the technical difficulty and partly because it seemed rude.

Her teams were famous for their quality. She selected them annually with a rigor that bordered on casting. Endurance athletes, technical climbers, specialists in crevasse rescue and high-altitude logistics — people whose CVs read like distillations of human capability. Her 2018 squad had included a former Royal Marine, a Chamonix guide with seventeen seasons on Mont Blanc, and a Sherpa from Phortse who had summited Everest without supplemental oxygen and described the experience as "okay." They won by forty-three minutes, a margin so absurd that the second-place team's captain filed a sportsmanship inquiry, not because he suspected cheating, but because he wanted to understand, on a philosophical level, what had happened to him.

This was the Mara Vasquez who, in March of the following year, sat in a rented apartment in El Chaltén with a laptop open to a website called GrouponArgentina.com and began, with meticulous deliberation, to construct the worst relay team in the history of the Cerro Ferro Ultra-Relay.

She did not do this hastily. She did not do it drunk or heartbroken or in the grip of some visible crisis. She did it the way she did everything: with her reading glasses on, a spreadsheet open in an adjacent tab, and a cup of maté going cold at her elbow. She reviewed applications. She cross-referenced. She made notes in a shorthand that no one else could read, in a notebook whose cover was worn soft from years of identical use. She was shopping, and she was shopping with the terrifying care of a woman who knew exactly what she wanted to buy.

The roster she filed with the race committee three weeks later contained no former Marines. No Chamonix guides. No Sherpas.

It contained a podiatrist, a pair of twins who were not speaking to each other, and a convicted forger on parole.

The race committee chairwoman, Lucía Demaría, called Mara personally. The conversation lasted eleven minutes. What Lucía said is not recorded. What Mara said, in its entirety, was: "They meet the medical and fitness minimums. Section 4.2. Check."

Section 4.2 was checked. The roster was approved.

And Mara Vasquez, six-time champion of the race the mountain does not negotiate, laced her boots, shouldered her pack, and walked toward the ice with four people who had no business being on it.

## II.

**Glenn**

The Groupon had cost forty-seven dollars.

Dr. Glenn Pryce — though he was not, technically, permitted to use the honorific anymore, the licensing board having made its position on that question both clear and public — had purchased the "High Altitude Adventure Relay Experience" at 2:17 a.m. on a Wednesday in November, sitting in the kitchen of the efficiency apartment he was renting month-to-month in Tucson, Arizona, because the house where his family lived was no longer a place where his family wanted him to also live. He had been eating peanut butter from the jar with his index finger. He had been wearing boxer shorts with a pattern of cartoon avocados on them that his daughter had given him for Father's Day two years earlier, back when she was still giving him things. The television was on. The television was always on. It was playing a program about people who restored antique furniture, and Glenn was not watching it, because Glenn was not watching anything anymore; he was merely occupying space adjacent to stimuli in the hope that the stimuli would eventually drown out the sound of his own thoughts, which had a quality like tinnitus — constant, high-pitched, and apparently incurable.

The Groupon listing had promised: *"Experience the thrill of Patagonian alpine competition as part of a guided relay team! Professional instruction included. No experience necessary. All equipment provided."*

The "All equipment provided" was what had done it. Not the thrill. Not Patagonia. The equipment. Glenn was a man who no longer owned things. His practice — dissolved. His medical license — suspended pending review, which was the board's way of saying revoked without giving him the dignity of a clean word for it. His house — occupied by his wife and his two daughters who had, at some point he couldn't precisely identify, stopped answering the phone when he called and started answering it when he texted, which meant they were willing to read his words but not hear his voice, a distinction whose cruelty he understood completely and could not argue with. His savings — given over to malpractice insurance payouts and legal fees and the month-to-month on the efficiency. What Glenn had left, in terms of physical possessions he could call his own, was: the avocado boxers, a 2011 Honda Civic with a cracked windshield, and a Groupon for a mountain he could not find on a map.

He had packed a duffel bag. He had flown to Buenos Aires, then to El Calafate, then taken a bus whose suspension seemed to have been removed as a personal insult. He had arrived at base camp with a sunburn, a digestive situation he preferred not to discuss, and a pair of rental crampons that he was holding upside down.

He was holding them upside down because he did not know which end was up. He had looked at them for a long time. They appeared to be a torture device designed by someone who had been briefly shown a shoe and then asked to improve upon it using only metal and malice. He turned them over. He turned them back. He consulted a laminated instruction card that had been included in the rental bag and that featured illustrations of a smiling cartoon figure whose proportions suggested that the artist had never seen a human body but had once had one described to them by a liar.

Glenn was fifty-three years old, five foot eight, and had a resting heart rate that his former cardiologist — a colleague, once, in the before-times — had described as "a conversation we should have." He did not belong on this mountain. He knew he did not belong on this mountain. But the Groupon was nonrefundable, and Glenn had discovered, in the months since everything fell apart, that nonrefundable commitments were the only kind he could keep — precisely because they removed the option of not keeping them. The Groupon was not a choice. It was a forfeiture of choice. And Glenn, who had made a series of choices that had cost him everything, found a strange solace in that.

He put the crampons down. He picked them up again, still upside down. He looked at the mountain.

The mountain did not look back, because it was a mountain, but if it had, its expression would have been one of sincere concern.

**Delia & Noor**

They arrived fourteen minutes apart, in separate hired cars, from separate hotels in El Chaltén where they had booked separate rooms under their separate married names — Delia Khoury-Okafor and Noor Khoury-Singh — as though the shared syllable at the hinge of their identities was a vestigial organ they had not yet managed to surgically remove.

Delia was first. She was the older twin by seven minutes, a fact she had deployed in arguments since the age of four and which had, over thirty-six years, calcified into a load-bearing element of her personality. She set up her tent with mechanical efficiency, inventory-checked her gear against a printed list, and ate a protein bar while standing, because sitting implied leisure and leisure implied contentment and Delia was not on this mountain to be content. Delia was on this mountain — though she would not have phrased it this way, and would have denied it if asked — to demonstrate to herself and to whatever residual audience the universe provided that she was the kind of person who climbed mountains. Capable. Directed. Upright. The kind of person who had not done what Noor had accused her of doing on Thanksgiving 2019, because a person who climbed mountains was not the kind of person who would do that.

She was setting up her tent's rain fly when Noor's car pulled in. She did not look up. She had an extraordinary gift for not looking up. It was a form of eloquence — the precise, theatrical calibration of attention withheld. An observer watching Delia not-look at Noor would have understood, instantly, that this was a woman who knew exactly where her sister was at every moment and was investing significant energy in performing the opposite.

Noor was younger by seven minutes and had spent approximately thirty years being told what that meant. She was shorter than Delia by an inch, darker by a shade, louder by several degrees. She set up her tent forty meters away, which at base camp was the maximum distance permitted by the designated camping grid and which Noor had calculated with the precision of a woman measuring the blast radius of her own feelings. She unpacked her gear without a list, because lists were Delia's thing, and Noor would rather freeze to death at 19,000 feet than be caught performing a behavior that could be attributed to her sister's influence.

Between them, in the forty meters of rocky ground separating their tents, communication moved like light through fiber optic cable: only via intermediary. A base camp volunteer named Paco — twenty-two years old, profoundly uncomfortable, studying engineering at the University of Buenos Aires — found himself conscripted into service as a diplomatic courier.

"Tell my sister that the weather briefing is at six," Delia told Paco.

Paco walked forty meters.

"Your sister says the weather briefing is at six," Paco told Noor.

"Tell my sister I know what time the weather briefing is, because I can read the schedule, because I am a literate adult," Noor told Paco.

Paco walked forty meters.

"She says she knows," Paco told Delia, editing for concision.

What had happened at Thanksgiving in 2019 was not yet clear to anyone at base camp, though its gravitational effects were visible in the way tides are visible: you did not need to see the moon to know it was exerting force. What was known — assembled from fragments of overheard phone calls, the occasional eruption in the meal tent, and a single devastating exchange witnessed by three other teams during equipment check — was that it involved a man named Amir, a set of house keys, a borrowed car that was returned with the seat adjusted, and a silence that began on November 28, 2019, and had not, as of this writing, ended. The twins had not spoken directly to each other in four years, two months, and eleven days. They had both signed up for the Cerro Ferro Ultra-Relay independently, through the same open-registration portal, within forty-eight hours of each other, and each had reacted to the other's presence on the roster with a fury that suggested the coincidence was not entirely a coincidence for either of them, and that the mountain was, for both Khoury sisters, a stage for something that had nothing to do with ice.

Paco, the courier, would later describe the experience of base camp to his roommate in Buenos Aires as "like being a child during a divorce, except both parents have ice axes."

**Tomás**

Tomás Gabriel Zúñiga Herrera sat on a flat rock at the edge of the base camp perimeter and watched the blinking light on his ankle monitor pulse green in the gathering dusk.

Green meant: you are within range. Green meant: the satellite can see you. Green meant: the Servicio Penitenciario Federal knows where you are, and where you are is allowed. The monitor had a geofence programmed into it — a virtual boundary, updated weekly by his parole officer in Mendoza, a man named Corvalán who had dead eyes and beautiful handwriting and who had signed off on this expedition only after a magistrate ruled that court-ordered community service could include "organized charitable or athletic events sanctioned by a recognized governing body," which the Cerro Ferro Ultra-Relay technically was, if you didn't look too hard at it.

Tomás was thirty-nine. He had served four years and two months at the Complejo Penitenciario Federal II in Marcos Paz for document fraud, specifically the production and distribution of forged identity credentials, tax instruments, and — in the count that had interested the prosecution most — a set of seventeen embossed certificates of deposit purporting to originate from the Banco de la Nación Argentina. The certificates had been beautiful. That was not vanity; it was the professional assessment of the forensic examiner who had testified at trial, holding one of the forgeries up to the light and describing its intaglio work as "genuinely remarkable" before recommending a sentence of six to ten years.
