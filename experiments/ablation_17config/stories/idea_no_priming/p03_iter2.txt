# The Courthouse

## I.

The tube in corridor 7-Left discharged a canister at 0914, and the canister rolled six inches along the brass lip of the receiving tray before the pneumatic jaw caught it and held it still. Inside: Form 1117-C, Motion for Reconsideration of Finding, triplicate, the tissue-weight pink copy already separating from the goldenrod and white where the gum adhesive had failed along the spine. A registrar's hand — gloved, sexless, emerging from a window too narrow to reveal the arm it belonged to — extracted the canister, unspooled the form, and applied a date stamp so worn that its digits had become a suggestion, a smudge that connoted *received* without specifying *when.* The canister was reloaded. The tube swallowed it with a bronchial sigh. Thirty feet above, in the ceiling's pneumatic arterial, it joined other canisters moving in both directions through translucent channels that branched and rejoined like a vascular diagram drawn by someone who understood circulation but had never seen blood.

The corridor itself was operational. This is the only honest way to describe it. It was not empty and it was not full. It was *in use.* The floor carried three lines — one the color of a hepatic duct, one the color of tarnished copper, one a blue so dark it read as black except under the flickering strips of bioluminescent tube-light that lined the baseboards, where it briefly, reluctantly admitted to being blue. The lines did not run parallel. They merged far ahead at a nexus the eye refused to resolve, a point where the corridor either ended or turned, and the geometry declined to clarify which. Along the walls, every eleven feet, a bench. Not every bench was empty. The occupied ones held figures whose posture suggested waiting, though "waiting" implies an expected end, and the figures' stillness had the quality not of anticipation but of sedimentation. They had been here long enough to become a feature of the corridor. The corridor had adjusted to include them. They were load-bearing.

Gravity in corridor 7-Left pulled at standard. This was notable only because in corridor 6-Left, one floor below and twelve feet to the east, gravity pulled at roughly seventy percent standard, and the body knew the difference before the mind acknowledged it, so that anyone stepping from the stairwell connecting the two corridors experienced a moment of nauseous buoyancy, a wrongness in the inner ear that the architecture did not apologize for. The stairwell itself had no signage. The building did not explain its variations the way a building with weather does not explain its weather. The shifts in gravitational pull were local conditions. You adjusted. If you stumbled, the stumble was yours.

Deeper in — past the registrar's window, past the junction where the hepatic line split and its tributary curved toward the eastern wing, past the alcove where four petitioners knelt before a bench whose surface was entirely hidden beneath stacked motions and a magistrate sat above them like a carved figure on a misericord, faceless, tilted slightly forward in what could have been attention or the posture of wood settling over centuries — the courthouse continued. It continued in every direction. Hallways opened onto hallways. Filing rooms opened onto further filing rooms, their cabinets rising to ceilings of variable height, some drawers sealed with wax, some with padlocks whose keys had been filed in other drawers in other rooms, some simply rusted shut, their contents legible only through the faint mildew-smell that seeped from their seams. There were courtrooms — hundreds of them, thousands, arranged not by floor or function but by some organizing principle that resisted mapping, so that Courtroom 1 was not near Courtroom 2, and Courtroom 2 did not exist on any directory the petitioners consulted, though its filings appeared regularly in the pneumatic system and its rulings were cited in documents that bore stamps from courtrooms that did appear. The building was not a labyrinth. A labyrinth has a center. The courthouse had procedure.

A gesture was being performed before a magistrate in Courtroom 1119. The petitioner — visible only as a back, a set of shoulders beneath fabric of no particular color, hands rising in the third motion of what manuals (if manuals existed) would have called the Formal Plea Sequence — held the position for the prescribed duration, then lowered the hands, then raised them again in the variation that indicated amendment. The magistrate's face, which was not a face but an absence where a face would have been — a smooth, slightly concave surface, like the interior of a gourd — reflected no light and produced no response. The petitioner held the amended gesture. Somewhere in the walls, a tube fired. The magistrate's head moved, or the shadows moved across it, or the petitioner's arms trembled in a way that made the stillness above them appear to shift. The record would show that the motion was received. The petitioner would file the next form. The process would continue. It had been continuing.

The building smelled of calcium and old paper and the particular metallic sweetness of pneumatic grease, and beneath that, so faint it registered only in the soft palate, something organic — not decay, not exactly, but the humid proximity of living systems in an enclosed space, the exhalation of ten thousand bodies processed through corridors whose ventilation was adequate but not generous. The smell was institutional. It was the smell of a place that metabolized.

A filing landed in a tray. A canister fired. A stamp fell. In a corridor three wings away and an indeterminate number of floors below, a floor line changed color — the dark blue blushing briefly to violet before settling back — and no one who saw it could have said whether the change was scheduled or anomalous, because no one who saw it had access to the schedule, and the concept of anomaly requires a baseline that the courthouse had never provided. Things happened in the courthouse. They happened with the regularity of respiration. You were in the body of it. You did not ask the body to explain its breathing.

## II.

She followed the hepatic line because the hepatic line was correct. She had confirmed this across thirty-seven filings, each one returned through the pneumatic system with a stamp that indicated receipt, processing, and continuation — the trefoil mark that meant *your case advances* — and the hepatic line had been consistent. Where it led, her motions were accepted. Where it did not lead, she did not go. This was not obedience. This was *method.*

Her name was not relevant to her case. The courthouse did not use names. Her case number — 1471-A/1471-A(i) through 1471-A(xvii), the sub-designations accruing over the course of her filings like rings in a tree — was the identity the building recognized, and she had long ago stopped experiencing this as a reduction. It was, if anything, a refinement. In the corridors outside the courthouse she had been many things, most of them legible only to herself: a woman who had lived for eleven years with a person whose voice had the texture of raw wool, who made coffee with a saucepan because the machine was always broken, who died in a manner that the records outside the courthouse called accidental and that the records inside the courthouse had not yet ruled on. Inside the courthouse, she was the petitioner in case 1471-A, and her case was the vindication of that death — a finding, a formal finding, a ruling that would make the record state what she knew: that the death was not accidental but the result of a failure, a systemic failure that had been filed and cataloged and stamped and archived and then un-filed, de-cataloged, the stamps redacted, the archive sealed. She was not here for grief. She was here for the record.

She knelt before the bench in Courtroom 441 and performed the Second Petition Gesture — hands raised, palms inward, fingers separated in the pattern she had learned from watching other petitioners in her first months here, before she understood that watching was insufficient and that the gesture had to be felt in the trapezius, in the particular tension between the shoulder blades that the magistrates' eyeless faces somehow read. She held it. She could hold it for a long time. She had trained herself the way a musician trains, not through repetition alone but through a deepening attention to the micro-adjustments that separated a gesture *performed* from a gesture *meant.* Her arms did not shake. Her breath was steady. The magistrate above her — they were always above, the benches always elevated, the geometry of the courtroom always enforcing the specific angle of neck and lifted eyes that transformed a standing person into a supplicant — tilted its featureless head a single degree to the left.

She lowered her hands. She opened the leather satchel she carried and withdrew Form 1471-A(xviii), the newest filing: a Motion for Disclosure of Archived Records Pertaining to Structural Liability in Cases of Institutional Negligence Resulting in Death. She had drafted it over what felt like weeks, though time in the courthouse was not reliably linear — there were corridors where a step forward aged the light by hours and corridors where the bioluminescent strips pulsed at a frequency that made seconds feel geological. The form was twelve pages. She had filled every field. Where the form asked for CASE HISTORY (SUMMARIZE IN 50 WORDS OR FEWER), she had written exactly fifty words, and each word was a load-bearing element in a structure she had been building across seventeen prior filings, each one citing the last, each one narrowing the courthouse's options for refusal. This was how the system worked. She understood this. You did not assault the system. You *addressed* it, in the rhetorical sense, the way a lawyer addresses a court, and if your address was sufficiently precise, sufficiently procedurally fluent, the system responded.

She placed the form on the bench's receiving surface — a shallow depression in the stone, slightly concave, like a font — and withdrew her hands in the Formal Submission Posture. The form lay there. In the silence — the immense, structural silence that was not the absence of sound but the presence of a prohibition so total it had become atmospheric — she could hear the pneumatic tubes in the walls, their distant susurrus, and the even more distant sound of stamps falling somewhere in the building, a sound that was almost rhythmic, almost a heartbeat, each impact arriving at an interval too regular to be human and too irregular to be mechanical.

The magistrate's hand — the first time in this session the magistrate had moved anything other than its head — extended from beneath the bench's overhang. The hand was gloved. The glove was the same non-color as the petitioners' clothing, and it was impossible to determine where the glove ended and the robe began, so that the hand appeared to be an extrusion of the bench itself, a functional protrusion. It touched the form. It drew the form beneath the overhang. It disappeared.

She waited. The trefoil stamp, when it came, would come through the pneumatic tube in the courtroom's eastern wall — the small brass mouth that protruded at shoulder height, its aperture just wide enough for a single canister. She had counted the intervals across her prior filings. On average, the return took between forty minutes and six hours. She settled into the kneeling position she had perfected: weight distributed, spine aligned, a posture she could maintain indefinitely because she had *made* herself capable of maintaining it indefinitely, the way the devout make themselves capable of holding prayer.

And while she waited, she did what she always did: she read the grain of the stone floor beneath her knees, its tiny fossils and mineral threads, and she thought about the saucepan coffee — the way it always tasted slightly of whatever had been cooked in the saucepan last, the way her partner would hand it to her with a gesture that was all in the wrist, a small rotation that presented the handle, and the handle was always oriented correctly, always pointed toward her right hand, every time, without fail, a precision that she had not recognized as love until the saucepan sat unused in a kitchen she no longer entered. She did not think of this as grief. She thought of it as *evidence.* The care in the handle's orientation was the same care she brought to the gesture before the bench: a discipline of attention that meant *this matters, the angle matters, the form matters,* and if the courthouse could not recognize that — if the system could not read in her filings the same meticulous love that had oriented a saucepan handle — then the system was not what it claimed to be.

But it was. She believed it was.

The tube coughed. A canister slid into the receiving tray. She unspooled the form inside: her original filing, stamped with the trefoil. Her case advanced. She permitted herself the smallest expression — not a smile but a minute relaxation of the muscles around her eyes, a private signal of confirmation — and she placed the form in her satchel beside the others, a growing sheaf of stamped documents that constituted, in aggregate, the architecture of vindication. She rose. She followed the hepatic line deeper into the building, toward the next filing window, the next courtroom, the next gesture before the next bench. The line was steady beneath her feet. It led where it always led: further in.

## III.

In the eastern wing, where the corridors were narrower and the ceilings lower and the bioluminescent strips pulsed with a faster, more agitated frequency, the man followed the copper line and did not look up.

Looking up was dangerous. Looking up meant seeing the pneumatic channels in the ceiling, and seeing the channels meant tracking the canisters, and tracking the canisters meant noticing the ones that were empty — tubes that fired with the same bronchial sigh but carried nothing, dry rounds in a chamber, and the empty firings were worse than the full ones because they suggested the system was running even when there was nothing to process, that the building was performing itself in a vacuum, that the machinery did not need content to continue, and if the machinery did not need content then the content — his filings, his case, *him* — was decorative, and if he was decorative he was dispensable, and if he was dispensable then the corridor he was walking in was not leading him toward a ruling but away from one, toward the place where the dispensable were dispensed with, and he had never seen that place but he had felt its proximity in the drop of temperature that occasionally fell across certain junctions like a draft from an open door that was not a door.

He did not look up.

His case number was 8086-K. He did not know what the K designated. Letters, in the courthouse's taxonomy, were not explained; they were *assigned,* and the assignment carried the authority of a sentence. He had received the designation upon his first filing — a Motion for Continued Existence (Standard), which the intake window had accepted without ceremony and returned, forty-one minutes later, stamped with a single vertical line that he had spent weeks trying to interpret. Other petitioners' returned forms bore the trefoil. His bore the vertical line. He did not know what the vertical line meant. The absence of knowledge was not a gap; it was a *condition,* the permanent weather of his case, and he moved through the courthouse under that weather the way animals move through winter: not by understanding it but by surviving it.

He knelt before the bench in Courtroom 2917 — a room identical to every other courtroom he had visited except for a hairline crack in the stone ceiling that he tracked with his peripheral vision because it was the only feature that distinguished this room from the room where his case might be denied, and if he could identify the room, he could prepare, and preparation was the only weapon available to a person who did not understand the system's taxonomy. The magistrate sat above. The faceless head was still. He raised his hands in the Second Petition Gesture, and his hands trembled.

They always trembled. He could not stop them. He had tried.
