# The Fumbling Five

## I. The Last Fall

The plaza held nine thousand people and not one of them was breathing.

Later, the historians would argue about the number. The Decorum Council's official report — the last one they would ever file, as it turned out — placed attendance at "an estimated gathering of civic malcontents not exceeding four hundred." But the potato seller on Vering Street had been working crowds for thirty years and she said nine thousand, so nine thousand it was. They filled Sovereign Plaza from the Council steps to the river wall. They packed the alleys. They sat on rooftops with their legs dangling. A boy had climbed the Monument to Ordered Feeling so that he straddled the bronze figure's shoulders, and from there he could see the whole of it: the crowd, the makeshift stage of overturned fish crates, and the five figures standing on it in their battered motley, waiting.

It was Pia who stepped forward first. It was always Pia.

She was not tall. Her spine had its famous leftward question mark, and her costume — patched so many times it was more thread than fabric — hung on her like a sail on a listing mast. She raised one hand. Not a wave, not a command. Just a hand, lifted. Like a child asking permission to speak.

And from nine thousand lungs, a sound like the whole city had been holding its breath for years and had just, at this moment, remembered it had a body.

The Five began.

Tomás went first because Tomás always went first. He ran three steps and his feet went out from under him on nothing — there was nothing there, the stage was dry wood — but he fell as if the ground had been buttered by God, a tremendous skidding launch that sent him pinwheeling across the crates with his arms flung wide and his mouth open in that perfect O of theatrical surprise. He hit Reva, who was already turning, who caught his momentum and converted it into a spin so that they became a two-bodied wheel rolling toward the stage's edge, and Marten — the mute, the tumbler — dove over both of them in a layout so pure it looked like flight, landing on his hands and springing up into Ludo's waiting arms, and Ludo, enormous Ludo, the former blacksmith's apprentice whose hands could palm a man's skull, caught Marten like a loaf of bread and threw him gently skyward, and for one held breath Marten floated, genuinely floated—

And the first manifestation broke the surface of the world.

It came from the crowd's left flank, from a cluster of dockworkers who hadn't laughed in months, whose shoulders ached from carrying grain under the Council's new labor quotas. A ripple went through them — not in their bodies but in the air above their heads — and suddenly there were fish. Silvery, darting, schooling fish made of something between water and light, and they swam through the air above the dockworkers in a shimmering murmuration, and the dockworkers looked up and their faces did something that faces are not supposed to do in Sovereign Plaza under the gaze of the Council Hall: they *softened*.

The fish were only the beginning.

From the milliner's quarter came a rising column of ribbons — not cloth ribbons, but liquid ones, warm and colorful and slightly viscous, like honey dyed in carnival shades. They spiraled upward and draped themselves across the Monument to Ordered Feeling, and the boy straddling the bronze shoulders laughed so hard he nearly fell, and his laughter manifested as a flock of tiny shoes — children's shoes, with bells on — that danced in a circle around his head.

From an old woman near the fountain: an enormous plum pudding, the size of a horse, steaming and fragrant, that sat in the middle of the crowd with the solidity and dignity of architecture. No one knew what it meant. Everyone knew what it meant.

Pia watched all of it, standing at the center of the stage while her troupe tumbled around her, and she felt the familiar opening — the way her body became a door when the Five performed, the way the audience's insides came outside and took on weight, texture, temperature, the warm stink of living feeling made real. Normally she could track it. Normally she could feel individual threads: this woman's grief, that man's secret joy, the particular curdled shame of someone who had done something forgivable but could not forgive themselves. She would feel these things enter the space the Five's falling opened, and she would ride them the way a sailor rides a swell — not controlling, just not drowning.

Tonight she could not track it. There were too many.

Nine thousand people who had been living under the Decorum Council's regime of emotional regulation — the Bureau of Appropriate Feeling, the Licensure of Approved Laughter (one to three instances per public gathering, smiles not to exceed four seconds in duration), the prohibition on weeping outside of Designated Grief Windows — nine thousand people whose insides had been packed tight as powder in a cannon, and the Five had just lit the match.

Tomás was still falling. He had been falling for what seemed like an impossible stretch of time, a single extended pratfall that had carried him off the stage and into the crowd, and the crowd was catching him and passing him overhead, and everywhere his body touched another body, something new erupted: a geyser of warm milk from a nursing mother who missed her grown son, a cascade of autumn leaves — real ones, though it was May — from an old farmer who longed for harvest and rest, a detonation of soap bubbles from a cluster of children who had no specific repression, only a boundless, furious, captive *aliveness* that had been told to sit down and be quiet for the last three years.

Reva and Ludo were performing their signature routine near the stage's edge — the one where Ludo tried to walk in a straight line and Reva kept rearranging the ground under his feet, pulling boards loose, tilting crates — but the audience's manifestations were altering the choreography in real time. A trapdoor opened where there had been no trapdoor. A staircase of crystallized sugar grew from the plaza cobblestones, leading nowhere, and Reva ran up it without hesitation and dove off the top into a landing that should have broken her and didn't, because Marten was there, always there, the silent axis around which the rest of them wheeled, and he caught her momentum and redirected it so that her dive became a roll became a slide became a skid that carried her through a sudden puddle of something warm and golden — someone's longing — and she came up dripping and shining and grinning the kind of grin that the Bureau of Appropriate Feeling classified as a Category Five Emotional Event punishable by fine.

The Council Hall doors opened.

Twelve Decorum Enforcers in their grey uniforms marched out in formation. They carried the Standard Equipment: the Composure Nets, the Solemnity Horns, the Jars of Prescribed Calm. Behind them, visible in the lit doorway, the seven members of the High Council sat in their tall chairs, watching through the gap like surgeons observing through a slit in the operating curtain.

The Enforcers reached the top of the Council steps. The senior Enforcer raised a Solemnity Horn to his lips.

Pia saw this. She was standing at the center of the stage, and she was the only one of the Five not in motion, and she understood — not in her mind but in her crooked spine, in the soles of her feet, in the place behind her sternum where other people's feelings entered her — that there was a choice arriving. Not a decision. A choice. The kind that is made with the body.

The Solemnity Horn sounded: a low, grey, flattening tone designed to suppress public emotion. It hit the crowd like a cold hand pressed to a hot forehead. People flinched. The darting fish above the dockworkers flickered. The ribbons sagged. The dancing shoes around the boy on the monument slowed, and one fell, and the tiny bell on it made a sound like something dying.

And Pia felt the crowd's reaction — not their obedience but their *reflex* to obey, the years of trained submission written into their muscles, the automatic flinch that said *sit down, be quiet, put it away, you are too much* — and she felt it as a physical force pressing the manifestations back down into the bodies they had come from, and she understood that if she did nothing, the nine thousand people in Sovereign Plaza would swallow their own insides again and walk home and the fish and the ribbons and the plum pudding and the longing and the leaves and the aliveness would rot inside them for another year, another decade, forever.

"Never hold back the fall," Gerta had said. "The fall is where the truth lives."

Pia ran.

Not away. Not toward. She ran the way a performer runs when the routine calls for the biggest finish in the catalog — the one that Gerta had called "the Total Commitment," the fall with no safety in it, no reservation, no wink at the audience to say *I'm in on the joke.* Just the body and the ground and the absolute willingness to meet it.

She ran toward the center of the stage. Tomás was being passed back toward her by the crowd. Reva was picking herself up from the golden puddle. Ludo was lifting Marten for another throw. They saw her running and they *knew* — all four of them knew, without signal or word, because this was what Gerta had trained into them, this unity of intention, this shared willingness — and they ran too, all of them, converging on the center of the stage from five directions, accelerating.

The Solemnity Horn sounded again, louder.

But nine thousand people were watching five small figures in battered motley running toward each other with absolute commitment, and the sight of that — the *willingness* of it, the unprotected audacity, the way they didn't slow down even though the math was obvious, the collision was certain, it was going to be spectacular — something about that sight reached under the Solemnity Horn and gripped the crowd by the chest.

The dockworkers' fish blazed back into brilliance, twice as bright, and began to multiply.

The old woman's plum pudding trembled, swelled, and split into seven smaller puddings that rolled through the crowd like friendly boulders, steaming.

From the rooftops, a rain of flowers that were also flames that were also music — they made sound as they fell, each one a different note, so that the plaza filled with a descending melody that no one had written and everyone recognized, the way you recognize a song from childhood that you didn't know you remembered until it played.

From the Council steps, one of the grey-uniformed Enforcers — the youngest, the one in the back row — dropped her Composure Net and made a sound that was neither laugh nor sob but the place where those two things share a border.

And the manifestations began to merge.

They found each other in the air above the plaza. The fish swam into the ribbons. The ribbons wound through the flowers-that-were-flames. The melody absorbed the bells from the dancing shoes and became richer, stranger. The golden longing in the puddle on the stage rose as mist and joined the whirling column that was now forming above the Five's convergence point, and the column grew and grew, and it was made of every suppressed feeling in the city, three years of mandated composure, ten thousand small surrendered joys and unfinished griefs and swallowed rages and confiscated wonderments, and it was all tangled together, it was *everything at once*, and it was beautiful and terrible.

The buildings around the plaza groaned. The cobblestones nearest the stage cracked. The Monument to Ordered Feeling — the bronze figure with its hands clasped tightly at its chest, an image of perfect emotional containment — rang like a bell, a deep harmonic tone, and a fracture line appeared running from its clasped hands to its sealed mouth.

Pia reached the center of the stage.

She did not slow down.

Tomás hit first, from the east. His body slammed into hers and they both staggered, and the stagger was a choreography older than thought, the way two people fold into each other when neither will stop. Reva arrived from the south, low and fast, taking Pia's legs, and the three of them went down in a tangle. Ludo came from the west, his enormous body crashing into the pile with a force that should have flattened them, and the sound was terrific, a huge meaty slap, and from the crowd — a gasp, and then laughter, involuntary, irresistible, the kind of laughter the Bureau had never managed to classify because it exists beyond category: the laughter of recognition. Marten came last, from above — Ludo had thrown him before the collision, a final perfect arc — and he landed on top of the pile with the lightness of paper, completing it.

Five bodies on a stage. A pileup. A pratfall.

The oldest joke in the human vocabulary: people falling down.

And Pia, at the bottom of the pile, feeling the weight of her family on her body, feeling the plaza's manifestations roaring overhead in a column that now reached the clouds, feeling the cobblestones cracking and the buildings groaning and the Solemnity Horns blaring uselessly against a tide that had no interest in being contained—

Pia pushed herself up.

Not all the way. Just enough to stand on her own two unsteady feet in the middle of her collapsed troupe, swaying, her costume torn, her crooked spine making its question mark against the swirling sky, her face wearing an expression that nine thousand people would describe nine thousand ways and that the potato seller on Vering Street — the only reliable witness — would summarize as: *She looked like someone who had just remembered something important and was about to say it, but the remembering was enough, maybe the remembering was the thing itself.*

And Pia took her last fall.

She didn't slip. She didn't trip. She didn't tumble or cartwheel or execute any of the Five's legendary slapstick vocabulary. She simply leaned forward — past the point of balance, past the point of recovery, past the point where the body says *catch yourself* — and fell.

A full, frontal, total-commitment fall. Face toward the ground. Arms at her sides. The fall of someone who trusts the ground absolutely. The fall of someone who has been falling her whole life and has learned that the ground is not the enemy; the ground is where you find out what you're made of.

She hit the stage.

And the plum pudding, the fish, the ribbons, the flowers, the flames, the music, the honey, the milk, the leaves, the soap bubbles, the golden mist, the spinning weathervane, the dancing shoes, the column of tangled feeling that rose above Sovereign Plaza like a second city made of everything the first city had forbidden—

Froze.

All of it. Suspended. Mid-cascade, mid-swim, mid-spiral. The flower-flames hung motionless, still burning. The fish held their silver poses. A ribbon curled in the air above the boy on the monument, stopped mid-flourish, its liquid weight balanced impossibly on the moment between falling and flying.

Nine thousand people looked up at the arrested sky.

The Solemnity Horns fell silent.

The youngest Enforcer on the Council steps stood with her dropped Composure Net at her feet and her hand over her mouth and tears on her face and she did not know why she was crying, only that it was not sadness. It was not sadness.

The story stopped here.

Except it didn't stop. It started — for you, for now — to go backward, and backward is the direction of understanding, which is the direction the heart travels when it wants to know why it is breaking.

## II. The Banana Peel Avalanche

Three weeks before the plaza, in a fortified manor on the Geltz Peninsula, the Fumbling Five performed for Governor Aldric Pell.

They did not want to be there.

The invitation had arrived as invitations from men like Pell always did: worded as a request, scented as a threat. *The Governor would be honored by the company of the celebrated Fumbling Five at his annual Midwinter Banquet.
