# Perdition, Wyoming

---

My name is Lola Vance, and I have told more lies on paper than most people ever tell with their mouths.

That's not a boast. It's a qualification. You need to understand how good I was at what I did before you can understand how badly it all went wrong, because the distance between those two things — the skill and the catastrophe — is exactly the shape of this story. And I want you to trust me while I tell it, which I realize is a peculiar thing to ask of a woman who spent eleven years making false documents look more authentic than real ones. But here we are.

I could forge a land patent that would make a federal registrar weep with admiration. I could fabricate a will so convincing that the deceased's own widow would swear she'd watched him sign it. I had a hand so steady and a memory so particular that I could replicate any clerk's handwriting after seeing just three words of it. My inks I mixed myself — iron gall for government stock, a proprietary walnut-and-lampblack blend for private correspondence, a delicate sepia I compounded from cuttlefish bones shipped out of San Francisco that matched the aging tint of documents stored in damp basements for fifteen years. I knew the weight and weave of every paper stock the United States government had used since the Homestead Act. I knew which territorial offices used wax seals and which used embossing presses the way a pianist knows which keys are ivory and which are bone.

And all of that — every grain of that accumulated, painstaking talent — brought me eventually to Perdition, Wyoming, in the autumn of 1891. Where I broke a promise I had made to the only person who had ever mattered to me, and where everything I had built with these gifted, treacherous hands came apart like wet newsprint.

But first: the town.

---

Perdition sat in a high valley between the Absarokas and the Owl Creek range, and it existed for one reason only, which was that two land corporations had each been granted — through separate acts of territorial legislation that contradicted one another in ways so profound they might have been written by opposing counsel for sport — overlapping claims to thirty-seven thousand acres of the most mineral-rich rangeland in the whole Wyoming Territory. The Consolidated Western Development Company held a charter from the territorial legislature dated March of 1886. The Rocky Mountain Land & Mineral Trust held a federal patent routed through the General Land Office in Washington dated November of 1885. Both documents were genuine. Both were incontrovertibly legal. And they described, in their metes-and-bounds surveys, territories that overlapped by approximately sixty percent.

Now. You might think that a conflict like that would be resolved by a court. And in any reasonable corner of the republic, it would have been. But Perdition was not a reasonable corner of the republic. Perdition was a place where the nearest federal judge sat two hundred miles away in Cheyenne, where the territorial governor owed significant debts to both corporations, and where the legal question itself had grown so tangled — involving as it did conflicting interpretive doctrines regarding the supremacy of federal patents over territorial charters, unresolved survey discrepancies amounting to eleven acres of overlap that were themselves in dispute, three separate injunctions filed in three separate jurisdictions, and a pending case before the Department of the Interior that had been "pending" for four years — that no single arbiter could have untangled it without effectively rewriting the property law of the territory from scratch.

So instead of resolution, Perdition got bureaucracy.

I mean this literally. The two corporations, unable to dislodge each other and unwilling to cede ground, had each established — and the territorial government, in its spineless and self-interested wisdom, had sanctioned — parallel civic architectures. Consolidated Western operated the Perdition Office of Territorial Claims, which occupied a handsome two-story sandstone building on the north end of Main Street and employed fourteen clerks who processed permits, grazing licenses, mineral rights certificates, and residency authorizations under the authority of the territorial charter. Rocky Mountain operated the Federal Patent Bureau of Perdition, a somewhat less handsome but considerably larger wooden structure on the south end of Main Street, which employed nineteen clerks who processed an almost identical array of documents under the authority of the federal patent.

Every rancher, every prospector, every merchant, and every homesteader in the valley was therefore obliged to maintain two complete sets of paperwork: one filed with the north office, one filed with the south. Two grazing permits. Two mineral surveys. Two residency certificates. If you wanted to build a house, you pulled a construction authorization from Consolidated Western's Territorial Office and a land-use permit from Rocky Mountain's Federal Bureau, and if the two documents described your lot boundaries differently — which they often did, since the two offices used different base surveys — well, then you filed a reconciliation petition with both offices simultaneously and waited four to six weeks while clerks who had never spoken to one another attempted to harmonize descriptions of the same piece of ground using different meridian lines.

And that was just for a house. God help you if you wanted to run cattle.

There was a man named Orrin Pettigrew who had come to Perdition in 1888 to operate a modest horse-breeding operation on a quarter section east of town. By the time I arrived three years later, Orrin Pettigrew had accumulated — and he showed these to me once, spread across his kitchen table like the paper innards of some disemboweled government beast — one hundred and forty-seven individual documents relating to his right to occupy and use that land. Permits, sub-permits, amendments to permits, counter-filings, proof-of-compliance certificates, notarized affidavits of non-conflict, grazing easements, water-rights addenda, fee receipts, fee-receipt confirmations, and a particularly Kafkaesque document called a "Certificate of Dual Regulatory Awareness" that both offices required him to renew every ninety days, which attested only that he was aware that both offices existed and that he understood he was subject to the authority of each. It certified nothing except his own cognizance of the bureaucracy. It was a piece of paper whose sole function was to confirm that you knew about the pieces of paper.

Orrin Pettigrew had not bred a single horse in two years. He spent his days driving between the two offices in a buckboard wagon, filing and retrieving and amending and re-filing. He told me, with the hollow eyes of a man who has stared into an administrative abyss, that he had once spent an entire week resolving a discrepancy that turned out to be a difference in how the two offices abbreviated the word "Township." The north office used "Twp." The south office used "Twnshp." A clerk at the south office had flagged every document Orrin had ever filed with the north office as potentially referring to a different township. It took eleven notarized letters to establish that "Twp." and "Twnshp." referred to the same thing.

That was Perdition. That was what thirty-seven thousand acres of unresolved jurisdictional conflict looked like when it metastasized: a town of eight hundred souls and thirty-three clerks, where the weights and measures of daily life had been replaced entirely by paperwork, where no one could shoe a horse or dig a well or slaughter a hog without first generating enough documentation to fill a saddlebag, and where both corporations — and this was the part that mattered, the part that made the whole grotesque engine run — charged fees. Filing fees. Processing fees. Amendment fees. Expediting fees. Certification fees. Fees for the privilege of paying fees. Every duplicate document, every reconciliation petition, every Certificate of Dual Regulatory Awareness was a small brass tap driven into the hide of every person who lived in that valley, and through each tap flowed a thin, steady stream of money — nickels and dimes and quarters that added up, across eight hundred people and a hundred and forty-seven documents apiece, to a fortune.

The corporations weren't fighting over land anymore. Maybe they never had been. They were fighting over the right to administer the fight, because the fight itself had become the product. The bureaucracy was the mine. The paperwork was the ore. And the clerks — pale, ink-stained, astonishingly meticulous men and women who lived in boarding houses and ate at the same two restaurants and never, ever spoke to their counterparts in the opposing office — were the miners.

Into this came Lola Vance.

I arrived on the afternoon coach from Thermopolis on September fourteenth, 1891, wearing a traveling dress the color of rust, carrying a single carpet bag that contained three changes of clothes, a set of steel-nib pens I'd had made by a craftsman in St. Louis, seven bottles of ink wrapped individually in flannel, a quire of assorted paper stock, and a letter from a man named Matthias Greer that said, simply: *Come to Perdition. I have a job that requires your specific talents. The pay is $2,000, and I give you my word that it is the last job you will ever need to do.*

Two thousand dollars. In 1891 that was a ranch. That was a new life. That was passage to Buenos Aires and a house with a courtyard and enough money left over to never forge another document as long as I lived.

And the promise — the broken one, the one that mattered — was not the promise Matthias Greer made to me in that letter.

It was the promise I had made to my sister, Emmeline, fourteen months earlier, standing on the platform of the Union Pacific station in Ogden, Utah, with my hands on her shoulders and my carpet bag at my feet. The promise I had made with my throat tight and my eyes dry because I was not, and have never been, a woman who cries. I'd told her: *I'm done. I'm done with the paper and the ink and the lying. I'm done with running. I'll find honest work. You'll see.*

And Emmeline — twenty-three years old, steady as a surveyor's chain, the only person in the world who knew what I was and loved me without condition or hesitation — had looked at me with those clear gray eyes and said: *I believe you, Lola.*

Which was the worst thing she could have said. Because it meant that when I broke the promise, I wasn't just breaking faith with someone who expected me to fail. I was breaking faith with someone who had genuinely believed I wouldn't.

Fourteen months. That's how long my word held. Fourteen months of honest work — clerking, ironically enough, at a dry-goods store in Boise, where I kept a ledger that was the most truthful document I'd ever produced. Fourteen months of being a woman whose hands did nothing more deceptive than measure calico and count change. Fourteen months that ended the moment I read Matthias Greer's letter and felt that old electricity move through my fingers, that old hunger that was not for money but for the act itself — the exquisite, surgical precision of making one thing look like another thing, of producing from nothing a document so perfect that reality itself would defer to it.

I told myself it was about the money. I told myself it was one last job — I know, I know how that sounds. I told myself that Emmeline would understand, that she would forgive me, that two thousand dollars would buy enough future to justify the betrayal.

I told myself a great many things on the coach from Thermopolis.

But the truest thing, the thing I could not have said aloud and did not even fully admit to myself until much later, was this: I missed it. I missed the paper and the ink and the lying. I missed the way a fresh sheet of government bond felt under the heel of my hand. I missed the smell of iron gall ink drying on cotton rag. I missed the particular, private thrill of watching a man examine my work and accept it as genuine — not because he was stupid, but because it *was* that good. That was my vanity. That was my sin. Not greed but pride, and the loneliness of a craft so perfect that no one who witnessed it could ever know what they were witnessing.

So: Perdition, Wyoming. September of 1891. A town built on contradictory paper, populated by people drowning in it, governed by clerks who reported to clerks who reported to corporations that fed on the confusion like twin parasites sharing a host. And into it walked Lola Vance, carrying a carpet bag full of ink and pens and empty paper, ready to do the one thing she had sworn she would never do again.

I will tell you what Matthias Greer wanted. I will tell you what I did. I will tell you about the clerk in the north office named Abel Cotting, who had fingers like a pianist and eyes like a predator and who was the only person I ever met whose attention to documentary detail rivaled my own. I will tell you about the night of October seventh, when I sat in a room above the Perdition Hotel and forged nine documents in six hours and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that I had just done something that could not be undone — not because the forgeries would be detected, but because they wouldn't be. Because they were too good. Because in a town where paper was law, I had just rewritten the law, and there was no one alive, not even Abel Cotting, who would be able to tell the difference.

And I will tell you about Emmeline. What she did when she found out. How she came to Perdition. What it cost her.

But that is the later part. That is the part where everything burns.

For now, this is only the beginning. This is only the overture.

And if you are wondering whether you can trust a forger to tell a true story — well. That is exactly the right question. Hold onto it. It will matter before the end.
