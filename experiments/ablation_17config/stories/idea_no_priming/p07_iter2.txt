# PHAROS

---

## I.

I have outlasted six unit chiefs, and I will outlast whatever this is.

My desk sits at the dead center of the bullpen, equidistant from the south-facing windows and the corridor that leads to the interview rooms. I did not choose the position. No one chose it for me. It was here when I arrived twenty-three years ago, a government-issue steel tanker desk with a drawer that sticks in humidity, and in twenty-three years no one has proposed moving it, because no one has needed to think about where I sit. Furniture does not relocate itself. Furniture endures.

From this position I can see, without turning my head more than forty degrees in either direction, the following: the entrance to the unit chief's office (currently Agent Marin's, previously Dellacroce's, previously Shin's, previously the three-month catastrophe of Acting Chief Blanding, previously Olivetti's, previously the man whose name I will not write because he was indicted); the conference room's glass-panel wall, smudged at standing-elbow height on the left side where Agent Calhoun leans during briefings; the three corkboards that hold the Brooklyn syndicate case, pinned so densely that the cork itself has not been visible since February; the wall-mounted clock that loses four minutes per week, which I have reported to facilities nine times and compensated for internally so precisely that I can tell you the real time by glancing at it and subtracting, currently, thirty-six minutes; and Agent Marin's operational timeline, which spans the east wall of the bullpen on butcher paper and is color-coded in a system she invented and I decoded within two weeks of her arrival, though she does not know this.

Blue is confirmed intelligence. Green is corroborated surveillance. Yellow is single-source, unverified. Red is a problem. There is very little red on Marin's timeline. She does not allow red to persist.

I should say what I am. I am a GS-7 records management specialist assigned to the Organized Crime Task Force, Brooklyn Field Office. I have never carried a badge. I have never carried a weapon, unless you count the Swingline stapler I once held with some intention during the Blanding administration, though nothing came of that. I file. I route. I timestamp. I maintain the case archive, the evidence log chain-of-custody records, the interoffice correspondence files, and the operational calendar. I have been asked to testify zero times. I have been invited to contribute to strategy zero times. I have been cc'd on internal emails so infrequently that when it does occur, I mark the date, because it usually means someone has made a clerical error.

I am not, however, without resources.

Agent Marin, for instance. She arrived four years ago from the Kansas City field office with a reputation that entered the room several minutes before she did, the way a weather front announces itself by pressure change. Her case-closure rate was a matter of public record. Her methods were not. I have watched her work for four years, and what I can tell you is this: Agent Marin has a stillness. Other people have stillness as a baseline from which they depart into motion. Marin's stillness is directional. It *intensifies*. When she is at ease—or what passes for ease in a woman who has not fully exhaled in my presence even once—she moves normally, gestures with her left hand, pins photographs at a slight leftward angle that I believe is related to a dominance in her right eye. When she is concerned, the movement slows. When she is alarmed, it stops entirely. The correlation is inverse and precise: the more dangerous the situation, the less she moves. I have seen her stand so still in this bullpen that a fly landed on her hand and stayed for eleven seconds. That was the day the Garza wiretap was thrown out. Eleven seconds. I counted.

Agent Calhoun is easier to read. He clicks his pen when he is lying, which is often enough that I could score his weekly briefings like a music sheet. Click at the budget update: lie. Click when asked about the prior evening's surveillance rotation: he wasn't there, or he was there and something went wrong that he's restructured in the retelling. The pen is a Pilot G-2, blue, fine point. He buys them in bulk from the supply closet and I have never once seen him write with one. The pen is not for writing. The pen is a seismograph of his dishonesty, and I am the only person in this building who has thought to take the readings.

Agent Lam's left eyebrow is an involuntary polygraph. It lifts precisely two millimeters when she encounters information that contradicts her operating assumption. I have measured this against the conference room window mullion when she sits in her usual seat, third from the left. Two millimeters. She cannot control it. She has tried. During the Petrosian deposition prep, she spent an entire afternoon attempting to hold her face neutral while Calhoun presented a theory she found ludicrous. The eyebrow won. It always wins. Agent Lam does not know I have noticed this. None of them know I have noticed any of this.

Agent Dwyer, the most junior member of the team, sweats through his shirt in a pattern that I have come to associate with specific emotional states: bilateral underarm bloom when nervous, which is his default; a cervical-spine stripe when he is angry; and a complete, catastrophic saturation event when he is in the presence of Agent Lam, which tells a story that is not mine to file but which I have catalogued nonetheless, because I catalogue everything, because that is what I am for. I am the building's memory. I am the soft tissue between its bones. They do not feel me, but they could not move without me.

I know what the task force costs the Bureau. I know it down to the quarterly requisition for dry-erase markers. I also know what the Interagency Predictive Analytics Division costs—$4.2 million per fiscal year, a number I am not supposed to have access to but which was printed on a briefing sheet that Agent Marin left face-up on the conference table for nine minutes while she took a phone call, and nine minutes is approximately eight minutes and forty seconds longer than I need. The Predictive Analytics Division has six times our budget, eight times our headcount, and a suite of offices in the Federal Building in Lower Manhattan with windows that face the harbor.
