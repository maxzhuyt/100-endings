# The Curse-Eaters of São Bento

## I.

Here is a town that works. You should be suspicious of it immediately.

São Bento do Morro Velho sits in a crease of the Serra da Mantiqueira where the fog comes down so thick in winter that the town's three churches vanish by mid-afternoon, their bells disembodied, clanging from inside a cloud. Coffee grows on the eastern slopes. Sugarcane once grew on the western ones, back when there were enough hands to cut it, back before the young people discovered São Paulo and never wrote home. The river bisects the main road, the main road bisects the market square, and in the market square there is a jacaranda tree so old that its root system has buckled the cobblestones into a topography that perfectly mirrors — and this is not metaphor, this is measurement — the surrounding mountain range. The tree has been there longer than the churches. Longer than the town. It will come up again.

You need to know about three groups of people.

First: the Spite Coven.

They live in a compound on the northern ridge, behind a gate made of wrought iron twisted into shapes that are not, technically, obscene, but that make children ask questions their parents would rather not answer. There have been Spite Covens in these mountains since before the Portuguese arrived, though the current one — four women, down from twelve a generation ago, down from forty in the century before that — practices a distinctly syncretic cruelty, stitching together West African *ebó*, Tupí binding words, and a Portuguese ecclesiastical cursing tradition so old that the Vatican denies it ever existed. They hex. This is what they do. They hex the way spiders web: persistently, architecturally, because the alternative is to sit still and starve. Each hex is a small, lovingly crafted pellet of malice — a soured womb, a craterous boil, a business deal that collapses on the morning of its signing, a marriage bed that fills with the smell of rotting figs every time both parties lie in it. The Coven does not hex out of personal hatred, though personal hatred is certainly available. They hex because hexing is the mechanism by which they remain necessary, and remaining necessary is the mechanism by which they remain alive. Remember this. It will matter when we get to the part about pity.

Second: the townspeople.

They are, by and large, decent. This is the worst thing I will say about them, and I mean it as an indictment. They are decent the way a fence is decent: upright, painted, enclosing exactly the amount of space they are comfortable defending. They did not ask to be born in a hexed town. They did not ask for boils, or soured wombs, or the fig smell, which is — and on this the entire population agrees — *really remarkably bad*. They pay their taxes. They attend the churches that the fog swallows. They are, when observed at a distance, interchangeable with the residents of any other small town in Minas Gerais, and they would like very much to keep it that way.

Third — and here the narrator's voice drops half a register, not from solemnity but from something closer to tenderness, the way you might speak about someone you have watched for a very long time and have never quite figured out how to help:

The Besteiro women.

The name means *crossbowman*, which is a patrilineal inheritance from some forgotten soldier and has nothing to do with anything. What matters is the matriline. For as long as there has been a Spite Coven on the northern ridge, there has been a Besteiro woman in São Bento who can eat a hex the way a snake eats an egg: whole, in one shuddering convulsion, shell and all. The curse goes in. It does not come out. What it does instead — inside the body, inside the marrow and the bile and the dark wet machinery of the gut — is a private catastrophe that each Besteiro woman learns to carry and none has ever adequately described.

The arrangement is thus: The Coven hexes. The Besteiro eats the hex. The townsperson who would have suffered does not suffer. The Coven, cheated but not defeated, hexes again. The Besteiro eats again. The town endures. This has gone on for so long that no one remembers who proposed it, or whether it was proposed at all, or whether it simply calcified out of repetition in the way that all truly durable injustices do — not by anyone's design but by everyone's quiet, daily failure to object.

The previous Besteiro was a woman named Odete. She is dead. She has been dead for six years. The town held a funeral that was, by all accounts, moving, well-catered, and attended by every single person who had, at one point or another, thought *Thank God it's her and not me* without ever hearing those words reach the open air. The priest said she was selfless. The mayor said she was a treasure. The baker — we will get to the baker — said she was the finest woman he had ever known, and he wept as he said it, and the weeping was real, and the finest-woman part was not, and both things were true at the same time because this is that kind of town and these are those kinds of people.

Odete left behind two daughters. Twins. This had never happened before — one Besteiro per generation was the rule, or at least the habit — and the town did not know what to do with the redundancy except to be performatively delighted about it. *Two protectors!* they said. *Twice the safety!* What they thought, in the parts of themselves they did not examine, was: *Good. If one breaks, we have a spare.*

The daughters' names are Mara and Lúz.

They are twenty-nine years old. They have their mother's hands — wide-palmed, blunt-fingered, hands that look like they were designed to catch things, which in a sense they were. They share a house at the eastern edge of town, close enough to the coffee slopes that the smell of the blossoms reaches them in October and, for a few weeks, makes the world seem like a place where things might work out.

They have not worked out. But I'm getting ahead of myself, and I promised I would be honest about where this goes, so let me say it now: this story does not end the way the town wants it to end. It does not end the way Mara wants it to end, either, or Lúz, or the Coven Mother, or — and the narrator admits this with genuine chagrin — the narrator. It ends the way it has to. The machinery was always going to produce this. The only question was what it would feel like when it did.

Let's begin with the baker's boil.

## II.

The hex arrived on a Tuesday, which Mara disliked on principle. Tuesdays were when the open market filled the square, when the jacaranda tree's shade became the town's living room, when every stall and voice and frying pan conspired to make São Bento feel like a place worth protecting. She preferred to work on Thursdays, when the square was empty and the only audience was pigeons.

But the Coven did not consult her calendar.

The baker — Senhor Alcides, sixty-one, proprietor of Padaria Santa Clara, famous for his *pão de queijo* and infamous for having once, in a moment of drunken candor, told his wife that her sister was the more attractive sibling in the hearing of both women — had woken that morning with a boil on his neck the approximate size and color of a plum. It throbbed with a rhythm that did not match his heartbeat, which was how you knew it was a hex and not a boil. A medical boil kept your time. A hexed boil kept its own.

Mara found him in the back of the padaria, sitting on a flour sack, his collar opened, the plum-thing pulsing. He did not greet her. He pointed at his neck. This was how it usually went.

"Tuesday," she said, by way of accepting.

She leaned close. The hex had a smell — they all did — something like burned sugar and old pennies, the Coven's signature, as distinctive as a thumbprint. She placed two fingers on the boil, and the boil *pushed back*, a small muscular contraction, alive and offended at her touch.

What happens next requires a brief vocabulary.

Eating a hex is not a metaphor. It is not "absorbing negative energy" or "cleansing the spiritual body" or any of the other gauze-soft phrases the town uses when it talks about what the Besteiro women do, which is rarely, and always in the conditional tense, as if it might not be happening at all. Eating a hex is *eating*. The curse detaches from the afflicted body — or from the object, the threshold, the water supply, wherever the Coven has planted it — and enters the Besteiro's mouth as a physical substance. It has texture. It has taste. It has, God help them, *mouthfeel*.

This one tasted like iron filings suspended in rancid lard.

Mara pulled it from Alcides's neck — the boil deflated instantly, leaving behind a circle of unmarked skin and one very relieved baker — and drew it into her mouth the way a child reluctantly accepts medicine: jaw tight, throat working, eyes focused on the middle distance. The hex went down. It always went down. That was the grotesque miracle of the Besteiro biology: no curse could resist the esophageal gravity of these women. Down, down, into the acid dark, where it would dissolve — slowly, painfully, over hours or days depending on severity — into something inert.

But first it fought.

Inside Mara's stomach, the little hex thrashed. She felt it the way you feel a cramp that is somehow also furious: a knot of intent, a pellet of someone else's will to harm, dissolving against the walls of her gut like an aspirin in vinegar. Her saliva went metallic. Her eyes watered. A headache, swift and blinding, clamped around her left temple — the Coven's hexes always attacked the left side first, a stylistic tic Mara had catalogued years ago and never found useful.

She breathed through it. Thirty seconds. A minute. The worst passed. This was a minor hex, a petty cruelty, barely worth the Coven's effort. It would be fully dissolved by evening. She would not vomit. She would not bleed from the nose. She would not spend three days in bed with a fever that smelled like ozone, which is what happened when the hexes were serious. This was, by the standards of her profession, a good day.

Now here is where the architecture of Mara's suffering reveals its second floor.

The telepathy had started when she was nineteen — three years after her mother's death, one year after she and Lúz had formally assumed the role, and exactly no time at all after she had begun to suspect that eating curses was doing something to her that eating curses had not done to Odete. (This was not, in fact, true. Odete had heard thoughts too. She had simply never told anyone, a secret so disciplined and so devastating that its discovery, years later, in a journal hidden behind the laundry basin, would be one of the things that fractured the hairline crack in Mara's sense of self into an open break. But that had already happened, and we are here now, in the padaria, and the baker is buttoning his collar.)

The thoughts came in like weather.

Not voices, exactly. Voices implied a singular source, a direction you could face or turn away from. This was omnidirectional. It was the cognitive equivalent of humidity — suddenly, oppressively everywhere, soaking through every barrier Mara had tried to build. She could not choose whose thoughts she heard, or when, or how loudly. Proximity was a factor. Emotional intensity was a factor. And the fresh consumption of a hex — any hex — seemed to amplify the reception the way a thunderstorm amplifies a radio signal, briefly and catastrophically boosting the range.

The hex was thirty seconds dissolved, and already the padaria's back room was filling with noise.

Alcides was thinking about his neck. The absence of pain. The relief — and it was genuine, she could feel its warmth, the sheer animal pleasure of a body restored to default — but beneath it, already, the relief was curdling into something else. She felt him looking at her. Not with his eyes, which were examining his collar button, but with the part of his mind that processed her presence, and what she heard there was:

*— smells like the hex now, always smells like them after, like she's carrying it around inside her, which I suppose she is, God forgive me but I don't know how Odete could stand to be touched after, I wonder if anyone touches this one, I wonder if anyone could —*

Mara's hands were steady. She was very good at steady hands.

*— doesn't even flinch, watch that, unnatural, her mother at least had the decency to look like it hurt, this one just stands there like a —*

His thought did not finish itself in words. It finished in an image, and the image was worse: Mara as something not quite human, a function wearing a woman's body, a municipal utility with Odete's hands. The image was not hateful. That would have been easier. It was *categorical*. Alcides was not a cruel man. He was a man who had, over the course of twenty years of receiving this service, developed a taxonomy in which the Besteiro women occupied a kingdom separate from his own. Not animal, not human. Something else. Something that eats what shouldn't be eaten and must therefore be constituted differently, down at the level of soul, if it could be said to have one, which —

Mara left the padaria.

The market square hit her like a wave.

Three hundred people, give or take. Three hundred minds, not all of them thinking about her, most of them thinking about produce and prices and children and lovers and the banality of weekday survival — but enough of them, *enough*, glancing up as she passed, noting the Besteiro girl, the one with the tired eyes, the one who always looked slightly ill, registering her the way you register a fire extinguisher mounted on a wall: *good that it's there, hope I never have to think about why* — and underneath, in the sediment layer, in the stratum where people kept the thoughts they'd never excavate voluntarily:

*Beast-keeper.* An old word used as a name. A name used as a verdict.

*— better her than me —*

*— wonder what she does with them, the curses, where do they go, she must be full of them by now, like a septic tank —*

*— the mother was the same, people forget, but I remember, she had that look, that gray look, like she was already composting —*

*— if we could just deal with the Coven directly, negotiate, maybe if we offered them —*

*— Lúz is the worse one, at least this one is quiet, the other one ENJOYS it and that's —*

*— my daughter asked why the Besteiro lady looks sick all the time, I said she doesn't, I said she's fine, I lied to my daughter about a woman who —*

The narrator would like to pause here.

Not for dramatic effect but out of a kind of structural courtesy, the way a surgeon might pause before the critical incision to let the room understand what is about to be cut. Mara Besteiro crosses the market square every Tuesday, and every Tuesday she hears this. Not a sampling. Not an edited highlight reel. *All of it*. The full, unabridged, high-fidelity output of three hundred human minds processing her proximity, and what that processing consistently produces — beneath the genuine smiles, beneath the offered fruit, beneath Dona Fernanda's weekly insistence that she stay for coffee — is a vast, humming, almost *consensual* revulsion that the town has never once discussed because the town has never once had to. The thoughts are private. The gratitude is public. The system works.

Mara went home.

She sat at the kitchen table.
