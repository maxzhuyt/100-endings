# The Wall of Napkins

---

I know how this sounds. I want you to understand that before I say anything else. I know exactly how this sounds, and I have known for — well. For most of my life. So you can stop looking at each other. I saw you look at each other when I sat down. That's fine. I would look at each other too.

Is it — yes, all right. The light is on. Good.

I want to begin with what everyone already knows, because what everyone already knows is the door, and if I don't go through the door you will not follow me into the room. So.

Nikola Tesla required that all things in his daily practice be divisible by three. Three napkins would not do. He required eighteen, which is three times three times two but more importantly is a number divisible by three, and they had to be stacked, not fanned, stacked in a single column to the left of his plate, and he would use them one at a time and refold each one into thirds before setting it aside. I can see you know this. Everyone knows this. It is in every article, every biography, every little magazine profile that treated him as — as what. As a broken clock that happened to keep perfect time.

He counted his steps before entering a building. If the count was wrong — wrong by his private arithmetic, not wrong by anyone else's — he would circle the block and begin again. He calculated the cubic volume of his food before eating. Not estimated. *Calculated.* He would not shake hands, and if you touched him without warning he would excuse himself and sometimes you would not see him for the rest of the day. He could not look at pearls. Pearls specifically. A woman wearing pearls could empty a room that contained Tesla faster than a fire.

There was also the matter of pigeons, but we will come to that or we won't.

This is the public record. These are the facts. Every one of these facts is correct and every one of them is wrong. Not the facts. The facts I cannot argue with. What they *were* is wrong. What every doctor and every journalist and every grinning biographer said they *meant* is wrong, because they looked at a man building a wall and they described a man having a seizure. They saw the bricks going up and they wrote about the trembling of his hands instead of asking what was on the other side.

I watched him build it. That is what I am here to tell you. I watched him lay every brick, and I know what was on the other side, because I heard it, once, very briefly, through a crack — and it was enough.

Every compulsion was a brick. Every ritual was mortar. Every set of three, every counted step, every folded napkin was a piece of a fortification that he maintained every waking hour of his life from the year 1899 until the night he died in that hotel room with no one in it except a pigeon and whatever was left of his mind. He was not mad. He was under siege.

I am very tired of being the only person who knows this.

---

Colorado Springs. Eighteen ninety-nine.

You know the story. Everyone knows the story, or the shape of it — the great laboratory on Knob Hill, the two-hundred-foot mast, the lightning he pulled from the sky. The wireless transmission of energy. The Promethean business. I am not interested in the Promethean business.

I was not there. I did not meet Tesla until — I will tell you when. But over the months that I knew him, he let pieces of it slip, the way a man with a splinter in his eye will sometimes tilt his head at an angle that tells you which side it's on without his ever mentioning the pain. He did not tell me what happened in Colorado Springs. He let me *reconstruct* what happened in Colorado Springs. There is a difference. A confession is a thing a man gives you. What I got, I had to steal, a sentence at a time, over many months, and then assemble in the decades after I last saw him, turning the pieces over and over the way a watchmaker turns gears he is not sure belong to the same clock.

I believe — I have come to believe — that during a specific resonance test, a high-frequency atmospheric oscillation experiment, something in the equipment or in the frequency or in the particular conditions of that night — altitude, air ionization, the storm pattern, I don't know, I am not a physicist, I *was* trained as an electrical engineer but I was never a physicist — something aligned in a way Tesla had not predicted, and for several minutes the boundary of his mind was — the word he used once was *abolished*. Not opened. That would imply a door, a controllable passage. *Abolished.* As in: the wall was there and then it was not there and everything on the other side came through.

Not thoughts. He was very precise about this, the one time he came close to describing it directly. Not words. Not sentences. Not the clear bright cognition that you imagine when you imagine reading someone's mind. What came through was underneath all of that. The — the *want.* The raw appetite. The underbelly. He said — I will tell you exactly what he said, because I wrote it down that night and I have read it so many times the paper is translucent — he said: "It was not what they were thinking. It was what they were *being*, underneath the thinking, and it was not clean."

Lust. Greed. The small, petty territorial jealousies. The cowardice — he spoke about the cowardice often, how it was the loudest frequency, how human fear had a particular quality of adhesion, how it stuck to him in a way that the other emanations did not. The self-pity. The irrationality of desire — wanting things that would destroy the wanter, and knowing this, and wanting them more for the knowing. The entire reeking underlayer of human consciousness, he said, pouring through him as though he had been a locked room for forty-three years and someone had knocked out every wall simultaneously.

The episode in its acute form lasted minutes. The sensitivity never fully receded. He could dampen it. He learned to dampen it. But the dampening was not a cure; it was a practice, an hourly discipline, and every one of those peculiarities that the biographers would later catalogue so gleefully was a technique of dampening. The sets of three were a focusing rhythm. The counting of steps was a concentrative barrier erected at the threshold of every building, because buildings contained people and people were the source. The eighteen napkins were — well. I'll come to the napkins.

He left Colorado Springs early. The public explanation was that his funding was in dispute, that his relationship with his backers had soured. His funding *was* in dispute. His relationship with his backers *had* soured — because he could feel, suddenly and involuntarily, the precise texture of their contempt for him, their calculations about how to exploit his patents, their small private glee at his dependence on their money. He could feel all of it, and he could not *unfeel* it.

He told me it was like standing in a sewer and discovering the sewer was inside every person you had ever admired. He said it exactly like that. I wrote it down. I wrote everything down.

[There is a pause on the recording here. When Petrović resumes, his voice is lower, and there is a sound that might be a glass being set down on wood.]

I want to say that I do not enjoy telling you this. I have imagined telling someone this for — for longer than you have been alive, probably, either of you, and in my imagination it was a relief, like lancing something, and it is not a relief. It is just the same knowledge in a different room.

---

I came to Tesla's laboratory in New York through a university connection — Professor Šimić, who had studied at Graz in the years after Tesla and had maintained a distant, largely epistolary relationship with him. I was twenty-three. I had finished my studies in electrical engineering in Zagreb and had come to America with the kind of ambition that now seems to me indistinguishable from stupidity. Šimić wrote a letter. Tesla, who was by then in his fifties and had difficulty retaining assistants — this was 1912, I should say, the laboratory on South Fifth Avenue, not the later one — Tesla accepted me for a probationary period.

I expected grandeur. I expected the cathedral of invention. What I found was a system of absences.

No one touched Tesla. This was understood by every member of the laboratory staff with the reflexive depth that a child understands not to touch a stove — not because anyone had explained the rule formally, but because the consequences of violating it were so immediate and so visible that the rule had become atmospheric. If you touched Tesla unintentionally — brushed his arm reaching for an instrument, stumbled into him in the narrow passage between the main bench and the storage cabinets — he would go still. Completely still, the way I have since seen animals go still. And then he would leave the room, and you would not see him for one hour, or two hours, or the rest of the day.

No one stood within a particular radius of him. I measured it, later, out of curiosity. It was approximately four feet, though it contracted and expanded depending on — I now believe — how much dampening he had managed that day. On bad days the radius was larger. On very bad days he did not come to the laboratory at all, and we would receive instructions by note, written in his meticulous hand, delivered by the hotel.

Specific objects occupied specific positions and were never moved. Every instrument on his bench had a location that was precise to the centimeter, and the positions were not ergonomic — they were not arranged for ease of use. They were arranged in patterns of three. Three instruments to a row. Three rows to a section. The overall geometry of his workspace, if you looked at it from above, which I once did by climbing to the mezzanine when he was out, was a kind of grid, a lattice, and the regularity of it was — I did not have the word then. Now I would call it *defensive.* It looked like a man had organized his physical environment into a pattern that could be held in the mind as a single shape, so that the mind had something rigid to hold onto while everything else poured through.

The other assistants shrugged. "The old man has his ways," Czito said — Kolman Czito, who had been with Tesla the longest and who I now believe understood more than he let on, though he and I never spoke of it directly. "Don't move his things, don't touch him, don't wear perfume, don't bring anyone unannounced, and you'll be fine." He said this cheerfully, the way a zookeeper describes the temperament of a dangerous animal he has come to love.

I got used to it. That is what I want to emphasize. I got used to all of it within weeks, and the getting used to it is what should have frightened me, because it meant I had stopped asking what the rules were *for.* I admired Tesla. I admired him the way a young man admires a great older one — with a hunger that is partly intellectual and partly something else, something closer to the desire to be *recognized* by the admired figure as worthy of inheriting whatever it is they carry. I wanted Tesla to see me. I wanted to be the one he chose to teach. This desire is relevant to everything that happened after.

I noticed things. I noticed that after meetings with investors — George Scherff would arrange these, and Tesla would tolerate them, but only on specific days, only in specific rooms, only with the eighteen napkins and the calculated food and the precise geometry of the table setting — after these meetings, Tesla's face had a quality I could not name then and can name now only by analogy. He looked like a man who had been forced to swallow something. Not food. Something that was still alive inside him and was thrashing.

I noticed that his hands trembled after unexpected physical contact.

I noticed that he avoided windows that faced the street during the hours when the street was crowded.

I noticed that on the evenings when he worked latest — eleven, twelve, sometimes past one o'clock, when the city outside had thinned to the occasional cab and the distant sound of the elevated train — his shoulders were different. Lower. Looser. As though a weight had been lifted, or a noise had stopped. He was a different man at one o'clock in the morning than he was at one o'clock in the afternoon, and the difference was the difference between a man in pain and a man in the memory of pain, which is not the same as a man without pain but which is, at least, breathable.

I am saying all of this to describe the atmosphere. The atmosphere was everything. The laboratory was not a place of invention; it was a scaffolding of rituals, and every ritual held something up, and what it held up was a man who should have — by his own accounting — collapsed decades earlier.

And then the small moment.

It was late. February, cold, the laboratory heated by the large iron radiator that clanked and spat. I had stayed to finish winding a set of coils — tertiary work, the kind given to junior assistants. I thought I was alone. I came around the corner from the supply room and Tesla was at his bench, sitting very straight, his hands flat on the surface in front of him, his eyes closed. He was not sleeping. He was — I think now he was *holding*. Holding the wall in place. Managing the bricks through some act of interior effort I will never fully understand.

He opened his eyes and saw me. And for a moment — it could not have been more than two or three seconds — he looked at me with an expression I did not recognize and could not have decoded if I tried. It was not surprise. It was not irritation. It was — I didn't have the frame for it then. I was twenty-three and I did not yet know what recognition looks like on the face of a man who recognizes something he wishes he didn't.

"Petrović," he said. Just my name. And then: "Do you sleep well?"

I told him I did.

"And your dreams," he said. "Are they — quiet?"

I laughed. I laughed because I did not know what else to do with such an odd question from such an odd man at such an odd hour, and I said yes, that I was a heavy sleeper, that my roommate complained that a cannon could go off and I would not stir.

Tesla nodded. He looked at me for one more second. Then he turned back to his bench and said, without looking up, "Good. That is good. Go home. It is late."

I did not think about this exchange again for nearly six months.

---

The streetcar was the first thing.

I rode the Sixth Avenue line to the laboratory each morning, and in March — I am almost certain it was March, because the windows were still closed against the cold and the car was dense with bodies — I began to notice that the ride made me ill. Not dramatically. Not the kind of illness you would report. A headache that began at the base of my skull and worked forward. A sensation of pressure, as though my ears needed to pop but would not. A general irritability that arrived when I boarded and dissipated, slowly, over the first half hour at the laboratory.

I told myself it was the crowding. I had never liked crowds. I told myself it was the ventilation, the winter air thick with coal smoke and unwashed wool.

But the headache had a quality to it that I could not fully explain, even to myself. It was not uniform. It *shifted.* It moved around the car with the passengers. When the heavy man with the leather case boarded at Twenty-Third Street, the pressure intensified, concentrated on the left side of my head, and then when he pushed past me to find a seat, it migrated as he moved. When the car emptied at Bleecker, the headache eased as though someone had opened a valve.

I mentioned it to no one. I am not — I was not a hysterical person. I was trained in engineering. I believed in instruments and measurements and things that could be demonstrated. A migrating headache was not a thing I could describe to anyone without sounding like a woman with the vapors, which is a terrible thing to say and I apologize for it, but I want to be honest about the kind of man I was then: a young man terrified of being thought unserious.

The restaurant was worse. I went to Kavalír's, a Czech place on East Seventy-Third Street where the food reminded me of home, and I sat at my usual table by the window and could not eat. Not because the food was wrong. Because the couple at the next table — a man and a woman, well-dressed, smiling at each other, to all appearances perfectly happy — were radiating something. I do not know how else to say this, and I have spent fifty years looking for a better word than *radiating* and there is not one. They were radiating a quality that I perceived not through my ears or my eyes or any organ I could name but through some other channel that had apparently *opened* in me without my knowledge or consent
