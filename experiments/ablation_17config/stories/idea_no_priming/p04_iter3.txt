# The Teacup Moves

---

## I.

### Novosibirsk, 1988

---

The apartment is eleven meters by four and a half, which is adequate for one person. The ceiling is two meters fifty-five. The radiator beneath the window is a KSK-20 model, installed in 1974 or possibly 1975, and it ticks in intervals she has never timed but has come to anticipate the way one anticipates the next beat of a pulse—not consciously, but with the body's dumb knowledge that the next sound will come and then the next. Outside the window, the temperature is minus eighteen, though it may be minus twenty; the broadcast this morning was unclear, or she was not listening closely. She was filling the kettle. The kettle is aluminum, one and a half liters, manufactured in Chelyabinsk. It has a dent on the left side near the base. She did not put it there. It arrived with the dent.

Yelena Voroshilova sat at the table and drank tea. Georgian blend, second grade, purchased Tuesday from the Univermag on Krasny Prospekt, the same day she collected her pension. The pension was sixty-four rubles per month. It went into her coat pocket in an envelope. She walked home. She did not take the tram because the tram involved standing near people in a confined space, and while nothing would happen—nothing had happened for years, nothing was going to happen—she preferred to walk.

She held the cup with her right hand. Her fingers were dry, the knuckles wide. She drank. She set the cup down. The surface of the tea shifted with the tap of porcelain against wood and then went still. Everything in the apartment was still. She had become a woman of extremely still rooms.

On the shelf above the sink, between a box of soda and a jar with eleven rubles in kopek coins she was saving for no specific purpose, there was a letter. The paper had been folded and unfolded so many times that the central crease had gone translucent, a pale vein through the ink where the fibers had worn to gauze. The letter was dated 14 March 1985 and signed *Katya*. The handwriting was careful—a schoolgirl's hand, though Katya would have been fourteen when she wrote it, no longer a schoolgirl in any sense that the word usually means. The letter said:

*Mama, I am well. The dormitory is heated now and we received new blankets in January. My marks in mathematics are average. My marks in literature are good. Valentina Petrovna says I read aloud well. I think of you. I hope you are well. I do not need anything but if you are able to send wool socks before March I would be grateful, the ones here itch.*

Yelena had read this letter perhaps one hundred and fifty times. Perhaps more. She had not sent the socks. She had purchased the socks—gray wool, women's size thirty-seven, which she estimated from memory because Katya's feet had still been children's feet the last time Yelena had bought her shoes, and three years could change the bones of a foot considerably—and she had wrapped them in brown paper and written the address of the boarding institution in Barnaul on the front, and then she had stood in the post office on Sovetskaya Street for eleven minutes and left without mailing the package because she could not determine whether contact from her would improve her daughter's life or damage it further. The package sat in the bottom drawer of the dresser. The socks were still wrapped.

She had not sent a reply to the letter either. This was not because she did not want to write. She had written, in her mind, hundreds of letters. She had composed them while walking to the pension office, while standing in the bread queue, while lying in the dark on the narrow bed listening to the KSK-20 tick. The letters said things she would never commit to paper because paper could be found, and read, and filed: *I am the same as I was. I have not changed. I love you in a way that the word love, as I was taught to use it, does not describe, because the word implies a thing that can be given and received between two people who are allowed to be in the same room, and I do not know if we are allowed.* She did not write these letters. She drank her tea. She washed her cup. She set it upside down on the drying cloth and it sat there, on the counter, going nowhere.

Her neighbor in the adjacent apartment was a woman named Rufina Grigoryevna who worked at the bread factory on the second shift. They did not speak, but they also did not avoid each other, and Yelena had come to understand this as a significant social achievement. When they passed in the corridor, Rufina Grigoryevna nodded—a compression of the chin toward the sternum, held for approximately half a second—and Yelena nodded in return and neither of them altered their pace. This was, Yelena understood, a form of coexistence. It was not friendship. It was not hostility. It was the condition of being two people who occupied adjacent spaces and had silently agreed to make no demands on each other's attention. Yelena was grateful for it in a way she could not have explained to anyone, because explaining it would require describing the years in which such a nod was not available to her, and she did not describe those years, not to anyone, not even in the letters she composed in her mind and did not send.

The overhead bulb was sixty watts. Under its light, the apartment was the color of weak tea. The walls were papered in a pattern of pale green diamonds that she had not chosen. The linoleum was beige with flecks meant to suggest stone. The furniture—a table, two chairs (though she used only one; the second had become a surface for folded laundry), a dresser, the bed—had been assigned with the apartment. None of it was hers in any sense other than proximity. She sat among these objects and the objects did not move and she did not move them.

Two point seven to three point one centimeters. This was what she could do: she could move a small aluminum disc approximately the width of two fingers. It took effort. It gave her a headache. Sometimes her nose bled. The disc moved, and then it stopped, and then a man in a laboratory coat wrote the number down.

She finished her tea. She rinsed the cup under the tap, which produced water of a temperature she would describe as not-quite-cold, and placed the cup upside down on the cloth. The cup was white with a thin blue line around the rim. It sat on the counter and did not move. Nothing in the apartment moved. The radiator ticked. Outside, Novosibirsk went about the business of being a city in January, and none of it had anything to do with her, and this was the arrangement she had made with the world, or the arrangement the world had made with her: she would be still, and the world would leave her alone, and this would continue until it ended.

She sat in the chair—her chair, the one without laundry—and looked at the place on the counter where the cup sat and did not move, and she did not move it.

## II.

### Novosibirsk, 1985–1986

---

The letter was dated 3 September 1985. It bore the official heading of the Zheleznodorozhny District Housing Committee, the reference number 8841/zh-K, and the signatures of twelve residents of Building 14, Entrance 3, Ulitsa Titova. Yelena held it with both hands. The paper was a standard А4 sheet, slightly rough, the ink from a typewriter whose *o* struck a fraction too deep, leaving small perforations in the page like insect holes.

She read:

> *To the Zheleznodorozhny District Housing Committee,*
>
> *We, the undersigned residents of Building 14, Entrance 3, Ul. Titova, respectfully submit this collective appeal regarding the continued residence of citizen Ye. S. Voroshilova (Apartment 27) in our building.*
>
> *Over a period of several years, the presence of citizen Voroshilova has created conditions of persistent social and psychological disturbance among residents of our entrance. It is well established, through both institutional documentation and the direct observations of multiple signatories, that citizen Voroshilova possesses biological capabilities of an anomalous and unclassified nature, the precise character of which remains a matter of ongoing institutional uncertainty. The absence of a definitive medical or scientific resolution to citizen Voroshilova's status has resulted in an atmosphere of anxiety incompatible with the collective well-being of our residential community.*
>
> *We emphasize that this appeal is motivated not by personal animosity toward citizen Voroshilova but by legitimate concern for the psychological health of residents, particularly children, who are exposed to ongoing uncertainty regarding the nature and safety of citizen Voroshilova's condition. We note that multiple residents have reported sleep disturbance, elevated anxiety, and reluctance among children to use common areas of the building unaccompanied.*
>
> *We respectfully request that the Housing Committee initiate proceedings for the administrative relocation of citizen Voroshilova to alternative accommodations, in accordance with Article 37 of the RSFSR Housing Code, on grounds of incompatibility with collective residential norms.*
>
> *Signed:*

The names.

Yelena knew the names. She read them the way one reads a list of the dead—not scanning, but stopping at each one, testing its weight.

P. A. Kravtsova. Apartment 24. Third floor. Polina Andreyevna, who was sixty-one years old and kept geraniums on her windowsill and who, in the winter of 1979, had slipped on the landing between the second and third floors and Yelena had heard the sound—a specific crack, not of bone, which would come later at the hospital, but of the bag of potatoes Polina Andreyevna had been carrying hitting the concrete—and had come out of her door and helped the woman to her feet, holding her by the elbow, and the potatoes had rolled down the stairs and Yelena had collected them one by one while Polina Andreyevna sat on the step weeping from surprise rather than from pain, and Yelena had said, *Sit, sit, I have them all, I think this one is bruised but the rest are fine*, and they had laughed slightly—two women on a staircase with a bag of potatoes—and none of this was in the letter, and none of this had been enough.

V. K. Shulgin. Apartment 22. Second floor. Valery Konstantinovich, whose son Dima had played with Katya in the courtyard every afternoon for three years. They had built a snow fort together in the winter of 1980 that reached Yelena's waist. From the kitchen window she had watched them packing snow, their mittens soaked dark, and Mikhail had come up behind her and said, *They'll need dry things when they come in*, and she had set two pairs of wool socks on the radiator, one for each child. And now Valery Konstantinovich had put his name beneath the word *deviant* and the phrase *atmosphere of anxiety*, and Dima would be fifteen now and did not play in the courtyard anymore.

The list continued. Twelve names. Twelve apartments. A. F. Moseychuk, the building's Party liaison, who once tasted Yelena's pickled garlic at a building social event and asked for the recipe, writing it down on the back of a cinema ticket. T. R. Bogdanova, who had borrowed Yelena's sewing machine in 1978 and returned it with a small box of chocolates as thanks. Each name a thread pulled from a fabric she had once believed was solid, and now she could see that it had never been solid, that it had always been provisional—a web of ordinary decencies that held together only as long as the ordinariness was not tested.

---

The meeting was held in the building's common room on the ground floor, a space that normally served for residents' committee gatherings, occasional lectures from the housing committee representative, and, once a year, the New Year's celebration where someone brought an accordion and the children were given tangerines. It smelled of paint and the particular sweetness of damp concrete. Fourteen metal folding chairs had been arranged in a rough semicircle. Yelena sat in a chair at the open end of the arc. She had been told to attend. The notification had been slipped under her door, handwritten on a quarter-sheet of lined paper: *Residents' meeting, 18:00, 11 September. Your attendance is required.*

She arrived at 17:55. Several people were already seated. None of them looked at her as she entered, which was itself a communication—a coordinated refusal of acknowledgment that required, she understood, more effort than acknowledgment would have. To not look at someone entering a room, twelve people must each independently decide where else to direct their eyes: at the floor, at their hands, at the window, at the person beside them. The choreography of avoidance was complex and imprecise. V. K. Shulgin stared at his knees with the expression of a man trying to become his own furniture. T. R. Bogdanova, the woman with the sewing machine and the chocolates, was speaking rapidly and quietly to her husband about something involving a broken washing machine, and the conversation had the frantic energy of a topic generated for the sole purpose of filling a silence that would otherwise be unbearable.

A. F. Moseychuk, the Party liaison, stood at the front. He was fifty-three, with a heavy face and the hands of someone who had once done physical labor and now did paperwork. He held a clipboard. He did not look at Yelena either, but his not-looking was different from the others'—more practiced, more administrative. He was not avoiding her. He was *processing* her.

"Comrades," he said. "We are here to discuss the collective appeal submitted on September third regarding the residential status of citizen Voroshilova."

She sat with her hands in her lap. Her coat was still buttoned because the common room was cold and because removing it would imply she intended to be comfortable, and she did not intend to be comfortable, and she did not want anyone to think she had confused this meeting with an occasion where comfort was available to her.

Moseychuk read the letter aloud. Hearing it was different from reading it. On paper, the words had been inert, administrative—language doing the work of procedure.
