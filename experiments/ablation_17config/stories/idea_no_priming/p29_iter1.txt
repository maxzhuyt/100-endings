# The Garden of Rotting Wards

---

## I.

The tea is a Darjeeling, which I mention because it matters — or rather, because I want it to matter, because I have spent the better part of a decade insisting that the small things matter, and if I stop insisting now the whole business falls apart. It's autumn in Vermont. The radiator clicks. The window over the kitchen sink looks out onto a sugar maple that has, this week, achieved the particular red that exists nowhere else on Earth and which I am convinced God created on a day He was feeling less ambiguous than usual. I have a cat. I have a pension. I have two shelves of books I'll never finish and a tin of biscuits I shouldn't open. I am telling you this so you understand: I am fine. I came through. Whatever you think this story is about, I want you to hold that fact in your hand like a warm stone. Father Mick Donovan, sixty-one years old, alive, intact, making tea with the fussy precision of a man who has decided that fussy precision is a theological position.

Now. The funny story.

In the spring of 1953, the United States Army — that great and lumbering organism, that beast of paperwork and good intentions and the occasional atrocity — needed to formally decommission a field hospital in the Philippine interior. Camp Gruber Annex, it was called on the maps, though nobody I ever met called it that. The men who built it in '43 called it the Gut Factory, for reasons that will become obvious. The Filipinos in the nearest barangay — seven miles of bad road — called it something I was told not to write down, and when I asked my friend Father Reyes to translate, he said the closest English equivalent was "the place that is still chewing."

I should explain why a hospital needed decommissioning at all. The war ended in '45. Most facilities were shut down, stripped, handed over, or simply abandoned within a year. Camp Gruber Annex was abandoned. It was enormous — built to serve a campaign that ate through men at a rate the planners had, for once, accurately predicted — and it sat in a stretch of jungle that did not forgive the intrusion. By 1946 the jungle was taking it back. By 1950 you could stand at what had been the main gate and see nothing but green. Canopy had closed over the surgical block. Vines had entered the autoclave rooms through the ventilation shafts and come out through the windows. The jungle did not reclaim Camp Gruber Annex. It *digested* it.

But the paperwork had never been filed. Somewhere in an office in Manila, and then in an office in Washington, the hospital existed as an open line item — still active on paper, still technically in operation, a ghost in the Army's filing system drawing phantom supplies. This happens more often than you'd think. The military is an organism that can lose a limb and keep sending blood to it for years.

Three teams were sent to close the file. I want to be precise about this, because the Army was not precise about it, and imprecision is how institutions hide their sins.

The first team went in August of 1951. Four men, a routine survey and inventory detail. They were to spend seventy-two hours cataloguing remaining equipment and structures, fill out forms DA-161 through DA-165, take photographs, and leave. They returned on schedule. Their paperwork was immaculate. Their photographs were in focus. Within three weeks, all four had requested transfers to combat postings — urgent, insistent, in language their commanding officers described as "uncharacteristically aggressive." One of them, a supply clerk named Wendell who had spent four happy years managing a warehouse in Subic Bay, told his CO that he'd been "playing pretend" and needed to "do the real thing." He was in Korea by Christmas. He died at Heartbreak Ridge in October, having, by all accounts, exposed himself to fire with a determination his squad leader called "not brave, exactly" in the after-action report. The squad leader crossed that out and wrote "courageous."

The second team went in February of 1952. Six men, this time with a demolition assessment. They came back in five days instead of the scheduled three. Their paperwork was incomplete. When asked why, the team leader — a decorated engineer named Villanueva who had built bridges under fire on Luzon and was by every measure a serious and competent man — said only that they had "realized the inventory approach was insufficient" and that the site needed to be "purified, not catalogued." He used that word. Purified. He then requested transfer to a combat unit. So did three of the others. Villanueva was denied. He went AWOL four months later and was found, alive but uncommunicative, in a bar in Olongapo, where he had apparently been telling anyone who would listen that he had "finally understood what the hospital was teaching" but refused to say what the lesson was. He was discharged. I tried to find him later. I couldn't.

The third team went in June of 1952. Eight men, including a psychologist — the Army had noticed the pattern by now, though it described the pattern with the phrase "morale disruption consistent with environmental stress factors." The psychologist, a Major Easterbrook, was to observe the team for signs of this disruption and intervene as needed. They stayed the full duration. When they returned, Major Easterbrook filed a report that was, I'm told, thirty-seven pages long. I was never allowed to read it. I was told it concluded that the site produced "an accelerated identification with combat-normative masculinity frameworks resulting in ego-restructuring events." When I asked Colonel Hargrove what that meant in English, he lit a cigarette, stared at me for a long time, and said: "They come back thinking they're finally real soldiers, Father. Every damn one of them. They come back thinking everything before was fake and the only real thing is what the jungle told them."

I said: "What does the jungle tell them?"

Hargrove said: "That the soft part of them is the part that's lying."

Seven of the eight requested combat transfers. Major Easterbrook did not. Major Easterbrook resigned his commission, left the military entirely, and entered, of all things, a Trappist monastery in Kentucky. I wrote to him once. He wrote back a single line: *It is better to say nothing for the rest of your life than to say what that place wants you to say.*

So. Three teams. Nineteen men. All of them came back — I want to stress this — *functional*. Healthy. Lucid. Able to fill out forms and tie their shoes and describe their experience in language that was, if you didn't listen too carefully, perfectly ordinary. They simply came back having killed something essential inside themselves and calling the killing a promotion.

The Army, to its credit — or its bureaucratic compulsion, which is the Army's version of credit — decided to send a fourth team. A smaller one, this time. Five people. And someone in some office had the notion, which I've always thought was either inspired or sadistic, that the team should include a chaplain. The reasoning, as it was explained to me, was that prior teams had lacked "spiritual resilience resources." I believe that is the only time in the history of the United States military that anyone has described me as a resource.

I was volunteered. That is the military's word for being told.

I said yes, which is the chaplain's word for not having a choice.

I assembled the team myself. They gave me that. They said: "You can pick your four, Father." I think they expected me to choose hard men, seasoned men, men who might resist whatever the jungle was selling. I did not. I chose the four people I liked best in the entire Pacific Theater, and if that sounds like a sentimental reason, I want you to understand that I chose it as a *strategy*, though I could not have told you why at the time. I think I believed — and I am not sure I was wrong — that whatever was out there fed on something that lived in the space *between* people, in the gaps where trust should be, and that the best defense was not toughness but the kind of easy, stupid, unearned affection that makes a group of strangers into something the military has no form for.

I was right about that. I was also wrong about it, in the way that is worse than being simply wrong, which is being right about the medicine and wrong about the dosage.

I should tell you now: I survived. I told you that already. I am telling you again because I need you to hold it.

I am not yet ready to tell you who else did.

## II.

I'll start with Lila Aurbach, because she was the first one I asked and because she said yes before I'd finished the sentence, which was characteristic.

Lieutenant Aurbach was a nurse — surgical, technically, though by 1953 she'd been reassigned to records management at Clark Air Base because she'd made the mistake of being too good at keeping people alive under fire and then having the further misfortune of being a woman about it. She'd served at a forward aid station on Okinawa, eighteen years old and already more competent than any three surgeons in the building, and she'd held men together — I mean that literally, she'd held organs inside bodies with her hands — for thirty-six straight hours during the last push, and when it was over and the commendations were written, her name appeared on none of them. The citations went to the doctors. Lila's name appeared on the nursing log. In the nursing log, she was "Lt. Aurbach (asst.)."

She never complained about this. That was the thing about Lila that broke your heart if you paid attention. She never complained because she had, somewhere deep in the architecture of herself, accepted the premise: that what she'd done was assisting. That holding a nineteen-year-old boy's liver inside his body while a surgeon's hands shook too badly to suture was a supporting role. She carried this belief the way some people carry a stone in their shoe — she'd adjusted her gait around it, and if you didn't look closely you'd never know she was limping.

What you'd notice instead was that Lila was *funny*. Funny in the way of people who have decided that if they can't be taken seriously they will at least be enjoyable. She did voices. She could do Hargrove — that sandpaper drawl, the way he paused before saying something stupid as though he were pausing before saying something wise — so well that I once laughed tea out of my nose, which is an experience I recommend to no one. She had a habit of narrating her own actions in the third person when she was nervous: "Lieutenant Aurbach approaches the filing cabinet with caution. Lieutenant Aurbach suspects the filing cabinet contains spiders. Lieutenant Aurbach would like to remind the United States Army that she ain't getting combat pay for this."

She kept a photograph of her mother in her footlocker. Whenever she looked at it, she tucked her hands behind her back, and I never knew why, and I never asked, and I should have.

I asked her because she was brave and didn't know it, and because she made me laugh, and because I thought laughter might be a weapon against whatever we were walking into. I was not wrong.

Tommy Navarro was a corporal, twenty-three, second-generation Filipino-American from Stockton, California, and he was the finest illustrator I have ever seen in or out of uniform. He'd been assigned to the Army Corps of Engineers' cartography unit, where his job was to produce technical drawings of bridges and fortifications, and what he produced were *works of art* — maps so beautiful that his commanding officer once said, in a tone that was not a compliment, "Navarro, this isn't a gallery, it's a goddamn war." Tommy had been gently and then less gently redirected away from anything that required creativity and toward pure mechanical reproduction: copying existing plans, duplicating forms, tracing maps drawn by men with less talent and more willingness to make their work look like work rather than something that might hang in a museum.

Tommy's wound was the one the military inflicts on anyone who makes beautiful things: the relentless, cheerful insistence that beauty is frivolous. He'd internalized it as a body posture — he hunched, he made himself smaller, he held his pencils like he was apologizing for them. When he drew — when he really drew, when no one was watching — his entire body changed. His shoulders came down. His breathing slowed. He became, for those minutes, the person he was supposed to be. Then someone would walk in and he'd snap the sketchbook shut and hunch again, and the transformation was so fast and so practiced that it made me want to weep.

He told a joke once — the only joke I ever heard him initiate, rather than respond to — about a man who goes to heaven and God shows him all the lives he could have lived, and the man says, "Can I see the one where I wasn't so afraid?" and God says, "You're looking at it." Tommy told this joke and then laughed too loudly and then went quiet for the rest of the evening, and I thought: *that boy is carrying something he thinks is a luxury and it's actually a lung.*

I asked Tommy because I wanted him to draw the hospital for our report, which was true, and because I wanted him to have three days in the field away from men who thought beauty was a synonym for weakness, which was also true and more important.

---

Doc — everyone called him Doc, and I will not break the habit now — was Master Sergeant Raymond Delacroix, forty-four, a medic who had seen more of the war than anyone else on the team and who carried that seeing like a man carrying water in his cupped hands: carefully, silently, aware that every step spilled some. He'd been at Bataan. He'd survived the march. He'd spent three years in a Japanese prison camp tending to men who died under his hands with regularity and variety that I will not detail here because Doc never detailed it and I will not do to him what he would not do to himself.

What the war had given Doc was competence — a profound, almost mechanical ability to assess damage, prioritize treatment, and act without hesitation. What the war had taken from Doc was the ability to believe that his competence *mattered*. He had saved, by his own rough count, something over two hundred men across the course of the war. He had lost more. The mathematics of triage meant that he had, on multiple occasions, *chosen* who would live — chosen by walking to one stretcher and not another, by spending his last morphine syrette on this man and not that one. These choices were correct. They were medically sound. They followed protocol. They were also, in Doc's private accounting, murders, and no amount of chaplaining I did — and I tried, Lord knows I tried — could shift that ledger.

Doc's wound was guilt wearing the uniform of stoicism. He spoke quietly, moved efficiently, and maintained at all times the calm, competent demeanor of a man in complete control of himself, and underneath that calm was a scream that had been going on for ten years and that he managed by simply never stopping *doing*. If his hands were occupied — rolling bandages, cleaning instruments, inventorying supplies, fixing a broken radio, repairing someone's boot — the scream was bearable. If his hands were still, it was not. I never once saw Doc sit with empty hands. He whittled. He sewed. He sorted and re-sorted his medical kit with the attention of a jeweler. His hands were always working, and the work was always a prayer, though he would have punched me gently in the shoulder if I'd said so.

I asked Doc because he was the steadiest man I knew and because steady men are the most brittle, and I thought he deserved someone watching out for him while he watched out for everyone else.
