# Buttons

---

The badge printer hummed a cheerful little song while it thought, and Darla loved it immediately. She loved the woman behind the lobby desk who told her the printer was "having one of its mornings," as if it were a cat or a toddler. She loved the fern by the elevator, which was enormous and dramatic and looked like it had opinions. She loved that the building smelled faintly of something she couldn't place — something clean and warm, like dryer sheets mixed with the air right after a rain — and she decided it meant the ventilation system was *excellent*, which meant someone at Helios Genomics cared about ventilation, which meant they cared about *people*.

The badge emerged with her photo already on it. In the photo she was smiling, which was convenient because she hadn't stopped.

"Fourth floor," the lobby woman said. "Gerald will meet you at the elevators."

"Gerald," Darla repeated, savoring it. Every name was a door. Every door today was going to open.

She had been temping for three years, which was long enough that certain agencies had started describing her file with adjectives like *resilient* and *adaptable* and, once, in a margin note she wasn't supposed to see, *relentlessly positive — place anywhere*. She had been placed at a dental supply warehouse. She had been placed at a bankruptcy law firm. She had been placed, for one galvanizing week, at an animal crematorium where the hold music was Enya. She had liked all of these. She had liked them specifically and genuinely and without defense. The dental warehouse had natural light. The bankruptcy firm had a woman named Sheila who made incredible lemon bars. The crematorium had Enya, which Darla already loved, and also a very gentle orientation on the dignified handling of remains, and Darla had thought: *Someone thought hard about how to say this kindly. That counts.*

Now she rode the elevator up with her badge clipped to her blouse and her purse held in both hands and her face arranged in the expression it always came home to when left alone, which was something between wonder and gratitude, and if anyone had been in the elevator with her they might have found it unnerving, all that sincerity aimed at nothing. But Darla wasn't aiming. That was the thing people misunderstood. She wasn't performing. She was simply, metabolically, at the cellular level, a person who found the world mostly good and was delighted to keep being proven right.

The elevator opened onto a floor of glass and blond wood and the kind of strategic open-plan sprawl that said *we collaborate* while meaning *we watch*. A man stood waiting. He was mid-forties, slim, wearing a shirt so blue it seemed to be trying harder than he was. His smile activated the moment the doors parted — a precise, spring-loaded smile, the kind that used specific muscles and knew their names.

"Darla! Welcome, welcome. I'm Gerald. Gerald Forsythe. We are *so glad* you're here."

He shook her hand, and his grip was perfect, which is to say practiced, which is to say there was a version of Gerald up at two in the morning reading an article about handshake pressure and thinking, *This is the year I get it right*, and then there was the Gerald extending his hand now with the full performance of effortless warmth, and if you could see both of these Geralds at once — the one who rehearsed and the one who delivered — you would understand why his left eyelid twitched, just slightly, in the pause between the shake and the release. But that would require seeing inside him, and Darla, who saw the twitch, thought: *He seems really nice. Maybe a little nervous. First-day jitters aren't just for temps!*

She beamed at him. Gerald's eyelid twitched again.

"Let me give you the tour," he said.

---

The tour took twenty-two minutes and passed through a kitchen with three kinds of milk, a "reflection room" with a door that locked from the inside, a standing-desk zone Gerald called "the Meadow," and a wall-mounted screen scrolling employee birthdays in a font that looked like handwriting but wasn't. Darla absorbed every detail with the earnest focus of a naturalist documenting a new biome. She complimented the milk selection. She complimented the font.

Gerald walked her through the teams — genomics on the east side, data operations on the west, regulatory in the corner by the window because, Gerald said with a conspiratorial smile, "they've earned the light." Darla laughed at this, not because it was funny but because Gerald clearly wanted her to, and making people feel heard was something she did the way other people breathed.

They ended at a long table near the kitchen where a woman in a linen blazer sat behind a small arrangement of branded materials: a folder, a pen, a single-page FAQ sheet, and something Darla couldn't quite see, partially obscured by the folder.

"And this," Gerald said, "is the best part." His smile shifted. Warmer now, oddly genuine, as if the rehearsal had briefly dropped away and something real had surfaced. "Margot handles our Comfort onboarding."

Margot from HR looked up. She had the particular stillness of a person who knew things she was paid not to say, and her face had settled, over years of this knowing, into an expression of immaculate, practiced calm. She could have been carved from soap.

"Darla. Welcome to your Care Package orientation."

She opened the folder. Inside was a single sheet of paper — thick stock, Helios teal, the text set in the same pseudo-handwriting font from the birthday wall. Across the top, in letters large enough to read from a distance: **YOUR COMFORT & YOU: A GUIDE TO YOUR HELIOS WELLNESS COMPANION.**

"At Helios," Margot said, in a voice that had clearly said this sentence enough times to sand all the edges off it, "we believe that well-being isn't a policy. It's a practice. That's why every member of our team — full-time, part-time, and contract — is issued a personal Comfort during their time with us."

She said *Comfort* the way some people say *opportunity* — capitalized, load-bearing, not to be questioned.

"What's a Comfort?" Darla asked, already delighted.

Margot turned the FAQ sheet toward her. Darla read:

> **What is a Comfort?**
> Your Comfort is a proprietary bio-organic companion designed by the Helios Genomics Wellness Division. Each Comfort is a living organism, cultivated from our patented BioHarmony platform, engineered to support your emotional health through responsive tactile interaction. Comforts are warm to the touch, respond to proximity, and adapt to your personal biorhythmic signature over time.

> **How does my Comfort work?**
> Your Comfort metabolizes ambient emotional biomarkers — the neurochemical microexpressions your body naturally produces — to sustain its growth cycle. In return, your Comfort provides a continuous calming stimulus through warmth, gentle vibration, and adaptive physical responsiveness. Think of it as a closed-loop wellness exchange: you nourish your Comfort, and your Comfort nourishes you!

> **What do I need to do?**
> Nothing! Simply keep your Comfort nearby. Most employees find that holding, cradling, or allowing their Comfort to rest against the skin produces optimal results. Your Comfort will do the rest.

> **Is my Comfort alive?**
> Your Comfort is a bio-organic organism, not a pet. However, many employees find that treating their Comfort with care and affection enhances the bonding cycle and improves wellness outcomes. We encourage you to personalize your experience!
