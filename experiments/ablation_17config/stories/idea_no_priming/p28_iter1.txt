# The Lattice of Mirela Voss

## I.

I want you to understand that I was very good at this.

Not good the way a dentist is good, where good means you didn't flinch and the numbness wore off by dinner. Good the way a butcher is good — the knife so clean you don't see the motion, only the result: two things that used to be one thing, lying on either side of a line you can't find anymore. That's what I did with people. I listened to the inside of their heads, and then I cut them away from the wrong life and set them down inside the right one, and they never saw the blade. They thought it was luck. They thought it was God. A few of them thought it was the algorithm on the dating app I told them to delete.

My name is Mirela Voss, and for eleven years I operated out of a rented office above a shoe repair shop on Avenida Córdoba, between a place that sold orthotics and a place that sold nothing as far as I could tell but remained, defiantly, open. I called myself a *consultora sentimental.* In English you might say matchmaker, though the word makes me think of sulfur and small fires, which — fine. Fine, that's not wrong.

People came to me saying they wanted love. They sat in my client chair, which I bought at a flea market in San Telmo and reupholstered in green velvet because green is the color of money and people unconsciously trust a chair that reminds them of money, and they said, *Mirela, I want to find someone,* and I listened — not to what they said, never to what they said, but to the bright, embarrassing, hyper-specific images cascading through the bony bowl of their skulls while they said it.

A man named Gustavo Paz came to me in March 2012, sat in the green chair, and said he wanted a woman who shared his values. His values: he pictured a woman watching him slice a *bife de chorizo* at a restaurant — not eating her own steak, not talking, just *watching him cut* — and finding the motion of his wrist erotic. That was the whole fantasy. The steak, the wrist, the witness. He'd have been mortified. He'd have sworn on his dead mother it was about shared values. But I heard it, the way you hear a dog whistle if you're the dog, and I sent him to a specific table at Don Julio on Guatemala Street at 8:30 on a Thursday, and I sent a woman named Sofía who had come to me a week earlier thinking, without knowing she was thinking it, *I want someone whose body does one thing beautifully,* and I told Sofía to order the *ojo de bife* and to watch the room. Reader, they are married. They have a child. The child is, I am told, already particular about cutlery. I take no credit and full credit.

A woman named Claudia sat in my chair and said she wanted a companion for her later years, someone to grow old with, and what I heard was: *I want someone in the room when I cry at the Piazzolla recording of "Adiós Nonino," the live one from '69, not the studio version, specifically, because the way the bandoneon cracks at the three-minute mark is the sound of my father's voice when he was trying not to be afraid, and I have never told anyone this, and I need someone to not tell it to while they sit beside me and know.* I sent Claudia to a tango show at Café de los Angelitos on a Tuesday night. I sent a retired engineer named Marcos to the same show, a man who had come to me thinking, *I want someone whose silence has a shape I can sit inside.* They've been together for nine years. Claudia tells people they met by chance. Marcos tells people nothing, because Marcos understands that some truths are architectural — they hold weight, and you don't tap the load-bearing wall to show your guests how solid it is.

And Roberto — no, wait, let me give you one more. Let me give you Estela, because Estela is the one I'm proudest of and pride is what I'm building here, pride is the tower, and I need you to see how tall it was so you understand the distance of the fall.

Estela came to me in the winter of 2015 and sat in the green chair and said she did not want to die alone, and what she actually thought, the image that screamed through her mind like a siren on Avenida 9 de Julio, was: *I do not want to die alone on a Tuesday.* Not Wednesday. Not the weekend. *Tuesday.* Because her mother had died on a Tuesday, in a hospital room where the overhead light buzzed at a frequency Estela still heard in dreams, and the awful thing was not the death but the *day* — the way Tuesday just continued, the way the nurses changed shifts and the cafeteria served its Tuesday menu and the parking garage charged its Tuesday rates, and the world treated the day her mother stopped being a person like any other day in the middle of the week, which it was, which it *was,* and that was the thing Estela could not forgive: that it was.

I sent Estela to the Jardín Botánico on a Saturday, because Saturday was as far from Tuesday as the week allows. I told her to sit on a specific bench near the Roman garden, the one with the wrought-iron back that had been repaired with a slightly different alloy so one section was darker than the rest, like a single gray hair in a dark head, and I sent a man named Aurelio to the same bench, a man whose mind, when I read it, contained a single luminous conviction: *I want someone who knows that the worst things happen on ordinary days and has not made peace with it.* They sat at opposite ends of the bench. They did not speak for forty minutes. Then Aurelio said, "The iron doesn't match," and Estela said, "No, it doesn't," and the conversation that followed lasted, by Estela's count, until the following March, and by my count it has not ended yet.

I was *good* at this.

I was so good at this that I stopped questioning what I was. A telepath. A freak of neural architecture. A woman whose temporal lobe, or soul, or whatever you want to call the thing that distinguishes a person from a very sophisticated coat rack, had developed — or been born with, or been cursed with by a God whose sense of humor I'll address later — the ability to hear human thought the way a sommelier tastes wine. Not vaguely. Not in broad strokes. I could taste the terroir. I could tell you the year. I could taste the specific soil of a person's need and tell you where it had rained and where it hadn't and whether the sun had come through the kitchen window at the right angle on the right morning when they were six and their father was humming and everything was, briefly, the correct shape.

I heard everything. I heard it casually, with the precision of cruelty, though I was not always cruel. I heard the grape and the barrel and the vintage, and I paired it with a meal — a place, a person, a specific quality of light at a specific hour — and I sent people there, and love assembled itself around them like a church built by a God who works fast and doesn't bother with blueprints, and I watched from outside, because the sommelier does not drink. The sommelier swirls, sniffs, names the compound, and spits.

That was me. That was the good part.

I'm telling you this the way I'd tell you about a city I burned down: with the loving, lingering detail of a woman who misses the buildings.

## II.

I kept a map.

Not at first. At first I kept nothing — I matchmade like a jazz musician, by feel, sending people to locations that rose in my mind the way the right note rises under a practiced hand: unbidden, inevitable, arriving with such ease that effort would have ruined it. The corner booth at Café Tortoni for the couple whose shared loneliness had the texture of old leather. The bench on the east side of the lake in Jardín Japonés for the two women whose desire circled each other like koi. The fourth-floor apartment on Calle Defensa where afternoon light entered the bedroom at exactly 4 p.m. and landed on the far corner of the mattress like a hand placed gently on a sleeping shoulder — I sent three different couples to that apartment over seven years, each one finding it through different landlords, different listings, each arriving at 4 p.m. on their first day as if by accident.

But after enough years, enough couples, enough locations, the data began to accumulate in me the way sediment accumulates in a river mouth. I started pinning locations on a paper map I'd tacked to my office wall, behind the door where clients couldn't see it.  Red pins for successful pairings. Blue pins for ongoing cases. A green pin for each location I'd identified as what I privately called a *sitio sagrado* — a sacred site, a place whose physical properties (light, sound, the specific acoustic relationship between ceiling height and ambient noise, the smell of the leather or the wood or the particular strain of jasmine in the particular courtyard) conspired to produce an architecture of intimacy. A room that wanted to hold love the way a bowl wants to hold water — by shape, by nature, by the mute fact of its curvature.

The map grew. The pins multiplied. And for a long time the map was simply a record, a professional tool, my version of a doctor's patient files or a general's campaign table. I looked at it and saw my career. I saw Buenos Aires annotated with desire, every neighborhood weighted with the specific density of need I had routed through it, Palermo thick with red pins, San Telmo studded like a pincushion, even the outer barrios — Flores, Liniers, Villa Devoto — carrying their sparse, proud constellations of love I had engineered.

Then: the Arrieta session.

October 2017. A woman named Lourdes Arrieta sits in my green chair. She wants love. They all want love. What she's thinking: she is picturing a restaurant she has never been to. This is unusual — most clients picture places they know, memories wearing the masks of fantasies. But Lourdes is imagining a place she has not visited: a small restaurant with exposed brick walls and a zinc bar and a row of wine bottles lit from below so they glow like devotional candles, and in this restaurant she is sitting across from a person whose face she cannot see, and she is happy, and the happiness has a taste — she thinks the word *ciruela,* plum — and the light in the restaurant falls across the table in a pattern she does not recognize, a pattern like — 

I lost my breath.

The pattern she was seeing — the specific geometry of light on the table, the angle, the way it pooled at the corner nearest the wall and tapered toward the center like something spilled and not yet cleaned — I knew it. I knew it the way you know your own handwriting. It was the light in the corner booth at Café Tortoni. The exact pattern. Not similar. Not reminiscent. *Exact.* Down to the soft diffusion at the edge where the spill of gold met the shadow of the napkin holder.

But Lourdes had never been to Café Tortoni. She was picturing a restaurant with exposed brick and a zinc bar, a place that looked nothing like Tortoni's mirrored walls and Art Nouveau woodwork. The *exterior* was completely different. But *inside* the light — inside the physics of the light — 

I told Lourdes I needed a moment. She waited while I pressed my fingers to my temples in a gesture I'd cultivated over the years because clients expected it, because people believe telepathy should look like a headache, and they're not entirely wrong. But I wasn't performing. I was reeling. Because as I held the image from Lourdes's mind — the imaginary restaurant, the zinc bar, the plum-taste of her projected joy — I became aware that the image *contained another image.* Nested inside the light-pattern on the table, like a reflection caught in a puddle on the surface of a painting, was the bench at Jardín Japonés. Not a picture of it. The *spatial signature* of it: the specific relationship between the body and the open air and the angle at which attention diffuses when two people sit side by side instead of face to face. The bench's essential geometry was encoded in the restaurant's light like a watermark in currency — invisible unless you tilted your perception to the exact right angle.

I sent Lourdes away with a recommendation — a wine bar on Calle Honduras that matched her surface desires closely enough — and then I stood in front of my map for three hours.

I visited Tortoni the next day. I sat in the corner booth at the hour when the light did its pooling trick, and I read the minds of the couple sitting two tables away, a pair I'd matched in 2014, and inside their heads I found it: the apartment on Calle Defensa. The 4 p.m. light on the corner of the mattress. Not as a memory — neither of them had ever been to that apartment — but as a *structural echo,* a spatial chord, the way a room can contain, in its proportions, the ghost of every other room that shares its ratios.

I went to the Jardín Japonés. I sat on the bench near the koi pond. I read the mind of a woman sitting alone, not one of my clients, a stranger feeding the fish and thinking about whether her brother would call on her birthday, and buried inside her spatial awareness of the bench around her — the iron slats against her back, the width of the seat, the way the pond's reflected light played on the underside of the overhead branches — I found the Tortoni booth. I found the Defensa apartment. I found the bench in the Jardín Botánico where Estela and Aurelio had sat in their forty minutes of healing silence. I found the specific corner of Don Julio where Gustavo had carved his steak while Sofía watched. 

Each space contained every other space.

Not metaphorically. Not by association. *Structurally.* The way a fractal contains its own shape at every scale, every love-space I had ever identified held within its geometries the encoded pattern of every other love-space. The corner booth contained the bench contained the apartment contained the courtyard contained the restaurant I'd never seen but Lourdes Arrieta had imagined. They were not separate points on my map. They were the same point, refracted through different materials — wood, iron, brick, light, the pliant medium of human desire — the way a single frequency of light becomes different colors when it passes through different prisms.

I stood in the Jardín Japonés with the reflected pond-light playing on my face, and I understood that I had not been placing pins on a map. I had been mapping a single room. A room that existed in every location simultaneously. A room that nested inside itself, that contained itself at every scale, that was — 

I didn't have the word then. I have it now, and I wish I didn't.

A lattice.

A recursive, self-referencing, infinitely nested lattice of sacred space, and I had spent eleven years building it without knowing I was building anything at all. I had thought I was matchmaking. I had thought I was solving a series of independent problems — this heart to that heart, this need to that place. But the places were not independent. They were nodes in a single structure, the way the knots in a net are not separate objects but expressions of one continuous rope.

I went home that night and took down my map. I didn't need it anymore. The pins were irrelevant. You don't need a map of a labyrinth when you realize you're standing inside it.

## III.

Let me tell you about Tomás and the ring.

This is November 2017, three weeks after the Arrieta session, and I am sitting at a small table at a restaurant called Elena in the Four Seasons, which I did not choose and cannot afford, but Tomás Herrera is proposing to his fiancée Ana Lucía at a table twelve feet from mine — a table I chose for them, at a restaurant I chose for them, at an hour I chose for them — and I am doing what I always do, which is the same thing I cannot stop doing, which is listen.

Tomás is on one knee. He has been on one knee for four seconds, which inside his head has lasted approximately as long as the Cretaceous Period.
