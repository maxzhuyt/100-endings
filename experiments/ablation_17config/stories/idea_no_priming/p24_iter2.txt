# The Sulphur Job

## I.

I want to be clear that I am telling you this because of a deadbolt.

A Schlage B60N, single-cylinder, satin nickel finish, which I installed on the side entrance of the boil shed behind Lyle Brossette's house on Beglis Parkway on a Tuesday in March. I know it was a Tuesday because I had moved Elaine Thibodaux's rekey to Wednesday, and I only ever did that for cash-on-completion jobs, which this was supposed to be. Two hundred and twelve dollars. The twelve was tax. Lyle Brossette looked at the invoice, folded it into a square, and said he'd send one of his guys by the shop. He did not send one of his guys by the shop. He did not send anyone anywhere. Two hundred and twelve dollars that I am still, as of right now, owed, plus whatever you want to calculate in mental compounding, which I have been doing nightly since March and which has accumulated to a figure I will not name because it includes interest rates that are, I admit, emotional and not financial and therefore difficult to defend in conversation, though I assure you they are real.

I should tell you what I know about Sulphur, Louisiana, and why I know it, and then you can decide for yourself whether a deadbolt is the kind of thing that could reasonably cause all this.

I have been the only bonded locksmith in Calcasieu Parish for twenty-three years. There was a period in 2011 when a man named Terrence Angelle opened a competing operation out of a storage unit off Ruth Street, but Terrence Angelle did not have insurance, did not have a proper key machine, and was, by my professional assessment, working from YouTube tutorials. He lasted four months. I lasted. I have lasted because I am the only person inside a forty-minute drive who can open a Sargent & Greenleaf 8500 without drilling it, and because I don't talk.

I want to be precise about that second thing: I don't talk. I didn't talk. What I am doing right now is not talking. What I am doing right now is something else, some thing that grew out of a Schlage B60N and two hundred and twelve dollars, and I can feel it in my hands the way I can feel a lock when the last pin is about to set — that slight give, that change in pressure, the mechanism about to commit itself to openness with no way back to closed.

Sulphur has two industries. The first one you can smell from the highway, and it's the one the town was named for. The second one is crawfish. I don't mean the crawfish are the second industry. I mean the crawfish are the *visible* second industry, the way the face of a lock cylinder is the visible part of a mechanism that extends four inches into the door. Behind the crawfish there is a distribution infrastructure that moves methamphetamine and fentanyl from the Port of Lake Charles to seven parishes and, from what I gathered from the safe combinations I've reset after particularly fractious business disagreements, parts of east Texas. But I am getting ahead of myself. The crawfish come first.

Lyle Brossette has been boiling crawfish professionally since 1994. He operates three boil sheds, owns a fleet of refrigerated trucks he leases through a company registered to his mother-in-law in Vinton, and employs, depending on the season, between forty and sixty people who believe they are in the crawfish business, and eight to twelve people who know they are not. Lyle is a big man — not fat, big, the way a compressor is big, dense and pressurized and warm to the touch — and he has a quality of friendliness that I have learned to recognize as the same mechanism as a lock that opens too easily: if the pins don't resist at all, someone has removed them, and you should ask yourself why they want you to walk through that door. Lyle smiles constantly. Lyle remembers your children's names. Lyle runs about thirty percent of the narcotics traffic in southwest Louisiana.

Ray Doucet runs the other seventy.

I say this not to diminish Lyle but to give you the accurate proportion, because the proportion matters. Ray does not have boil sheds. Ray has a crawfish farm — three hundred acres of managed ponds south of town, adjacent to the Intracoastal Waterway, which is a coincidence the way a key is coincidentally shaped like a key. Ray is not friendly. Ray is thin, and he wears pressed shirts even in August, and he speaks at a volume designed to make you lean in, which means you are always slightly off-balance in his presence, which is the point. I rekeyed Ray's office at the farm after he fired his operations manager in 2019, and I rekeyed it again after he fired the next one in 2020, and again in 2021, and this is how I learned that Ray Doucet goes through operations managers the way a poorly maintained lock goes through springs — constantly, destructively, and because of a fundamental misalignment at the center of the mechanism.

I know the combination to Lyle Brossette's safe because I installed it. I know the combination to Ray Doucet's safe because I installed it. I have cut keys for every building both men own, lease, or use without a lease. I have been in their warehouses at three in the morning when no one else was there because a foreman needed emergency access and I was the only one who could provide it, and while I was there I saw things stored alongside the crawfish that were not crawfish, and I said nothing, because I am a locksmith. I see the infrastructure. I do not use the infrastructure. I am the man behind the walls, the man who understands the skeleton of every locked space in this town, and I have kept that knowledge in my head the way I keep a key code — memorized, precise, filed, and inert.

Until March.

Two hundred and twelve dollars. Lyle Brossette's side entrance. The Schlage B60N that is still, as far as I know, performing beautifully, protecting a door that leads to a room where I once saw, stacked beside a hundred-pound sack of Zatarain's crab boil, twelve vacuum-sealed bricks of something that has never been featured in a Zatarain's product line.

I waited thirty days. I called. I waited thirty more. I mailed a second invoice, then a third. On the fourth month I drove to the boil shed on Beglis and stood in the parking lot, and one of Lyle's men — a kid, maybe twenty-two, with a neck tattoo of a crawfish that was anatomically wrong in ways that offended me for reasons I cannot explain — told me Lyle was busy, and I said I understood, and I drove home, and I sat in my shop behind the counter, and I looked at the wall of key blanks, hundreds of them, brass and nickel and aluminum, each one a potential entry to a space that someone believed was secure, and I felt something turn.

That's the only way I can describe it. Something turned. The way a cylinder turns when the shear line clears. Some obstruction I had been maintaining inside myself for twenty-three years — the obstruction that kept my professional knowledge separate from any actionable intent — that obstruction dropped away, and the cylinder turned, and I was open.

Because of a deadbolt.

I want you to believe this, because the alternative — that I was waiting for an excuse, that the two hundred and twelve dollars was just the shear line and the real key was something much larger and sadder that I will not examine — the alternative is not something I can work with. I need the deadbolt. I need the pettiness of it, the way it fits in the hand, the way it is solid and specific and mine. Two hundred and twelve dollars. A Schlage B60N. A reason I can live with.

Three weeks after I stopped trying to collect, Maren Lusk walked into my shop.

## II.

The first thing I noticed about her was her key ring.

This is not a detail I am selecting for narrative convenience. I am a locksmith. I notice key rings the way, I assume, a dentist notices teeth. Hers had four keys: a GM ignition key that was not original to the car she was driving (aftermarket blank, poorly cut, the kind of duplicate you get at a hardware store from a teenager who doesn't understand shoulder stops), a Kwikset house key that she'd been carrying long enough to wear down the tip of the first cut, a small flat key for a filing cabinet or safe deposit box, and a fourth key I couldn't identify from a distance but that I later learned opened a storage unit in Beaumont, Texas, and that this storage unit contained the remains of a career she had detonated with the same precision and indifference she would eventually apply to Sulphur.

She came in at two-fifteen on a Thursday afternoon. I know this because on Thursdays I do my books between two and three, and I had just opened the ledger, and I looked up when the bell rang because the bell was a small brass unit I had tuned myself and it had developed a dullness that suggested the clapper spring needed adjustment — and I made a mental note about the spring and then I saw her and I forgot the spring.

She was not beautiful in the way that makes you think about beauty. She was built like a problem: narrow, angular, with the kind of posture that suggested she had identified gravity as an adversary and was winning. Mid-forties. Hair cut to a length that communicated efficiency rather than style. She wore no jewelry. She scanned my shop the way I scan a room — professionally, assessing ingress and egress, reading the space for what it could tell her about the person who maintained it. I saw her eyes move across my key wall, my pin kits, the safe in the corner where I keep high-security blanks, and I knew — the way you know when a pick finds the first pin — that she was categorizing.

"I need a copy of this," she said, and she placed a key on the counter.

It was a Medeco. Original, factory-cut, with a code stamped on the bow that I recognized because I had issued it. I had cut that key eleven months ago for a rental property on Henning Drive that was managed by Claude Desormeaux, who managed properties for Ray Doucet, who owned property the way a man who needs to launder money owns property — enthusiastically and without architectural taste.

"This is a restricted keyway," I said. "I'd need authorization from the property manager before I could duplicate it."

"Claude Desormeaux," she said, and the way she said his name — without hesitation, without searching for it, placing it on the counter next to the key like a matching piece — told me she already knew about Claude and the properties and probably about Ray. She was not asking me to copy a key. She was showing me what she knew and seeing how my face responded. She was reading me the way I was reading her, and I think we both understood this, and I think that mutual understanding is the reason everything that followed became possible.

"Claude's not easy to reach," I said.

"Claude's easy to reach," she said. "He's at parish lockup. Got picked up Tuesday." She paused. A pause that was architectural — it created a space and waited to see what I would put in it. "You didn't know that?"

I did know that. I knew Claude Desormeaux had been arrested on a probation violation that was not really a probation violation but was, according to what I'd gathered from rekeying his office two days before his arrest, an attempt by the Calcasieu Parish Sheriff's office to pressure Claude into explaining why a property on Henning Drive had been receiving deliveries at four in the morning from a refrigerated truck registered to a woman in Vinton who did not own a refrigerated truck or, to anyone's knowledge, any crawfish. I knew this. She knew I knew this. The key on the counter was not a request. It was a handshake.

"I can make the copy," I said. "Take about ten minutes."

She nodded. She did not sit in the chair I keep by the door for waiting customers. She walked along my key wall, reading the blanks like a catalog, and she stopped at the section where I keep the high-security commercial keyways — the Abloys, the Mul-T-Locks, the Medeco M3s — and she said, without turning around, "You do work for both of them."

It was not a question.

"I'm the only locksmith in the parish."

"That's not what I asked."

"I do work for everyone who needs locks."

She turned around. "Natalie Guidry's funeral was eight months ago," she said. "I want to know how she ended up on a coroner's table in Lake Charles with a cause of death that reads 'accidental drowning' when she had four broken fingers on her left hand and an abrasion pattern on her wrists that the coroner described as" — she pulled a folded paper from her jacket, read from it — "'consistent with ligature contact of indeterminate duration.' Accidental drowning. With ligature marks."

I put the key in my machine. I aligned the blank. I did not look at her while I cut, because looking at her while she talked about Natalie Guidry would have told her things about my face that I was not ready to share.

Natalie Guidry. I had installed a deadbolt on her apartment door seven years ago — a Schlage, same series as the one on Lyle's boil shed, and I am aware of the symmetry and I do not like it. Natalie was thirty-one years old at the time of her death and she had worked, sequentially and then simultaneously, for both Lyle's operation and Ray's, which is a thing that people in Sulphur do when they are either brave or trapped, and Natalie was, from what I could tell through the hardware, both. I had rekeyed her apartment twice in fifteen months. The first time, she told me her ex had a copy. The second time, she didn't explain, and I didn't ask, and her hands were shaking, and I charged her forty dollars, which she paid in cash, which she counted out on the counter from a roll that was too large for a woman who worked at a crawfish processing plant, and I said nothing, and I filed the key code, and eight months before Maren Lusk walked into my shop, Natalie Guidry was found in Bayou d'Inde with four broken fingers and rope burns and a coroner's report that said *drowning* the way a lock can say *secure* while the pins are sheared and the bolt is hanging by its last thread.
