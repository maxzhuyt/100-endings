# The Honeymoon Republic

## I.

Begin, as the tribunal did, with her full name, since there is some dispute about what it means and whether, in its totality, it ever designated a single coherent person.

Captain Elara Anunciación Voss née Kessler, latterly of the Hydrographic Office of the Crown Surveying Authority (Colonial Division, Eastern Meridian Section, Subsection: Reef and Shoal Delineation — a subsection that at the time of her appointment employed eleven cartographers and at the time of her dismissal employed ten, the mathematics of which are revealing). Born 1861 in a town whose name the intake officer rendered variously as "Elberfeld," "Elmsford," and "illegible" across three successive pages of her commission papers, suggesting either that the town itself was unstable or that the officer had been drinking, which, given the broader institutional culture of the Hydrographic Office, was the more charitable interpretation and therefore the less likely one. Educated at the Königlich-Technische Hochschule, or possibly the Polytechnicum at Zürich — her records claim both, on separate occasions, and no one ever checked because no one imagined a female cartographer would be worth investigating until it became necessary to destroy her, at which point every contradiction in her file was excavated with the exquisite tenderness the Crown reserves for those it intends to bury. Graduated with a certificate in geoditic—geodetic—the intake form says "geoditic" and we are quoting—surveying, 1883. Published a monograph on triangulation error in coastal reef mapping, 1886, which was praised in the *Annals of the Geographical Society* as "meticulous to the point of pathology," a phrase the reviewer certainly intended as a compliment and the tribunal's prosecutor would, twelve years later, quote with the emphasis reversed.

She was stationed in warm places. This is what the Hydrographic Office did with its cartographers: dispatched them to latitudes where the coral grew in colors that had no names in German and the administrative apparatus of empire required maps the way a body requires a skeleton — not for beauty, but so the thing could stand up. She charted harbors. She charted shipping lanes. She charted, with the finest steel-point nibs imported from Sheffield, the positions of reefs that existed and — this is the tribunal's word, not ours — "confabulated" the positions of reefs that did not. Or did. The record contradicts itself. Exhibit 14-A: a chart, signed by Captain Voss, depicting a reef formation at 11°47'N, 62°03'W, which the frigate *Resolute* failed to locate during a verification survey in 1894. Exhibit 14-B: a sonar reading from 1911 — sixteen years after the tribunal, submitted to no one, published in an unrelated geological report — confirming a submerged coral structure at 11°47'N, 62°03'W, approximately four fathoms below the depth the *Resolute* had been measuring. She had mapped a reef too deep for her accusers' instruments. Or she had guessed well. Or the reef had grown in the intervening years. Or the reef, as reefs do, had its own opinion about where it was.

The tribunal convened in November 1895, in a building whose address is recorded and whose interior layout is not, though we know the windows faced east because the prosecutor complained about morning glare during the afternoon sessions, which tells us something about the reliability of prosecutors. Captain Voss was charged with Falsification of Navigational Records, Dereliction of Cartographic Duty, and — here the filing system grows coy — a third charge whose description in the index reads only "see supplemental," and the supplemental is missing. She was found guilty. Her license was revoked. Her instruments were confiscated — the personal set, the rosewood-cased theodolite and the brass sextant with her initials engraved where the manufacturer's mark should have been, tools she had purchased with her own salary, which is to say she was stripped of things that belonged to her rather than things that belonged to the Crown, because the Crown's instruments were replaceable and hers were not, and the tribunal understood this, and that was the point.

There is also a medical report. It is dated three months after the tribunal, filed by a Dr. Herreira at a naval clinic in Port of Spain, and it describes the results of a physical examination Captain Voss was required to undergo before her pension claim could be processed — because even disgrace has paperwork, even ruin requires a stamp. The report notes a resting tremor in both hands, intermittent; pupillary irregularities; a lesion, healed, on the left forearm; and "early neurodegenerative changes consistent with the secondary or tertiary sequelae of lues." Syphilis, that is. The disease of ports, of empire, of bodies that traveled to warm places and brought back souvenirs the Hydrographic Office did not chart. Dr. Herreira recommended further evaluation. The recommendation was filed. Captain Voss did not return for further evaluation. Her pension claim was denied on grounds that the medical report introduced "questions of cognitive reliability during the period of service," which meant the disease that was destroying her brain retroactively justified the tribunal that had already destroyed her career, a redundancy of ruin so efficient it approaches the architectural.

Now.

Regarding Luz.

Luz Amara Voss née Delgado. Born 1864, Cartagena de Indias. Died 1896, same city, of cholera, during an epidemic that killed four hundred and sixteen people, each of whom presumably had a story this detailed and none of whom had a cartographer to write it down. She was Elara's wife, a term we use in its practical rather than legal sense, since no jurisdiction in which they resided recognized the union, a fact that the relevant bureaucracies noted with the same bored precision they brought to reef coordinates. They lived together for nine years. The evidence for the quality of those years is inadmissible in any court and consists entirely of letters — forty-three letters — written during Elara's surveying voyages, stored in a cedar chest with the stationery they'd been written on, and retrieved by Elara from the house in Cartagena after Luz's death, when Elara arrived too late for the funeral because the Hydrographic Office had not forwarded the telegram, which was not malice, probably, but was the kind of negligence that functions identically to malice and is, in the economy of suffering, priced the same.

Forty-three letters. Several contain coordinates.

They describe a place called Isla de Corazón Perdido. Island of the Lost Heart. The name is, we acknowledge, a bit much — florid in the way of private jokes that have calcified into mythology, the kind of name lovers give to their future the way parents name children: aspirationally, absurdly, as though the syllables could compel the thing into being. Luz writes about this island the way the devout write about paradise, with an earnestness so absolute it crests into the comic and keeps going until it reaches whatever is on the other side of comedy — which may be faith, or may be the particular madness that faith becomes when the object of devotion is removed. The coordinates she provides do not agree with one another. This could mean she encoded them. This could mean she invented them. This could mean different letters describe different islands, or no island, or an island that exists the way a marriage exists between two women in 1892 — by mutual insistence, in defiance of every official record.

Captain Elara Voss, disgraced cartographer, charter of phantom reefs, widow of a woman the state would not allow her to be widowed by, carrying forty-three letters and a sextant she no longer officially owns, has hired a schooner. The schooner is called the *Santa Obscura*. Its hull leaks in two places. Its captain — the real captain, a man named Ferreira who needed the money — has been paid to sail toward a dot that does not appear on any chart, navigating by coordinates extracted from love letters written by a dead woman.

A mapmaker chasing an unmappable destination.

This is what passes for a premise.

The name, again: Isla de Corazón Perdido. Say it aloud. Hear how the vowels open like a wound dressed in lace. Hear how the consonants click shut like a locket. It is ornate. It is faintly ridiculous. There is something cruel inside its prettiness, the way there is something cruel inside every name that promises more than the world is structured to deliver.

## II.

Below deck, the timbers speak. This is a factual claim about the *Santa Obscura*, whose oak ribs groan with each swell as though the vessel itself is composing a monologue on the subject of its deterioration, but it is also the kind of sentence a narrator writes when trying to delay the next thing that happens, because the next thing that happens is difficult, and the narrator — who has, until now, maintained the mannered distance of a clerk summarizing evidence for a file no one will read — is not certain he is equal to it.

The cabin is seven feet by five. The ceiling is six feet. Elara is five feet and eight inches, which means she can stand upright if she does not straighten her neck, and she has not straightened her neck in some time, owing to the fever, which began on the ninth day and has since established itself as a permanent resident of her body, paying rent in sweat and occasional delirium. The hammock sways. A tallow candle, wedged into a bottle, provides light of the quality and color associated with paintings of purgatory. The cedar chest is lashed to a beam. The letters are spread across the hammock, the floor, and Elara's lap, in an order that is not chronological, not geographical, not alphabetical, but which appears, to anyone watching from the doorway — and no one is; Ferreira's crew avoids the cabin the way dockworkers avoid a ship under quarantine flag — to follow a logic as private and inviolable as prayer.

She reads them aloud.

We can quote. The dead have no copyright and the living have no shame, and the narrator is uncertain which category he belongs to.

From the letter dated March 7, 1891: *My navigator, my mapmaker, my impossible woman — when you are charting your reefs and your rocks and your miserable little shoals, remember that I am charting you. I am taking the measure of every degree of you. I know where your shallows are. I know where you drop off. When you come home I will show you the map I have drawn and it will be more accurate than anything your Hydrographic Office has ever produced, because I have surveyed the territory with instruments your English lords cannot manufacture, being as they are largely unfamiliar with the concept of touching another person with attention…*

From the letter dated June 22, 1892: *The island is waiting. Our island. I have added a grove of mango trees to the western shore — you will see, when you arrive, that I have been busy. The coordinates are as follows, and I have changed them again, because the island moves, or perhaps I move, or perhaps the world itself is unreliable and only we are fixed…* Then numbers. Then a pressed flower, disintegrating.

From the letter dated September 3, 1893: *Corazón Perdido is not a place we go to. It is a place we bring with us. It is the country of which we are both citizens and the exclusive population. Its flag is the color of — * well. We'll spare you. You understand the register. Lush, laced with irony that was private before becoming posthumous, dense with the mythology two people construct when the world outside their door has declined to recognize their existence: a shared nation, an invented cartography, a republic of two.

There is more. There is much more. There are forty-three letters spanning five years and Elara reads them all, nightly, in rotation, like a priest working through the liturgical calendar, and the narrator could quote them at length and thereby fill many pages with Luz Delgado's voice, which was, by the evidence of the letters, warm and intelligent and given to extravagance in the way certain people are given to extravagance not because they lack restraint but because they have measured the poverty of the literal world and found it wanting and have decided, with full deliberation, to furnish it with excess. The letters are beautiful. They are beautiful the way the overcoat is beautiful: because someone who had nothing poured everything into them.

But the letter Elara reads on a loop — the one she returns to after the mythology, after the coordinates, after the pressed flowers and the private nation and the island that moves — is not beautiful.

It is dated November 15, 1894. It is short. It fits on a single page and does not fill it.

*Elara,*

*It rained this morning. The gutter on Calle de la Sierpe is overflowing again and the landlord says he will fix it and he will not fix it. I went to the market. There were no good limes. I bought the bad limes. They will have to do.*

*The cat has been sleeping on your pillow. I let her because I am not above using a cat as a proxy for a person. You would laugh at me. You should be here to laugh at me.*

*I miss you when you're in the other room. This is what it has come to. You are not in the other room. You are on a boat somewhere being precise about things. But I miss you the way I miss you when you're in the other room, which is to say: unreasonably, and with full knowledge that you will be back shortly, and with the absolute certainty that the interval is unsurvivable.*

*Buy limes if you see good ones.*

*Yours, always and in specific,*

*L.*

Elara reads this letter. She reads it again. She holds the paper close enough that her exhaled breath moves it. She reads it a third time. The candle trembles in its bottle and the shadow of the page enlarges and contracts on the bulkhead as though the letter itself is breathing. The fever makes the words swim: *limes* slips into *lines*, *room* rhymes with *ruin* in her ears though not in any language she knows. She reads it a fourth time and now she is speaking it from memory, her eyes closed, the paper pressed against her sternum, and what comes out of her mouth is not quite the text and not quite something else but a thing in between, the way a hymn is in between speech and music, the way weeping is in between breathing and not.

The sound rises through the deck boards.

A sailor — young, recently employed, from an island whose name Ferreira pronounces differently every time — hears it while coiling rope near the foremast. He pauses. He cocks his head. He listens to the sound coming from below: rhythmic, hitching, rising and falling, punctuated by silences that are worse than the sound. He works the rope. He considers.

He tells Ferreira: "The lady is laughing down there."

Ferreira, who has been paid not to ask questions, does not correct him, and the misidentification enters the informal log of the voyage as fact, the way all misidentifications enter all logs: confidently, and without amendment.

## III.

In the morning she brought the instruments on deck.

The narrator notes this without commentary, or rather, notes it with the specific commentary of precise material description, because what follows is an examination, and examinations require evidence, and evidence is the only language left to a woman whose other languages — the romantic, the professional, the domestic — have each, in turn, been revoked.

The sextant first. She unlatched the rosewood case — water-stained along the left hinge, a bloom of white mineral deposit where the brass clasp met the wood, but still functional, still clicking open with the clean authority of a device built to be trusted. Inside: the instrument, nestled in green baize gone bald at the contact points. An index mirror. A horizon glass. A graduated arc, marked in degrees and minutes, the numerals hand-engraved so finely that each digit wore a tiny serif like a hat. She had bought this in London in 1887 with three months' salary and had, at the time, held it the way one holds a newborn: with terror, with adoration, with the unbearable knowledge that its fragility is the precise measure of its value. She lifted it out. The arc caught the morning light and threw a line of reflection across the deck like a sundial's finger.

Then the charts.
