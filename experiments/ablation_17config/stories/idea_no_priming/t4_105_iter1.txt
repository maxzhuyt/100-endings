# Hugo's Two Summers

---

The thing about Lena's tomatoes is that they were, with complete sincerity and no exaggeration, the worst tomatoes I have ever encountered in the Rhône Valley, and I say this as a man who has eaten tomatoes from gas stations, from hospital cafeterias, from the plastic-wrapped trays they sell at Carrefour in January when no tomato should exist. Lena's were worse. They had the texture of wet chalk. They split at the seam like cheap shoes. They sunburned — I did not know tomatoes could sunburn until Lena's, which blistered on top and stayed green underneath, as though each fruit were engaged in a private civil war about whether to ripen.

She grew them in the courtyard of our building on the Rue des Remparts, in Lyon, in the 4th arrondissement, in planters she had built herself from salvaged wine crates. The soil was wrong. I told her the soil was wrong. Fournier told her the soil was wrong. Even Keiko, who is not a gardener and claims to have killed a cactus through what she describes as "emotional negligence," told Lena the soil was wrong. The pH was high. The drainage was essentially theological — you poured water in and prayed it went somewhere. Lena had read a book, or part of a book, about permaculture, and had decided that the soil knew what it was doing, that intervention was a colonial impulse, that the tomatoes would find their way. The tomatoes did not find their way.

But she was proud of them. She carried them around.

I am telling you this on a Tuesday afternoon, from a café terrace on the Presqu'île, where I have been sitting long enough that the waiter has stopped asking if I want anything else and has begun simply bringing me espressos at intervals, like a nurse administering medication. The light is good here in the late afternoon. It comes down the Rue de la République at an angle that makes the limestone facades go the color of warm bread, and the plane trees along the quay are doing what they do in June, which is to exist so aggressively that you cannot look at them without feeling personally addressed by the concept of summer. I want you to see this. I want to be precise about this, because precision matters, because the world is specific or it is nothing.

Fournier. Let me tell you about Fournier. Fournier was our upstairs neighbor on the fourth floor, a retired lycée teacher who had taught history for thirty-one years and emerged from the experience with the unshakable conviction that he was interesting. He was not, as a rule, interesting. But he was kind, and he brought wine to every gathering — not good wine, not bad wine, but exactly the wine you would expect from a man who had once explained the Congress of Vienna to teenagers for three decades and believed that adequate was a virtue. He had a habit of arriving early and positioning himself near the food so that he could narrate the dishes as people served themselves. "Ah, the tapenade, you see, is Provençal in origin," he would say, to people who were from Provence.

Keiko lived on the second floor. She was from Osaka originally, had come to Lyon for a postdoctoral fellowship in epidemiology — but I'm getting ahead of things. She had come to Lyon, and she had stayed, the way people stay, because the city closed around her like a hand and she found she did not mind the grip. She laughed at things a beat late, always, as though the joke had to travel a longer distance to reach her, and when it arrived she gave it more than it deserved. Her laugh was too loud for her body. People forgave her for it instantly.

Pavel was on the third floor, directly below Fournier, and the two of them had a long-running dispute about footsteps that had evolved from grievance into something closer to a marriage. Pavel was Bulgarian, an engineer, and he brought the same bottle of Bulgarian grape brandy to every dinner, every gathering, every occasion for which a bottle might be appropriate and several for which it was not. The brandy was not good. He knew it was not good. He brought it with the solemnity of a man presenting his credentials, and if you did not drink some, he watched you with the eyes of a dog you have declined to pet.

There were others. I will name them. I will name all of them, because they deserve to be named in the good version first.

Marianne, who was seventy and fast and had once been an operative for the DGSE, or so she claimed, and who made a ratatouille that was either the best in Lyon or the second best, depending on whether you asked her or the woman at the market on Quai Saint-Antoine, with whom she had had a disagreement in 1997 that showed no signs of resolving. Tomás, from Lisbon, who repaired accordions and played none of them. Ndéye, who was finishing her PhD in microbiology — but again, I'm — she was finishing her PhD, and she had a way of listening to people that made them feel they had said something more intelligent than they had.

And Lena.

Lena lived directly across the courtyard from me, on the ground floor, behind the tomatoes. She was German. She had been in Lyon for nine years. She was a virologist at the P4 laboratory — the biosafety level 4 facility, the Jean Mérieux laboratory, which is the only one of its kind in France, and which sits on the campus of the INSERM like a concrete secret. She did not talk about her work at dinner parties. She talked about her tomatoes. She talked about a recipe for Kartoffelsalat that her mother made and that she had been trying to replicate for six years without success. She talked about whether the courtyard got enough light, whether the pigeons were getting worse, whether anyone had noticed that the bakery on the corner had changed its flour supplier and the bread was not the same.

Her blood type was AB-negative.

I mention that because —

No. We are still at the table. We are still in the courtyard. It is the first summer, the one that happened, the one with the candles in the wine bottles and Fournier holding forth about bouillabaisse — he maintained, against all evidence and local pride, that Marseille's version was superior to anything Lyon could produce, and he said this *in Lyon*, at a table of people who *lived in Lyon*, with the calm certainty of a man who has confused conviction with courage. Lena argued back. She said the Lyon version had more finesse. She did not know anything about bouillabaisse, she was from Hamburg, but she argued with a precision that I later came to understand was simply how she was built — she approached even trivial claims with the rigor of someone who had spent her professional life in a laboratory where imprecision killed people.

The evening was warm. The courtyard caught the last angled light and held it against the limestone. Keiko had made a salad — greens, feta, tomatoes from the market, not Lena's, because even Keiko was not that generous. Pavel's brandy made its rounds. Tomás said nothing for forty-five minutes and then made a joke so dry and so delayed that the table went silent for a beat before Marianne barked out a laugh that scared a pigeon off the roof.

I watched all of this. I want you to know that I watched it. I was a postal worker. I spent thirty-two years sorting and delivering mail for La Poste, and the thing that occupation teaches you, more than anything, is the practice of attention: the weight of an envelope, the change in a name on a mailbox, the thinning of correspondence that means someone has died or moved or simply been forgotten. I noticed things. I was good at noticing things. I noticed, for instance, that Lena's hands were beautiful, which is not a thing you are supposed to notice about a colleague's hands, but I am telling you what happened, and what happened is that the candle caught her fingers as she reached for the bread, and I thought: *those are the hands of a person who is careful with the world.*

I noticed also that she looked tired. But everyone looks tired in June. The heat does not settle in Lyon; it accumulates, like a debt.

This is the dinner party. This is the good summer. I am going to stay here as long as I can.

The facility was Soviet-era, Vozrozhdeniya Island, Aral Sea, and "island" is generous — by the time they arrived, the sea had retreated so far that you could drive to it across cracked salt flat that had been ocean floor within living memory. The Vector program had tested open-air biological weapons there in the 1970s and '80s: anthrax, plague, smallpox, tularemia, and several hemorrhagic agents that were, in the nomenclature of the time, "chimeric" — which is a clinical way of saying that someone had taken naturally lethal things and made them unnaturally worse. The buildings had been decommissioned. The decontamination had been, in the word Lena later used, "optimistic."

I was not there. Let me be clear about that. I was in Lyon. I was delivering mail. I was watering Lena's tomatoes because she had asked me to water her tomatoes, and I was doing it wrong, because I poured from the top rather than at the base, and the leaves got wet and started spotting, and when she came back she would tell me I had —

The facility was concrete and rust. The ventilation had failed in several wings. The team arrived in August on a WHO rapid-response mandate after a hemorrhagic cluster was reported in three villages within forty kilometers — Kantubek, which was a ghost town, and two fishing settlements to the south that were no longer fishing settlements because there was no longer anything to fish. Seventeen dead. The case fatality rate suggested something worse than Crimean-Congo, worse than the Marburg variant that had circulated in the region in 2014. The samples from the dead matched nothing in the WHO reference library, or rather they matched something that was supposed to no longer exist — a chimeric filovirus from the Vector program's 1988 research line, preserved, it seemed, in the soil of the island, in the dust of the buildings, in the particular alkaline crust of the retreating sea, and reactivated by conditions that Lena's subsequent report would describe, with controlled fury, as "entirely predictable given the available climate data."

Her blood type was AB-negative, which meant her plasma had neither anti-A nor anti-B antibodies, which meant —

But that isn't what I'm telling you about.

At the dinner party. At the dinner party, Lena passed me the bread, and our fingers touched, and it was nothing — it was bread, it was a Tuesday, it was Lyon in June — but I am fifty-seven years old and I have been a postal worker for most of my adult life and I know the weight of what passes between people in small moments, and I tell you that something passed.

Fournier was arguing about bouillabaisse. The light was going.

---

Let me tell you who was at the table, and then let me tell you who was at the facility, and you will see that I am talking about the same people, and you will understand why I need both.

At the table: Fournier, at the head, because Fournier always sat at the head, not out of authority but out of a profound inability to imagine himself peripheral. He had wine. He was explaining something. To his left, Keiko, whose helmet — whose *glasses* had fogged, because she'd been laughing at something Pavel said. She wiped them on her shirt. She put them back on crooked. Next to Keiko: Ndéye, who was listening, who was always listening, with that quality of attention that made you want to be worth it. Then Tomás, silent as a stone, holding his wine like a man holding a small bird. Marianne, who had made the ratatouille and was monitoring its consumption with the vigilance of a former intelligence operative, or possibly a woman who simply cared a great deal about eggplant. Pavel, with his brandy, pouring for anyone who would hold out a glass and several who would not. Lena, across from me, her hands on the table, her eyes catching the candle.

And me. Hugo. At the foot of the table, because someone has to be at the foot, and because I have always been a man more comfortable watching than performing.

Now.

At the facility: Fournier — and I know what you are thinking, what was a retired lycée teacher doing at a biosafety deployment on the Aral Sea, and the answer is that Fournier was not a retired lycée teacher, or rather he was, but before that he had been a logistics coordinator for Médecins Sans Frontières for eleven years, and before *that* he had been an army medic, and the man who stood at the head of lecture halls explaining the Treaty of Westphalia to sixteen-year-olds had once triaged a cholera ward in Goma with nothing but oral rehydration salts and an unwillingness to stop. He talked too much on comms. He transmitted when he should have listened. Every team leader who had ever worked with him had noted this, and none of them had ever wanted a different logistics man.

Keiko, in the gentle version, laughed late and her glasses fogged. In the real version, she was a field epidemiologist with WHO's Global Outbreak Alert and Response Network, and her hazmat helmet fogged because she laughed — she laughed in decontamination, she laughed during briefings, she laughed during the first suit-integrity drill, and it fogged her visor every time, and every time she wiped it with a gloved hand that only smeared the moisture, and whatever was on the other side of the visor waited for her to see it clearly, which she always eventually did.

Ndéye, finishing her PhD. Ndéye, who listened. Ndéye, who was the team's molecular biologist and who had sequenced the original Vector chimera from degraded samples in a French government archive and was therefore the only person alive who knew exactly what the pathogen was supposed to look like, and what it looked like now, and the distance between those two things, which was — she told Lena, Lena told me — "enough to make you religious."

Tomás, who repaired accordions and said nothing, and who in the real version was a Portuguese biocontainment engineer, a man who had designed the negative-pressure systems for three BSL-4 labs in Europe, and who said nothing because he had learned that in a sealed facility with a compromised ventilation system, the sound of his own voice made him aware of his breathing, and the awareness of his breathing made him aware of the air, and the awareness of the air made him aware of what was in it.

Marianne, who made the ratatouille. Marianne, who had been DGSE. This was not a dinner-party fiction. Marianne had been DGSE, and she was there because someone in Paris had decided that a chimeric Soviet filovirus reactivating in a geopolitical seam between Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, and whatever territorial claim the receding Aral Sea now fell under was not purely a public health matter. She was sixty-eight years old. She carried a sidearm under her hazmat suit, which Lena told me was both against protocol and the single most comforting thing about the entire deployment.

Pavel, with his brandy. Pavel, who was a structural engineer specializing in Soviet-era biocontainment architecture, who had literally written his doctoral thesis on the ventilation failures of the Vector program facilities, and who had packed a bottle of Bulgarian grape brandy in his duffel alongside the atmospheric sensors and the architectural schematics, against every regulation, because Pavel understood something that regulations did not, which is that people in sealed buildings need evidence that the outside world still exists, and a bottle of bad brandy from Melnik is as good an evidence as any.

And Lena. Lena, who led the team, because Lena was the one the WHO called when something hemorrhagic appeared that wasn't in the books. Lena, who grew the worst tomatoes in the Rhône Valley and who could identify a filovirus variant from an electron micrograph faster than Ndéye's sequencer could boot up. Lena, whose hands were careful. Whose blood type was AB-negative.

I was not there. I was in Lyon. I was watering the tomatoes and ruining them and receiving, every four or five days when the satellite connection held, a message from Lena that said things like "All fine here, don't drown the basil," or "Fournier won't stop talking, pray for us," or "Tell the tomatoes I'll be home soon."
