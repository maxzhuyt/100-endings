# Margaux Fontaine-Willis

## I.

It should be stated at the outset — and the narrator is aware that beginning with a throat-clearing apology is the literary equivalent of arriving at a party and immediately explaining why you almost didn't come — that the Carmel estate of Henrik Lautréamont-Willis was not, by any honest metric, a bad place to live. This concession costs something. It would be more convenient, narratively, if the house were a mausoleum, if the fog that rolled each evening through the Monterey cypress carried the scent of something rotten, if the locks were visible. The locks were not visible. There may not have been locks at all. This remains, eleven years later, a matter of some interpretive difficulty, and the narrator asks in advance for patience with the ambiguity, which will not resolve.

The house sat on eleven acres above the Pacific, which Henrik had purchased in 2007 from a retired cellist who'd developed a late-life conviction that the ocean was speaking to her in Czech. Henrik did not believe the ocean spoke Czech. Henrik did not believe the ocean spoke at all. He believed in extraction systems, in climate-controlled cellars, in the thermal mass of rammed-earth walls, and in the principle — never stated, only enacted — that a life could be made so compositionally complete that the question of whether one *wanted* it became moot, in the same way that one does not *want* oxygen. One simply continues to breathe.

Margaux Fontaine-Willis, née Fontaine, had been breathing in this house since she was twenty-six years old.

She was now thirty-seven, and on most mornings — this morning, for instance, a Tuesday in late September — she rose at 6:40 without an alarm, because alarms were unnecessary in a house where the light had been engineered. The east-facing clerestory windows in the primary bedroom admitted a band of sun at precisely the angle required to reach the sleeper's eyelids by 6:35 in autumn, 6:50 in winter, and not at all in summer, when Henrik preferred they rise later. She had not set an alarm in eleven years. She had not needed to decide when to wake up. These are different facts, and the difference between them is the subject of this story, or part of the subject, or adjacent to the subject, which keeps moving.

She descended the staircase — poured concrete, no railing, each step cantilevered from the wall in a way that a structural engineer once described as "theoretically hostile to humaneli" before Henrik's gaze silenced him — and entered the kitchen, where her tasks were arranged.

*Tasks* is the narrator's word. Margaux would have said *morning*. She would have said: this is what morning looks like. And she would have been, in the way that a person drawing a map of the only country they have ever visited is both accurate and catastrophically incomplete, correct.

She set water to heat in a copper kettle that Henrik had commissioned from a metalsmith in Nara, Japan, who worked exclusively in copper reclaimed from Edo-period temple roofs. The kettle cost four thousand dollars. Margaux did not know this, because prices were not a feature of compound life. Objects arrived. They were beautiful. They had provenance, which Henrik would narrate over dinner in the same warm, unhurried baritone he used for everything — the provenance of a kettle, the provenance of a Côtes du Rhône, the provenance of Margaux herself, whom he had, depending on the telling, *discovered* or *recognized* or *recovered* at a friend's gallery opening in 2012, standing in front of a Gerhard Richter squeegee painting with a face that suggested she understood it, which she did not, which Henrik found — he would say this, touching the back of her wrist — *more honest than understanding*.

While the water heated, she arranged his vitamins. This was a sequence, not a task, because sequence implied an order that could be performed incorrectly, and Margaux had not performed it incorrectly since 2015, when she had placed the methylated B12 after the magnesium glycinate and Henrik had not said a word about it but had rearranged them on his palm with one finger before swallowing, the gesture so small and so total that she had understood it as a complete communication, which — the narrator must be honest — it was. Vitamin D3: first. Omega-3, krill-derived: second. Magnesium glycinate: third. Methylated B12: fourth. Ashwagandha, KSM-66 extract: fifth. Each in its own ceramic dish, hand-thrown by a potter in Shigaraki whose name Margaux had once known and now simply thought of as "the dish woman," which is not a diminishment but an intimacy, the kind of shorthand that develops inside any closed system.

(A 2021 study in the *Journal of Involuntary Domestic Aesthetics*, Vol. 14, found that subjects in long-term curated environments developed what researchers termed "provenance fatigue" — a state in which the biographical density of one's possessions exceeds the cognitive budget available for maintaining their stories, resulting in a paradoxical flattening effect whereby a $4,000 Edo-period copper kettle becomes simply "the kettle" and a hand-thrown Shigaraki ceramic becomes simply "the small blue one." The study was funded by a grant whose origins no one investigated thoroughly enough.)

She made Henrik's coffee. A Kalita Wave pourover, 205-degree water, 1:16 ratio, forty-five-second bloom, total brew time three minutes and fifteen seconds. She did not time it anymore. Her body timed it. Her body had become, over eleven years, an instrument calibrated to his preferences so precisely that the calibration had become indistinguishable from her own instinct, in the way that a pianist who has practiced a nocturne ten thousand times can no longer hear the difference between Chopin's intention and her own desire. This metaphor is too generous. The narrator is aware that it is too generous and is choosing, for the moment, to leave it, because the less generous version — that Margaux had been *tuned*, like a instrument, *by* Henrik, *for* Henrik — is true but not yet ready to be said plainly. It will be said plainly later. The narrator apologizes for the delay.

She brought his coffee to the study, where he was already reading — always already reading, as though he had not slept but merely transitioned from one form of absorption to another. He looked up. He smiled.

"Chablis day or Margaux day?" he asked.

This was their game, or his game, or the game. Every morning, he assessed the weather, her mood, the quality of light, and declared it a day suited to a particular wine region, which would then determine the bottle opened at dinner. A Chablis day was cool, bright, mineral. A Margaux day was warmer, softer, structured but with give.

"It feels like a Margaux day," she said.

He looked at her for a moment longer than the exchange required.

"It always does," he said.

She understood this as a compliment. The narrator has nothing to add at this time.

The meals were the architecture of the day, and if the narrator has already given the impression that these were simple dinners — a man and a woman, a table, food — then a correction is necessary, and the narrator takes full responsibility for the inadequacy of the earlier description, if an inadequacy occurred, which it may not have, but one can't be too careful.

Dinner at the compound was a three-hour event. Not because the food was elaborate (it was, but this was incidental) and not because Henrik was slow (he was deliberate, which is different, except when it isn't). The dinners were long because they were, in structure and function, the primary social, intellectual, and emotional event of the day, serving the role that might in another life be distributed among friendships, office interactions, therapy, arguments in traffic, overheard conversations in coffee shops, the passive socialization of simply *being among people* — all of this was compressed, in the compound, into the hours between seven and ten p.m., during which Henrik and Margaux sat across from each other at a twelve-foot slab of bookmatched black walnut and conducted what Henrik called "the conversation," as though there were only one.

Topics were not assigned. But there was a grammar. Henrik would introduce a subject — the ethical implications of rewilding, the decline of the French left, whether Rothko's later work was a fulfillment or a betrayal of the earlier paintings — and Margaux would respond, and Henrik would listen, and then Henrik would add something, and Margaux would feel, as she always felt, that his addition had revealed a dimension of the subject she had not accessed but *should have*, and this feeling — this gentle, persistent sense of almost-but-not-quite sufficiency — was the emotional weather of the compound, constant as the fog.

The wine was opened at 7:15. If a Margaux day, a Margaux: always a bottle from the estate itself, Château Margaux, which Henrik cellared in quantity and which shared, with the woman sitting across from him, nothing but a name, which was everything, which was the whole game, which — the narrator promises this will be addressed directly later, and is aware that multiple promises of future directness risk becoming their own form of evasion, for which the narrator can only offer this acknowledgment, which is itself a further delay, for which —

The wine was opened at 7:15.

She poured. He swirled. He smelled. He closed his eyes.

"Do you know," he said once — not this Tuesday specifically but on a Tuesday very much like it, which the narrator is collapsing into this one for reasons of economy that Henrik would have approved of — "what makes the Margaux appellation? It's not the grape. Everyone has the grape. It's the gravel. The soil drains so perfectly that the vine is under *constant moderate stress*. Never enough to damage it. Just enough to prevent complacency. The vine reaches deeper. The roots search harder. The wine becomes — " He paused, and in the pause was the entire relationship. "The wine becomes itself."

Margaux drank her wine.

"I think that's a compliment," she said.

"It's a fact," he said. "Facts are better than compliments. Compliments require someone to be generous. Facts just require someone to be paying attention." He looked at her. "I pay attention."

He did. God help her, he did. He paid the kind of attention that most people experience only in medical emergencies or new love, and he had sustained it for eleven years, and the effect of being *that seen* for *that long* was not, as a naïve person might expect, comfort. It was something closer to the opposite. It was the suspicion — never confirmed, never dispelled — that she had become a *project of attention* so total that the boundary between being loved and being *curated* had dissolved, and that she had not only failed to resist the dissolution but had, somewhere around year four, stopped wanting to.

The fog came in. It came in every night, which meant it was not weather but furniture.

She cleared the dishes. He read. The house was silent except for the sounds it had been designed to make: the settling of rammed earth, the distant ocean, the specific nothing that is the acoustic signature of a space from which all accidental sound has been removed.

She went to bed. She did not wonder, that night, whether she was happy, because the compound had replaced the question of happiness with the question of *whether anything was out of place*, and nothing was, and this was either the answer or the reason the question could not be asked, and the narrator is not certain which, and suspects that Margaux was not certain either, and suspects further that this uncertainty — this inability to distinguish completion from containment — is the most honest thing that can be said about the years she spent there.

Eleven of them.

She would leave on a Wednesday.

## II.

She left on a Wednesday.

This fact — its day, its plainness, the refusal of the event to be anything other than calendrically banal — may constitute a kind of violence, or at least an indignity, and the narrator wants to prepare the reader for the possibility that the most significant action of Margaux Fontaine-Willis's life to date will resist, with a stubbornness that borders on hostility, any attempt to render it cinematically. There will be no rain. There will be no score. There will be no letter on the kitchen island and no final backward glance. The gate — iron, hand-forged by an artisan in Oaxaca whose family had been working iron since before the Conquest, which Henrik had explained to Margaux during its installation in 2016, a fact she resented not because it was boring but because it was interesting, because *everything he told her was interesting*, which is its own kind of trap — the gate was not locked.

It was 2:20 in the afternoon.

She put on shoes. Not the Celine flats Henrik preferred, the ones that made a specific sound on the poured-concrete floors — a sound she would later realize she had internalized as the soundtrack of her own movement, so that walking in other shoes would feel, for weeks, like walking in silence, like being erased from the feet up. She put on a pair of sneakers. White. Common Projects, because even her rebellion was curated, because she did not own a pair of shoes that Henrik had not either chosen or approved, and this pair he had chosen, which meant that even the act of leaving was being *performed on his stage*, in his *costume*, and the narrator recognizes that this observation risks melodrama but would like to point out that melodrama is sometimes just accuracy with a higher pulse rate.

She picked up a bag. A small one. A weekender in waxed canvas that she had packed with — the narrator hesitates, and the hesitation is genuine, because the contents of this bag will establish what Margaux Fontaine-Willis believed she would need in the outside world, and the gap between that belief and the reality constitutes a kind of diagnostic that is almost too intimate to report. She packed: four days of clothing, all of it beautiful, none of it appropriate for any life that included a laundromat. A single book — Sei Shōnagon's *Pillow Book*, which Henrik had given her in their second year and which she had been rereading ever since. A bottle of Château Margaux 1996 — a vintage older than their relationship, a bottle Henrik considered among the finest in the cellar, a bottle that was, she understood, worth more than the car she would eventually buy (a 2011 Honda Civic, oatmeal-colored, with a dashboard crack that would become the first truly *hers* object in her new life, but this is later, this is much later, and the narrator again apologizes for leaping ahead, a tendency that reflects not poor discipline but a genuine anxiety about leaving the reader in the departure too long without the promise of survival). She packed skin care. She packed the Shigaraki dish, the small blue one.

She did not pack a phone charger, because her phone was Henrik's phone, by which the narrator means not that he monitored it (he may have; this was never established) but that he had chosen it, configured it, loaded it with apps he considered appropriate, and, most importantly, pre-populated its contacts with people who were, without exception, *his* people — the sommelier, the ceramicist, the contractor, the doctor, the other doctor, the trainer, the accountant — every number in her phone was a tributary of Henrik, so that even the theoretical act of calling someone for help was an act performed within his infrastructure.

She walked to the gate.

She opened it.

(The estimated mean departure latency for individuals exiting long-term curated domestic environments is 6.3 years from first ideation to physical departure, according to a widely circulated 2020 paper in the *International Review of Domestic Sequestration Studies*, Vol. 9, No. 2, which was retracted eight months after publication when it was discovered that the lead author had fabricated the data set after failing to leave her own husband's ranch in Sonoma. The retraction notice cited "irreproducible methodology and unresolvable conflicts of interest." The finding, however, has proved difficult to discredit experientially.)

Margaux opened the gate and stepped onto Spindrift Road.

The road was not symbolic. It was paved, poorly, by Monterey County, and it led, after a quarter mile of eucalyptus-lined curve, to Highway 1, which led to everything she had not done for eleven years. The sun was out. The ocean was audible but not visible, which felt, to Margaux, like a statement about her entire life up to and including this moment, but was actually just topography.

She walked.
