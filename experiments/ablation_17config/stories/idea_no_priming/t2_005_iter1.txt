# I'm Fine, Don't Worry

## I

You learn to hold the envelope at an angle. Not perpendicular to the steam — that warps the gum and leaves a tideline the recipient's mother will notice, will press her thumb against and wonder who else has been inside her son's words. You hold it at thirty degrees, roughly, and you rotate it, slowly, the way you'd warm a piece of bread over a flame if you wanted it soft but not scorched. The flap opens like a mouth in sleep.

I'd been doing this for five months by then. December 1943. A requisitioned schoolhouse outside Caserta with a gas ring, a kettle, a desk that still had a child's compass scratch in the wood — a crude circle someone had started and never closed. I sat at that desk ten hours a day and I opened other people's souls and I read them and I put them back.

My name is Lena Modesto. I was — I am, I suppose, since the title never left me, only the purpose — a Base Censor, United States Army Postal Service, attached to Fifth Army, II Corps. My job was to read every outgoing letter written by every enlisted man in our section before it was sealed and sent home. I was looking for violations. Troop positions. Unit designations. Named beaches, named towns, named dates of planned advance. Anything the enemy could use if the mail plane went down over the wrong valley. I had a pot of black ink and a regulation brush, and when I found a violation I painted it out, a clean dark rectangle, and I initialed the margin. L.M. My initials are on eleven thousand letters. I counted once, then stopped counting, the way you stop counting drinks.

You want to know what the men wrote. Everyone does. The answer is: the same thing, over and over, in a thousand variations that never stopped breaking apart in my hands.

They wrote about food. The taste of a specific peach from a specific tree in a specific yard. They wrote about women, sometimes coarsely, more often with a strained gentleness that was worse than crudeness because you could see the effort. They wrote about boredom. They wrote about weather. They wrote about bowel movements with the clinical remove of surgeons. They wrote about God — briefly, usually, and only at night, as though prayer were a thing to be done quickly and in the dark, like a man stepping outside to relieve himself.

And they wrote the same lie, the same five words, with such ritual frequency that I began to think of it as a kind of collective psalm, a liturgy of the Americas sung by men who did not know they were in a choir:

*I'm fine, don't worry.*

PFC Daniel Alvarez of Oneonta, New York, wrote it to his mother every week, and every week he also described, in a separate paragraph that he perhaps thought I would not connect to the first, the sound that the wind made coming through the olive groves at night, and how it was not like any wind at home, and how he had begun to be afraid of sounds that were not mechanical, because at least a machine gun had a source.

I blacked out the name of his fire base. I left the wind.

That was the job. You learned the interiors of men who had not invited you in, and you carried those interiors around in your body, and you said nothing, because who would you tell, and what would you say? I know that Richard Tomaszewski of Hamtramck, Michigan, is sleeping with a nurse from the 56th Evac and has written about it to his cousin and not to his wife? I know that Sergeant Victor Moreau hasn't written home in six weeks because he is composing, draft after draft, a letter to his daughter explaining why he re-enlisted when he could have stayed? I found four drafts in four different outgoing bags. Each one broke off at the same place: the sentence that would have had to contain the word *wanted*.

You didn't tell anyone. You opened the kettle. You angled the envelope. You read. You blacked out what could kill them and you left what was already killing them, and you sealed it up again and put it in the outgoing pile, and you went to sleep in a cot that smelled like chalk and damp wool, and in the morning the pile was there again, and the kettle was cold, and you started the water.

I am telling you this first because you need to understand what I knew and how I knew it. I am not a novelist. I am not God. I did not invent the private longings of the men in this story. I read them on paper, in pencil and ink, in handwriting that I came to know better than my own — the left-leaning scrawl of a boy from West Virginia, the cramped block letters of an accountant from Trenton, the calligraphy of a kid from San Francisco whose father was a sign painter and who formed every capital letter like a minor monument.

When I tell you what a man was thinking, I am telling you what he wrote. When I tell you what he felt, I am telling you what he sealed in an envelope and handed to me to open. I did not ask for this knowledge. It was given to me by the United States Army, along with a kettle and a brush and a pot of black ink, and I have not succeeded in giving it back.

I bring this up because of what happened with the roast, and with Aldo, and with Billy, and with the men who walked with us into the valley and did not walk out. But I need you to understand the instrument before I play you the song. The instrument is me. I am the woman who read the letters. I know what was inside.

One more. Before we start. I'll read you one more, just so you have the pitch in your ear.

This is from a letter dated November 29th, 1943. The author was Corporal James Daley of Buttermilk Falls, Pennsylvania, assigned to a supply platoon in our section:

*Dear Pop, I'm fine, don't worry. It rained again. They gave us new socks. The socks are green. I don't know why I'm telling you about socks but it is the most interesting thing that happened today and that is either good or bad depending. Tell Ma the sauce she sent did make it but the jar cracked and it got on Pruitt's extra shirt and he was sore about it. It was good sauce though. Tell her it was.*

*Your boy,*
*Jim*

I blacked out nothing. There was nothing to black out. Socks and sauce and rain. That was the whole letter. It weighed less than a communion wafer. It was the heaviest thing I have ever held.

## II

Aldo Ferretti arrived in my life sideways, the way most things did in the Army — through paperwork. I processed his mail before I met his face. By the time he showed up at the schoolhouse door in person, I already knew that he wrote to his father in Italian and to the Army in English, and that the two Aldos who lived in those two languages were not the same person. In English, Aldo was a quartermaster — precise, clipped, correct. He called his commanding officer "sir" in writing, which no one did. He filed requisition forms in triplicate when duplicate would have sufficed. He was an accountant's son from Bensonhurst who wanted, in every English sentence, to demonstrate that he belonged.

In Italian, he was someone else. He wrote to his father about the light on the Campanian hills and how it made him furious, because it was the same light his father had described from childhood, the same golden-hour wash over the same dry grass, and his father had come to America to escape it, and here was Aldo, sent back by America to stand in it, and what did that make him? American? Italian? A hyphenation the Army could file under "ethnic, Mediterranean, suitable for manual labor and translation duty"? He never wrote these things in English. In English he was fine. Don't worry.

Billy Pruitt I knew from the sauce letter. He was the Pruitt whose shirt had been stained. What I also knew, from seven months of his outgoing mail, was this: Billy's mother was named Edna, and she was — had been — a schoolteacher in Clarksburg, West Virginia, and Billy wrote to her with the earnest, slightly formal grammar of a boy who has been corrected too many times to ever fully relax into a sentence. He called her "Mother" and never "Ma" or "Mom." He signed his letters "Your loving son, William." He told her about every meal the platoon ate, in detail, because Edna Pruitt was diabetic and had lived for years on a restricted diet, and Billy believed — I could see it letter by letter, the way you watch a conviction harden — that if he described enough food, vividly enough, he could feed her by proxy. A boy feeding his sick mother through the mail from across an ocean.

What Billy did not know — what I knew, because it had come through my desk on the incoming side, which I was not supposed to read but did, once, because the Red Cross stamp was smudged and I thought it might be misrouted — was that Edna Pruitt had died on November 14th. Three weeks before this story begins. A Red Cross notification sat in my desk drawer for three days before I passed it along to the chaplain's office. Three days during which I opened Billy's outgoing letters and read him telling his dead mother about the rain and the socks and the sauce, and I sat there with the notification in my drawer and I did not know whether mercy meant giving it to him or holding it, and I still don't, and the chaplain took it from me and said he'd handle it, and as far as I knew, as far as I could tell from the letters Billy kept writing, he had not handled it yet. Billy was still writing to his mother. His mother was in the ground in Clarksburg, West Virginia, and Billy Pruitt was describing his Thursday breakfast to a woman who would never read about it.

They came to me on a Tuesday. Aldo had the plan. Billy had the need.

"We've got a surplus," Aldo said, standing in my doorway with a clipboard — Aldo always had a clipboard, the way a knight has a sword — "and we've got a problem."

The surplus: fourteen cases of boot polish, Kiwi brand, black, arrived on a manifest addressed to a battalion that had been reassigned to the Adriatic coast three weeks earlier. Nobody wanted them. Nobody had signed for them. They existed in the liminal space of military logistics — too documented to steal, too orphaned to requisition. Alongside the boot polish, by a miracle of Army bureaucracy, sat a case of penicillin, twelve vials, that had appeared on our inventory without a corresponding requisition number. It was pharmaceutical ether — real, valuable, and, according to every regulation Aldo could cite, unassignable.

"There are people out there," Aldo said, meaning the countryside, meaning the Campanian villages and farms that the front had rolled over and left behind, "who need penicillin more than we need fourteen cases of boot polish."

"Those people also have livestock," Billy added.

"Some of them."

"Most of them."

"Some of them, Billy."

"A pig. Or a goat. Or a turkey. My mother—" Billy stopped himself. Restarted. "I told my mother I'd bring the boys something warm for Christmas. A real dinner. Not cans. Something you have to carve."

He said this as though it were a military objective, as though the Joint Chiefs had issued a directive: *Pruitt, William, PFC, will procure and prepare one (1) holiday roast for the men of his section, by any means necessary, failure not an option.* His jaw was set. His eyes were the eyes of a man who has made a promise to someone whose approval matters more than survival, and I looked at Billy and I thought about the notification in the chaplain's office, about Edna Pruitt's quiet death in a clapboard house in West Virginia, about the letters that were still going out, stamped and sealed and addressed to a ghost, and I understood that this was not about a roast. This was about a boy keeping faith with a woman he did not yet know was beyond keeping.

Aldo's reasons were different. Aldo's reasons were always different and always the same. He wanted to do something for the men — something extra, something visible, something that would make the company say, *Ferretti came through*. Not because Aldo was generous, though he was. Because Aldo was Sicilian, and the Army had classified him, in its great Linnaean taxonomy of ethnicity, as something between trustworthy and provisional. He'd been passed over for promotion twice. His CO called him "the Italian" — not with malice, necessarily, but with the breezy carelessness that is its own kind of malice, the kind that never has to answer for itself because it never raises its voice. Aldo wanted to bring home a roast the way a medieval knight wanted to bring home a grail: not for the thing itself, but for what the thing would prove about the man who carried it.

"We need you," Aldo said to me.

"For what?"

"You speak Italian."

"I read Italian. I read their Italian. Southern dialects, mostly, and badly."

"Better than anyone else in this building."

This was true. The Army, in its infinite wisdom, had assigned an Italian-American woman from the Bronx to censor Italian-theater mail, and my nonno's Neapolitan had proven, against all odds, useful.

"And," Aldo said, shifting his weight, looking at the kettle, at the stack of letters, at the black ink, "you know everyone. You know what everyone needs. If we're going to trade across a dozen farmhouses, we need someone who understands what people want."

I could have said no. I should have said no. My orders were clear: I was to remain at the base, open mail, black out coordinates, seal, repeat. But it was December, and I had read four thousand letters that month, and in those four thousand letters I had found men describing cold and hunger and fear and the taste of powdered eggs and the sound of artillery and the specific, annihilating boredom of waiting to die in a beautiful country, and not one of them had described anything like hope. And here were two men standing in my doorway with a plan that was stupid and possibly brilliant and definitely unauthorized, and they were asking me to help them steal a pig from the countryside and bring it home and cook it and feed it to men who had written to their mothers that they were fine, don't worry, and I thought: if I read one more letter today I will dissolve. I will become ink and steam and dissolve.

"Who's the escort?" I asked.

"I got a squad from Braker's platoon. Five guys. All volunteers."

Five guys. I knew them all, of course. Not personally. By letter.

Corporal James Daley of the socks and sauce. PFC Thomas Woodhull, who wrote to his sister in Rochester about wanting to be an architect after the war and included small pencil sketches of Italian buildings in every letter — the curve of an arch, the proportions of a door. PFC Reed Osterman, who wrote to no one except, once, a letter to a girl named Clarice that he never sent, that was in his outgoing pile but addressed to a town in Tennessee with no zip code and no street number, as though he were mailing it to the idea of her rather than the actuality. Corporal Sal Fontana, who wrote dirty limericks to his brother and kept a running tally of rats killed in the latrine, and whose handwriting was so bad that I occasionally could not tell whether I was reading a place name or a profanity, which meant I blacked out more than I needed to, which may have mystified his brother or may have improved the limericks. And PFC Martin Goss, the youngest in the section, nineteen, from Buttermilk Falls, Pennsylvania — same town as Daley, which is how they'd ended up inseparable — who wrote to his mother every three days with a consistency that suggested not habit but compulsion, as though the letter were a rope and he was lowering himself down it into the dark and if he let go he would fall. Martin Goss described a county fair in his letters. Three times. Three separate letters. The same fair, the same night, the same girl. He had kissed her behind the Tilt-A-Whirl. He was trying to get the memory right. In the first letter, her hair was brown. In the second, it was dark. In the third, he gave up on the hair and focused on the way she laughed, which he described as "like she was surprised she was laughing," and that version he seemed satisfied with, because he didn't write about the fair again.
