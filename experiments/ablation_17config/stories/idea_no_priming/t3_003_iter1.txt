# The War of Attrition at Lakeridge Preparatory Academy

## I.

There are rooms in every school that do not appear on the architect's plans—rooms that exist only at certain hours, only when certain people are inside them. Room 104 of Lakeridge Preparatory Academy was, by day, merely Mrs. Huang's AP Government classroom: twenty-eight desks in regulation rows, a SmartBoard with a persistent dead pixel in the upper left corner, and a laminated poster of the three branches of government that had been there so long it had begun to yellow at the edges like a letter from another century. But at 3:45 on Tuesday and Thursday afternoons, when the last of Mrs. Huang's fifth-period stragglers had departed and the building had exhaled into that particular stillness that descends on high schools between the final bell and the custodial shift, Room 104 became something else entirely. It became the war room. It became the chapel. It became the place where seven teenagers did the most impractical, extravagant, and secretly sacred thing that adolescents can do: they cared, with ferocious and voluntary precision, about something that would never appear on a transcript in any form that mattered.

The Lakeridge Preparatory Academy Model United Nations team had not lost a tournament in four years.

This fact was recorded in the trophy case in the main hallway—a glass-fronted cabinet between the athletics display and the performing arts showcase, stocked with a progression of brass and acrylic obelisks that tracked, in the language of competitive hardware, an unbroken dynasty. But the trophy case told the story the way a box score tells the story of a baseball game: accurately, and completely beside the point. The real record of those four years was in Room 104 itself—in the configuration of desks that had been rearranged so many times that the linoleum bore faint scuff-marks like the footprint diagrams in a dance studio, in the stack of stolen hotel notepads from every conference they'd attended, in the ambient smell of Takis and dry-erase marker and the particular variety of stress sweat produced by seventeen-year-olds who have just realized that the delegate from Finland has made an argument they did not anticipate.

Seven of them, on this afternoon. Let us see them as they were.

Margaux Ellison-Park sat at the front of the rearranged desks—not because she had assigned herself to the front, but because the room had, over time, oriented itself around her the way iron filings orient around a magnet. She was seventeen, angular, and possessed of the kind of dark blond hair that she kept in a bun so tight it seemed to be an engineering project. She did not raise her voice because she had never needed to. She ran the room the way an air traffic controller runs a radar screen: with a calm that was, upon close inspection, indistinguishable from a profound and total inability to tolerate disorder. At this moment she was reviewing Sudan's position on water rights with a red pen that she held like a scalpel, making small, precise annotations in a handwriting so controlled it might have been mistaken for a font.

To her left, Dev Chandrasekaran was doing two things at once, as he always was: reading a Foreign Affairs article on his phone and eating Takis with the hand that was not scrolling, his fingers stained the particular nuclear orange of Fuego dust. Dev had a gift that was both his greatest asset and, on certain days, his most antisocial quality: he could hear an argument once and, within thirty seconds, produce the counterargument, the counter-counterargument, and the obscure 1987 UN General Assembly resolution that made both of them moot. He did this while eating. He did this while appearing to be asleep. He mouthed counterarguments the way other boys mouthed song lyrics—unconsciously, perpetually, as if geopolitics were a frequency only he could receive.

Across the aisle, Yael Mendelson-Katz sat with her legs folded beneath her—a posture she adopted so reflexively that she had once done it during a formal committee session, realized her mistake only when the chair called her out of order for having both feet off the floor, and responded, without missing a beat, by citing the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child's provisions on physical comfort. She was the team's procedural savant. She had memorized Robert's Rules of Order the way some people memorize the periodic table or batting averages—not as an academic exercise but as a passion, an aesthetic experience, the way a pianist knows scales. Points of order delighted her. Procedural motions were, in her hands, instruments of quiet devastation. She could table a resolution the way a chess player could pin a queen: with three words and an expression of absolute innocence.

Behind her, writing in a composition notebook with a mechanical pencil, sat Theo Kaplan-Reyes, who was the team's writer—not in the sense that he drafted position papers, though he did, but in the deeper sense that he was the one who understood that Model UN was, beneath its procedural surface, an exercise in *narrative*. A resolution was a story about the world, and Theo could make you believe it. He was slight, prone to allergies, and had the kind of face that adults invariably described as "sensitive," which he privately hated because it was true. He wrote opening speeches that made committee chairs sit up straighter. He also wrote other things—observations, fragments, small precise cruelties about the human condition—in the margins of his notebooks, which he showed to no one.

Adjacent to Theo, occupying approximately twice as much desk space as the desk was designed to provide, was Amara Okafor-Williams, who had joined Model UN as a freshman because her older sister had done debate and Amara had wanted, with the particular ferocity available only to younger siblings, to do something *adjacent but superior*. She had stayed because she had discovered that she was good at something she had not expected to be good at: she was good at *people*. Not in the glib, social-butterfly sense, but in the sense that she could walk into a committee of sixty strangers and, within an hour, identify the four delegates who actually mattered, determine what each of them wanted, and construct an alliance that gave them all just enough to vote her way. She called this "reading the room." Margaux called it "Amara's thing." It was, in competition, roughly as valuable as a nuclear submarine in a bathtub.

The sixth member was quiet, and had always been quiet, and would have been unremarkable in any setting that did not reward the particular form of intelligence that Lucas Wendt possessed, which was the ability to sit in a room full of noise and see the *structure* underneath it. Lucas was the team's researcher. He built the binders—three-ring, color-tabbed, thick as hymnals—that contained every fact, every statistic, every precedent that the team might need when the floor shifted beneath them. He found the provision in the 1994 Cairo Programme of Action that won them their first regional title. He found the footnote in the 2003 Maputo Protocol that broke a deadlock at state. He did not speak often in committee. When he did, it was because he had found something, and when Lucas found something, committees went quiet the way forests go quiet before weather.

And finally, folded into the corner desk closest to the window, with her shoes off and her cardigan sleeves pulled over her hands, was Nina Chen, who was the youngest member by five months and the most talented by a margin that no one, including Nina, had yet fully understood. Nina had a quality that experienced MUN judges sometimes described, in their private shorthand, as *the ear*—an instinct for when a room was about to turn, when a coalition was hollowing out from the inside, when the moment had arrived to stop talking and start listening. She was sixteen. She bit her nails. She had a habit of looking slightly past the person she was speaking to, as if consulting an invisible advisor just over their left shoulder, and this habit—which was in fact simple social anxiety—read, in committee, as a kind of oracular detachment that unnerved opponents twice her age.

Seven of them. In the room. Binders open, resolutions drafted, the late-October light falling through the windows in long amber parallelograms that touched the floor like the fingers of something gentle and temporary. Dev was arguing with Yael about whether a motion to reconsider could procedurally override a tabled amendment while simultaneously extracting the last Taki from the bag with the delicacy of a surgeon. Amara was showing Nina something on her phone—a tweet from a rival school's delegate that was either an insult or a compliment, and the determination of which had occupied them for the better part of ten minutes. Lucas had his binder open to a section labeled WATER SECURITY — NILE BASIN and was adding a Post-it note with a fact he had found that morning, a small act of devotion as unconscious as breathing. Theo was writing in his notebook. Margaux was watching all of them with an expression that might have been mistaken, by someone who did not know her, for mere assessment, but which was in fact something closer to the look a gardener gives a living thing she has helped grow.

They had not lost. They did not think about losing. Losing was something that happened to other organizations in other schools in other districts—schools that did not have Margaux's preparation or Dev's counterarguments or Yael's procedural mastery or Lucas's binders or Amara's coalitions or Theo's speeches or Nina's ear. The trophy case in the hallway was not a source of pride so much as a fact of nature, like the building's foundation or the direction of magnetic north. It was simply there. It had always been there.

This was a Tuesday in late October, and it was the last afternoon all seven of them would be in Room 104 without knowing what was coming.

The narrator knows this. The narrator has always known this. And if the narrator lingers here—on the amber light, on the Taki dust, on the way Nina's cardigan sleeves swallowed her hands, on the sound of seven people breathing in a room they had made into a country—it is because some things, once dismantled, can only be preserved by someone who was paying attention before the dismantling began.

They were so good. They were so prepared.

They had no idea what they were unprepared for.

Pursuant to the district's ongoing Fiscal Sustainability Initiative and in accordance with Board Resolution 2024-17 (adopted September 12), please be advised that the following programmatic adjustment will take effect at the conclusion of the current semester:

**The Lakeridge Preparatory Academy Debate Society** (Program Code: ECA-0114) will be discontinued effective December 20.

This decision reflects a comprehensive review of enrollment trends, per-student expenditure ratios, and competitive outcome metrics across all extracurricular offerings. The Debate Society's three-year enrollment has declined from 22 participants to 9 (a 59% reduction), while per-student program costs have increased by 34% in the same period due to fixed coaching and transportation expenditures. In the context of the district's obligation to align resource allocation with student engagement and demonstrable outcomes, continued funding at current levels is not sustainable.

Affected students are encouraged to explore alternative extracurricular opportunities. The district remains committed to providing a robust and diverse menu of co-curricular programming consistent with its mission of academic excellence and fiscal responsibility.

Faculty advisors with questions about transition protocols may contact the Office of Student Activities.

Dr. Harold R. Kessler

&nbsp;

Nobody brought it up at practice.

This fact requires a moment of attention, because it is a small thing, and the story of how institutions die is almost always a story of small things.

The Lakeridge Model UN team learned about the elimination of the Debate Society the way most people learn about most eliminations: through the ambient circulation of information in a building where everyone shares the same hallways and half the same classes. Kyle Yoon sat in Dev's calculus class. The debate coach, Mr. Ferrante, shared a hallway with Mrs. Huang. The memo itself was not secret—it was posted, per district policy, on the school's internal bulletin board, where it occupied the same visual register as a notice about parking lot repaving or a reminder about the staff holiday potluck. It was designed to be seen without being *noticed*. It succeeded.

The seven members of the Model UN team registered the news in seven different ways, across seven different moments in the forty-eight hours between the memo's distribution and their next practice session. Dev saw Kyle's face in calculus and felt a pang that he would later describe to himself as sympathy and that was, in fact, something more complicated. Yael heard about it from a friend in AP Lit and experienced a brief, reflexive impulse to draft a procedural challenge to the superintendent's authority before her rational mind reminded her that Robert's Rules did not apply to reality. Theo wrote three lines in his notebook about it and then tore the page out. Lucas looked up the Debate Society's enrollment figures and found that the memo's numbers were accurate, which bothered him in a way he could not articulate—as though accuracy and rightness were the same thing, and he had just discovered they were not. Amara texted Nina a screenshot of the memo with a single question mark. Nina did not respond for four hours, and when she did, all she wrote was: *that's bad*.

Margaux read the memo on the school's bulletin board during the four-minute passing period between second and third period. She read it once, quickly, the way she read everything—scanning for the operative clauses, the structural logic, the load-bearing sentences. She noted the enrollment figures. She noted the per-student cost calculation. She noted the phrase *demonstrable outcomes* and understood, with the particular clarity available to people who think in systems, that this phrase was a weapon that could be pointed in any direction. Then she went to AP European History and did not think about it again until practice.

At practice, she did not bring it up. No one brought it up.

Here is the thing about not bringing something up: it is an act. It is a decision with a shape and a weight and consequences, the same as any other decision. The seven members of the Lakeridge Model UN team walked into Room 104 on that Thursday afternoon and they arranged their desks and opened their binders and prepared for the regional qualifier with the same focus and discipline they had always brought to their work, and the silence about debate was not an oversight. It was a moat.

Because here is what each of them knew, and none of them said: the Debate Society was their rival. Not a blood rival, not in any dramatic sense—they shared no members, competed in no overlapping circuits—but in the deeper, more structural sense that both clubs drew from the same pool of funding, the same pool of administrative attention, the same pool of parental pride, and the same pool of students who might, in a given September, choose to argue about policy in one room or simulate the UN General Assembly in another. The Debate Society's decline had been, if you squinted—and all seven of them squinted, some more consciously than others—the Model UN team's gain. Nine students were not enough, the superintendent had determined. Model UN had seven, but Model UN also had the trophy case. The math was different when you were winning.

They did not say: *We should fight this.* They did not say: *If they can do this to debate, they can do this to us.* They did not say anything, because the silence was comfortable and the loss was someone else's and the castle felt strong and the moat felt wide.
