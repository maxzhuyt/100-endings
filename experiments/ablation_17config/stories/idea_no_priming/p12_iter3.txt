# The Spa of Overlapping Surveillance

---

## I.

The helicopter smelled like divorce—leather conditioner and something underneath it, chemical and sweet, the olfactory equivalent of a man who'd had his sweat glands removed so his third wife wouldn't know he still felt things. Marta pressed her temple against the window and watched the Alps grind past like the jawbone of something that had died grand and slow.

The pilot was thinking about his daughter.

Not tenderly. Not the way you'd want. He was thinking about the nineteen euros she owed him from when he'd spotted her at a petrol station in Friedrichshafen, and how she'd said she'd Revolut it, and how she hadn't, and how this was, in the architecture of his resentment, proof that his ex-wife had taught the girl to see him as a resource rather than a person—the thought running in its hamster wheel even now, while he compensated for a crosswind that could paste them both across a north-facing cliff, nineteen euros, nineteen euros, she didn't even say thank you, nineteen euros—

Marta closed her eyes.

That was the thing no one understood, the thing she'd stopped trying to explain sometime around her second divorce and her first involuntary commitment. It was never the dark secrets. The dark secrets were almost a relief—at least they had *structure*, narrative shape, the decency to organize themselves into something with a beginning and an end. The thing that actually hollowed you out was the ordinary stuff. The petty recirculating grievance that constituted ninety-one percent of all human cognition. She had not arrived at that number casually. She had been doing this for thirty-three years. Ninety-one percent. The remaining nine was divided between sexual fantasy (four percent, and she was being generous, and the fantasies were almost never about the person the thinker claimed to love), low-grade logistics such as whether the dishwasher had been run (three percent), and a final two percent that she would classify, reluctantly, as genuine consciousness—moments of actual awareness, actual presence, actual selfhood breaking through the slurry like a diver surfacing for one raw gasp before being pulled back under by the weight of all the nothing they insisted on thinking.

Two percent. That was what she was listening for. That was always what she was listening for.

Nineteen euros. The crosswind shoved them sideways. The pilot corrected without breaking his internal stride. She didn't even say thank you.

Marta opened the dossier on her lap, though she'd memorized it days ago. Three guests at the Klinik PrivAlpin had disappeared over fourteen months. Disappeared was doing generous work. Two had been reported missing by staff—beds unslept in, luggage still in suite, charges still accruing to a black Amex that no one contested. The third had simply stopped existing in any database anywhere, which was the kind of disappearance that cost more than the other kind. Swiss police had done exactly what Swiss police were designed to do with wealthy foreign nationals who vanished from altitude: they had been discreet. They had been thorough. They had found nothing. They had filed the nothing with meticulous precision and moved on.

The people who had hired Marta were not Swiss police. They were a firm that did not have a name, only a billing address in The Hague that she suspected was a mailbox inside another mailbox. She had taken the job for the money, she told herself. The money was extraordinary. She had taken the job because the alternative was a February in Wiesbaden with no cases and the growing suspicion that her liver was staging a work-to-rule action. She had taken the job because—

No. She'd taken it for the money. The money was the reason. She underlined something in the dossier she'd already underlined twice and stared at the ink until the other reasons, the ones that had to do with the fact that the third missing person had been, twenty years ago, someone she'd once shared a weekend with in Trieste—a weekend she did not think about except every single day—receded to a manageable distance.

She was very good at manageable distance. It was the only skill telepathy actually conferred.

The Klinik materialized through cloud cover like a bone breaking through skin. White concrete. Cantilevered terraces. Floor-to-ceiling glass that reflected the mountains back at themselves, because of course it did, because the kind of person who came here had long ago stopped distinguishing between a window and a mirror. It sat on a shoulder of rock at twenty-two hundred meters, above the tree line, below the snow line, in that specific altitude band where the air was thin enough to make clients feel they were achieving something just by breathing. The architect had won a prize. Marta had read about the prize. The jury had praised the building's "dialogue with impermanence." She wanted to ask impermanence how it felt about the conversation.

The helicopter settled on a pad marked with a cross—medical, she assumed, though with this clientele you could never rule out religious—and the pilot powered down and thought, clear as a bell, *Nineteen euros. She could have at least said danke.*

Marta climbed out into air that tasted of snow and altitude and the total absence of anything living. She pulled her coat tighter. She did not say danke.

---

## II.

They processed her through a security vestibule that had been designed to look like a gallery in a museum of contemporary art—poured concrete walls, a single Ellsworth Kelly print (the red one, naturally, the one that said *I cost what your house cost*), indirect lighting calibrated to the color temperature of a particularly flattering sunset. The biometric scanner was hidden inside what appeared to be a reception desk carved from a single piece of Carrara marble. The man behind it had the face of a Bernese Oberland ski instructor and the mind of a mid-level intelligence operative running a cover so deep he sometimes forgot which parts of his personality were issued and which were original. Right now he was both genuinely welcoming her and taking a high-resolution mental photograph of her bone structure against a database he'd memorized. Marta let him find nothing. Her cover—visiting specialist in somatic therapy, referral from a Zurich clinic that existed only as a website and a phone number that rang to a woman who would confirm anything—had been backstopped well enough to survive biometrics. What it could not survive was a telepath scanning the scanner, so she looked at the Ellsworth Kelly and pretended to appreciate it and did not mention that he was also, in a completely unrelated tranche of cognition, ranking the female guests by the likelihood that they would sleep with him if he upgraded their room, and that he had developed a spreadsheet system for this that he found genuinely innovative.

"Frau Voss, welcome to PrivAlpin. Your orientation session begins in forty minutes. May I have your devices?"

She handed over the phone. She kept the other phone, the one that wasn't a phone, in the lining of her bag where it would read as a portable white-noise machine if scanned, because it was also a portable white-noise machine, because her cover was the kind of person who traveled with a portable white-noise machine, because her cover was, in most respects, her.

The atrium beyond security was a cathedral of hush. Her shoes made sounds on the heated limestone floor that she could hear because there were no other sounds. No music. No fountain. No murmured conversation. The Klinik PrivAlpin had engineered silence the way other luxury establishments engineered ambiance—as a product, a deliverable, something you paid eleven thousand euros a night to have applied to you like a poultice. The air smelled of eucalyptus and something she identified after a moment as ozone, faintly, the way the future smells in movies. Everything was white or pale gray or the kind of green that exists only in bottles of things that cost three hundred euros per hundred milliliters. A woman in cashmere the color of fog glided past with a glass of something that contained chlorophyll and, Marta could not help but register, the firm conviction that her husband's business partner was embezzling from a foundation that existed to launder the proceeds of a mining concession that had displaced forty thousand people in the eastern Congo, and that this was fine, but the thing that was *not* fine was that the business partner's wife had worn the same Loro Piana coat as her to a dinner in Gstaad, which was a declaration of war, and she would need to discuss this with her astrologist before Thursday—

Marta turned the volume down. Not literally. There was no volume knob. But over three decades she had developed a set of crude internal mechanisms—visualization techniques, mantras, a particular way of clenching her back teeth that somehow narrowed the aperture—that could reduce the inflow from a torrent to a stream. The stream was still there. The stream was always there. She had not experienced actual silence since she was nine years old and the first mind she'd ever read, involuntarily, on a school bus in Essen, had been her best friend Katrin's, and Katrin had been thinking, with the calm, unperformative cruelty that only a nine-year-old can produce, *I wish Marta would stop talking to me because her breath smells like ham and I want to sit with Lena but I don't know how to tell her without her crying because she always cries—*

That had been the first one. The wound the document opened. Everything since was annotation.

She was shown to a suite on the third floor—south-facing, the Matterhorn visible if she cared about the Matterhorn, which she did not, because the Matterhorn was essentially a geological narcissist and she'd had enough of those. The suite had a bathroom larger than her apartment in Wiesbaden and a bed that looked like it had been engineered by people who understood sleep as a form of surrender. She sat on the bed and opened the aperture.

The building detonated.

Not physically. Telepathically it came apart like a piñata. Fifty-three minds at varying distances, in varying states of guardedness, poured through her. She let them come. This was the survey—the first wide-band read of any environment, the part that felt like sticking your head into a beehive and trying to count species.

Third floor, east wing: a man in his seventies, Emirati by the flavor of his internal monologue (she could not explain what she meant by flavor; she had tried; the psychologists in Hamburg had given up), reviewing with great care the provenance of a Rothko he was considering as a gift for a mistress he did not like but who liked the Rothko, and his anxiety was not about the money but about whether the mistress would understand that the Rothko was a test—if she took it, she was venal, and he could leave her with a clean conscience, but if she refused it, she had taste, and he would have to stay—

Second floor, west wing: a woman, Russian extraction, British education, currently in a robe, thinking in three languages simultaneously about the thirteen-step skincare routine she'd been prescribed and also, on a completely different cognitive channel that ran beneath the skincare the way a sewer runs beneath a boulevard, about the intercepted communication her handler had shown her in Bern last Tuesday and what it implied about the solvency of a certain private bank in Liechtenstein—

Ground floor, kitchen: a sous-chef, thinking about nothing except brunoise, beautiful clean brunoise of shallot, the knife hitting the board in a rhythm that constituted, in Marta's professional opinion, the most contented mental state in the building—

Fourth floor, penthouse level: a man whose name she knew from magazines and tribunals, a man who had been photographed shaking hands with four different heads of state, all of whom were now dead or indicted, and he was thinking—gently, tenderly, with a specificity that made Marta's throat constrict—about his granddaughter's piano recital last month, a Clementi sonatina, how she'd fumbled the repeat in the development section and looked up at him in the audience with an expression of such transparent panic that he'd wanted to rush the stage and hold her, and how the moment she'd recovered, found her place, kept going, he had felt something he could not name but which Marta could, because she was hearing it, and it was love, the real kind, the tectonic kind, the kind that did not care that its vessel was a man who had authorized things in the 1990s that she'd read about in classified files and could not unread—

She shut the aperture down. Hard. Her teeth ached from clenching.

This. This was why she was the way she was. Not because people were worse than they pretended. They were. But also because they were, intermittently, *better* than they had any right to be, and the whiplash—the moral whiplash of hearing a war criminal love his granddaughter with more authentic tenderness than most people could muster for their own spouses—was the thing that had actually broken her. Not the ugliness. The unauthorized beauty in ugly people. That was the part she couldn't metabolize.

She showered. She put on the robe they'd left her, which felt like being embraced by a cloud that had a graduate degree in textiles. She reviewed the guest list she'd compiled from open sources and the less-open sources the Hague mailbox people had provided. Forty-seven registered guests. Six she could identify as probable intelligence assets based on biographical lacunae (the holes in a résumé were always more legible than the résumé itself). Another eight who were *certainly* intelligence-adjacent, the gray zone of consultants and fixers and people whose LinkedIn profiles said "strategic advisory" the way a shark's dorsal fin says "ocean."

She found Declan Foye in the orientation session.

He was already seated when she arrived—a conference room designed to look like a yoga studio, with fourteen guests arranged in a semicircle and a facilitator named Brigitte who radiated the particular Swiss-German serenity that comes from being paid enough to believe your own program. Marta took a chair and scanned the room and immediately wished she hadn't, because twelve of the fourteen minds were running the same basic subroutine: *What am I doing here, this is absurd, I could be in Mustique, I wonder if anyone recognizes me, I hope no one recognizes me, the person across from me is definitely someone I've seen in a deposition—*

And then there was Declan Foye, and his mind was an ambush of a different kind.

He was forty-eight, maybe fifty, with the build of a man who had once been athletic and was now in a negotiated détente with his body. Blue eyes, slightly too close together, which gave him the look of someone perpetually trying to read small print. He was wearing the Klinik-issued robe like a man wearing a costume he found personally humiliating but had committed to on the level of principle, the way you commit to a bit. His cover—she'd been briefed—was mid-level American tech executive burnout, which was almost insultingly plausible. His actual employer was Langley, and his operational brief, as far as the Hague mailbox people understood it, overlapped with hers in ways that had been explained to her as "synergistic," a word she despised with a passion she usually reserved for people who said "journey" when they meant "thing that happened."

His mind hit her, and she almost laughed.

He was thinking about his hair.

Not casually. Not the way a person glances at a receding hairline in a bathroom mirror and sighs and moves on. Declan Foye was thinking about his hair the way medieval monks thought about God—with single-pointed devotion, with ecstatic suffering, with a faith that the object of contemplation might yet reveal its deeper meaning if he only looked *harder*. He was calculating the rate of loss. He was comparing the morning's shower-drain evidence to yesterday's. He was constructing a mental timeline that began three years ago, when a barber in Arlington had said "We should probably talk about your crown" and his entire cosmology had shifted from one in which hair was a given to one in which hair was a project, a campaign, a rearguard action fought with minoxidil and biotin and a shampoo that cost forty-one dollars and smelled like a laboratory's idea of the ocean. He was thinking about this now, in this room, in this building full of people who could collapse economies with a phone call, and he was thinking about it with a *sincerity* that was, to Marta, almost extraterrestrial.

She went deeper. Involuntarily, the way you lean in when someone whispers.
