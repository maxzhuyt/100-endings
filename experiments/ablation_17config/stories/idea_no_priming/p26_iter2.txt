### FOREWORD

The document you are about to read represents six years of continuous observational work conducted through the Bridewell Institute's Remote Lens Array¹ into the topological region designated **Universe 6-Bravo** — a late-stage collapsing pocket universe first cataloged in the Bridewell Multiverse Census of 2037, and identified as a site of significant ethnographic interest following the detection of organized human settlement persisting well beyond the onset of irreversible stellar degradation.²

I wish to be candid about the nature of this project from the outset, as candor has been in short supply among my critics. What follows is not mere documentation of a dying world — many worlds are dying, and many are documented, and the archives sag under the weight of the unremarkable.³ What follows is a case study in a far rarer phenomenon: the persistence of institutional identity — specifically, the identity of a law enforcement officer — in an environment where the institutional framework underwriting that identity has been, by any rational measure, annihilated. The subject of this study, U.S. Marshal Dina Calk, badge number 4407 (a designation traceable to an analogue of the United States Marshals Service, Western District of a political subdivision called "Oklahoma"), operated for approximately nineteen months inside a city that had been partitioned by two competing eschatological cartels, had no functioning judiciary, no federal authority, no plausible future, and — as of the eleventh month of her activity — no sun of sufficient luminosity to sustain agriculture beyond a single additional growing season.

She continued to serve warrants.

I do not use the word "fascinating" in scholarly contexts, as it is the vocabulary of the amateur. But the reader will, I trust, understand why the Bridewell Institute saw fit to dedicate a Remote Lens to this particular subject for the duration of her observable activity.⁴

The structure of this account proceeds chronologically through Marshal Calk's operations in the city of Tulsa, Universe 6-Bravo, from the date of her first documented appearance to the date of — but I am getting ahead of myself. We will arrive at the end when we arrive at it. What matters first is the beginning: the apparatus by which the account is assembled, the methodology by which it is verified, and the scholarly framework within which its significance can be properly understood.

I will note, for the record, that the peer-review process for this document has been unusually contentious, and that certain reviewers have seen fit to append their objections directly into the margins of my text.⁵ I have permitted this, as the Bridewell Institute values open discourse and because I am confident that the weight of the evidence supports my interpretive framework. The reader may evaluate the marginal commentary as they see fit. I suspect they will find it illuminating, if not always in the ways its authors intend.

— Dr. Ellsworth Kaan
The Bridewell Institute
November, 2043

## PART ONE: THE SUBJECT ARRIVES

---

### Chapter 1: Provenance of a Subject

On the morning of March 7th — by the local calendar of Universe 6-Bravo, which still adhered to the Gregorian system despite the increasingly dubious relevance of solar timekeeping⁶ — a woman was found unconscious on the shoulder of a road designated Interstate 44, approximately three miles east of what had been the Tulsa city center, now referred to by local inhabitants as "the Midline" due to its position between the two primary factional territories.

She was wearing a dark blue windbreaker with the letters U.S. MARSHAL stenciled across the back in yellow block capitals. She was carrying a firearm — a Glock 19M, standard law enforcement issue, loaded with a full magazine and one round chambered. She was carrying a badge. She was carrying, in the left pocket of her windbreaker, a protein bar, partially unwrapped, which forensic analysis of Remote Lens imagery suggests was manufactured by a company that does not exist in any cataloged universe, including our own.⁷

She had no identification beyond the badge. She had no memory of how she had arrived on the shoulder of Interstate 44. Based on subsequent behavioral analysis and her own intermittent, fragmentary statements — captured by the Remote Lens but not, I must stress, elicited by any contact from the Institute — she appeared to retain procedural and semantic memory (she knew how to clear a weapon; she knew what a U.S. Marshal was; she knew the Miranda warning verbatim) while lacking episodic memory almost entirely. She did not know her middle name. She did not know if she had been born in the state whose government had presumably issued her badge. She knew her last name only because it was engraved on the badge itself.⁸

The sky above her, when she opened her eyes, was the color of a television tuned to a dead channel — not gray so much as *attenuated*, as though the spectrum had been placed under gentle but insistent compression. The sun, which eleven months earlier had measured 0.52 degrees of arc when observed from the surface of this Earth (already anomalously small compared to ours, for reasons detailed in Appendix A), now measured 0.41 degrees. It was warm enough to sustain life. It was not warm enough to sustain comfort. The shadows were wrong — too sharp in some places, too diffuse in others, their angles inconsistent in ways that a physicist from our own universe would find difficult to reconcile with a single light source, however diminished.⁹

Dina Calk sat up. She looked at the road. She looked at the sky. She looked at the gun in her hand, which she had been gripping, unconsciously, the entire time.

And then — and this is the first datum of genuine ethnographic interest, the first moment at which the subject distinguishes herself from any other confused person waking in trauma — she checked the safety. She holstered the weapon. She stood up. She brushed the gravel from her pants.

One cannot help but observe that this sequence of actions — from supine confusion to upright, armed, procedurally correct readiness — took less than forty seconds. One cannot help but observe that a person with no episodic memory, confronted with a sky that behaves wrongly, chose as her first conscious act to *verify the operational status of her sidearm.* The implications for our understanding of the relationship between institutional identity and embodied automaticity are significant, and I will develop them at length in Part Three.¹⁰

What the Remote Lens showed next was less theoretically elegant but, I will concede, more immediately gripping.

She walked east, toward the city. Within twenty minutes she encountered the first checkpoint.

### Chapter 2: The Checkpoint at Memorial Drive

The checkpoint at Memorial Drive was a Stoic installation — a fact that Dina could not have known, as she had not yet learned the factional taxonomy that will be detailed in Part Two. What she knew was this: there were concrete barriers across three lanes of the four-lane road, channeling all foot traffic into a single lane flanked by sandbag walls. Behind the sandbags, three people in mismatched tactical gear — one in a police-style vest, one in a hunting jacket, one in what appeared to be a marching-band uniform with the brass buttons replaced by duct-taped patches — sat on folding chairs with rifles across their laps. A hand-painted sign above the lane read:

**TULSA PROVISIONAL AUTHORITY — SECTOR EAST**
**DECLARE ALL PROVISIONS. PRESENT IDENTIFICATION.**
**NO PASSAGE AFTER DUSK.**

A second sign, smaller, affixed below the first:

**"In the diminishing, dignity."**

Dina approached the checkpoint with her hands visible — another instance of procedural automaticity that merits note, as it suggests her embodied training overrode her lack of conscious knowledge about the specific threat environment.¹¹ The guard in the marching-band uniform — later identified through cross-referencing the Remote Lens footage as a man named Curtis Wyatt, age twenty-three, former trombone player in the Booker T. Washington High School Bulldogs marching band — raised one hand in a gesture that was half-wave, half-halt.

"You armed?" he said.

"Yes," Dina said. Then, after a pause that lasted approximately two seconds but which the Remote Lens captured with sufficient resolution to observe the micro-expressions crossing her face — confusion, then something harder, something that resolved into a posture I can only describe as *professional*: "I'm a U.S. Marshal."

Curtis Wyatt looked at her. The guard in the police vest looked at her. The guard in the hunting jacket did not look up from the paperback he was reading, whose title the Remote Lens could not resolve.

"A marshal," Curtis said.

"That's right."

"Ma'am, there isn't a U.S. anything. Not for — what's it been?" He looked at the guard in the police vest. "Nine months? Ten?"

"Eleven," the police-vest guard said. "Since the governor's convoy."

"Eleven months. No federal government, no state government, no county, no city. We're the Provisional Authority, Sector East. You're in our jurisdiction."

Dina looked at the sign. She looked at the sandbags, the rifles, the folding chairs. She looked at the marching-band uniform. Her right hand was near her holster — not on it, but near it, and any trained observer would recognize the posture as one of readiness rather than aggression. Curtis Wyatt appeared to recognize it.

"I'm not looking for trouble," Dina said. "I need to get into the city. I need food and I need to know what happened."

Curtis studied her for a long moment. Then the guard in the hunting jacket finally looked up from his paperback and said, flatly, without introduction: "She doesn't know."

"Know what?" Dina said.

"Anything," he said. "Look at her face. She doesn't know anything."

This was, by any reasonable assessment, correct.

What followed was, from an ethnographic perspective, a remarkably compressed act of world-orientation. Over the next eleven minutes — timed precisely by the Remote Lens — Curtis Wyatt explained the following to Dina Calk, in the following order, while she stood at a military checkpoint with a gun on her hip and no memory of her own middle name:

1. The sun had been shrinking since April of the previous year. Scientists had no explanation. The last working observatory had been dismantled for parts by September.

2. The federal government had collapsed in June, following a series of cascading infrastructure failures that Wyatt described with an economy and vividness that suggests he had delivered this summary before, possibly many times, to other confused arrivals.
