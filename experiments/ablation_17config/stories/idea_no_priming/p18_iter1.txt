# Marlo Voss and the Copper Town

## I.

The town of Río Seco sits in a trough between two ridgelines the way a body sits in a bathtub — settled, waiting, not going anywhere on its own. The Chihuahuan Desert gives it no reason to exist except the copper, and the copper, after a hundred and eleven years of continuous extraction, has mostly stopped giving reasons too. What remains is a functioning smelting works at the southern end of the central arroyo, and the two families who need it, and seventeen hundred people whose lives are organized around the fact of that need the way a rosary is organized around a string.

The Ortega compound occupies the eastern ridge. Cinderblock and rebar. Satellite dishes. A retaining wall originally poured by the Anaconda Mining Company in 1943 and subsequently improved with the addition of a machinegun nest that everyone in town calls *la pajarera* — the birdcage — because of the way the corrugated-steel wind guard whistles when the evening thermals come up from the valley. From *la pajarera* you can see the entire town: the motel with its twelve rooms and its kidney-shaped swimming pool drained to a film of green chemical scum, the church of San Rafael with its two bell towers of unequal height (the southern tower was rebuilt after the earthquake of 1978 and the diocese ran out of money four meters short of symmetry), the shuttered union hall with its windows boarded and its murals of dignified, historically implausible miners fading in the alkaline light, and, directly across the valley on the western ridge, the Batista compound, which is newer and uglier and has a helipad.

The Batistas arrived in 2011. The Ortegas have been here since the mines were Mexican-owned, which is to say since before the mines were Mexican-owned, which is to say always. This is a relevant distinction in Río Seco. The Ortegas believe it entitles them to the smelting works and everything that passes through its furnaces — the copper concentrate that arrives by truck from three active pit mines to the south, and the other materials that arrive by truck from elsewhere and leave as other materials still, processed, refined, stripped of their identifiable molecular signatures in ways that copper smelting makes chemically convenient. The Batistas believe their helipad and their federal connections and their newer, uglier compound entitle them to the same. The town sits between these two beliefs the way a tongue sits between two rows of teeth. It serves both. It belongs to neither. It gets bitten often.

The smelting works itself is a cathedral of rust and function. Four Hoboken converters, only two operational. A sulfuric acid plant that leaks. Slag heaps to the east that have acquired, over a century, the geological permanence of foothills — gray-black and vitreous, their surfaces pocked with rainwater pools that turn an arsenic teal after storms. The copper is everywhere because copper is promiscuous: it greens the rain gutters, verdigris the church bells, blooms in blue-green rosettes on the exposed pipes of the motel's inoperative heating system. It turns the local dogs' paw pads a faint celadon. It is in the water. It is in the teeth of children. The Anaconda Mining Company went bankrupt in this very valley, and the Ortegas inherited its equipment, its tunnels, and its fundamental economic theology, which is that a place exists to have its insides removed.

On the main road — Río Seco has one, running north-south through the arroyo — there is a cantina called *El Pilar* with a mural that was painted by a WPA-adjacent program during the Cárdenas presidency. It depicts copper miners in the socialist-realist mode: enormous hands, noble brows, musculature that no miner who ever lived possessed. The mural is flaking. The miners' enormous hands are losing fingers. Their noble brows are developing the texture of eczema. On the opposite wall of *El Pilar,* someone has hung a flat-screen television that plays telenovelas and fútbol at the same volume, and between the socialist miners and the television is a row of plastic tables where men from both families sometimes drink at the same time, carefully, the way two scorpions share a boot.

The church of San Rafael, with its mismatched towers, hosts a priest named Father Lourdes who is sixty-one and diabetic and has brokered more cease-fires than the Diocese of Chihuahua has any interest in knowing about. The motel — the Álamo Motor Inn, named with an irony its original owner probably did not intend — is managed by a woman named Graciana Perea, who is forty-seven, childless, and capable of looking at a man holding a gun and a man holding a suitcase and knowing immediately which one is more dangerous, which is usually the one with the suitcase. The retired deputy, Esteban Clare, lives in a cinder-block house at the north end of town where the road begins its climb toward the border, and from his front porch — a slab of concrete with an aluminum awning and two chairs, one for himself and one for whoever comes to tell him what's happened now — he watches the ridge roads with a pair of Zeiss binoculars he bought in a better decade and a patience that is either philosophical or merely old.

These buildings, these people, this layout: the two ridges, the arroyo between them, the smelting works at the south end, the motel and church and cantina in the middle, the road running through like a suture. Note it. The geometry is the grammar. Every noun in this paragraph will eventually be a witness or a crime scene or both. The copper is already green. The dogs are already sleeping under trucks. The mural is already flaking, the pool is already drained, the birdcage is already whistling on the eastern ridge.

Marlo Voss has not yet arrived. The road from the north is empty and the motel's vacancy sign is on, twelve neon letters buzzing in the frequency of flies, and the town is doing what it does in the hours before things happen to it, which is the same thing it does in the hours after: it waits, and the copper oxidizes, and the slag heaps cool by fractions of degrees nobody measures, and the two families on their opposing ridgelines watch each other the way the two bell towers of San Rafael's watch each other — unevenly, perpetually, with an asymmetry that precludes peace but not, somehow, coexistence.

Not yet.

## II.

Three coffins. Pine, because the carpenter had no oak. The church of San Rafael is full in the way churches are full when attendance is mandatory and refusal is death — which is to say there are no empty seats and no one is praying.

The Ortega delegation occupies the left side of the nave. The Batista delegation occupies the right. Between them, in the center aisle, Father Lourdes stands with his hands on the middle coffin as though steadying a boat. His homily concerns the mercy of God, which he delivers in the flat tone of a man reading terms and conditions. He does not look at the congregation. He looks at a point above the congregation, where the southern wall meets the ceiling and a crack in the plaster has been growing for four years in the shape of the Río Grande.

Coffin one contains Renaldo Batista-Jerez, age thirty-four, the family's logistics coordinator, who managed the trucking routes between the smelting works and the distribution points to the north. He had a scar on his left forearm from a childhood accident involving a sheet-metal fence, and he was, by the testimony of several women and the private assessment of this narrator, not handsome but *structured* — a face of planes and angles that suggested someone had carved it in a hurry with good tools.

Coffin two contains Teodoro Ortega-Muñoz, age twenty-nine, a cousin on the payroll side, who handled the smelting works' books and knew which numbers were real and which were architecture. He had described Marlo Voss's eyes, fourteen days before the present moment, in the parking lot of the Álamo Motor Inn, while buttoning his shirt with the slow deliberateness of a man constructing an alibi against his own loneliness, as "like being forgiven for something." He was quoting, though he didn't know it, a Luis Miguel lyric from 1994. He was also, though he didn't know this either, six days from being dead.

Coffin three contains Graciana Perea, the motel owner, age forty-seven, who never slept with anyone relevant to this story and whose body is in a pine box because she answered her telephone at 2:14 a.m. on a Wednesday and the person on the other end of the line was not the person the caller expected.

Two of the three coffins held people who, as recently as eleven days ago, were sleeping with the American woman sitting in the seventh pew on neither the left side nor the right but exactly in the center, in the aisle seat, in a black cotton dress that she bought at a Soriana department store in Chihuahua City because she does not pack for funerals but has learned to shop for them locally. Marlo Voss is thirty-eight. Brown hair, features that suggest a midwestern plainness until they move and then suggest something else — a focused, almost mechanical attention that makes each person she looks at feel, briefly, curated. She is looking at the coffins. Her face is composed in an expression that this narrator would normally classify without hesitation: professional regret — the look of a surgeon who lost a patient to a complication she'd flagged in the pre-op notes. But the narrator notes, with uncharacteristic reservation, that Marlo's left hand is gripping the edge of the pew with a force that has turned her fingertips white, and that this detail does not taxonomize as neatly.

She is not looking at any of the three coffins.

She is looking at the space to the left of the first coffin, where a woman in a gray blouse is standing with her hands clasped and her mouth set and her eyes closed — not in prayer, but in the manner of a person who is holding a door shut from the inside.

But that is later. Or rather, that is now, and what comes next is before.

---

Seventeen days earlier — rewind the tape, let the blood pull back through the pine boards, let the carpenter un-plane the wood, let Father Lourdes swallow his homily in reverse — Marlo Voss is sitting in a rented Nissan Sentra in the parking lot of the Álamo Motor Inn, applying lipstick in the rearview mirror. The lipstick is a muted rose, not red. Red is a promise, muted rose is a possibility, and Marlo deals in possibilities the way a futures trader deals in corn — not the thing, but the implication of the thing, the derivatives. She presses her lips together. Checks the result. Adjusts. She is performing this ritual with the specificity of a woman who knows that the difference between a mouth that says *I am available* and a mouth that says *I might be available* is approximately two millimeters of color and forty thousand dollars in intelligence yield.

She gets out of the car. It is 6:47 p.m. and the light in Río Seco at 6:47 p.m. in October is the color of a healing bruise — the yellows and purples of a desert sunset filtered through a permanent haze of sulfur dioxide from the smelting works. Her boots crunch on the gravel. She walks past the drained swimming pool, past the ice machine that produces neither ice nor sound, past Room 6 (hers), toward the cantina at the end of the block where the socialist miners are slowly losing their mural and Renaldo Batista-Jerez, whose coffin will be first on the left, is already on his second Tecate and watching the door.

He will offer her a drink before she reaches the bar.

He will be dead in sixteen days, but his scar will pull when it rains for the next nine, and in that window — in that narrow, anatomically specific interval between an American woman asking about his scar and a pine box in a church with mismatched towers — an entire economy of desire and information will be transacted, and the narrator, who has already counted the receipts, invites you to watch.

## III.

Marlo's methodology has the precision of a liturgy and the adaptability of jazz. The narrator has observed its deployment in other towns, other countries, other beds — has watched her work a Sinaloa underboss in a Mazatlán hotel room and a logistics contractor in Bogotá and a perfectly pleasant mid-level Camorra accountant in a Neapolitan apartment with a view of Vesuvius and a persistent leak in the bathroom ceiling — and can report, with the authority of repetition, that the essential structure is always the same. She listens. Not passively — that would be an insult to the craft — but with a kind of *architectural* attention, as though she is mapping the load-bearing walls of a person's psyche and identifying which ones, if removed, would cause the most productive collapse.

With Renaldo Batista-Jerez, the logistics man, the method deployed over three encounters across five days:

Night one, *El Pilar.* She lets him buy the drink. She asks three questions about himself and volunteers exactly one fact about herself (a fiction: she works in mining consultancy; this is not entirely a lie, or rather it is a lie that contains a truth the way a geode contains a crystal, and the truth is that Marlo is always, always consulting the mine). She touches his scar — the one on his left forearm, the sheet-metal accident — and says, "Does it still pull when it rains?" He looks at her the way a man looks at a woman who has just noticed the thing about him that he privately considers the most interesting thing about him. He says yes. She says good night.

Night three, Room 6 of the Álamo Motor Inn. The intelligence yield of this encounter, extracted during the specific post-coital interval between 11:40 p.m. and 12:15 a.m. — the interval Marlo has identified, across numerous operations, as the window when a man's defenses are lowest and his desire to be *known* is highest — includes: the approximate shipping schedule for the smelting works' northbound outflow (Tuesdays and Fridays, alternating trucks, the Batista family's arrangement with a customs officer at the Ojinaga crossing), the name of a Batista cousin in El Paso who handles the American-side warehousing, and, most valuably, Renaldo's specific resentment toward his uncle Porfirio, who controls the family's finances and who, Renaldo believes, is skimming from the trucking revenues to fund a mistress in Parral. "He thinks we don't know," Renaldo says, and his voice carries a vibration the narrator recognizes, because the narrator has heard it in every story like this: the vibration of a man who has just told a woman something he should not have told anyone, and who is already converting the danger of the confession into proof of the confession's intimacy, which is to say proof that the woman deserved to hear it — that she, specifically, was the correct recipient.
