# The Houseboat

## I.

The lake was the color of a dirty nickel, and the houseboat sat on it at an angle that had nothing to do with the water.

Nola killed the engine of the truck at the top of the shell road and let it coast the last thirty yards to the bulkhead, the tires popping over oyster fragments, because that was how her grandmother had always arrived — quietly, as though the lake might startle. December 23rd. She was early by design. The rest of the Desormeaux family would not begin arriving for hours, and she needed the hours the way a diver needs the decompression stops on the way up: intervals of adjustment between the pressure of one depth and the pressure of another.

She stood on the bulkhead and looked at the vessel.

The houseboat listed four, maybe five degrees to starboard. It had listed for as long as she could remember, and for longer than that — in photographs from the 1970s the tilt was already visible, the waterline climbing the starboard hull like a tide that never receded. Now the waterline had risen another inch, maybe two. The portside freeboard showed a band of hull paint that had not been underwater last Christmas: a pale green strip, almost white, like the skin beneath a peeled bandage. The galley window on the near side was fogged from inside. Nola stared at this. No one had been aboard in weeks. The condensation was the houseboat respiring on its own, the bilge exhaling into the closed cabin, the vessel maintaining, in the absence of the family that owned it, the faintest simulation of habitation.

She crossed the gangway. The plank bowed under her weight, and the water beneath it was close enough to hear — a soft, black lip-smacking between the hull and the dock pilings, intimate and indifferent. The deck had a grit of mildew on it. She did not slip. Her feet knew.

Inside, the houseboat was twelve feet wide and forty-two feet long, and every one of those feet was at an angle. The floorboards ran fore to aft and had warped over decades into a topography she could have navigated blind. She removed her shoes. The wood was cold and faintly damp. She moved through the main cabin, past the table that was bolted down because it had to be, past the row of hooks where the life jackets hung swollen and probably useless, past the head with its curtain on a wire — the curtain a faded cotton print of magnolias, chosen by someone who wanted this place to feel like a house and not a hull.

She stopped in the galley.

The space between the stove and the beginning of the bow taper was exactly five feet. She knew this because her grandmother had told her so, had said it in the particular Desormeaux way that substituted fact for feeling: *Five feet from burner to bow, baby, just enough room for a slow one.* Nola had been six. The stove had been warm then — her grandmother kept it lit through December gatherings, less for heat than for the orange light it threw across the low ceiling — and her grandmother had taken her hands and they had danced in the five-foot space with the floor slanting beneath them, the boat groaning faintly at the shift in weight, and the dance had been a negotiation with the tilt, every step a micro-correction, and her grandmother had not corrected the tilt but *used* it, leaning into the starboard slant on the turn and then pushing gently uphill on the return, and the music had been — there had been no music. Her grandmother had hummed. A note, not a tune. A frequency.

Nola stood in the five feet of space. She did not dance. The stove was cold, and she put her hand flat against the iron surface of it, and the cold entered her palm like information.

Above her, a beam had started to separate from the cabin roof. She could see a seam of gray sky through the gap — a hairline of outside, barely wider than a fingernail. The wood around the separation was dark with moisture, and the darkness had the quality not of something recently wet but of something that had been wet for so long it had forgotten what dry meant. She looked at the seam of sky. The boat groaned on its mooring lines: a low, intestinal sound, the sound of something digesting its own structure.

She stood there for a long time. The fog on the galley window thickened, thinned, thickened again in cycles that seemed almost respiratory. After a while she sat on the slanted floor with her back against the stove and her knees drawn up and watched the light move through the cabin as the sun, hidden behind the overcast, tracked across the southern sky and threw shifting densities of gray across the warped floorboards.

She was waiting. Not for the family. For the willingness to be here when they arrived.

## II.

They came like weather: a slow front of vehicles and voices and carried things that rolled across the shell road and broke over the bulkhead and flooded down the dock.

Uncle Luce arrived first, by pirogue, from the east, sliding across the lake surface in a silence that ended the moment he tied off and hauled his cooler up the dock with a grunt that Nola heard from inside the cabin. The grunt was performative. Uncle Luce was seventy-one and could still carry an outboard motor over his head; the cooler was not heavy. But he wanted to arrive with effort, with visible strain, because effort was his dialect of affection — every physical exertion in the family's presence was a demonstration that he was still willing to labor for them. He boarded without pausing. His weight hit the deck and the houseboat adjusted, the starboard list deepening by a degree, and Uncle Luce adjusted his stance to match — left foot slightly uphill, right foot bracing — without appearing to notice he had done it. The adjustment was in his body the way the Cajun nasal was in his speech: acquired in childhood, invisible to the carrier, legible to anyone watching.

Nola watched.

The trucks began arriving. Cousin Renard and his wife, whose name Nola had known for eleven years and had to re-derive every time from context. Three of Aunt Patrice's four children, the fourth being in Beaumont for reasons that were gestured at — a hand waved eastward, a grimace — but never, even in a story with dialogue, would have been specified. Aunt Patrice herself, in the passenger seat of a sedan driven by a man Nola did not recognize, a man who carried two folding chairs to the gangway's edge and then retreated to the car and sat in it, engine running, as though Aunt Patrice had a meter going. The big cousins: Thierry and his wife and their twin boys, who hit the deck at a run and did not understand the tilt and fell immediately, almost in unison, toward the starboard rail, and were caught by Uncle Luce, who had been standing near the rail for exactly this reason, because the twins fell every year and every year Luce caught them and every year the catching was treated as spontaneous, as lucky, and not as the rehearsed and necessary rescue it was.

Within twenty minutes the houseboat held nineteen people, and the waterline had risen so that the starboard deck scuppers were awash. No one looked at the scuppers. Cousin Renard strung a line of Christmas lights from the cabin roof to the stern cleat. Half the bulbs were dead and the surviving half emitted a light that was less festive than forensic — a pallid, irregular blinking that illuminated faces from below, turning everyone into their own ghosts. Renard plugged in the lights and turned to the gathered family and spread his hands as though presenting a accomplishment. Several relatives nodded. One of Thierry's twins applauded.

Aunt Patrice set out food. The table was bolted to the deck at a compound angle — the boat's list plus the table's own warp — and the dishes began their annual slow migration toward the starboard edge. Patrice set down a bowl of potato salad and it traveled two inches toward the rail before she stopped it with a folded paper napkin wedged beneath the base, a gesture she performed without looking at it, the way a pianist reaches for a chord in the dark. Behind her, Renard's wife was cutting a king cake on a surface that tilted the crumbs into her lap. Next to her, someone had placed a bottle of Tabasco on a saucer to keep it from rolling. The saucer itself was propped on a wedge of cardboard. The cardboard was damp and beginning to sag. The Tabasco was migrating.

Nola observed all of this from the galley doorway.

She watched each relative arrive and, in the moment before boarding, perform a small private reckoning with the gangway — a pause, a breath, a micro-hesitation that was not reluctance but its cousin: the gathering of will. She watched Thierry's wife stand on the dock for a full four seconds, looking at the listing vessel and the blinking lights and the family already aboard, and in those four seconds the woman's face passed through something that was not quite dread and not quite grief but occupied the same neighborhood — a flicker of *this again, this still, this* — and then the face reassembled into warmth and the woman boarded with a smile that was, in its way, magnificent.

Because the performance *was* magnificent. Nola could see this even as she cataloged its mechanics. The commitment was total. Once aboard, every relative submitted to the conditions — the tilt, the damp, the lights that buzzed and died and buzzed again — with a cheerfulness that had the density of a professional discipline. Nobody braced themselves visibly. Nobody looked at the waterline. When the boat groaned, someone laughed, and the laugh was picked up and replicated around the deck like a protocol, a shared script that translated structural failure into character, into charm. *Listen to her complain. She's saying hello. She missed us.*

Nola knew what the groaning meant. She had looked up the sounds a hull makes when its structural members are fatigued, when the keel has warped, when the caulking between planks has dried and cracked and the water that was always trying to get in has started to win. The groaning meant the boat was dying. It had been dying for longer than some of the people aboard it had been alive.

But the dying was the point, and this was what Nola could see and not yet understand. The effort required to compensate for the listing deck, to stabilize the migrating dishes, to laugh at the sounds of disintegration — this effort was the family's true gathering. It gave nineteen people who did not know how to be in a room together something to do with their bodies. It replaced the need for conversation with the need for physical management. It allowed every relative to demonstrate care — *watch your step, hold this, I've got the rail* — without ever having to name what the care was for. The boat was falling apart and so the family had a project, and the project was the boat, and the boat was the reason they came, and the coming was the tradition, and the tradition could not be questioned because it was a tradition, and around and around the logic went, a closed loop, a circuit of obligation and love and habit that generated just enough warmth to keep everyone coming back and just enough dysfunction to keep anyone from asking whether the warmth was real.

Nola watched her mother arrive.

She arrived last, alone, on foot from a car parked at the top of the shell road. She was a tall woman who had been taller, or who had been a woman who stood at her full height, and the difference between the two was visible in her shoulders, which were braced high and tight as though she were perpetually walking into a cold wind. She carried nothing — no dish, no cooler, no gift. She walked the dock with her arms at her sides and her eyes on the houseboat, and her expression was the one Nola had studied for thirty-two years without ever decoding it: a face that seemed to be solving a problem it had no intention of sharing.

She boarded. She walked to the corner of the stern deck where a bench had been built into the rail, and she sat down, and she folded her hands in her lap, and she looked at the lake. Relatives moved around her. Someone placed a plate of food near her. She did not eat it.

Nola watched her mother's hands. They were still in her lap, but the thumbs were moving — pressing against each other, circling, pressing again — in a rhythm that was either self-soothing or self-punishing and that Nola had first noticed at age five and had never seen stop.

The gathering continued. The boat held its angle. The lights blinked their arrhythmic semaphore across the water.

## III.

The family slept aboard, as they always did on the night of the 23rd — the tradition was to wake together on the 24th and have coffee on the deck and watch the lake, and the waking-together required the sleeping-aboard, and the sleeping-aboard required an act of faith in the vessel that was, in the dark, with nineteen bodies shifting in bunks and on floor pallets and the water audible beneath the hull, indistinguishable from an act of denial.

Nola did not sleep.

At some hour past midnight she moved through the cabin, stepping over and between sleeping relatives with a precision born of years of navigating this exact obstacle course — the placement of the bunks, the predictable overspill of limbs, the location of the creaking floorboards that would wake Aunt Patrice, who slept like a military sensor. She made it to the bow. She was looking for the caulking gun she had seen Uncle Luce store somewhere forward last year, a practical errand she had been thinking about since she noticed the seam in the cabin roof, and the errand was real, was genuinely practical, but it was also a permission slip to be alone in the part of the houseboat that no one else occupied at night — the forward storage area, where the hull narrowed to its point and the headroom dropped to four feet and the accumulated objects of five decades of Desormeaux habitation were stacked in plastic bins and cardboard boxes that had long since molded into the shapes of their contents.

She lowered herself through the forward hatch. The crawlspace — it was too low to call it anything else — smelled of bilge and cypress and something older, something vegetal, as though the wood itself were slowly returning to the swamp it had been milled from. She crouched. The hull planking was close enough to touch on both sides, tapering toward the bow, and she could feel the water on the other side of the wood — not hear it, *feel* it: a faint, irregular pressure, a pulse, the lake leaning against the planking with the patience of something that intended to win.

The caulking gun was not where she expected it. She moved deeper into the crawlspace, her hands finding bins by touch: Christmas ornaments, their boxes soft with moisture. A tackle box. Stacked life preservers from an era when life preservers were made of cork and canvas. And then, against the hull itself, wedged into the angle where the keel met the ribs of the bow, a metal box.

It was not locked. It was an old marine first-aid kit, repurposed. Inside, arranged with a care that someone had taken years ago and humidity had slowly undone, were documents. She did not have a light. She took the box, moved backward through the crawlspace to the hatch, and sat beneath the single battery-powered emergency lamp clipped to the overhead frame.

The documents were a compressed geology of the family: insurance papers for the houseboat, for cars that no longer existed. A title transfer from her great-grandfather to her grandfather. A marriage certificate — the paper almost translucent with age — for grandparents whose faces she knew only from the photographs bolted to the cabin wall. A stack of birth certificates, each in a separate plastic sleeve, seven in total. The births of a generation: her mother, her aunts, her uncle.

And hers.

She pulled it from its sleeve. The paper was heavier than the others, newer by decades. State of Louisiana. Parish of Orleans. Her name, her date, the unremarkable data of her appearance in the world. She read it as she had read the other documents — as inventory, as record — until she turned it over.
