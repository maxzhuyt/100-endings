# The Gutter Unit

## A Chronicle by Desiree Lam

---

## I.

I should tell you what I am before I tell you what they were, because the light that falls on a subject is only as honest as its source, and I have never been an honest source—not in the way that matters. I have been an accurate one. There is a difference the size of a life.

My name is Desiree Lam. For twenty-three years I served as an evidence custody clerk for the city's Ninth Precinct, which means I spent the droning bulk of my adulthood in a basement cage receiving, cataloguing, and preserving the artifacts of other people's catastrophes: shell casings in manila envelopes, blood-drunk clothing sealed in paper bags so the moisture wouldn't breed rot, cell phones whose cracked screens still held the last text a dead girl ever sent. I was the woman behind the chain-of-custody counter. I wore latex gloves and a lanyard. I initialed forms. I was, in the bureaucratic taxonomy of law enforcement, a civilian employee—unsworn, unarmed, ineligible for the fraternal shorthand that real officers extend to one another like a secret handshake performed with the eyes. I was adjacent. I was the margin next to the poem.

That metaphor is not accidental. In my other life—the life I conducted on lunch breaks, on the J train, on weekends when the loneliness of my studio apartment drove me to the public library's periodical room—I wrote sonnets. Still do. Petrarchan, mostly, because I have always preferred the architecture that turns on itself at the eighth line, the *volta* that pivots a poem from proposition to revelation the way a good detective pivots from evidence to accusation. I have been published in small quarterlies whose circulations could be measured in the low hundreds. I won an honorable mention from the Sewanee Review in 2011. No one in the Ninth Precinct ever read a word of it, with one exception, and that exception is the reason I am writing this now.

What I am writing is a history. Not the official kind—there will be no official kind, because nothing that happened was sanctioned, and the people who did it will never be decorated, and two of them can no longer speak for themselves. I am writing the history because I was there. I was always there. I was the clerk. The clerk sees everything because no one watches the clerk.

And what I saw—what I had the privilege and the affliction of seeing, over the decade that the Gutter Unit operated and the seven subsequent years during which it existed only in my memory and theirs—was a small constellation of people who were, without qualification or apology, the best I have ever known. I understand that "best" is a word that does no analytical work. I understand that a historian who begins by declaring her subjects heroic has foreclosed the very inquiry she claims to undertake. I understand this, and I do not care. Or rather: I care, and I am doing it anyway, because the people I loved most in this precinct were incapable of seeing themselves clearly—they were too busy surviving to sit still for a portrait—and if the portrait is to be painted at all, it must be painted by the one who watched. By the one who loved them. By me.

Call it bias. I will not argue. I have been building this monument since before I knew it needed building, since the days when they were just five officers working an underfunded narcotics detail in a precinct so neglected by the department brass that their operational nickname became a point of perverse pride. The Gutter Unit. As in: we work in the gutter. As in: this is where the city drains, and we are the ones who wade through it. As in: if you are looking for glory, you are looking at the wrong address.

Let me give you their names. I have carried these names so long they have worn grooves in me.

Sergeant Neva Christoff, who led them—a woman built like a question mark, all forward lean and angular intention, whose tactical instincts were so precise that men twice her size deferred to her in doorways not because policy demanded it but because something in the way she entered a room made you understand that she had already cleared it in her mind. Neva, who read García Lorca in the original and never told anyone except me. Neva, who could disarm a man with a chokehold or a sentence, depending on what the situation permitted.

Officer Marcus Cole, their designated marksman, whose hands were so steady that the range instructor once told me—I was logging seized firearms that day, and he came to the cage shaking his head like a man who'd witnessed a minor miracle—that Marcus could put five rounds through a playing card at fifty yards and afterward complain that the grouping was sloppy. Marcus was the unit's center of gravity: quiet in the way of genuinely dangerous men, gentle in the way of men who know exactly how much damage they could do and have decided, every single morning, not to do it.

Officer Eddo "Pops" Salazar, their breacher—a man whose body was a topographical map of the doors he'd put his shoulder through. Eddo had hands the size of dinner plates and a laugh that sounded like furniture being moved in the apartment above you. He called everyone *mijo*, even Neva, even suspects, even the precinct commander once during a disciplinary hearing, which did not help his case.

Officer (later Detective) Yolanda Soong, surveillance. Yolanda could sit in a parked car for eleven hours without moving, without complaint, without losing focus. She had the rarest gift in law enforcement: the ability to see the pattern in the ordinary. A man who ties his shoes before entering a bodega. A car that slows at an intersection but does not stop. The rhythm of a corner that has changed key. Yolanda heard frequencies others couldn't, which will become, in the course of this narrative, an irony so pointed I can barely bring myself to set it down.

And Officer Gerald Koh, the wheelman, who drove the way some people pray—with his whole body, with absolute commitment, with a faith in the physics of momentum that bordered on the theological. Gerald's cruiser had more dents than a golf ball, each one representing a pursuit that ended in what he called a "negotiated stop" and what the motor pool called a requisition for body repair.

Five officers. One clerk. A precinct basement where the evidence cage shared a wall with the boiler room, so everything I catalogued vibrated faintly, as if the artifacts of violence were humming with residual life. These are the materials. This is the instrument—my voice, my syntax, my lover's eye. I will try to tell you what happened. I will fail at objectivity. I will not fail at the telling.

## II.

Seven years is long enough to learn to live without someone. It is not, I discovered, long enough to stop composing letters to them in your head.

Neva vanished on a Tuesday in October. The word "vanished" is doing more work than it should—there was process involved, federal marshals, a threat assessment thick as a phone book, a relocation protocol whose details I was not cleared to know and have never learned. What I knew was this: the Gutter Unit had made a case that reached too high, a distribution network whose capillaries led into the offices of two city councilmembers and one assistant district attorney, and the resulting grand jury testimony painted a target on Neva's back so large that even the department's institutional indifference couldn't ignore it. Witness Security. U.S. Marshals Service. A new name, a new city, a new life without us in it.

She did not say goodbye. I have written forty-seven sonnets about this fact. Forty-seven attempts to find the volta that turns *she is gone* into something I can survive. None of them worked. Poetry is not medicine. It is diagnosis.

The unit dissolved. Marcus transferred to a quieter command. Yolanda took a disability pension—the tinnitus, which none of us understood then, had already begun its occupation of her inner ear. Eddo retired with a back injury and opened a locksmith shop that specialized, with characteristic irony, in the same doors he'd spent his career destroying. Gerald drove for a car service, then a delivery company, then not at all; his knees went, one by one, replaced by titanium engineered to outlast the body it served. I stayed in the evidence cage. I stayed because leaving would have meant admitting that the thing I'd stayed for was over, and I have always been better at cataloguing endings than experiencing them.

Seven years of silence. Then a letter.

It was postmarked from a town in Idaho I will not name, and it arrived at a P.O. box I had rented for my literary submissions—a mailbox whose address appeared in print only in the contributors' notes of journals with circulations so small that finding it would require either an exhaustive search of the literary-magazine universe or an intimate knowledge of my habits. The envelope contained a single sheet of paper. On it, handwritten in an angular cursive I would have recognized in my sleep, was a Petrarchan sonnet.

I will not reproduce it here. Some things are mine.

But I will tell you what it did. The octave was—ostensibly—a meditation on distance, on the "cartography of severance" (her phrase; Neva's metaphors always leaned toward maps and coordinates, the spatial imagination of a woman who thought in floor plans). It was accomplished. It was not, if I am being honest, great poetry—Neva's line breaks had always been more enthusiastic than musical, and her rhymes tended toward the serviceable rather than the inevitable. But the volta. The turn at line nine.

The volta was a set of GPS coordinates disguised as a rhetorical figure—latitude and longitude embedded in what appeared to be a sequence of numbered reflections on exile. If you were a person who did not know Neva, who did not know the private cipher she and I had constructed over years of passing notes across the evidence counter—one of us on the sworn side, one on the civilian side, the counter between us always—you would read a poem. A decent poem about longing. You would miss the message entirely.

I did not miss it.

Three days later I stood in the parking lot of a diner off an interstate exit whose number I will also not share, and I watched a woman get out of a truck the color of dishwater, and the woman was Neva, and she was not the same.

She was thinner. This is the kind of observation that, in a lesser narrative, would serve as shorthand for suffering, but I mean it literally: Neva had lost mass. The architectural angularity I described earlier—the forward lean, the question-mark posture of a woman always advancing toward the next problem—had sharpened into something closer to erosion. She looked like a photograph of herself left in the sun. There was less of her. Not spiritually. Neva's spirit could have powered a precinct. But something behind her eyes had been extinguished, or banked so low it no longer cast visible light.

She ordered coffee. I ordered coffee. She placed both hands flat on the table between us—a gesture I'd seen her use a thousand times in interrogation rooms, a gesture that meant *I am about to tell you something, and I need you to hear all of it*—and she told me.

The fentanyl pipeline. She said those words the way a doctor says the name of the disease: clinical, precise, a terminology designed to hold the unspeakable at arm's length. It had metastasized—her word—through the neighborhoods south and west of the Ninth Precinct, the same blocks the Gutter Unit had once patrolled, the same corners where Yolanda had sat in unmarked cars reading the rhythm of the street. Sixty-seven overdose deaths in the last eighteen months. She knew because she'd been monitoring from a distance—monitoring in violation of every Marshals Service protocol, using tools and channels and favors she would not describe and I did not ask about, because I understood that Neva had spent seven years in Idaho or wherever it was, and she had spent every one of those years watching the neighborhood she'd sworn to protect collapse into a sinkhole of synthetic opioid, and the watching had burned out something essential in her and replaced it with this: a plan.

Not a bust. She was emphatic about this. These were not arrest warrants she was chasing; she lacked the standing and the credibility and, frankly, the evidence. What she had was a map—a map of supply points, distribution nodes, the physical addresses where the pipeline surfaced into the community—and what she wanted was extraction. A community operation. Identify the families most exposed, the kids most at risk, the houses that were closest to the pipeline's heat, and *move them.* Get them out. Provide the resources, the transportation, the temporary safe harbor that official channels had failed to provide because official channels were understaffed, underfunded, or—she paused here, and her jaw did something I will describe later—compromised.

She needed the Gutter Unit.

She needed *me.*

I want to describe what I felt in that moment with the precision it deserves, because it is the emotional foundation on which everything that follows was built, and if I get it wrong, the entire structure collapses. What I felt was not surprise. It was not even joy, though joy was in it somewhere, a bright vein running through the darker ore. What I felt was the sensation of a lock turning in a door I had convinced myself was a wall. Seven years of composing letters I could not send, and here was Neva, sitting across from me in a diner that smelled like burnt butter, asking me to be the operation's spine. Logistics. Documentation. The one who holds the pieces together. The clerk.

I said yes. I said yes before she finished asking. I said yes so fast that Neva looked at me with an expression I had catalogued many times—her face when a plan came together faster than she expected—and then her expression changed into something I had catalogued only once before, the night before she disappeared, when she stood at the evidence counter and said nothing and I said nothing and the boiler room hummed through the wall and we understood that we were out of time for all the things we'd never said.

She reached across the table and took my hand.

I am aware that a historian should not record the moments that ruined her. I am recording it anyway. This is not reportage. It was never reportage. It is devotion shaped into prose, and I will not apologize for the shape.

## III.

Reassembling them was like restoring a fresco from its fragments: you could see where the masterpiece had been, and you could see what weather had done to it, and the distance between those two things was the whole story.

Eddo was first because Eddo was easy—easy to find (his locksmith shop was four blocks from the old precinct, its awning bearing the name SALAZAR & SONS, though there were no sons, only Eddo and a cat named Deadbolt), and easy to convince, because Eddo had never required convincing. Not once, in all the years I knew him, did Eddo ask *why.* He only asked *when* and *where* and, on one memorable occasion involving a battering ram and a reinforced steel door, *how many times.* He came to the door of his shop when I knocked, and he looked at me, and he squinted—a squint I noticed but did not yet understand—and he said, "Desi. You look good, *mija.* Tell me what we're doing."

I told him.

"Okay," he said. "Let me close up."

He closed up. He also locked the door with three separate mechanisms, and I watched his hands as he did it, and his hands were the same—dinner plates, dexterous, undiminished. But the squint. He had squinted when he opened the door, and he squinted now as he pocketed his keys, and I filed this the way I filed everything: in the mental evidence log I'd been maintaining since 1998, the running inventory of details that might or might not matter, the clerk's compulsion to tag and bag every observable fact against the possibility that one day someone will need to retrieve it.
