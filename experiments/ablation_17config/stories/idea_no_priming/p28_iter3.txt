# The Same Hammer

---

I have sat in better courtrooms. The Prettyman building pretends to gravitas — the mahogany, the seal, the ceiling that suggests without delivering cathedral height — but I have been in the well of the Supreme Court when it was not yet fashionable to call what I do a legacy, and I can tell you that this room, this particular room with its government-issue fluorescence buzzing at a frequency only women past sixty seem to hear, does not impress me. The gallery bench is built for contrition, not comfort. I am not here for comfort. I'm here because Angela Liu is delivering her first federal closing this afternoon, and I have not missed a first federal closing in thirty-one years. Before Angela there was Vasquez, before Vasquez there was Kessler, before Kessler there was Okafor-Briggs, who is now deputy solicitor general and who still calls me on the anniversary of that first conviction to say — her word, not mine — *thank you*. Thirty-one years. Forty-three prosecutors. A record of federal convictions that even my detractors at Georgetown stopped trying to contextualize because the context was simply this: they won. My students won and kept winning, in this circuit and others, in narcotics and in fraud and in the particular species of public corruption that requires a mind capable of holding seventeen threads simultaneously and pulling the right one at the right time, and I am the person who taught them which thread and when to pull. I taught them the architecture of a jury's belief. I am not boasting. I am providing a frame of reference so you understand the precision of my observation, because I am, above all else, an observer. I did not come to this courtroom to feel anything. I came to watch.

And Angela is — I'll say it plainly — performing. She has arranged her exhibits with the lateral fanning technique I introduced in the Trial Practice seminar in 2014, the one Hastings attempted to publish a rebuttal to before realizing I was right. Her posture at counsel table is exactly what I prescribe: shoulders open, hands visible, legal pad angled so the jury sees preparation but not dependency. She rose for her opening this morning and I heard my cadence in her third sentence — the controlled deceleration before the operative fact, the half-beat of silence that lets the accusation land before the evidence buries it. She did the thing I taught her to do, which is to make the jury feel that the story has already ended, that the verdict is simply a matter of their courage in acknowledging what everyone in the room already knows. And she did it well. I watched her handle the detective on direct — building the chronology in modular blocks the way I built it in *United States v. Almeida*, the technique I developed because juries do not think in timelines, they think in episodes, and the prosecution that respects episodic cognition wins — and I thought: *yes*. That is the work. That is what twenty-seven months of my attention produces. Angela Liu is the instrument; the music is mine.

I do not say that with arrogance. I say it with the specificity of a craftsperson who knows where the joints are.

She has not been perfect. Her redirect on the forensic accountant was loose — she let the witness wander into methodology when the jury needed only the conclusion, a mistake I corrected in our third preparation session and which she evidently forgot under the pressure of the moment. And she paused too long before her objection on the 404(b) issue, which gave the court a window to let it in, though the judge would have let it in regardless because Judge Llewellyn has never met a piece of character evidence she didn't want to cuddle. Small things. Recoverable. The architecture holds.

I haven't mentioned the case itself because the case is, in a meaningful sense, beside the point. The defendant is accused of something; the government believes it can prove the accusation; a jury will decide. The machinery is what concerns me. Whether the machine runs cleanly. Whether the right hands are on the right levers. That has always been my concern. I have never been interested in the particular contents of a case the way lesser academics fetishize facts, the way clinical professors prostrate themselves before narrative as though *story* were a substitute for *structure*. Structure is what wins. I built the structure. I am watching it perform.

Which is why what happens next is — I want to say *interesting*. Let me say interesting.

---

Deshawn stands.

Deshawn Watts stands from the defense table, and the room reorganizes itself around him the way rooms always did, even when he was twenty-four and sitting in the third row of my seminar pretending he hadn't already read ahead. He was the best student I ever taught. I don't say that the way professors usually say it, with the soft nostalgic blur that lets them claim intimacy with someone else's brilliance. I say it the way an engineer says a bridge is the finest she ever designed: structurally. Analytically. Deshawn had the kind of mind that did not merely absorb legal reasoning but *metabolized* it — broke it into its components and rebuilt it in configurations I hadn't anticipated, configurations that were, I admit, sometimes more elegant than the originals. He could see the skeleton inside an argument the way certain chess players see the board six moves deep without effort, and I spent three years — three years of independent studies, of directed readings, of hours in my office that I did not give to anyone else — teaching him to use that vision the way it was meant to be used. I built him for prosecution. I built him for the Eastern District, for Main Justice, for the kind of career that would have made my own legacy not merely impressive but *permanent* — a lineage, Marianne Cloister to Deshawn Watts, teacher to epoch-defining prosecutor, the way Wechsler begat a generation and Freund begat another. I do not think it is grandiose to say I saw in him the vindication of everything I had spent my professional life constructing. He had the rigor. He had the instinct. He had, and I must be honest about this even now, the moral architecture.

And then he went to the other side of the room.

Not the appellate side, where at least the work is structural, where at least one can argue that the project is about constitutional principle and not about the simple, squalid business of putting guilty people back on the street. No. He went to trial defense. Federal trial defense. He takes drug cases, fraud cases. He took a public corruption case in the Fourth Circuit last year that I declined to read about but heard about nonetheless because my colleagues, who know my relationship to him, enjoy a particular kind of cruelty they dress up as intellectual curiosity. *Did you see Watts got an acquittal in Richmond? Isn't he one of yours?* He is not one of mine. He was. He chose to leave.

I don't use the word *betrayal* because it is imprecise and sentimental and I have never been either of those things. What I will say is that Deshawn Watts took every tool I gave him — the episodic construction, the modular cross-examination framework, the interpretive methodology I spent a decade refining — and turned those tools against the house they were built to fortify. He uses my framework to *dismantle* prosecutions. He takes the structural vision I cultivated in him and deploys it in the service of — what? Of reasonable doubt. Of the elegant lie that the absence of certainty is the presence of innocence. He has made himself into a weapon, and the weapon is pointed in the wrong direction, and what makes it unbearable is that the weapon is *mine*. I forged it. I sharpened it. I put it in his hands and told him where to strike. And he strikes, now, at my students.

He is striking at Angela.

---

He begins with the forensic accountant. Of course he does. He identified the same looseness in Angela's redirect that I identified — because he and I see the same structures, because I am the reason he sees them — and he drives into it with the precision of a man who knows exactly where the load-bearing wall is because he learned to build the house from the same blueprints. He asks three questions. Three. They are not clever questions. I want to be clear about that. They are not the kind of inspired, intuitive questions that reveal native genius. They are *mechanical* questions, questions that follow a decision tree I recognize because I designed the decision tree. He isolates the forensic accountant's methodology from its conclusion, forces the witness to explain the margin of error in language the jury can hold, and then — this is the technique, this is my technique, this is the third module of the cross-examination seminar and he is executing it like a parlor trick — he sits down. He doesn't press. He doesn't overreach. He lets the doubt sit in the room like humidity.

No. That's not right. He doesn't let the doubt *sit*. He *installs* it. The way I taught him to install a theme in the jury's mind during the first thirty seconds of cross — not by stating it, never by stating it, but by creating the negative space in which the only conclusion the jury can reach is the one you've already built for them. Deshawn is building right now. He is building with my materials.

And the jury is watching him with the particular quality of attention that I have spent my career teaching prosecutors to command and that Deshawn is commanding now, easily, almost lazily, with a physical stillness at the lectern that I recognize as the control technique from the Advanced Advocacy module, the one I tell my students will make a jury trust you more than movement ever could because *movement is anxiety and stillness is authority* — my words, my exact words, from a lecture I have given perhaps two hundred times, and he is using them against Angela, who learned the same lesson and is sitting at counsel table watching a mirror version of herself take apart her case with the same tools from the same toolbox built by the same hands.

He turns to the detective. What he does to the detective is — I'll say it — theatrical. He is performing. He takes the episodic chronology Angela built on direct and he *reverses* it, walks the detective backward through the timeline until the detective is no longer telling a story but reciting a sequence, and the difference — the devastating difference — is that a story has momentum and a sequence has gaps, and the gaps are what Deshawn wants the jury to see. He does this with seven questions. He has prepared — he has clearly prepared, the way I require, with the obsessive granularity I demand — and each question is a scalpel cut along a joint that Angela's construction left exposed. He is not finding weaknesses she missed; he is *exploiting structures she built*. The same architecture that organized the prosecution's case is now organizing its destruction, because the architecture was never prosecutorial or defensive. It was *analytical*. It was mine. And mine is a tool. A hammer. And a hammer does not care what it strikes.

No. I reject that. A hammer cares in the sense that its wielder directs it, and direction is a moral choice, and the direction Deshawn has chosen — but I'll come back to this.

He's addressing the jury now. Not formally — this is within a cross-examination, and he is still technically asking questions — but every question is a door he opens so the jury can see the room behind it. He is not arguing; he is *narrating*. He is constructing reasonable doubt not as an absence but as a counter-story, an alternative architecture that is, I can see, precisely as sturdy as Angela's because it was built with the same engineering. The jury is with him. I can see it in — this is trivially legible if you know what to look for, and I have always known what to look for — I can see it in the orientation of their bodies, the micro-adjustments of posture that indicate narrative capture, and they are captured, they are inside his construction, and what I feel is not admiration because admiration would require me to endorse the use to which these skills are being put, and I do not endorse it. What I feel is recognition. Cold, technical recognition. I see every move. I named every move. I could diagram the examination on a whiteboard and label each transition with the module number and the year I introduced it and the case study I used to teach it, and the fact that I can do this — the fact that his excellence is so legible to me — is precisely what makes it so—

He's good. I'll say that. Since precision requires it: he is operating at a level that I have seen perhaps four or five times in three decades of watching courtrooms, a level that implies not merely competence but *fluency*, the kind of mastery that has metabolized technique into instinct, and if he were doing this at the prosecution's table I would be in my office tomorrow morning writing a letter to the Attorney General recommending his immediate appointment to whatever position would use him most visibly. But he is not at the prosecution's table. He is at the other table. And the same skills that would make him the finest prosecutor of his generation are, here, being used to — let me find the right word — to *corrode*. To dismantle the work of someone who is using those skills *properly*. He has taken the instrument and played it backward and produced a sound that is, I will admit, very nearly music, but it is music in the service of noise.

I'm aware of how that sounds. I am aware that a certain kind of person — the kind of person who writes law review articles about the romance of the defense bar — would hear what I've just said and call it bias. But bias implies a conclusion reached without evidence, and I have spent thirty-one years accumulating evidence that the adversarial system works best when the most rigorous minds are on the side of the state, because the state bears the burden and therefore requires the greater discipline, and to put that discipline in the hands of the defense is to create an asymmetry that the system was not designed to sustain. The system assumes that defense attorneys will be adequate, occasionally competent, rarely brilliant. It does not account for Deshawn. It does not account for what happens when someone with his training, my training, decides to use the full weight of that preparation to exploit every structural vulnerability in a case that would be — that *is* — sound.

The case is sound. Angela's case is sound. The preparation was thorough. I reviewed it myself.

---

Judge Llewellyn is the problem. I've said for years — I've said to anyone who would listen, and several people who would not — that Llewellyn is constitutionally incapable of exercising the kind of evidentiary discipline that a complex federal case requires. She let in the 404(b) material, yes, which initially helped the prosecution, but she also sustained four of Deshawn's objections during the detective's redirect, and each sustaining cut a thread from Angela's reconstruction. Llewellyn is too permissive. It's not a judicial philosophy; it's a temperament. She wants to see everything. She thinks transparency is the same as fairness, and it is not — fairness is *structure*, fairness is the rules applied *correctly*, and correct application sometimes means exclusion, sometimes means the jury doesn't get to see what the defense wants them to see, and Llewellyn—

And the jury. I want to be careful here, because I am not the kind of person who blames a jury for its composition — that's a failure of voir dire, and Angela's voir dire was competent, I made sure of it — but this particular panel has a foreman who leans forward every time Deshawn speaks and a retired schoolteacher in seat nine who has been nodding, *actually nodding*, during his cross-examinations, as though she is in a lecture hall and not a federal courtroom, and if that is not susceptibility to performance over substance then I have misread thirty-one years of jury behavior, which I have not.

And Angela — Angela was not aggressive enough on redirect. I told her. I told her in the preparation sessions that the redirect of the detective was the fulcrum of the entire case, and she needed to rebuild the chronology *forward* after Deshawn inevitably reversed it, because he would reverse it, because that is what I taught him to do, which means I knew it was coming, which means I *told her* it was coming, and she did not adequately prepare for it because she did not believe — she could not believe, having never seen him in person — that anyone could execute that technique at the speed and with the control that Deshawn—

That Deshawn—
