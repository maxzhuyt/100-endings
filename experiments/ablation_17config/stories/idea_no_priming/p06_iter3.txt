# The Flippers

## I.

If you have not heard of competitive pinball machine restoration, that is because you are a person with a normal life and functioning relationships, and this story is not about people like you.

The playfield of a 1977 Bally "Eight Ball" contains forty-seven individual components, not counting the wiring harness, which contains an additional hundred and twelve soldered connections, each one a small act of faith that copper and tin and rosin will continue to do what physics says they should. When the machine left the factory in Bensenville, Illinois, it retailed for $1,050. Its drop targets featured the likeness of a billiard ball. Its backglass — the upright illuminated panel that faces the player and gives the machine its face, its identity, its soul, if you are the kind of person who believes a machine can have a soul, and the men in this story are exactly that kind of person — depicted a mustachioed hustler leaning on a pool cue with the come-hither confidence of a Burt Reynolds who had been flattened into glass and pigment and backlit by two 47-watt bulbs.

There are, as of the most recent census anyone has bothered to conduct, eleven active competitive pinball restoration crews in the United States. They restore machines to original or better-than-original condition and enter them in juried competitions where other men — they are almost exclusively men, a fact the men themselves acknowledge with the uneasy jocularity of people who have noticed a pattern and would prefer not to examine it — evaluate the results according to criteria so specific and so passionately contested that the rulebook for the National Pinball Restoration Invitational runs to forty-one pages, nine of which are devoted solely to the question of acceptable playfield clear-coat thickness.

Three of these crews are relevant.

The Silicon Cranks, based in Portland, Oregon, are the youngest crew in competitive restoration. Their average age is forty-four. They have a website. They have an Instagram account with eleven thousand followers, which in the economy of competitive pinball restoration makes them approximately the Beatles. They restore machines in a converted loft space with exposed brick and supplemental lighting that suggests someone once watched a YouTube video about cinematography. They are good at what they do, and they know it, and they want you to know that they know it, and this need — to be seen being good — is the crack in their architecture, though they cannot see it, which is how cracks work.

The Tilt City Restorations, based in Memphis, Tennessee, have won the Invitational four of the last seven years. Their leader, a retired electrician named Walt Perkins, does not have an Instagram account. Walt Perkins does not have email. Walt Perkins communicates with the Invitational's organizing committee by telephone and, when the matter is sufficiently important, by letter, the physical kind, written on stationery from a Holiday Inn he stayed at in 1994 and apparently took in bulk. Tilt City does not document their process. They do not explain their methods. They arrive at competitions with machines that look as though they have traveled backward through time, and they accept their trophies with the grim satisfaction of men who have been proven right about something they should not have needed to prove.

And then there is the Rust Belt Replay Collective, based in Dayton, Ohio, which is the crew this story is about, though they do not yet know they are in a story, because nobody does, because that is the nature of stories: they happen to you before you understand their shape.

---

The Collective operates out of Hank Vicarro's basement, which Hank's wife, Denise, refers to as "the basement" in a tone that contains an entire unwritten novel about the negotiation of domestic space across a thirty-six-year marriage. The basement is eleven hundred square feet of poured concrete, drop ceiling, and fluorescent lighting that gives everyone's skin the bluish pallor of people who have been recently embalmed. Along the east wall, a pegboard holds tools organized with the sort of precision that, in another context, might be called devotion. Along the west wall, three pinball machines stand in various states of undress: cabinets open, playfields removed, wiring exposed like the nervous systems of creatures that have been carefully, lovingly vivisected.

Hank Vicarro is sixty-four, retired from the phone company, and has the particular bearing of a man who spent forty years solving problems no one thanked him for and has decided to spend his remaining years solving problems no one asked him to solve. He is the crew's electrician, its diagnostician, its most fluent speaker of voltage and amperage and the silent language of circuits. He arrives at the workshop at 8:15 every morning. He tests his multimeter's batteries before each session, even though the batteries are fine, because Hank Vicarro is a man who trusts the world only after he has verified it, which is another way of saying he does not trust the world.

Larry Koenig is sixty-two. He owns four hundred dollars' worth of reproduction mylar decals — the thin adhesive graphics that adorn a machine's cabinet sides — organized in plastic sleeves in a three-ring binder he keeps in his truck. He will never use most of them. They are for machines he does not own and will not likely acquire, but he buys them anyway, from a specialty supplier in Michigan who is one of three people on Earth who still prints them, because the act of purchasing and cataloging the decals satisfies something in Larry that the decals themselves do not need to be applied to satisfy. Larry arrives at the workshop before anyone else, most days by 7:30, and sets out the coffee — a drip machine he brought from home after Hank's Mr. Coffee finally died, an event Hank described as "a component failure" rather than as the death of an appliance, which tells you everything about Hank. Larry sets out five mugs. He does not discuss why he arrives early. He does not discuss much at all, in the manner of a man who has learned that speech is primarily a mechanism for revealing things about yourself that can later be used as evidence. He has not spoken to his adult son, Kevin, in three years. The reasons are specific and involve a Thanksgiving, a political opinion, and a door that was closed at a volume that rearranged the architecture of a family. Larry does not talk about this, and no one asks, because the workshop is a place where no one asks, which is both its great virtue and its quiet catastrophe.

Marcus Reed is sixty-one, retired schoolteacher, the Collective's self-appointed historian and its most vocal advocate for period correctness. Marcus can identify the production run of a Bally playfield by the particular shade of green in the silk-screened graphics — the 1977 runs used a slightly warmer green than the 1978 re-issues, a distinction approximately no one on Earth cares about except Marcus and perhaps God, if God has a position on silk-screen pigmentation, which seems unlikely but cannot be ruled out. Marcus touches machines the way other men might touch something they love. His left hand rests on a cabinet rail while he talks, not gripping, just resting, the way you might rest a hand on a child's shoulder while you speak to another adult about something else. He does not notice he does this. Everyone else has noticed.

Carl Jessup is sixty-three and the crew's artist — the man responsible for backglass restoration, which is the most specialized and least forgiving discipline in competitive pinball. A backglass is a sheet of tempered glass, reverse-painted in layers: the final image is built backward, highlights first, shadows last, so that when viewed from the front through the glass, the colors have a luminosity that no canvas painting can replicate. Restoring a damaged backglass means matching paints that were mixed forty years ago by men who did not record their formulas, working with brushes sized for model trains, and accepting that a single misplaced stroke cannot be corrected because the paint is on the wrong side of the glass and there is no erasing what has already been laid down. There is a metaphor here. Carl would not appreciate your noticing it. Carl is a retired draftsman with two ex-wives and a cat named Chairman Meow, which is the only joke Carl has ever made that anyone in the Collective has found funny, and which Carl himself has never seemed to understand is a joke, which is why it's funny.

Doug Yates is the youngest at fifty-eight, a retired plumber who joined the Collective two and a half years ago after his wife, Marie, died of pancreatic cancer and his days acquired a particular emptiness that he does not describe and that no one in the Collective has ever asked him to describe. Doug is not a specialist. He is not an electrician or an artist or a historian. He is a man who holds things when asked to hold them. He passes tools. He makes bad jokes — puns, mostly, the kind that produce not laughter but a specific silence that functions, in the emotional economy of the workshop, as a form of tolerant acknowledgment, which Doug appears to accept as sufficient. He fetches lunch on days when someone remembers to eat. He swept the workshop floor for three straight weeks when he first joined, until Hank told him the floor didn't need sweeping, a statement Doug absorbed without expression before finding something else unasked-for to do. Doug is the member of the Collective that the other four men would, if pressed, have the most difficulty describing with any precision, not because Doug is absent but because Doug's presence is the kind that fills a room's gaps rather than occupying its center, and people generally do not notice gaps until they appear.

These are the five men. They have organized their self-respect around the ability to restore broken things to working order — to take something that the world has used up and discarded and make it function again, make it beautiful again, make it *worth something* again — and if this sounds like it might be about more than pinball machines, please be assured that the men themselves would reject that interpretation firmly and with slight embarrassment, because the first rule of channeling your emotional life into inanimate objects is that you must never acknowledge that you are channeling your emotional life into inanimate objects.

Their entry for this year's National Pinball Restoration Invitational is the 1977 Bally "Eight Ball." They have been working on it for fourteen months. It is, by the Collective's standards, the best thing they have ever done.

But that is getting ahead of things.

## II.

The restoration of a pinball machine is, in its gross outlines, a simple process: you disassemble it, you clean or replace everything that is broken, you reassemble it, and you hope. The details, however, are where men build their churches.

On a Tuesday in September, the following things happen in Hank Vicarro's basement, and they are recorded here not because they advance a plot but because they are what actually occurs when five men spend a morning in a room together, and the distance between what actually occurs and what narratives typically bother to include is the distance between a life and a story about a life.

7:34 a.m.: Larry arrives. He unlocks the basement with his own key — when Hank gave this to him is disputed; Larry says three years ago, Hank says eighteen months, and the truth is neither man remembers precisely because the key was given without ceremony and received without comment, which is how significant things happen between men who have not learned any other way. Larry starts the coffee. He sets out five mugs, even though Doug sometimes doesn't arrive until after ten and Carl once didn't show up on a Tuesday at all. Five mugs, every day. The mug he sets at the end of the bench, the one with the faded Cincinnati Reds logo, is Doug's mug, though Doug did not choose it and no one assigned it to him. It became his mug through the process by which all territorial claims in the workshop are established: repetition, silence, and the passage of enough time that the arrangement acquires the force of natural law.

8:17 a.m.: Hank arrives, two minutes later than usual because of construction on North Dixie Drive, a fact he reports to Larry with the solemnity of a man filing an incident report. He tests his multimeter batteries. The batteries are fine. They have been fine every morning for seven months, since he last replaced them. He opens the "Eight Ball" cabinet, which is canted on its back on two sawhorses like a patient on an operating table, and begins tracing the wiring harness from the transformer to the solenoid board, checking each connection against a hand-drawn schematic he made himself because the original Bally documentation, he has explained more than once, "was written by people who apparently hated anyone who would ever need to read it."

9:05 a.m.: Marcus arrives with a small paper bag containing a rubber bumper cap he has sourced from a collector in Terre Haute. He places it on the bench next to the playfield and does not speak about it immediately, because Marcus understands that a part, newly arrived, requires a period of communal contemplation before it can be discussed. The bumper cap is white with a red ring. It is the size of a thimble. It costs eleven dollars, which in the economy of obsolete pinball components is neither cheap nor expensive but occupies the middle ground of quietly unreasonable that defines the entire hobby. After four minutes of silence, during which Larry refills his coffee and Hank does not look up from a solder joint, Marcus says, "Terre Haute came through." Larry nods. Hank says, "Let me see it when you're ready." This exchange contains no useful information. Its purpose is purely ritualistic, a call and response confirming that the congregation is present and the service may continue.

9:22 a.m.: Carl arrives carrying a tackle box that contains his brushes and paints. He opens it on the folding table he has claimed as his workspace — a card table with a lamp clamped to its edge, positioned near the basement's one window for natural light that is, in October in Dayton, approximately equivalent to the natural light inside a moderately well-lit coffin. Carl is working on the backglass. The "Eight Ball" unit the Collective acquired had a backglass with three areas of paint loss — flaking, likely caused by moisture exposure during the years the machine spent in someone's unheated garage in Wilmington, Ohio — and Carl has spent the last four months color-matching and repainting these sections, which together comprise an area roughly the size of a playing card. Four months. For a space smaller than a man's palm. When the work is finished — *if* the work is finished to Carl's satisfaction, which is not guaranteed, because Carl operates on the principle that anything worth doing is worth agonizing over until the agony becomes indistinguishable from the doing — the repair will be invisible. That is the point. The highest achievement in backglass restoration is not beauty but disappearance. The best work you are ever capable of is work no one can see. There is a metaphor here too, and Carl would appreciate your noticing this one even less.

9:38 a.m.: Doug arrives. He says, "Morning, fellas." No one responds, not out of cruelty but because the greeting has been repeated so many times that it has been absorbed into the ambient sound of the workshop, like the hum of the fluorescents or the occasional clank of the furnace. Doug stands for a moment, locates his mug — the Reds mug, at the end of the bench, where it always is — pours coffee, and positions himself near the "Eight Ball" cabinet, slightly behind Hank's sightline, available but not obtrusive, present but not quite there, the way a moon is present to a planet: exerting a pull no one measures but everyone, on some level, relies upon without knowing it.
