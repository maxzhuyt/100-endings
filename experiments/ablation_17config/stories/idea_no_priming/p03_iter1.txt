# The Pool House

## I.

Three of the bulbs on the left strand had gone to milky nothing, and one more flickered with the particular stubbornness of a thing deciding whether to die. Della unscrewed them one at a time, dropping each dead bulb into the pocket of her canvas jacket where they clicked together like glass teeth. She'd bought replacements at the hardware store on Grover — not the same brand, the old ones were discontinued, but close enough in color temperature that Cass probably wouldn't notice. Cass noticed color the way most people noticed sound, peripherally and absolutely, and a wrong white would sit in the air between them like an offkey note neither mentioned.

The pool house was thirty-two feet long and fourteen feet wide, poured concrete walls with a transom window at the east end that had lost its glass sometime during the second Bush administration. Someone, decades before Della was born, had tiled the floor in a pattern of cerulean and bone that was meant to echo the pool it had served, and though a third of the tiles were cracked or missing — dark squares of underlayment showing through like gaps in a mouth — the ones that remained still held their color with an almost geological stubbornness. When the Christmas lights were on, and they were always on, the intact tiles caught the warm light and gave it back blue, so the room seemed to exist at the bottom of something.

She swept the leaves first. They came in through the transom and collected in the northeast corner the way they'd collected every week for ten years, and she swept them into the same dustpan and carried them out the door and shook them into the same stand of hackberry that had probably produced them. Then she came back and checked the books.

There were forty-one. She knew this without counting because she'd catalogued them in a pocket notebook she kept in the ammunition box under the sink — the sink that no longer had plumbing but which held a jar of wildflowers Della replaced on Wednesdays. The books were arranged on cinderblock-and-plank shelves along the west wall, spines out, organized by a system she and Cass had invented at eleven: not alphabetical, not by genre, but by what they called *weight*, which meant the density of feeling the book produced divided by the number of pages it took to produce it. Flannery O'Connor's collected stories occupied the top shelf, leftmost position. *Housekeeping* by Marilynne Robinson sat beside it. Further down, in the middle ranges: *Jaws* by Peter Benchley (Cass's contribution, defended ferociously), a field guide to North Atlantic marine invertebrates, a water-stained copy of *The Collected Poems of Frank O'Hara* that Della had taken from the Severna Park branch library four years ago by sliding it under her shirt and walking out with her arms crossed over her chest like a girl cold in summer.

She straightened the spines now, pushing each book flush with the cinder block edge. *Beloved* had been leaning into *The Member of the Wedding* again. She corrected it. She wiped the plank surface with a rag she kept folded in the ammunition box. She checked the reading lamp — a clamshell-shaped thing from Goodwill, $3, with a sixty-watt bulb that turned the pages of whatever you were reading the color of weak tea — and turned it on, then off, then on again.

The new bulbs were close enough. She screwed them in and stepped back. The left strand blazed in an unbroken line now, and its reflection ran across the tile floor like a contrail, and the room was what it always had been: the most beautiful place she knew, which was also, technically, a condemned building on city property behind a public pool that had been filled with gravel in 2014. The demolition notice had been posted on the door five times. The first was when they were twelve. Della had peeled it off and hidden it under a tile, as if the building's survival depended on the notice not being seen, which in a way it did: the city forgot what the city did not look at, and no one looked at this place except them.

She sat on the daybed — a futon mattress on a pallet frame, covered with a quilt Cass's aunt had been throwing away — and took out her phone. 7:14. Cass was supposed to be here at seven. This was not unusual. Cass was late to things the way weather was late to forecasts, not out of disrespect but because she existed in a temporal framework that was only loosely pegged to clocks. Della had never once said anything about it. She used the waiting time the way she always used it: to prepare.

The gas station story. She'd been saving it for three days, carrying it around like a stone in her cheek. Wednesday morning, Fas Fuel on Route 2. Della had gone in for coffee and found a man in a Santa hat — in June — arguing with the cashier about whether a corn dog counted as a breakfast item. The man's position was that anything consumed before ten a.m. was breakfast by definition. The cashier, a teenager with acrylic nails so long they curved slightly, like parentheses, had countered that a corn dog was a corn dog at any hour and that the breakfast discount applied only to items from the breakfast menu, which was pictured, and which he was invited to consult. The man had then produced, from somewhere in his person, a laminated card — *laminated* — on which he had typed a personal definition of breakfast, with citations.

Della had been refining the delivery. The key was the laminated card. You had to delay it. You had to let Cass think the story was about the corn dog, was a mildly funny story about a regional eccentric, and then you produced the card the way the man had produced the card: with an air of bureaucratic finality, as if this settled things. The lamination was the fulcrum. Cass would lose it at the lamination. Della could hear the laugh already — that specific laugh, not the polite one or the surprised one but the one that came from deep in the belly and made Cass's shoulders hunch forward and her eyes squeeze shut, the laugh that meant Della had *done* something, had made something happen in the world that justified her presence in it.

She checked her phone again. 7:19.

She got up and adjusted the reading lamp so it pointed at the daybed from a better angle. She folded the quilt's edge under so the fraying seam didn't show. She stood in the center of the room and turned slowly, checking, the way a set designer checks before the house lights go down. Everything was where it should be. The titles were straight. The lights were whole. The flowers in the jar on the dry sink — black-eyed Susans this week, because they grew along the hackberry stand and because their centers were the exact dark brown of Cass's eyes in low light, not that Della had ever said this or fully allowed herself to think it — were fresh and upright and open.

She sat back down. She waited.

## II.

Cass came in the way she always came in, which was the way certain people come into any room: as if the room had been waiting for her and she was sorry to have kept it. She was still wearing her bait shop shirt, pale blue with the sleeves rolled past her elbows, and she smelled like minnow water and sunscreen, and her hair was up in the knot she tied it in when she couldn't be bothered to think about her hair, which meant strands had fallen around her face in the particular arrangement that made her look like a Baroque painting of a girl pretending not to be a Baroque painting.

"The filter broke again," Cass said, dropping onto the daybed beside Della. "The big one. Shrimp tank. So I've got like forty dollars' worth of ghost shrimp in a five-gallon bucket in the back of my car right now and if they die Mr. Khoury's going to make me pay for them, which — I looked it up — I'm pretty sure is illegal, but he's also the only person who'd hire me, so." She pulled one leg under herself, reaching back to adjust the quilt without looking at it, her hand finding the familiar topography by feel. "How was your day?"

"I have a story," Della said.

"Tell me."

Della told her. She did the man's voice — not an impression, she wasn't good at impressions, but a quality of voice, the particular blend of aggrievement and procedural dignity that belongs to people who laminate things. She delayed the card. She built the cashier's nails into a recurring motif, the way Cass had taught her stories worked, so that by the time the laminated card appeared, the cashier's nails had become a kind of structural counterpoint to it: two people armed with their respective instruments of authority, facing off over a corn dog at 9:47 a.m. on a Wednesday in June.

Cass's laugh came where Della had predicted it would come, at the lamination, and it was the right laugh — the deep one, the helpless one — and the feeling that moved through Della was so precise and so total that it briefly abolished every other feeling she had ever had. She was sitting next to Cassandra Yeong in the most beautiful room in the world and she had made her laugh, really laugh, and the Christmas lights were reflecting off the tile so that the blue wavered on the ceiling like water, and nothing was wrong anywhere.

"Laminated," Cass said, wiping her eye. "He had *citations*."

"MLA format. I think. I couldn't see clearly."

"God. People are incredible." Cass leaned her head back against the wall. The fairy lights lit the planes of her face from below, turning the hollows under her cheekbones into small shadows. "I'd have given him the discount."

"I know you would have."

"Breakfast is a state of mind."

"I'm going to get you that on a pillow," Della said, and Cass laughed again, smaller this time but warm, a coal from the first fire, and Della held it.

She held all of it. She had always held all of it, every iteration of Cass in this room across the years, filed and cross-referenced in the archive she maintained without ever deciding to maintain it. Cass at ten, announcing that this would be their place, standing on the dry sink to hang the first strand of Christmas lights, which she'd taken from her own garage. Cass at thirteen, reading aloud from the marine invertebrates book, saying the word *nudibranch* with such visible pleasure that Della had felt the word change shape in her own mouth forever after. Cass at fifteen, the week her parents' divorce went through, lying on the futon mattress in perfect silence for three hours while Della sat on the floor and read *Housekeeping* aloud until her voice cracked, and then Cass had said, without preamble, "Keep going," and Della had, and that was the night she understood what she was for.

What she was for. The phrase lived in her so deep it had no grammar, no syntax she could surface and examine. It was not a thought. It was an architecture. Cass was the person who had looked at her at age nine — Della Brennan, the quiet one, the second daughter, the one who made herself small in rooms so the room would forget to reject her — and said, *You are my best friend*, and meant it, and kept meaning it. Before Cass, Della had understood herself as a thing that happened to people, an accident of biology that her mother had once, in a phone conversation Della overheard from the upstairs hallway at age seven, described with the clinical detachment of a woman discussing a car repair that had cost more than the car was worth: *The second pregnancy was the one that ended everything.* Not cruel. Not angry. Just factual. A statement of cause and effect. The pregnancy, the ending. Della, the thing between the two.

She had never told Cass about this. She did not need to. The sentence had done its work silently, the way a crack in a foundation does its work: not by being seen but by shaping what was built above it. Everything that Della had built above it was in this room.

Cass was talking about her father now — a new argument, the same argument, about money and the boat he refused to sell and the child support he considered optional — and Della tracked each sentence with the focus of a court stenographer, noting not just the words but the gesture that accompanied each one: the way Cass pulled at the collar of her bait shop shirt when she was frustrated, the way her jaw tightened on her father's name, the way she used her hands to sculpt the air when she described things she cared about, as if the world were clay and she was perpetually, absentmindedly shaping it.

"Did you know," Cass said, turning to Della with the sudden brightness that meant she was done being angry and had decided to be interested in something instead, "that there's a species of sea slug that photosynthesize? Like a plant. *Elysia chlorotica.* It eats algae and steals the chloroplasts and then just — runs on sunlight. It's basically solar-powered."

"You're going to name one," Della said. "When you discover a new species. What are you going to name it?"

"Bold of you to assume I'll discover just one."

"What will you name the first one?"

Cass considered this with a seriousness Della loved — the willingness to treat an absurd question as if it deserved real thought. "Something honest," she said. "Not one of those names that's just a Latin version of your own name. Something about what the animal actually does."

"Like what?"

"I don't know. *Persistens.* For something that just — keeps going. Adapts. Stays alive in conditions that should have killed it."

Della looked at her. The Christmas lights pulsed faintly — they always pulsed, a barely perceptible fluctuation that she'd long ago stopped seeing, the way you stop seeing your own breathing — and in the shifting light Cass's face was the face it had always been, specific and irreplaceable, the face of the only person who had ever made Della feel like a discovery rather than an accident.

Something in Cass's expression shifted. Minutely. A half-second of something Della registered the way you register a car alarm in the distance — present, noted, immediately filed under *not relevant*. Cass looked down at her hands. Looked at the books on the shelf. Looked back at Della with a smile that was almost the smile Della knew but had, at its edges, a faint translucency, as if something behind it were showing through.

"What?" Della said.

"Nothing. I'm glad you're here." A pause. "I'm glad this place is here."

"I replaced three bulbs today. And one of the flickery ones."

"I know. I can tell. The south strand?"

"North. Left side, near the shelf."

"It looks good."

"It does," Della said. "It looks good."

## III.

The letter was from the University of Iceland, and it had come eleven days before Cass told her about it.

Later — weeks later, when Della would allow herself to think backward through the sequence with anything like honesty — she would wonder about those eleven days. What Cass had done with the information during that interval. How many times Cass had walked into the pool house carrying the knowledge of her departure the way Della carried dead light bulbs in her pocket: small, glass, clicking together in the dark. But that was later. In the moment, Della heard the words — *accepted, Reykjavík, marine biology, twelve months, full funding, I leave in three weeks* — and she did what she had always done when the floor vanished: she built a new one out of the nearest available material, which in this case was enthusiasm.

"Oh my God," she said. "Oh my *God*, Cass."

She said it with the right amount of wonder. She leaned forward, she grabbed Cass's hands, she asked the right questions — *When did you apply? What's the program called? Will you be doing fieldwork? Will you see puffins?* — and each question was a brick she laid over the opening that had appeared in her chest, and each of Cass's answers gave her another brick, and she laid them quickly, and the wall held.

It held through that night. It held through the next morning. It held for approximately forty-one hours, at which point Della found herself standing in the hardware store on Grover, staring at a display of ceramic tiles, thinking: *The floor near the shelf needs work.
