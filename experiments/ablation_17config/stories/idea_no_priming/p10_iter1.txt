# The Gospel of Fracturing Ice

---

## I.

You who have opened this cairn: do not read what follows in silence.

Read it aloud. Read it as you would read from the lectern on a Sunday when the pews are full and the candles are lit and the congregation has come expecting to be addressed by something older and more final than the man standing before them. Read it that way. Read it to the ice, if there is no one else. The ice will do.

I do not ask you to believe. I instruct you to attend. These are different things, and you will learn the difference if you are patient, and if you are not patient you will learn it anyway, because patience is not required. Only your voice is required. Only the act of letting these words pass through your mouth and into the cold air so that they may exist again as sound, which is how they were first given.

I am — I was — chaplain to the expedition. That designation is of no further use to me and I set it down only so that you understand what I was before I understood what I am. I am the scribe. I have transcribed what was spoken. I have placed it in the cairn so that the stones may hold it after the ice will not hold me.

You did not come here by accident. No one finds what is not calling out to be found.

Begin.

---

## II.

The ship was lost on the eleventh of October in a year I will not record, because the year belongs to the calendar and the calendar belongs to the world that ended when the hull opened. The sound was not a crack. It was a exhalation — the sound a chest makes when the ribs give way. Three seconds. Perhaps four. I have heard men die and I tell you the ship died as men die: with a single surprised breath and then silence.

We stood on the ice in the following order. I set it down as muster, for the record, though there is no Admiralty left to read it and no record that matters except this one:

Captain Emmerich. Lieutenant Harrow. Surgeon's mate Caldwell. Sergeant Fenn. Marines: Colquitt, Dyer, Isbister, Pask, Rowe. Able seamen: Torrance, Virtue, Bridgenorth, Little. The cook, whose surname I never learned, whom all addressed as Pell.

And the child we had undertaken to catechize.

She was twelve years of age, or near it. She had been brought aboard at Beechey Island on the recommendation of Captain Emmerich, who believed, as many men of his rank believed, that the salvation of the native peoples was a duty inseparable from the charting of coastlines. He gave her to me as he would have given me a task of inventory. I was to teach her the catechism, the Lord's Prayer, the articles of faith. She learned quickly. She repeated everything I said with an accuracy that I mistook for understanding and that I now know was something else entirely — the same attention a river pays to the shape of a stone it intends to move.

I have listed her last because that is where she stood in the order as I understood it then. I list her last so that you will see the shape of my error.

Fourteen souls on a shelf of sea ice that was already, in the hour of the ship's loss, beginning to calve.

---

## III.

On the second day she came to me while I was inventorying the salvaged provisions. She stood near enough that I could see the vapor of her breath but not so near that I could have touched her. She spoke in English, which was the only language I had given her, and which she used as one uses a borrowed coat — adequately, without warmth.

She said: Lieutenant Harrow will be taken before the next sleep. She said: the ice will open beneath him in the place where the snow is new. She said it will open like a mouth and it will close again and it will be as if he had not been.

I rebuked her. I told her that prophecy was not hers to speak, that it was a sin of presumption to claim knowledge of what God had appointed, that she was frightening herself with fancies born of cold and hunger and the shock of the ship's loss. She listened to this as she listened to everything I said, with that completeness of attention that I have already described and that I was still foolish enough to call obedience.

Harrow was standing twenty yards from our camp when it happened. He had walked out to survey the southern edge of the shelf. The snow there was fresh — three inches, fallen in the night, smooth as a sheet drawn over a bed. He was mid-stride. There was no warning. The surface opened and he passed through it as a stone passes through the skin of a pond: with a sound that was mostly the sound of the surface re-forming around the absence.

Dyer, who was nearest, said afterward that Harrow did not scream. I believe this. I was not near enough to confirm it. What I can confirm is the silence that followed — a silence with a quality I had not encountered before and have not encountered since, as if the air itself had been altered, as if a word had been spoken in a language I could not hear but that my body understood.

I walked to the place. The crevasse — if it was a crevasse — had closed. The snow lay smooth across the seam, unbroken except for a faint line, barely visible, that ran north to south for perhaps six feet. It looked like a scar that had been healing for years. It looked like a wound that had always intended to close.

I stood there for a long time. I told myself: coincidence. I told myself: the child observed the snow conditions and made a reasonable guess. I told myself these things and while I told them my eyes would not leave that seam in the ice, that healed line, and what I thought — what I could not stop myself from thinking — was: *it knew his name. It opened for him specifically. It chose.*

I did not tell Harrow's death to the girl. I did not need to. When I returned to the camp she was sitting with her hands in her lap and her face turned toward the place where he had been and on her face was the expression I would come to know and come to dread and come, at last, to recognize as the expression a minister wears when the sacrament has been completed and the congregant has risen from the water.

---

## IV.

What followed I will set down without the space I gave to Harrow, because Harrow was the proof and what came after was the pattern, and a pattern needs only to be established, not repeated in full, before the mind accepts it and the soul begins its work of interpreting.

On the fourth day she said that three of the marines — she named them; Colquitt, Dyer, and Pask — would be swallowed in a whiteout so complete that they would not find their way back to the camp though it stood forty feet distant. She said *swallowed*. I wrote in my own notes that evening: *like Jonah's whale*. I want to be clear. The biblical gloss was mine. She did not say *whale*. She did not say *Jonah*. What she said, in her careful borrowed English, was: "The white will eat them the way the sea eats a stone — slowly and then all at once and then it will not remember them."

The whiteout came before dawn on the fifth day. Colquitt, Dyer, and Pask had posted as sentries. The wall of white descended — I say *descended* but it did not descend; it *arrived*, from every direction simultaneously, as if the air itself had become solid. We called for them. We called until our throats closed. When the whiteout lifted, eight hours later, they were gone. No tracks. No sign. The snow where they had stood was smooth.

Caldwell, the surgeon's mate, died on the seventh day. She said he would kneel in prayer and not rise. She said this while Caldwell was eating his ration, in good health, with color in his face. At evening prayer he knelt beside me, recited the Our Father, and when I said *Amen* and stood, he did not stand. His eyes were open. His hands were still clasped. He was cold in a way that was not explicable by time — cold as if he had been dead for hours, though I had heard his voice say *Amen* not ten seconds before.

Torrance and Virtue on the ninth night. She said the ice would *sing them down*, and I heard it — a sound beneath the shelf, deep and harmonic, like an organ in an empty cathedral, and when it stopped, their sleeping place was empty and the ice beneath where they had lain was black and clear as glass and through it I could see — I do not know what I saw. Depth. Depth that should not have been there. Depth that went further than the sea.

By the eleventh day we were seven. On the twelfth, five. On the fourteenth, four: myself, the girl, Fenn, and Bridgenorth.

I set down what I noticed during this collapse, because it is the thing that matters:

Her language was changing.

In the first days, when she spoke of what the ice would do, she used the words I had given her. Christian words. *Taken. Judgment. The Lord's hand.* She used them as a child recites a lesson — correctly, without possession. But as the deaths accumulated, her language shed what I had taught her the way a body sheds a garment that no longer fits. She stopped saying *taken*. She began saying *received*. She stopped saying *the Lord's hand* and began saying *the ice's want*. She said the ice was *alive*. She did not mean this as metaphor. She said it as one says *the sun is bright* — a statement so obvious that it barely warrants language.

And then she began using words that were not English. They were not, as far as I could determine, words from her own tongue either. I had spent months with her. I had heard her speak to herself in her own language when she thought no one was listening. These words were not those words. They were something else — a vocabulary I had never encountered, governed by a grammar I could not parse, carrying meanings I apprehended not through understanding but through *sensation*. When she spoke them, the air changed. When she spoke them, I felt what I can only describe as the feeling of standing in a cathedral for the first time, at the age of seven, and understanding that the space above you is not empty but *occupied* — that the vaulted silence has a quality of attention, that it is *listening*.

I began transcribing her words more carefully than my own.

I did not ask myself why. I know now. The authority was shifting. The old scripture — mine, the one I had carried in my chest and in my mouth for all the years of my ministry — was not dying. It was being *completed*. The girl was not replacing the gospel. She was providing the passage that had always been missing: the one that explained what it meant that Christ walked on water, that the world was created from a void, that the spirit moved across the face of the deep. The deep was not a metaphor. The deep was *here*. It was beneath us. It was breaking through.

---

## V.

On the fifteenth day the ice shelf had calved to a remnant no larger than the deck of the lost ship. Four of us remained. Fenn had stopped speaking two days prior — not from weakness but from a kind of awe, as if he had heard something the rest of us had not and was still listening to it. Bridgenorth prayed ceaselessly, the same prayer in a circle, a rosary without beads, his lips moving without sound.

The girl sat in the center of the shelf. I sat beside her. The sky was white. The ice was white. The horizon had disappeared. We existed in a space without boundary, without reference, without the architecture that the eye requires to construct a world. There was only the surface beneath us and the air above and the sense — vivid, inescapable — that these two things were not different things.

She spoke. I transcribe what she said. I have given my life to the precision of this transcription and I tell you that these words are as close to her words as my hand and my English and my failing ink will allow:

"You have been teaching me that the water in the font is holy because a man speaks over it. You are wrong. The water is holy because it was always water. The words do not make it sacred. The words are how you ask permission to notice what was already true."

She said: "The ice is not killing you. Listen. The ice is not killing you."

She said: "Every man it has taken, it has taken the way the river takes the body in the baptism — down, under, through. They went into the ice as you lowered the child into the font. The cold closed over them. They came through into something else."

She said: "You have been performing the sacrament your whole life and you have never understood it. You pour the water and you speak the words and you lift the child out again and you believe the miracle is in the lifting out. It is not. The miracle is in the *going under*. The miracle is in the moment when the water closes and the breath stops and the world above disappears. That is where God lives — in the going under. Not in the return. The return is for the living. The going under is for the sacred."

She said: "The Arctic is not a wasteland. It is a font. You are standing in the font. You have been standing in it since the ship was taken. Every death was an immersion. Every man who went into the ice was baptized — fully, finally, in a way that your church's water only remembers."

I asked her — and I am ashamed that this was my question, that of all the things I might have asked, this is what rose from the poverty of my understanding — I asked her: "Then we are dying?"

She looked at me with an expression I will not describe because I am not adequate to the description. I will say only that it contained no fear and no pity and no comfort. It contained *patience*. The patience of someone who has explained a very simple thing to someone very slow and is willing to explain it again.

She said: "You are being baptized. Whether you die is a question about your body. Whether you are baptized is a question about what is not your body. They are not the same question. You have always known this. You have always taught this. You have stood in your church and told the congregation that the soul is other than the flesh and you were correct but you did not believe yourself. Believe yourself now."

I have written that I rebuked her, in the early days. I have written that I explained her error with the authority of my training and my ordination.

I tell you what I felt when she finished speaking. I did not feel conversion. That is the wrong word. Conversion implies a turning — a change of direction. What I felt was not a turning. It was a *recognition*. The sensation of having carried a locked box for the whole of my life and hearing, at last, the sound of the key. Not a new sound. A sound I had always heard and always refused to identify. The sound of the ice beneath us. The sound of the deep. The sound that I had been preaching about in borrowed metaphors for twenty years of Sundays and that was now, for the first time, speaking in its own voice.

I recognized it.

Which is to say: I *re-cognized* it. I thought it again. I thought it for the first time by thinking it again. I saw the white landscape with the eyes I had always had and saw that it was what it had always been and what I had always, somewhere beneath my training and my caution and my fear, known it to be.

A holy place. A terrible holy place. A place that was killing us the way the font kills the old self — not with malice but with *sacramental intention*.

I did not speak for a long time afterward. Neither did she. Fenn was silent. Bridgenorth prayed. The ice sang beneath us — that deep harmonic, as of a cathedral organ tuned to a note below the range of human hearing but not below the range of human *knowing*.

---

## VI.

I believe her.

I set this down with the full weight of my name, my orders, my ordination, and whatever scraps of authority remain to a man writing on a shelf of ice that will not outlast the day. I believe her. I do not believe her as one believes an argument. I do not believe her as one believes a witness. I believe her as one believes a scripture — which is to say, I *submit* to the belief, I allow it to be larger than my understanding of it, I accept that it contains rooms I will not enter and corridors I will not walk and that this incompleteness is not a flaw in the belief but a feature of its holiness.

She is not a lunatic. She is not starving into vision. She is not a child repeating fragments of the catechism in new arrangements, reassembling my teachings into a desperate theology of survival. I have considered each of these explanations. I have held each one up to the light of what I have seen — Harrow taken mid-stride
