# The Doubles at the Spa

## I.

The Vee Spa Hotel had been built in 1974 to house the minor ailments of minor functionaries—men with liver complaints and women whose doctors had prescribed sea air in the clinical tone reserved for conditions no one intended to treat. It was concrete, five stories, shaped like a domino laid on its side. The thermal pool in the basement had been drained in 2003. The sauna still worked, intermittently, like a conscience. The breakfast room had one window facing south across a snowfield that ran, without interruption or apology, all the way to a stand of birch trees whose white bark made them look, in certain light, like they were already ghosts of themselves.

Priit Tamm arrived at half past two on Friday with a leather briefcase that had belonged to his father and a laptop bag that had not. He was forty-three, employed by the Tax and Customs Board, Region West, and had been assigned to complete an asset inspection of the property before its closure—a formality requiring him to walk through each room with a clipboard and agree that the furniture matched the depreciation schedule. He had driven from Tallinn in a Škoda whose heater rattled in D minor, a fact he would not have known and would not have cared about. At the front desk he noted the year of the register, the brand of pen offered (Jotter, blue ink, cap cracked), and the number of keys remaining on the pegboard behind the woman's head. Fourteen rooms, six keys out. The building was dying. His job was to count its teeth.

"Room 12," said the woman behind the desk. She had the face of someone who had stopped performing hospitality sometime in the previous decade but still remembered the choreography. "Second floor. The lift is not reliable above three, but you are not going above three."

"The inspection requires access to all floors," Priit said.

"Then you will use the stairs," she said, and handed him the key with a motion that was not quite a shrug but contained one, folded small and placed at the center of the gesture like a seed.

This was Maret Kask, sixty-seven, owner and final employee of the Vee Spa Hotel, and she had been watching people arrive at this desk for thirty years with the same expression: a courtesy so complete it had passed through warmth and come out the other side into something cooler and more durable, the way stones in a river are not wet but *shaped by* wet. She had been diagnosed in October. The tumors were in her lungs and had visited her spine. She took her pills at odd hours and slept in the office behind the desk on a daybed whose springs she knew by name. When she watched Priit carry his briefcase up the stairs—his footsteps receding in the concrete stairwell with a sound like someone slowly applauding—she noted two things: that he held the briefcase slightly away from his body, as though it contained something embarrassing, and that the radiator on the second-floor landing had begun weeping again, a thin line of rust-colored water tracing the grout between tiles that had been cream in 1974 and were now the color of teeth in an old photograph.

The elder Soome sister, Kadri, arrived at quarter past three. She was fifty-one, an Estonian-language teacher at a gymnasium in Pärnu, and she entered the lobby with the specific vigilance of a woman who had already decided what she would not look at. She did not look at the guest register when she signed it, keeping her gaze on her own hand as though her signature were a small animal that might bolt. She did not look at the corridor leading to the east wing. She mentioned Room 34 to Maret in a voice stripped down to its transactional minimum—she was here to collect the personal effects of Valve Soome, deceased, her aunt, whose ashes had been held at the hotel per the terms of a handwritten will that specified, with the particular cruelty of the recently dead, that the collection must be performed in person, by family, within sixty days.

"Room 22," Maret said. "The urn is in 34. I can take you there now or—"

"Tomorrow," Kadri said.

Maret gave her the key and watched her walk to the stairs without looking back. Maret had read the will. She knew there were two nieces. She knew they had not spoken in eleven years. She knew this because Valve had told her, in the weeks before the end, with the unburdened specificity of a woman who had stopped protecting anyone's story, including her own. Valve had died in Room 34 on a Tuesday in November, in the late afternoon, with the snow light coming through the curtain and Maret holding a glass of water she never drank, and the last thing Valve had said was not about her nieces or her life but about the smell—she said the room smelled like the pool, like minerals, like something underground and very old, and Maret had said yes, the pipes, and Valve had said no, not the pipes, and then she had stopped talking and two minutes later she had stopped everything else.

Now Maret set the pen back in its holder and listened to the building. The heating pipes ran through the walls like a circulatory system that had developed opinions. They knocked. They groaned. In certain rooms, at certain hours, they produced a low, sustained hum that guests over the years had variously described as wind, as traffic, as music, and, on one occasion in 1996, as breathing. Maret did not describe it as anything. She had lived with the sound for so long that silence, when it occasionally fell, struck her as the aberration—a skipped beat in a pulse she had learned to sleep inside.

The younger Soome sister, Luule, arrived at ten to four, and the look on her face when she saw the name in the register—*K. Soome, Room 22, 15:17*—was the kind of expression that lasts less than a second but contains, compressed within it, an entire archaeology of feeling: surprise, then recognition that the surprise was naive, then anger at the naivety, then something beneath the anger that was older and less nameable, something that had the texture of grief but the temperature of shame. She was forty-four, a freelance translator who lived in Tartu and specialized in Finnish crime novels, a profession that required her to spend most of her days inside other people's language, finding the Estonian bones beneath Finnish syntax, and she had developed the habit of seeing double meanings in everything, which was useful in her work and catastrophic in her family.

"Room 23," Maret said, without comment on the register. "Next to your—"

"I can read," Luule said. Then, after a pause that recalibrated something: "Thank you. Is there dinner?"

"At seven. The kitchen is—limited."

Luule took her key. At the stairs she stopped, one hand on the banister, and looked back at Maret with an expression that wanted to ask something—whether Kadri knew, whether there was another floor, whether the sixty-day window could be extended by acts of cowardice—but the question never formed. She went up. The stairwell holds sounds the way a throat holds a swallowed word, and Maret could hear both sisters' doors close within the same minute, the sounds separated by a wall that was—and this Maret knew, because she had seen the maintenance reports—eleven centimeters of poured concrete, cracked along the seam where the building had settled unevenly over five decades, hairline fractures running through the aggregate like the lines on a palm.

The cellist arrived at dusk. His name was Zheng Wei, he was thirty-six, and he had been traveling with a chamber ensemble that was now in Kuressaare, on Saaremaa, or possibly in Haapsalu, or possibly on the bus between, because the distinction had ceased to matter when the tour manager had called to say the festival was canceled, the funding had evaporated, and would Zheng please make his own arrangements. His own arrangements consisted of a Gofriller cello in a carbon-fiber case and approximately ninety euros, and when the bus had stopped at the junction outside the Vee Spa Hotel he had seen the sign—*ROOMS AVAILABLE*—and calculated that a room here would cost less than a room in Haapsalu because everything costs less when it is about to stop existing. He was correct. Maret charged him thirty euros for two nights and did not ask about the cello case, which he carried on his back with the tenderness and resignation of a man transporting a sick relative.

The lobby, as Zheng crossed it, produced a sound that made him stop. Not the pipes—he had not yet learned to hear those—but the room itself: the particular dead acoustic of a large concrete space with low ceilings and hard surfaces, a room that would swallow a pianissimo and spit back a fortissimo with a half-second delay and a metallic edge. He had played in Soviet-era halls before. They were not built for music. They were built for speeches, which is a different species of sound—the human voice projected outward as authority, flattened by parallel walls into something that arrives at the ear not as song but as instruction. He noted this the way he noted all rooms, automatically, the way a painter entering a building looks first at the light. The Vee Spa Hotel had the acoustic signature of a space that did not want to be listened to. He would remember this later.

"The forecast," Maret said to no one in particular as she watched the snow through the lobby window, "is for rain."

It rained. Not dramatically—the sky did not open, no thunder performed its pantomime of consequence—it simply rained, steadily, through Friday evening, the temperature hovering at that nihilistic margin between snow and sleet where water cannot decide what it wants to be. The bridge road connecting the hotel's spit of land to the village sat in a depression between two rises, and by eight o'clock the road was water, and by nine the water was moving, and by ten it was clear to Maret, who had seen this happen four times in thirty years, that no one was driving out until it subsided, which would be Monday at the earliest. She informed each guest by slipping a handwritten note under each door. The notes were identical: *Road out. Monday likely. Kitchen open 7-9, 12-1, 6-8. M. Kask.* She did not add, because it was not her habit to editorialize, that the hotel had lost phone signal with the first gust of wind, or that the internet router, which had been installed in 2011 and updated never, was showing a red light it had not shown before. These were facts. The building was good at producing facts. The building had, in its half-century of operation, produced an unbroken chain of facts—leaking roofs, settling foundations, frozen pipes, peeling paint, departed guests—and Maret had recorded them all in a series of logbooks she kept in the office, the way a doctor keeps charts, with the same clinical detachment and the same underlying knowledge that all patients, ultimately, share a single prognosis.

Five strangers in a building that was not going to let them leave. The wind found the cracks in the south-facing windows and made a sound that was not a whistle and not a moan but something between—a exhalation, a slow letting-go, as though the building itself were breathing out and had been breathing out for fifty years and was nearly empty.

## II.

Priit could not sleep. This was not unusual. He had not slept well since his divorce, a word he still used with the caution of a man handling a tool he did not trust—*my divorce*, he would say, and the possessive pronoun sat strangely, as though the event belonged to him the way a scar belongs to a body, by accident and permanently. He lay in Room 12 and listened to the pipes. The pipes spoke in a low, arrhythmic register that was almost, but not quite, language, the way the hum of a fluorescent light is almost, but not quite, music. At one-forty he rose, put on his trousers and undershirt, and walked to the corridor to find the toilet, which was shared, at the end of the hall, because the Vee Spa Hotel had been built in an era when private bathrooms were considered a bourgeois indulgence and the proletariat was expected to urinate communally.

The corridor was lit by a single emergency fixture that gave off a light the color of old newspaper. Priit walked its length in his socks. He noted, because he was a man who noted things, that the carpet was worn to its mesh backing in a path down the center, that the room numbers were brass and seven of the twelve had oxidized to green, and that the fire extinguisher near the stairwell was three years past its inspection date, which he would include in his report. He noted these things, and then he stopped noting things, because the corridor was no longer empty.

There was a figure standing at the far end, near the window that overlooked the parking lot. It was luminous. Not bright—not the harsh white of a bulb or the sterile blue of a screen—but *luminous* in the older sense, the way medieval paintings use gold leaf, a light that seemed to come not from a source but from the substance of the thing itself, as though the figure were made of a material that had light as one of its properties, like weight or temperature. The figure was shaped like a man. The figure was shaped like Priit.

It was not a reflection. There was no glass, no mirror, no surface that could account for it. The figure stood in the corridor with the patient stillness of someone who had been there for a long time and expected to be there for longer, and it was engaged—with its hands, with its luminous body, with the unhurried attention of ritual or devotion—in an act so specific, so particular to the interior of Priit's private mind, that seeing it performed in the hallway of a hotel by a creature made of light produced in Priit a sensation he had no name for. It was not fear. Fear is the body's response to threat, and this was not a threat; it was a *description*. It was the most accurate description Priit had ever encountered of something he had spent twenty years keeping in a room in his mind with the door closed and the lights off and the key in a pocket he checked, compulsively, the way a man with a heart condition checks his pulse.

The figure was him, and it was with someone, and the someone was also luminous, also patient, also unbothered by the concrete walls and the emergency lighting and the expired fire extinguisher, and what they were doing together was not violent or transgressive by any standard Priit could have articulated—it was, in fact, tender, almost gentle, the kind of act that becomes unspeakable not because it is monstrous but because it is *too specifically wanted*, because the particularity of the desire is itself the nakedness, the exact angle of the head, the exact placement of the hands, the precise quality of attention that Priit had imagined ten thousand times in the privacy of his insomnia and that now stood before him in the corridor, glowing softly, like a prayer made visible by someone who did not understand that prayers are not supposed to be *answered*, they are supposed to be *held*.

Priit did not move. He did not breathe in any way that his body would later remember as breathing. He stood in the corridor for an interval that his mind would compress into a single moment and his body would record as eleven minutes, and then he returned to Room 12, and he closed the door, and he sat on the edge of the bed with his hands on his knees, and he looked at his hands, and his hands looked the same as they always had, and this felt like a lie.
