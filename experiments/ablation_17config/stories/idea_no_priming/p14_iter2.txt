# The Definitive Text

## I.

The room was not the room he had been promised.

Gerald Moser stood in the doorway of what the lodge's proprietor had described, in correspondence conducted over eleven weeks and four increasingly specific emails, as the Steinbock Suite — a corner room with a writing desk, a view of the Dachstein massif, and, critically, a door that locked. What he found instead was a narrow chamber on the lodge's second floor, fitted with two beds separated by the width of a nightstand, a single wardrobe of dark pine leaking the smell of camphor into the cold air, and a writing desk, yes — one writing desk, shoved beneath a window so fogged with condensation that the mountains beyond were reduced to a luminous grey absence.

The woman was already unpacking.

She had claimed the bed nearest the window and the only chair, and she had done so with the unhurried efficiency of someone who had arrived not merely first but *inevitably*. Her suitcase, open on the mattress, was a battered leather thing — the kind that communicated not poverty but indifference to the signaling function of luggage. From it she was removing, in a sequence that struck Gerald as almost liturgical: a stack of Moleskine notebooks held together with a wide rubber band, a toiletry bag of waxed canvas, a charger for a phone model he did not recognize, and a copy of *The Krampus Tradition: Ritual, Fear, and the Pre-Modern Alpine Imagination* — his book, his book, the book that had taken him eleven years and had been called, by the *Journal of European Folklore Studies*, "unlikely to be superseded in this generation" — with so many colored adhesive tabs protruding from its pages that it resembled a small, dying hedge.

"Ah," she said, without looking up. "Professor Moser."

"Dr. Steinmetz."

He knew her. Of course he knew her. Lena Steinmetz, University of Graz, Department of European Ethnology, author of *Perchten, Schemen, Schiachperchten: Toward a Non-Christian Taxonomy of Alpine Masking Traditions*, a monograph published eighteen months ago by a Viennese academic press so small that Gerald had initially and happily assumed it would vanish into the sediment of minor regional scholarship. It had not vanished. Instead, it had begun to metastasize through the footnotes of every Central European journal that mattered — each citation a small, precise incision into the foundations of Gerald's own work. She had never attacked him directly. She did not need to. Her method was the method of the footnote: a redirected citation here, a supplementary reading there, each one a quiet suggestion that the definitive text was not quite so definitive after all.

"There has been a booking error," Gerald said.

"Evidently."

"I was promised the Steinbock Suite."

"As was I." She held up a printout — his printout's twin, the same letterhead, the same date, the same room. "The proprietor says the pipes burst. Or that the ceiling is being replastered. He was vague. The condition of this building suggests both are plausible simultaneously."

She was right about the building. The Jagdhaus Grünstein was the kind of Alpine lodge that had been built for the minor aristocracy of the late Hapsburg period and had been dying, with various degrees of dignity, ever since. The hallways smelled of wood smoke and mildew. The staircase listed perceptibly to the left. The taxidermy in the corridors — chamois, red deer, a fox whose glass eyes had been replaced at some point with mismatched buttons — spoke to an era of robust confidence in the decorative potential of dead animals. Gerald had chosen the venue precisely *because* of this atmosphere: the Krampusnacht gala, held here annually for the past decade, attracted a particular kind of academic and cultural patron — people for whom the crumbling grandeur was not a drawback but a credential, proof that one's interest in Alpine folk tradition was serious enough to tolerate a boiler that worked only intermittently and a host whose hospitality had the bracing quality of a man who resented his own guests.

Gerald set his suitcase — a Rimowa, hard-shell, purchased the year his book went into its third printing — beside the remaining bed. From it he removed: three pressed shirts, a dinner jacket in a garment bag, two ties (one for the opening dinner, one in reserve, because experience had taught him that Krampus performers wielding birch switches near a buffet table were a dry-cleaning liability), a leather folio containing the typescript of his keynote remarks, a first edition of Grillparzer's *Der Traum ein Leben* that he had brought because a man should have something to read that is not his own work and because he imagined being seen reading it, and a copy of his own book — a clean copy, unmarked, for signing.

Dr. Steinmetz glanced at the signing copy. Something moved across her face — not quite a smile, more the muscular suppression of one.

"Will you be giving a talk?" he asked, hearing in his own voice the particular brightness that meant he was performing casualness and despising himself for it.

"Just observing. I have fieldwork in the region this spring. I wanted to see the performers." She slotted a notebook into the pocket of a cardigan that was the precise grey of institutional concrete. "And you are giving the keynote, I think."

"I am."

"I know. I have read the abstract." She pulled a tab from the book — his book, his *book* — and examined the page beneath it with an expression of detached clinical interest, the expression of a surgeon reviewing a scan. "It references Vernaleken's 1859 classification system."

"Foundational to the field."

"Foundational to a certain period of the field, yes."

She replaced the tab. Somewhere below them, in the lodge's ground-floor reception hall, there was a crash — a dropped tray, or a piece of scenery falling — followed by laughter and the low, resonant clanging of cowbells. The performers were assembling. Through the fog of the window, Gerald could just make out a shape crossing the courtyard: fur, horns, the exaggerated forward stoop of a man carrying a heavy wooden mask. The shape paused, as if sensing observation, then moved on.

"I will speak to the proprietor," Gerald said. "About the room."

"I already have. He suggested we 'make an arrangement like adults.'" Her accent, when she quoted the proprietor, shifted into a broader Austrian register — Styrian, he thought, or possibly Carinthian — and back again so smoothly that the mimicry felt less like mockery than like a reflex, the involuntary competence of someone who carried multiple dialects the way other people carried keys. "His words. He also suggested that the lodge is quite full and that we should be grateful."

Gerald looked at the two beds, the single nightstand, the writing desk beneath its sweating window. On the wall above his bed, a mounted chamois head regarded him with an expression of resigned contempt that he felt, obscurely, he had earned.

"Fine," he said.

"Fine," she said, and opened her notebook to a fresh page, and began to write.

---

From below, the sounds of the gala's preparation rose through the floor like a second, subterranean weather: the scraping of chairs being arranged, the tuneless warming-up of an accordion, a gust of freezing air that rolled under the door each time someone opened the lodge's front entrance. Gerald hung his dinner jacket in the wardrobe and found that Dr. Steinmetz's coat was already there — a dark wool thing, unstructured, smelling faintly of woodsmoke and something sharper, pine resin or juniper. Their garments hung side by side in the camphor dark like the shed skins of two incompatible animals forced into the same den.

He dressed for dinner. She did not change. He noticed this.

At six-fifteen, the cowbells began in earnest — not the distant, decorative sound of Alpine tourism but a clamorous, arrhythmic din that seemed to pour up through the building's stone foundations, vibrating in the plaster walls. Gerald caught himself standing very still, one hand on his tie, listening. The sound had a quality he recognized from recordings but had never encountered live: not festive but *processional*, the sound of something being summoned or driven, though which of these it was depended on which layer of the tradition you consulted. His layer — the published, definitive layer — said festive. A controlled inversion. Carnival logic. The monstrous as social safety valve.

He straightened his tie and descended.

## II.

The dining hall had been dressed for the occasion with the maximalist enthusiasm of a stage designer who had been told "traditional" and had interpreted the instruction as a challenge. Antler chandeliers — three of them, enormous, bristling — hung from the blackened beams, each fitted with electric candles whose filaments trembled in the drafts. The long tables were draped in white linen already spotted with wine. Pine boughs had been heaped along the windowsills, releasing a sharp green scent that mingled with the room's baseline atmosphere of roasted meat, cold stone, and the pungent, animal smell of the Krampus costumes.

The performers circulated among the seated guests with what Gerald understood, professionally, to be a careful calibration of menace: close enough to startle, theatrical enough to reassure. Their costumes were extraordinary. He granted this without reservation. The masks — *Larven*, hand-carved from pine or linden, each one a singular grotesquerie of horns and teeth and distended jaw — had the quality of objects that had been made not merely to represent a face but to *replace* one. Sheepskin and goatskin pelts hung from the performers' shoulders in matted cascades. Cowbells the size of a man's head swung from leather harnesses at their waists. When they moved through the dining hall, they moved in a way that Gerald could only describe as *anatomically committed* — the forward stoop, the swaying gait, the sudden explosive lunges toward a shrieking guest — and the effect was not of men in costumes but of something more disquieting: men who had found, in the costume, an architecture of movement that their ordinary bodies did not permit.

Gerald was seated, by place card, at the head of the center table. The position of honor. He settled into it with the practiced ease of a man who had occupied such positions many times: the slight backward lean, the proprietary scan of the room, the glass of wine accepted with a nod that communicated both gratitude and entitlement. On his left: the lodge's proprietor, a florid man in a waistcoat whose buttons appeared to be engaged in a labor action. On his right: a cultural attaché from the provincial government, a woman of approximately sixty whose earrings — small silver Krampus figures — swung when she turned her head. Across from him, four seats down, visible but not adjacent: Dr. Steinmetz.

She had not changed for dinner, and the contrast between her grey cardigan and the room's sartorial effort — the dinner jackets, the dirndls, the attaché's earrings — should have diminished her. It did not. She sat with the self-contained composure of someone who had opted out of a competition she considered irrelevant, and the effect was that the competition itself seemed, briefly, foolish. Gerald noticed that the man beside her — a professor of cultural studies from Innsbruck whose name Gerald could not recall but whose book on carnival traditions Gerald had reviewed, unfavorably, three years ago — was leaning toward her with the helpless attentiveness of a plant bending toward light.

Dinner was roasted pork, dumplings, red cabbage. Gerald ate with appetite. Wine was served with the liberality of an institution that had calculated the per-head cost and decided against restraint. The room grew warm. Conversations braided together, pulled apart, rebraided. A Krampus performer stalked between the tables, rattling his chains, and a woman at the far end of the hall let out a scream that collapsed, almost immediately, into delighted laughter. The sound had a particular quality — the laughter of someone who has been frightened and is now performing the retrospective discovery that the fear was pleasurable — that Gerald had written about extensively. Chapter seven. "The Social Function of Ritual Fear."

It was during the cheese course that he made his move.

The conversational opening was natural enough: the attaché asked about the tradition's history, and Gerald, with the fluency of a man who had delivered this particular performance dozens of times, began to speak. He spoke about his fieldwork in the Gastein valley in the winter of 2006 — the year he spent embedded with a Krampus troupe, the *Pass*, attending rehearsals, documenting the carving of masks, earning the trust of men who had initially regarded an English-speaking academic with the warmth typically reserved for tax auditors. He described the night he had been permitted to walk in the procession itself: the weight of the costume, the heat inside the mask, the way the cowbell's rhythm entered the body through the sternum and reorganized something in the nervous system. The table quieted. Gerald had always been a good storyteller — it was the quality his detractors most resented and his admirers most distrusted — and he could feel the room's attention gathering, tightening, the particular silence of people who are being given something they will later repeat.

"The crucial insight," he said, turning his wine glass by its stem — a gesture he had developed for moments of rhetorical summation, the glass becoming a small, glinting metronome — "is that the Krampus functions as a *controlled* inversion. The community produces the monster, and in producing it, masters the fear. The figure is terrifying precisely *because* it is contained. The ritual frame — the date, the procession route, the relationship to St. Nicholas — these are not incidental. They are the mechanism by which the community asserts its sovereignty over the frightening. Remove the frame, and you have not a tradition but a pathology."

He paused. The pause was calibrated — long enough to let the formulation land, short enough to prevent interruption.

The interruption came anyway.

"The Gastein fieldwork," said Dr. Steinmetz, from four seats down, in a voice pitched to carry exactly as far as it needed to and not one decibel farther. "That was the winter of 2006, you said?"

"It was."

"I ask only because your book dates the Hufnagel mask — the one you describe as being carved during your residency, the centerpiece of your chapter on material culture — to that same winter. But Anton Hufnagel died in March of 2005. I spoke with his daughter last autumn. She was quite clear about the date. She showed me the *Parte*." A beat. "The death notice," she added, for the benefit of the non-German speakers, with a courtesy so elaborate it was indistinguishable from condescension.

Gerald felt something shift in his chest — a small, cold rearrangement, like a key turning in a lock he hadn't known was there. "The chronology in the book reflects the composition process. I was synthesizing multiple visits—"

"Of course. I only mention it because the anecdote, as told, implies a single continuous residency, and the mask's attribution is" — she lifted her wine glass, drank, set it down — "central to the argument. You describe watching Hufnagel select the wood. You describe the grain of the linden."

The attaché's silver Krampus earrings swung as she looked from Steinmetz to Gerald.

"The ethnographic specificity," Dr. Steinmetz continued, "is what makes the passage so vivid. One sees the old man's hands. It is" — and here she smiled, not unkindly — "very good writing."

The compliment landed like a blade sheathed in tissue paper. Gerald recognized the technique because he had used it himself: praise the style to indict the substance. Across the table, the professor from Innsbruck was studying his dumpling with the intense focus of a man trying to become invisible.

"The broader point holds," Gerald said. "The mask's provenance—"

"Yes, the broader point." She set her knife and fork together on her plate — a small, precise gesture of completion. "
