# A Bigger Map

---

## 1.

I am a librarian in a city that has been, at various points, a garrison, a monastery, a free port, a crater, and a parking lot, though not in that order. The current iteration is my favorite, or at least the one I've grown least willing to leave, which is the same thing if you're patient enough. I work at the Vaszary branch, which occupies the ground floor of a building the city directory lists as mixed-use and which I think of, with the tenderness reserved for the slightly decrepit, as mine.

My name is Maren. I shelve books. I water the fern by the circulation desk, which is the fourth fern I have watered in that spot, because ferns have the decency to die on a comprehensible schedule and I find that restful. I charge late fees with a lenience my supervisors would disapprove of if my supervisors could hold a thought about me for longer than a few minutes, which they cannot, for reasons I do not intend to explain yet.

The chair by the east window—the one with the particular quality of light that falls in a rhombus on the reading table between two and four in the afternoon—is, by unspoken consensus, Lior's chair. Lior is a regular. Lior is a friend, in the way regulars at a library become friends: through the slow accumulation of preference data and the mutual courtesy of pretending that seeing someone three times a week does not constitute intimacy. Lior reads widely and returns books on time and once brought me a coffee so precisely calibrated to my taste that I understood it as an act of serious attention and was, briefly, afraid.

The city is good for people like us. By which I mean: people who need to be unremarkable. It is large enough to be anonymous and old enough to tolerate strangeness—it has that quality of cities that have been rebuilt too many times, a deep civic indifference to permanence. You can live here for decades and no one will ask where you came from, partly out of politeness and partly because the city itself has forgotten where *it* came from, its foundations layered over foundations in the Carolingian style, if you'll allow me a reference that dates me more than I'd like.

I like the work. I like the particular silence of a library at ten in the morning, which is different from the silence of a library at closing, which is different again from the silence at three a.m. when I am alone with the collection and the smell of binding glue and the slow, planetary patience of objects that do not need to be anything other than what they are. I have been many things. I find I am suited to this.

Lior comes in on Tuesdays, Thursdays, and Saturdays, except when Lior comes in on other days, which is more often than the pattern suggests. We talk about what Lior is reading. We talk about the weather, which in this city is an infinite subject because the microclimate shifts block by block due to the river and the old thermal vents beneath Királyi Street, which have been there since before the street was called that, since before streets were streets, since the vents were just wounds in the ground that the earth hadn't figured out how to close. I sometimes say things like that. Lior never asks what I mean. This is one of the reasons I am fond of Lior. The other reasons are more complicated, and I will get to them, because getting to them is the point of all this, but I want you to understand the fondness first. I want you to trust it. It's real. That's important. The realness of it is what makes the rest of it difficult.

---

## 2.

Let me tell you what Lior does.

On a Tuesday in October—the specific Tuesday doesn't matter, but I am a librarian and specificity is a professional deformation—Lior arrived at the Vaszary branch wearing the face of a young man with a close-cropped beard and a slightly asymmetrical nose, the kind of face that suggests a background in something physical, carpentry or boxing, a face designed to be trustworthy without being interesting. This was the face Lior had been wearing for about three weeks, which is longer than usual, which I noted.

At 10:40 Lior left to get lunch. At 11:15 Lior returned as a woman in her fifties with reading glasses on a chain and the particular proprietary air of someone who has been coming to a library for years, which was, in its way, the most honest face Lior had ever worn in my presence. At 2:00 Lior went outside to take a phone call and came back as someone new entirely—late twenties, androgynous, dark-haired, with a way of occupying space that suggested postgraduate education and mild social anxiety. This was the face Lior used to talk to me about the Borges collection, and I could feel the face's intelligence like a garment Lior had put on because Lior wanted the conversation to happen at a certain altitude.

I watched all of this with the pleasure of watching anyone do anything supremely well. Because Lior is not merely a shapeshifter—a word I dislike for its connotations of horror films and involuntary transformation, of wolfsbane and full moons—but something more like a rhetorician of the body. Each face is an argument. Each shift is a response to a context, a social micro-climate, a reading of the room so precise and so rapid that it constitutes a kind of genius. The young man's face puts the elderly patrons at ease. The woman in her fifties passes without friction through the bureaucratic interactions of the day—bank, post office, pharmacy—where a certain age and gender render you both visible and unthreatening. The androgynous scholar is for me, or for the conversation Lior wants to have with me, or perhaps for the version of Lior that Lior becomes when talking about literature, and I choose not to interrogate which.

I have watched polyglots switch languages mid-sentence, their faces rearranging around the phonemes, becoming briefly someone else as the grammar shifts. Lior does this with *selves*. And does it with the fluency that only comes from centuries—

I should say practice. That only comes from practice.

Here is the thing I want you to understand: Lior is *good at this*. Not tortured by it. Not suffering through it. Good at it the way a concert pianist is good at scales—automatically, joyfully, with the deep pleasure of a capacity fully realized. When Lior shifts, there is a moment, barely visible, when the new face settles and Lior *inhabits* it, and in that moment there is something like delight. The delight of a perfect fit. Of a key turning in a lock it was made for.

I once asked Lior—casually, in the way you ask someone about their weekend—what their original form looked like. Lior considered this the way you might consider a question about the color of a sound or the weight of a number. Not with pain. With genuine semantic confusion. The question didn't have a referent anymore. It was like asking what shape water is when it's not in a container. There was a silence, and then Lior said, "I don't think that's a real question," and went back to reading, and I shelved three carts of returns before I trusted my hands to be steady.

Because I understood what that answer meant. I understood it better than Lior did.

I should tell you about the flinch.

It happened on a Thursday, when Lior was shifting for the second time that day—from the fiftyish woman to a younger face, male, with light eyes. I was at the circulation desk and I saw it: a microexpression, a contraction so small it was almost subcutaneous, a tremor at the corner of Lior's jaw as the new face assembled itself. What happened was this: the face Lior was reaching for overlapped, by perhaps a three-percent margin of resemblance, with a face Lior had worn six months earlier. Not the same face. Not deliberately repeated. But close enough that something in Lior—something beneath the virtuosity, beneath the craft, in whatever continuous thread still ran through all those discarded physiognomies—*flinched*. The way you flinch when you hear your own voice on a recording. The involuntary recoil from an echo of yourself.

Lior corrected it instantly. The light eyes darkened. The jaw reshaped. The resemblance vanished so completely that if I had blinked I would have missed it. But I do not blink at important moments. This is one of my few unambiguous talents.

The flinch told me three things: that Lior's control, which presented as absolute, had a seam. That beneath the shifting, somewhere, there was a continuity—a someone who kept count, who tracked the faces, who knew when a repetition was approaching and could not bear it. And that this someone did not want to be repeated because repetition would imply a preference, and a preference would imply a self, and a self was precisely the thing Lior had spent—how long? I knew the answer, though Lior did not know I knew it—had spent four hundred and eleven years learning to do without.

I went back to shelving. Lior went back to reading. The library hummed its ten-in-the-morning silence. Outside, the city went about its business of being the latest version of itself, and I thought about what I was going to do to the person sitting in the rhombus of light by the east window, and whether it could be called kindness, and whether that mattered.

## 3.

Sable arrived on a Wednesday in November, which was wrong—not the month but the day, because Wednesdays at the Vaszary branch are quiet and Sable was not quiet. Not loud, either. Concentrated. She had the particular intensity of someone who has been looking for a specific thing for a long time and has just been told it might be in this building.

She asked for the cadastral collection. This is not something most people ask for. It is not something most people know we have. It is a set of property maps, technically municipal records, stored in the basement archive in acid-free folders I maintain with perhaps excessive care. I led her down. She handled the maps with the focused reverence of a surgeon, and I recognized her immediately—not her face, which I had never seen, but her *type*. The ones who map what shouldn't be mappable. I've met a few, over the years. Centuries. Over the years.

Her name was Sable. She told me this while examining a 1927 survey map of the blocks east of the river, holding it at an angle to catch the light, and I saw her notice—not the map's content but its *absences*, the places where the surveyor's line hesitated or skipped, the micro-lacunae that most people read as drafting errors and that Sable read as data.

"These gaps," she said. "They're not mistakes."

"No," I said, because I have found that the most effective way to guide someone toward a discovery is to confirm what they already suspect and add nothing.

"They're places the surveyor *couldn't draw*. Not wouldn't. Couldn't. Because the geometry of the space wasn't stable."

I said nothing. She looked at me, and I saw her decide I was a helpful but essentially ignorant librarian, which was exactly what I wanted her to decide.

Over the next two weeks, Sable became, like Lior, a regular. Unlike Lior, she did not come for the reading room. She came for the archive, and for me—or rather, for the curated version of me that answered her questions with just enough knowledge to seem unusually well-read and not enough to seem suspicious. I introduced her to Lior on the second Thursday, which I did as though it were an afterthought, which it was not.

What Sable did, professionally, was this: she mapped what she called love-places. She used the term with neither embarrassment nor irony, which I admired. She meant it technically. Love-places were spaces that existed only in the relational field between two specific people—rooms, corridors, enclosures that had no objective location, that could not be found by a solitary searcher, that materialized only when two particular individuals crossed a threshold together. They were not metaphorical. They were *architectural.* They had walls and floors and sometimes ceilings. They could be measured, if you could get inside them. They could be drawn.

Sable explained this to Lior on a Saturday afternoon while I shelved nearby and did not listen too carefully, which is a skill I have perfected. She had her notebook open on the reading table—a thing of beauty, that notebook, hand-bound in a cover of dark bookcloth with a spine reinforced in the Japanese stab-binding style, its pages dense with a notation system Sable had invented herself: a shorthand for mapping paradoxical geometries, part technical drawing and part musical score, with symbols for spatial recursion that looked like treble clefs turned inside out and a marking she used for thresholds that resembled, deliberately or not, the symbol for infinity standing on its side. The pencils were Palomino Blackwings, the ones with the soft graphite, sharpened to a point so precise it seemed aggressive.

"The first one I want to show you," Sable said, "is a bedroom."

She turned the notebook so Lior could see. The drawing looked like an Escher staircase that had been taught to feel—a room mapped from the inside, its dimensions annotated, its impossibility rendered in clean graphite lines with the serene confidence of a blueprint.

"The door opens into itself one hour earlier," Sable said. She spoke about this the way a field biologist might describe a species that violated every known principle of evolutionary development and was, nevertheless, undeniably alive. Professional precision and genuine wonder, cohabiting. "When you enter, you encounter the room as it was—*as it will have been*—one hour before your arrival. Which includes the moment of deciding to arrive. So you enter the room and find yourself already in the process of deciding to enter it. The recursion is finite—it stabilizes after three or four iterations, depending on the individuals—but while it's active, you are, spatially, inside your own decision. Inside the act of choosing to be intimate. You can look at it. You can walk around it."

Lior leaned forward. I could see the fascination assemble itself across whichever face Lior was wearing that day—a quiet face, unremarkable, the kind I had come to think of as Lior's *listening face*, though Lior would not have recognized it as a recurring choice.

"What does that feel like?" Lior asked.

"It varies," Sable said. "Some people find it clarifying. Some people find it unbearable. The room doesn't care which. It's architecturally neutral—the recursion operates on spatial logic, not emotional logic. But the effect is that you cannot enter the room without confronting the fact that every act of intimacy retroactively revises the self that chose it. You are not the same person coming out as going in. Not because the room *changes* you—it doesn't—but because the room makes visible a change that was always already happening. The room is honest. That's all."

"That's all," Lior repeated, and smiled, and the smile was not any face's smile but somehow all of them, a thing that happened behind the features rather than with them.

"I'd like to take you there," Sable said. "Both of you." She glanced at me. "If Maren is willing."

"Oh, I'm not the one to go," I said. "Bad knees. Worse sense of direction. I'd get turned around in the recursion and end up shelving books that haven't been written yet."

They laughed. I laughed. It was a good line. I had written it four months ago and saved it for this moment with the precision of a chess player who sees the board eleven moves deep, though I want to be clear that I do not think of Lior or Sable as pieces, or of what I was doing as a game. The metaphor fails because in chess both players can see the board, and in this case only I could see it, and what was on it was not a strategy but a building—rooms I had spent a very long time designing, for a person I had spent an even longer time watching, and the game, if it was a game, was against something I do not have a name for, except the name I gave it in the rooms themselves, which is: the geometry of refusal.

"Just the two of you," I said. "Sable can draw. Lior can look. I'll keep the lights on here."
