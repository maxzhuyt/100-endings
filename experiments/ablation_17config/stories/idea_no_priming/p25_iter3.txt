# The Beautiful Conspiracy of Surfaces

## I.

The luncheon was Gerald's idea, which meant the wine would be adequate, the seating chart would be passive-aggressive, and at some point before the cheese course Gerald would find a way to mention that he had been long-listed for the Wolfson Prize. These were the certainties. I clung to them the way one clings to the handrail of a familiar staircase: not because the descent is dangerous but because the hand knows the shape and is comforted.

Margaux was already seated when I arrived, halfway down the long table in the Senior Common Room, between Peter Babcock from Medieval History and a visiting lecturer from Warwick whose name I had been told twice and would never retain. The room was doing what it always did in late October: catching the last of the river light through the leaded windows and distributing it unevenly, so that some faces were gilded and others merely grey, and the effect was of a painting in which the artist had favorites. Margaux was gilded. She had a way of sitting in light that seemed collaborative, as though she and the photons had worked it out in advance.

"You're late," she said, not looking up. She was buttering bread with the focus of a woman defusing a bomb. "Peter's been telling me about his gallstones."

"I have *not*," said Peter Babcock, who absolutely had.

I sat down across from her, next to Gerald's wife, Lin, who smiled at me with the compressed warmth of someone who has been smiling at people since half past eleven and is running low on material. Lin taught in the Education faculty and had the particular exhaustion of a woman who spent her professional life explaining things she believed in to people who did not, and then came home to a man who explained things he did not believe in to everyone. I liked Lin. Liking Lin was one of the quiet moral achievements I allowed myself.

"Pell," Gerald said from the head of the table, as though announcing me to a room that might not have noticed an adult woman sitting down and pulling in her chair. "We were just discussing the new workload allocation model."

"Riveting," I said. "Don't let me interrupt."

"No one was discussing the new workload allocation model," said Margaux.

"I was about to," Gerald said.

"Gerald, the last time you discussed the workload allocation model, Peter developed gallstones," Margaux said, and Peter Babcock made a sound that was either a laugh or a medical event, and the table relaxed into its customary shape: the shape of people who have known each other too long to be interesting and not long enough to be honest.

I should describe the room, because the room mattered, or would come to matter, or perhaps had always mattered and I had simply been too settled in it to notice. The Senior Common Room at Dunstan College is not beautiful in the way that the older colleges are beautiful — it has no hammer-beam ceiling, no portraits of men who built empires from the labor of people whose names are not on the portraits. It is a 1960s addition, low-ceilinged, with wood paneling that aspires to warmth and achieves the aesthetic of a regional bank branch. But the windows face the river, and in autumn the light enters at an angle that makes the paneling briefly gorgeous, and the long table — solid oak, donated by a benefactor whose name is on a small brass plaque that everyone sits near and no one reads — has the particular authority of a surface where enough wine has been spilled and enough reputations have been gently dismantled that the wood itself seems to carry a charge. We were sixteen that day. I remember because I counted, which is something I do without deciding to, the way some people unconsciously track the exits in a room. Sixteen bodies, sixteen weather systems, sixteen performances of a self suited to the occasion.

Gerald's smile, when he talked about the Wolfson long list — which he did, as prophesied, before the cheese — had the particular width of a man performing happiness for a room he plans to leave. I don't mean he planned to leave the room. I mean the expression had the quality of rehearsal, of someone who has practiced delight in a mirror and is now delivering it to an audience whose opinion he simultaneously craves and resents. Lin, beside me, ate her bread quietly and studied the grain of the table with the attention of a woman who has memorized all the patterns her husband's face makes and is looking for something, anything, that she has not yet learned to predict.

Across the table, the visiting lecturer from Warwick was telling Margaux about a conference in Berlin, and Margaux was listening with the generous, slightly tilted focus she brought to all conversations, the look that made people feel not merely heard but *curated*, as though their words were being arranged in a private museum where the lighting was superb. Margaux was fifty-one. I was fifty-three. We had been friends for fourteen years, which in academic terms is either a geological era or the blink of an eye, depending on whether you measure by publications or by the slow accumulation of evenings in each other's kitchens where the wine outlasted the conversation and the silence that followed was not empty but furnished. She was the person in my life to whom I had come closest to telling the truth, which meant she was the person from whom I had withheld it most carefully, because you cannot almost-tell someone something for fourteen years without the not-telling becoming a kind of architecture, a load-bearing wall you have decorated so thoroughly that no one, including yourself, remembers it is a wall.

She was in the middle of a sentence — something about a paper she was reviewing, a mildly funny observation about the author's habit of over-citing themselves — when it happened.

It was tiny. I need you to understand how tiny it was. If I had not spent thirty years learning the surfaces of people's faces with the desperate attention of a woman who once did not need to look because she could simply feel, I would have missed it. Peter Babcock, six inches from her left elbow, missed it. The visiting lecturer, looking directly at her, missed it.

Her face seized. Not visibly, not in any way that the muscles announced. Behind the eyes. A flinch in the architecture of her attention, as though something had struck her from inside and she had caught it before it reached her expression, the way you might catch a glass at the edge of a table — so fast that the act of catching it erases the evidence that it was falling. A half-second, maybe less. Then the social mask re-sealed, seamless as water closing over a dropped stone, and she finished her sentence, and laughed at the visiting lecturer's response, and reached across the table for the bread basket with the ordinary grace of a woman whose hands were not, I was suddenly certain, entirely steady.

No one noticed anything. This is not because the people at the table were unobservant. Gerald was, admittedly, unobservant, but Lin was acute, and Peter Babcock had the quietly watchful quality of a man who has survived forty years in academic administration by tracking micro-expressions the way a prairie dog tracks shadows. They noticed nothing because there was nothing, on the visible surface, to notice. The conspiracy of surfaces held. Margaux passed the bread. Gerald poured more wine. The river light moved an inch to the left and the visiting lecturer from Warwick was no longer gilded but merely present, and the conversation turned to parking, and the afternoon continued as though the world had not, for one half-second, cracked.

I ate my cheese. I laughed at the right moments. I was, from the outside, a woman enjoying a Tuesday.

After lunch I caught up with Margaux on the path along the river, where the college's ambitions meet the city's indifference and the result is a strip of gravel between institutional hedgerows and brown water that generations of students have romanticized and generations of maintenance staff have cursed. The willows were dropping their last leaves onto the surface of the Cam, and the effect was of a river being slowly dressed in something gold and ruined.

"Walk?" I said.

"Mm."

We walked. Margaux was quiet, which was not unusual — she had silences that were companionable and silences that were working, and I had learned to distinguish them the way a sailor distinguishes swells. This was neither. This was a third kind, one I had not catalogued, and its unfamiliarity was, I found, a small cold thing lodged beneath my diaphragm.

"Decent lunch," I said, because I am, when afraid, a woman of staggering banality.

"Gerald looked terrible," Margaux said. "Have you noticed? He's lost weight. Not in a healthy way."

"Gerald's always looked like that."

"No," Margaux said. "He hasn't." Then, in a tone I can only describe as puzzled — not gossipy, not shrewd, but genuinely confused, as though she had come across an unexpected result in a familiar equation: "He doesn't actually like his wife anymore, does he."

It was not a question. It was delivered the way you might report a road sign — *A14 Northbound, 3 miles* — a plain fact encountered in transit, mildly surprising only in its location.

"I think they're fine," I said. I sounded, to my own ears, like a woman reading from a script she had not written and did not believe. "They've been married thirty years. It's just the texture of —"

"No," Margaux said again. She stopped walking. She stood on the gravel path and looked at the river, and the river, as rivers do, looked back at nothing. "It's not texture. It's — Never mind."

"Are *you* all right?" I asked, and the question came out more carefully than I intended, more loaded, and Margaux heard the weight and turned to look at me and for one terrible moment I thought she was going to tell me something I was not ready to receive.

"Tired," she said. "That's all. Bad week for sleep."

"Duncan snoring?"

"Duncan is always snoring. No. Just — too much in my head."

"Join the club," I said, and hated myself, and we walked back, and the willows dropped their gold onto the water, and I said goodbye at the Porter's Lodge, and I went home.

My kitchen is a small room at the back of a small house on a terraced street that the estate agents call "the quiet end of Romsey Town," which means it is a street where the houses are slightly too close together and the gardens are slightly too small and the residents have achieved the particular détente of people who can hear each other's televisions through the walls and have tacitly agreed never to mention it. I have lived alone in this house for eleven years, since David left — politely, gradually, with the kind of extended withdrawal that made the actual departure almost redundant, the way the last guest at a party who has been edging toward the door for an hour finally steps through it and everyone realizes they had already been speaking as if the room were emptied. David and I had been married for twelve years. He was a good man. He could not have articulated what was wrong, and I could not tell him, and so we lived inside that mutual silence until it calcified into something that looked, from the outside, like mere incompatibility.

I stood in the kitchen. I did not turn on the light. The window above the sink faced the garden, where the October dark was settling with the unremarkable gravity of an English evening, and the last of the day lay in a thin orange line above the fence, and I stood in the dimness and held the edge of the counter and ran through the list.

She's overworked.

She's having a marital crisis.

She's depressed. She's perimenopausal. She's picked up a virus. She's not sleeping well and the not-sleeping is making everything louder, more acute, the way exhaustion strips the insulation from your nerves and suddenly the world is all signal, no buffer.

She's stressed. She's drinking too much. She's anxious about the REF. She had a weird moment at lunch. Everyone has weird moments at lunch. I have weird moments at lunch. It doesn't mean anything. The thing I am afraid it means is so statistically improbable, so unlikely, so specifically and personally terrifying that the fear itself is evidence of my own pathology, not hers. I am projecting. I am an aging woman with a long-suppressed secret, and I am seeing my own ghost in someone else's face, and the responsible thing — the sane thing — is to let it go.

I let the list unspool. I tested each explanation. I held them up to the light the way you hold a banknote up to check the watermark.

None of them held.

I knew what I had seen. I had seen it once before, thirty years ago, in a mirror in a rented bathroom in Lyon, on a face that was my own and was, in that moment, barely recognizable, because behind the eyes something vast and uninvited had moved in and was rearranging the furniture.

I stood in the kitchen for a long time. Outside, someone's cat walked along the fence with the unhurried authority of a creature that has never, not once, in its entire evolutionary history, been required to pretend it cannot hear what it hears.

## II.

The next two weeks had the quality of a time-lapse film of a house settling on bad foundations. You could not, on any given day, point to the moment of collapse. You could only line up the days and see that the floor was, by degrees, no longer level.

Monday: Margaux cancelled her afternoon seminar. This was unprecedented. In fourteen years I had known her to teach through migraines, a fractured wrist, the morning after her mother's funeral. She emailed her students forty minutes before the session with a one-line apology — *unwell, rescheduled to Thursday* — and when I texted to ask if she needed anything, the three moving dots appeared, held for eleven seconds, and then vanished. No reply came. An hour later she sent a photograph of her cat sitting in a cardboard box, no caption. I understood this to be a communication in a language that bypassed the one in which she would have to explain.

Wednesday: I saw her in the English Faculty library, standing at the shelves with an open book she was not reading. She was wearing the same jumper she'd worn on Monday. Her hair was unwashed. These details may seem trivial. They are not. Margaux was not a vain woman, but she had a relationship with her own appearance that was orderly, intentional, the way some people have a relationship with their desk: not because it matters in itself but because the order is a signal to the self that the self is in charge. The jumper was a message. She looked up when I approached and smiled, and the smile was perfect, and the perfection was wrong, because a real smile from Margaux had imperfections — an asymmetric pull at the left corner, a slight bunching around the eyes that made her look momentarily, gorgeously, like a woman who had been caught doing something she enjoyed. This smile was bilateral, even, and precisely calibrated, which meant it was constructed, which meant she was monitoring her own face with the kind of attention you only bring to a performance you are afraid of failing.

"You look well," she said, and the sentence had a strange emphasis on *you*, as though she were drawing a contrast.

"You look like you haven't slept," I said.

"Sleep is bourgeois," she said, and turned back to the book, and I let her, because I am a coward, and the particular shape of my cowardice is that it looks, from the outside, like tact.

Thursday: She taught the rescheduled seminar. I know this because James Hargrove, a colleague in her department who relays information the way a municipal drain relays stormwater — copiously, indiscriminately, with no awareness of its own function — mentioned in the corridor that Margaux's class had been "extraordinary, really extraordinary, she practically took the poem apart molecule by molecule." His face, as he said it, wore the expression of a man who has witnessed something he suspects he should admire but does not fully understand, and who is compensating for his confusion with enthusiasm. I asked what poem. "Bishop," he said. "'In the Waiting Room.' She spent forty minutes on six lines. Students were riveted. She was — I don't know how to put it — it was as if she already knew what they were going to say before they said it."

I carried that sentence home with me. I put it in the kitchen with the others. It joined the inventory of things I was not yet willing to assemble into a shape I recognized.

Friday: nothing. Margaux was nowhere. Not in her office, not answering texts, not at the coffee shop on King Street where we went on Fridays when the week had been long and the world had been its usual self. I walked past her house. The curtains were drawn. The car was in the drive.

Saturday: Duncan called me.
