Everything you have just read is a surface. Step closer and the ink lifts off the page like a skin, and beneath it is another page, and beneath that another, and they go down a very long way, and at the bottom of them all is a woman breathing.

Not a POLYANA. Not an asset. Not a confirmed anomalous cognition capability. A woman, fifty-seven years old, seated on a metal chair bolted to a concrete floor in Detention Unit 3 of the United Nations International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia, Scheveningen district, The Hague, the Netherlands, on the eleventh morning of a silence that has begun to frighten even the guards, who have seen many kinds of silence and can usually distinguish the tactical from the broken, but cannot classify hers, because it is neither. The fluorescent light above her head has been buzzing at 50 hertz since she arrived, a frequency that happens to fall precisely within the range of the Schumann resonance, which is the electromagnetic heartbeat of the cavity between the Earth's surface and its ionosphere, which is to say that the light fixture in this cell hums at the same frequency as the planet thinking, and she can hear both, and has always been able to hear both, and that is why she is here, and that is why she has not spoken, and that is why, as of nine minutes ago, the investigator across from her — Jan-Willem Brouwer, a Dutchman, forty-three, who had been a public prosecutor in Rotterdam before joining the Office of the Prosecutor, who has a daughter aged seven whose name is Lotte and whose face he pictures every evening as a way of emptying himself after the day's testimonies, and whose left knee aches when it rains because he tore his meniscus playing football at university in Leiden in 1983, all of which Yevgenia knows without having asked, without wanting to know, the way one knows the temperature of the air against one's skin — has set his pen down and simply looked at her.

She is both the witness the prosecution needs and the accused the prosecution has charged.

This is not a contradiction. It is the entire problem.

Brouwer's recorder was running. It had been running for eleven days, capturing nothing but the ventilation system and, beneath that, at the very margin of audibility, the sound of her breathing — slow, measured, a woman conserving something, though he did not know what. He would not know what until much later, if he ever did. The recorder's red light stared at her like a small dull eye. It would capture what came next. What it would not capture, because no magnetic medium yet devised can encode it, is what was happening inside the silence: the vast, crowded, sedimented architecture of every mind she had ever entered, turning and murmuring in the dark of her skull, one hundred and seventeen distinct human interiors — their fears, their evasions, their private acts of beauty and degradation — pressed together like pages in a book that had been soaked and left to dry into a single fused mass, so that opening to any one of them tore the others.

One hundred and seventeen. She had stopped counting at some point in the late 1980s, when counting became indistinguishable from cataloguing the dead. Then, in 1993, in a place whose name she cannot say without her hands beginning to shake, she had started counting again, because the precision of numbers was the last architecture she could build that was entirely her own.

Brouwer shifted in his chair. The recorder turned. The fluorescent light hummed at the frequency of the planet.

Yevgenia Morozova breathed.

## II.

---

On the twenty-third of June, 1971, at 04:17 Moscow time, an array of fluxgate magnetometers at the Lovozero Geophysical Observatory on the Kola Peninsula, 150 kilometers south of Murmansk, recorded an anomalous dip in the horizontal component of Earth's magnetic field.

The dip lasted eleven seconds.

Senior technician Pyotr Afanasievich Koltsov, who had been monitoring the instruments through a night shift that had yielded nothing of note beyond a brief substorm at 02:40, noted the event in his logbook with the measured handwriting of a man who had been recording geomagnetic variations for fourteen years and had learned to suppress excitement, because in geophysics, as in certain other disciplines, what appears anomalous at first glance usually resolves into instrumentation error upon review. He wrote: *04:17 — Anomalous H-component depression, magnitude approximately 120 nT below baseline, duration ~11 sec. No corresponding D or Z variation observed. No associated substorm activity. Flagged for review.*

He then drank his tea, which had gone cold.

What Koltsov did not know — what no one would assemble into a coherent picture for nearly three decades, until a German geophysicist named Ullrich published a comprehensive retrospective analysis in *Nature* in 1999 — was that the Lovozero event was not local. At the same moment, plus or minus a margin attributable to clock imprecision, similar anomalous dips had been recorded at observatories in Sodankylä, Finland; Lerwick, Scotland; Fredericksburg, Virginia; Alibag, India; and Hermanus, South Africa. Six stations, spanning four continents, each registering a brief, simultaneous weakening in the magnetic field — not in its local variations but in its deep structure, the dipole itself, the great invisible architecture that extends from the planet's molten iron core outward into space, where it meets the solar wind and deflects it, and has done so for 3.4 billion years, and without which the atmosphere would have been stripped away and nothing that breathes would exist.

The field had faltered.

For eleven seconds, the shield that separates the interior of the Earth from the exterior of the universe had thinned, as if the planet had forgotten, momentarily, to maintain the boundary between itself and everything else.

Simultaneously — because the omniscient voice can hold simultaneity in a way that no single human perspective can, and because this simultaneity is the point, is the very engine of what happened, is the thing the documents and the tribunals will spend decades failing to contain — simultaneously, ten degrees of latitude south, in a communal apartment on Vasilyevsky Island in Leningrad, in a room shared with two other families where the wallpaper was the color of weak tea and the pipes knocked like a fist against the wall at unpredictable intervals, fourteen-year-old Yevgenia Arkadyevna Morozova woke, turned toward her mother sleeping beside her, and heard.

Not with her ears.

It came as pressure — a second pulse appearing inside her skull, syncopated against her own, carrying with it not words but the architecture of another person's inner life: images arriving at a rate and density that no language could transliterate, that could only be experienced as a kind of drowning.

She saw:

The Nevsky Prospekt in winter, but not the winter she knew. A different winter. THE winter. January 1942. Snow not white but gray with ash from the fires that burned in the buildings no one could save because there was no one to save them because the people were dying at a rate of — no, don't count, don't count — and the avenue empty except for figures that moved like damaged machinery, pulling sleds, and on the sleds, wrapped in sheets or sometimes not wrapped in anything, bodies, stacked with the practiced efficiency of people who had done this many times, and the sound the sled runners made on the packed snow was a sound like a long exhalation, and her mother — Darya Konstantinovna, factory worker, born 1929, who had been twelve years old during the siege and was now forty-two and had told Yevgenia nothing, absolutely nothing, had maintained a silence so complete it functioned as a second wall around the apartment — her mother had pulled one of those sleds.

Yevgenia saw what was on it.

A girl. Smaller than Yevgenia. Seven, perhaps, or six — it was hard to tell because the body had been diminished by months of starvation into something that looked both older and younger than any age. Wrapped in a coat that had belonged to an adult. Eyes closed. Irina. Her name was Irina. Yevgenia knew this not because anyone said it but because the name existed in her mother's mind the way a nail exists in a wall after the picture has been removed: a point of damage, permanent, marking the shape of what had hung there.

Irina. Her mother's sister.

And now the images came faster, tumbling over and into each other like water through a broken wall, and Yevgenia could not slow them and could not stop them and could not close anything because there was nothing to close — there was no door, there was no mechanism, there was just an open channel where eleven seconds ago there had been the ordinary silence of a sleeping room:

The taste of joiner's glue dissolved in hot water, which had a sweetness to it, an almost floral sweetness that was the cruelest thing about it because it made the body believe food was coming.

The weight of wallpaper paste on the tongue, the specific consistency of it, like cold porridge made from nothing, which her mother had scraped from the walls of an apartment whose occupants were already dead and boiled for forty minutes and eaten with a spoon from a chipped blue bowl that still existed somewhere in this apartment, that might be the very bowl sitting at this moment on the shelf above the stove.

The sound of the metronome broadcast on Leningrad Radio — fifty beats per minute during quiet times, one hundred fifty during shelling — which her mother still heard in any repetitive sound, the ticking of clocks, the dripping of faucets, the pulse of blood in her own ears at night, so that her entire auditory world was underlaid with the rhythm of a city counting down to its own annihilation.

The faces of the dead in the street who were no longer the dead because they had been the dead for so long they had become the landscape, the topography, the texture of daily life, and Darya Konstantinovna, twelve years old, had walked past them on her way to fetch water from the Neva, which was frozen solid, and had stepped over a man whose face she could still describe if anyone ever asked, which no one had, not once, not in thirty years.

And Irina again. Irina on the sled. The weight of her. How light she was. How the lightness was the worst part, because a child should not be that light, a child that light was no longer entirely a child, was already half-translated into something else, and Darya pulling the sled toward the cemetery that was no longer a cemetery but a trench, and lowering Irina in, and the sound the frozen earth made when the men with shovels began to cover her, a sound like —

Yevgenia screamed.

She screamed the way the body screams when a limb is broken — involuntarily, absolutely, the scream preceding any decision to scream by a margin that revealed the decision to be an illusion.
