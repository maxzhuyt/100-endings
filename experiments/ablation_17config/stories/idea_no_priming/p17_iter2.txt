# The Underside of Every Spoken Thing

## I.

The gas station on Parish Road 14 has two prices for diesel and one of them is posted. The clerk, Renée Thibodaux, née Arceneaux, does not think of herself as someone who adjusts. She thinks of herself as someone who *assesses*, and the assessment runs without interruption beneath her customer-service smile, a ticker tape of actuarial notation that no one has ever heard and that goes, in its full internal richness, something like: *White truck, white man, Carhartt jacket with the cuffs frayed which means working but not earning, which means he'll price-check so give him the real number. Black truck, Black man, polo shirt tucked in which means he thinks presentation earns him something so let him think it earned him the posted price. Silver sedan, woman, doesn't matter what color because women don't argue the pump, they argue the receipt, and by then I've already made the drawer right.* Renée has a daughter in the eighth grade at Sacred Heart who scored in the ninety-third percentile in mathematics, and Renée considers this genetic.

Three-tenths of a mile south, Deacon Arthur Melancon is driving past the house of Mrs. Eulalie Broussard, who has been dying of pancreatic cancer since August, and his grief is genuine in the sense that he is genuinely producing it: manufacturing it breath by breath the way a man might warm up his voice before singing, because he will need it ready when the time comes, when he stands at the reception after the funeral with a plate of boudin and offers his condolences to the sons who live in Houston and do not want a half-acre lot in an unincorporated parish that floods in any storm with a woman's name. He has already spoken to a surveyor. The grief is not dishonest. He will miss Eulalie, her roses, the roses especially, which he plans to keep.

On Rue Theriot, in the double-wide with the Virgin Mary birdbath that lists to port in the subsiding lawn, Dana Guidry holds her four-year-old on her lap and thinks: *sixteen years, four months, and approximately eleven days until this one is someone else's monthly calculation.* The child is asking about clouds. Dana is performing the maternal tilt of the head, the soft focus, the voice she has learned from other women who seem to mean it, and beneath the performance she is running the cost of the child against the cost of the years the child has replaced, the years she would have had, the body she would have kept, and the math does not come out. It has never come out. The child says the cloud looks like a rabbit and Dana says, "It sure does, baby," and inside the sentence the word *baby* lands on something genuine, some vestigial warmth, and the warmth makes the math worse because it means she cannot purely resent, cannot cleanly cut, must instead live in the scrim between performed love and actual love, which is where most people in this parish live most of the time without anyone hearing a thing.

At the Piggly Wiggly on Landry Avenue, the butcher is wrapping pork chops for the school principal's wife while internally composing a letter to the woman who cleans the school principal's house, a letter he will never write and never send, a letter that is not romantic but *territorial*, a long and strangely legalistic document asserting prior claim on her attention, because the butcher believes that his seven years of silent longing constitute a form of deed — sweat equity in an emotional property he has never once mentioned to the woman in question, who does not know his first name. The butcher cuts the pork chops with the precision of an argument he is always winning against an adversary who has not agreed to the contest.

Two streets over, at the Tuesday evening meeting of the Waterways Reclamation Advisory Board, the chair is delivering a presentation on culvert capacity while thinking, with the fluency of long practice: *The Tran property floods first because the Tran property was sold the lot that floods first, and the lot that floods first was sold to whoever would buy it without asking the right questions, and the people who don't ask the right questions are the people who weren't told which questions are right, and the people who weren't told are the people whose last names don't appear on the streets, and the streets were named by the people who sold the lots, and that's not history, that's just hydrology, and hydrology isn't anyone's fault.* He clicks to the next slide.

In the rectory of St. Anne's, Father Castille is preparing his homily on the Good Samaritan while considering, with the weighing motions of a man sorting mail, which parishioners' sons have been arrested this quarter and whether arrest correlates more strongly with family surname or with the location of the family property relative to the refinery, and whether the correlation, if he were to mention it from the pulpit, would cost him the Melancon tithe or the Thibodaux tithe, and whether the Gospel can be applied with enough generality to satisfy his conscience and enough vagueness to protect his operating budget, and he decides it can, because it usually can, because the whole machinery of parish Catholicism in this part of the state is built on the grammatical technology of applying sacred specificity in secular generalities, a translation that loses the meaning but keeps the music, and Father Castille has a beautiful singing voice and knows it.

And under all of it — beneath Renée's ticker tape and Arthur's rehearsed sorrow and Dana's bifurcated tenderness and the butcher's silent deed and the advisory chair's hydrological absolution and the priest's budgetary Gospel — under all of it runs a hum, or not a hum exactly but an *upwelling*, a pressure from below, as if the silted waterways and the subsiding ground and the buriedGeography of plantation boundary lines still vibrating at their original frequencies were producing a sound that is not sound, a broadcast that is not broadcast, a field of involuntary transmission that blankets the parish the way humidity blankets it: total, indifferent, and so constant that it has become architectural, a feature of the landscape as fundamental as the water table, which is also rising, which is also no one's fault, which is also killing everything slowly and from below.

Two people in this parish hear all of it. All the time. Every unvoiced calculation, every performed grief, every silent deed, every hydrological alibi. They hear it the way other people hear traffic — not each individual engine but the *roar*, the aggregate, the full-spectrum wash of human interiority unfiltered by the mercy of skin and skull.

Their names are Margaux and Colm Deshotel. The surname appears in parish records as far back as the records go, which in this part of Louisiana means as far back as the French thought it was worth writing things down, and the Deshotel entry always contains the same quiet notation, in different handwriting across different centuries, that translates roughly as *the ones who hear.* A gift, in the early records. A condition, in the later ones. A diagnostic category in no medical literature and a subject of no clinical study, because the Deshotels have always known what everyone else is thinking, and what everyone else is thinking has always included what should be done with people who are different, and the Deshotels listened, and stayed quiet.

They are the last. The bloodline, such as it is — not a bloodline in the aristocratic sense but in the hydrological one, a channel carved by repetition, a path the water takes because it has always taken it — ends with them. Margaux is thirty-nine. Colm is forty-one. Between them they have no children, no heirs, no one to inherit the frequency. The narrator notes this the way the parish clerk notes the end of a survey line: without sentiment, with precision, as a boundary that has been reached.

What happens after the boundary is the subject of this account.

## II.

They met at a fish fry.

Specifically: the Friday evening Lenten fish fry at St. Anne's parish hall, March 2005, the one where the Knights of Columbus set up the folding tables too close to the industrial fryer and Buddy Comeaux's wife burned her forearm on the splatter guard and spent the rest of the evening holding a napkin of ice against the wound while, internally, cataloging every time Buddy had failed to protect her from things, the burn becoming a synecdoche for a marriage she described to herself as being *left near the heat*, and Margaux, who was twenty-one and leaning against the cinder-block wall with a paper plate of fried catfish she couldn't eat, heard every word of it.

She heard everything that evening. She always heard everything. There was no volume knob and there had never been one. Her grandmother, the last Deshotel before her who had carried the frequency, had told her when she was nine years old that the hearing was like the weather: you didn't adjust it, you dressed for it. Her grandmother had dressed for it with Communion wine and silence. Margaux dressed for it with distance. She attended required events and stood at their margins and let the noise wash over her like a woman standing in surf — not fighting the waves, not enjoying them, just keeping her footing — and she went home after and sat in the bathtub with the water running because running water was the closest thing to quiet she had found.

Colm Deshotel was her third cousin. In a parish where everyone is someone's cousin, this was not remarkable. What was remarkable was that he existed at all. Margaux had known, abstractly, that her grandmother had mentioned others — *the ones who hear* — but she had understood this as history, as the kind of thing old women said about old things. She had never met another telepath. She had assumed, in the way that isolated people assume, that her isolation was a category of one.

Colm had driven down from a rig job in Terrebonne Parish for his mother's birthday. He'd attended the fish fry out of obligation and was sitting at the end of a folding table, eating nothing, drinking a Bud Light with the focus of a man trying to make a single beer last the length of a social contract. He was thinking about the way light moved on standing water at dusk — not the sunset colors, which were a postcard cliché, but the texture of the light itself, the way it thickened near the surface, as if photons moved slower through the last inch of air above a bayou, as if the water was exerting a drag on what was visible. It was an idle thought, the kind of thing that filled his mind when he was trying to drown out the roar. A private hobby. A mental place to stand.

Margaux was fourteen feet away when the noise changed.

It did not stop. This is important. The accounts that the Deshotel family tell themselves — the mythology that accrues around moments like this — always want silence, always want the sudden absence of all that unwanted sound, because silence would be clean and dramatic and would make for a better story. But what happened was not silence. What happened was that a new signal entered Margaux's field, and the signal was not quiet but *clear*. A single mind thinking about the weight of light on water, and the thought had no second layer. No subterranean calculation. No performance calculus. No difference between its surface and its interior. It was, in the language of a later century's acoustics, an uncompressed signal: what it sounded like was what it was.

She turned her head.

He was already looking at her.

What he had heard from her direction — later, he would try to describe it to her, and the description would fail the way descriptions of relief always fail, because relief is defined by what it is not, and what it is not is everything that preceded it — was a melody. Not a melody she was remembering or performing but one she was *building*, unconsciously, the way a person might fidget with a piece of wire, bending it and unbending it into shapes that mean nothing and require everything. The melody had no audience. It was not for anyone. It was the sound of a mind keeping itself company.

He heard it and his chest did something that was not his heart and was not his lungs and was not any organ he could name. An opening. A dilation. As if a muscle he had been clenching since birth — since the first time he'd heard a thought that was not his and understood that the world was not what it appeared — had released, and the release was so total that for a moment he could not breathe, because he had been breathing around the clench for so long that he did not know how to breathe without it.

She crossed the room.

He stood up.

And the narrator, who has access to both their minds and to the long Deshotel record and to the precise mechanics of what happened next, must tell you that what passed between them in the first thirty seconds was not introduction, not greeting, not any of the conventional choreography of two people meeting at a church fish fry. What passed between them was the experience of hearing a mind that did not hurt to hear. And the experience was so unprecedented, so completely outside the frame of anything either of them had believed possible, that it registered not as emotion but as *physics* — a change in the properties of the medium they lived in, as if the air had become a different gas, one that could be breathed without effort.

"I can hear you," Margaux said, out loud, which was redundant.

"I know," Colm said.

And the thing that he was thinking, which she could hear, was: *So this is what it was supposed to feel like.* And the thing that she was thinking, which he could hear, was the melody, still building, except now it had a listener, and the knowledge of being listened to — of being heard without being *decoded*, without the hearing carrying the weight of judgment or use — changed the melody the way sunlight changes a room: not by adding furniture but by making visible what was already there.

They did not kiss at the fish fry. They did not touch. They stood three feet apart and breathed each other's uncompressed signals and around them the parish roared on in its full-spectrum hypocrisy — Buddy's wife cataloging failures, the Knights of Columbus treasurer skimming the crawfish fund, Father Castille calculating the per-head donation yield of the evening — and none of it went away, but it became, for the first time in either of their lives, *background*. Not silence. Not absence. But a figure-ground reversal, as if the noise had been demoted from the thing they lived inside to the thing that lived outside them, and in the space between their two minds there was something that could be called, without irony, a room.

They went home separately that night. Margaux lay in her bathtub with the water running and for the first time did not need it. She could still hear the parish — every mind within range, the same rosary of petty calculations and performed goodness — but she could also hear, beneath it, or through it, or woven into it at a frequency that didn't compete, the fading signal of Colm's mind as he drove north on Route 182, the light-on-water thought gradually replaced by something she had never encountered in another person's broadcast: *the thought of her*. Not a fantasy. Not an acquisition plan. Just the persistent, undecorated awareness that she existed, that he had stood near her, that the standing-near had been — the word that surfaced in his mind, and he did not edit it, and she heard it unedited — *inherited*. As if their bodies had recognized each other the way the bayou recognized rain. As if they had been *designed* for this convergence, two channels cut by the same ancient hydrology, and the meeting was not accident but completion of a circuit that had been waiting, *fitted* and ready, for as long as the Deshotel line had carried the frequency.

She did not question the word. She was too grateful to question it.

The narrator questions it now.

Within a year they were living together in the house on Rue Sabine that had been Margaux's grandmother's and her grandmother's before that — a raised Creole cottage with cypress beams and a porch that faced the subsidence rather than the road, as if the house had opinions about what was worth looking at. The house had been built by Deshotels in 1923 and had been, according to the family's informal history, the only structure in the parish where two people could sit together and hear nothing but each other.

This was not metaphor. The house was not insulated or enchanted or blessed. The hearing did not stop at its walls. But within the house, with only each other's minds in close range, the signal-to-noise ratio shifted so dramatically that the outer broadcast — the parish, the gas station mathematics, the deacon's real estate grief — became a murmur, a distant weather, something that could be lived with the way people in New Orleans live with the knowledge that they are below sea level: acknowledged, accommodated, never fully escaped.

What remained, in the house, was each other.
