# The Lake of Every Tuesday

## I.

Asa kept his workshop the way other men kept churches — swept pine floor, tools hung on shadow-boards so each awl and calipers had its own silhouette waiting, the air smelling of bone-dust and brass filings and the particular mineral loneliness that accumulates in rooms where one person has been precise for too long. I am telling you about this workshop in the past tense because it is gone, but you should understand that when I say past tense I mean something different than you do. For you, the past is a country behind you. For me it is a lake I am standing in, water to my hips, and every Tuesday that ever happened or will happen is in the water with me, and I can feel the shape of each one against my skin. So when I say the workshop *was*, I also mean the workshop *is*, and the difference between those two words is just the direction I happen to be facing.

He hunched when he believed he was alone. This was the thing I loved first, or loved most, or loved in the way that survives when other loves erode — the particular curvature of his spine when no one was watching, like a parenthesis with nothing inside it. He was a tall man and a careful one. His hands knew the inside of a clock the way a midwife's hands know the inside of a woman: by feel and by faith and by a patience that looks, from the outside, very much like prayer. He had come to the Llano Estacado because it was flat and empty and the light was consistent and there was nothing between him and the horizon to interfere with his measurements. He was measuring something. He was always measuring something. I think now that what he was measuring was the distance between one moment and the next, and that the terrible thing was not that he found it, but that the distance turned out to be so small.

The contraption sat on his bench like something born rather than built. I will not call it a machine because machines have the decency to remain what they are. This was — I have tried, across every version of every telling, to find the right language — this was a wound. Something had opened in the room one day, some seam between what-was and what-would-be, and Asa had simply built a housing around it, the way you'd build a barn around a sinkhole. Gears, yes. Springs, yes. A pendulum of white bone that I did not ask the origin of. But the thing breathed. I don't mean this as metaphor. In the early mornings when the shop was cold I could see its exhalation, a faint mist that smelled of tomorrow — which, if you have never smelled it, smells like rain that hasn't decided where to fall. It ate yesterday and spat out tomorrow and what remained in between was a residue that collected on the windowsill like pollen from a flower that did not exist in any single season.

Dunstan was there too. I should tell you more about him. I should have watched him more carefully.

He swept the floors. He oiled the lathes without being asked. Once, when a gear cracked — a toothed wheel the size of a half-dollar, hand-cut by Asa from a piece of nickel silver — Dunstan sat down with his pocketknife and a scrap of brass and carved a replacement that fit so perfectly Asa held it up to the window and turned it in the light for a long time without speaking. Dunstan was young. His hair was the color of creek water in October. He had come from somewhere east and he did not talk about it and I did not look for it in the lake because there are faces you trust precisely because you have not seen all their versions, and this was my mistake, though I do not think the word *mistake* is large enough for what I mean.

I saw everything. I still missed what mattered.

He loved Asa. I am certain of this, even now, even after. He loved Asa the way a river loves the particular geology that gives it shape — completely, and with no awareness that the love and the erosion are the same thing.

## II.

I woke in the night and the house was wrong. Not a sound-wrong or a sight-wrong but a weight-wrong, as if gravity had shifted two degrees east while I slept and every object in the room had resettled into a version of itself that was almost but not quite identical. The quilt had a thread of red I did not remember. The moon through the window was in the right place but wore the wrong phase. These are the kinds of mistakes you notice when you carry every Tuesday inside your body like a stone.

Asa was standing in the workshop doorway. From behind, he was just a shape, a long dark absence against the slightly less dark absence of the room beyond. The bench was bare. The shadow-boards still held their silhouettes, but the tools were gone, or rearranged, or had never been the same tools in the first place. The wound on the bench — the contraption — was absent. The air where it had been was still faintly warm, the way a bed is warm after someone rises.

Dunstan was gone.

Asa did not shout. He did not saddle a horse. He walked to the kitchen table and sat down and put his head in his hands, and I stood in the doorway and saw — because I cannot stop seeing, because seeing is not something I do but something I *am* — that in one timeline he was weeping, the sound thin and mechanical like a clock that has been wound past its tolerance. In another timeline he was laughing, and the laughter was worse than the weeping because it meant he understood what had been taken and found the understanding funny the way a man falling from a cliff might find the view funny. In a third timeline his chair was empty and his body was in the workshop in a posture I will not describe and Dunstan was standing over him looking at his own hands as if he could not identify them as belonging to a person.

I poured coffee in the version where he was still alive enough to drink it. He wrapped his hands around the cup. The ceramic was white with a single chip on the rim, and I watched him turn the cup so the chip faced away from his mouth, and this small habitual gesture — this was what broke me. Not the theft. Not the rupture. The way his hands still knew how to hold a cup.

We sat without speaking. There was nothing to say that would have fit in language we both understood. His language ran on gears and escapements, cause followed by effect, the neat logic of a mainspring releasing stored intent. My language ran on water. We had been married for eleven years and this had always been the distance between us — not a distance of affection but of grammar, of the way each of us conjugated the verb *to happen.* For Asa, things happened *then.* For me, things happened *always.* We could not discuss the contraption any more than a clock could discuss the lake it had fallen into.

The next morning the creek behind our property had moved two hundred yards east. The old creekbed was dry, the stones still dark and wet-looking, like an animal that has just stopped breathing. The new creek ran in a channel that did not exist yesterday, cutting through soil that showed no sign of having been disturbed. It ran cold and clear and it did not know it had been moved.

Neither of us mentioned it.

## III.

Asa saddled the roan and rode west. I watched him from the porch until the land swallowed him, which on the Llano Estacado happens quickly — a man on horseback diminishes against that flatness the way a word diminishes at the end of a sentence, becoming smaller and then becoming grammar and then becoming silence. He took his revolver and a canteen and a coil of rope. He did not take food. This meant he believed he would find Dunstan quickly, or it meant he did not care about eating, or it meant he had forgotten that hunger was something that applied to him. All three were true, layered on his departure like three transparent maps of the same territory.

I did not follow. Not yet.

Instead I stood in the lake of every Tuesday and watched him arrive at a town I had not seen before, which is how I knew Dunstan had already been at work — the town was new and it was old and it was neither, it sat on the caliche flat like a word that had been erased and rewritten over itself so many times the paper had gone thin and soft and you could feel every version with your fingertip. Let me call it Caliche. In one founding it was called Perdition. In another it had a Comanche name I will not write here because some words are not meant to exist in a language that moves in only one direction.

Asa rode in on a Wednesday. The general store was the first building, or the last, depending on which founding you followed. Inside, a man named Goss stood behind a counter made of pine. His wife, Elma, was in the back room sorting dry goods, and she remembered the counter being oak. Their son, a quiet boy of fifteen or so named Porter, did not remember either counter because in the version of Caliche he remembered the store had been established just last year by a man matching Dunstan's description, and the counter was made of mesquite, and the whole building smelled of fresh-cut wood and the future.

They did not argue about this. That is the thing I need you to understand. People argue about facts they believe exist in a shared world. The Gosses did not argue because each of them lived in a world that was internally whole. Goss could put his hand on the pine counter and feel the grain and the grain was pine and it had always been pine and the memory of the wagon train in '71 — the creak of the axles, the way the oxen smelled in the July heat, the particular color of the dust as they descended the caprock — all of this was solid, was real, was his. And Elma could put her hand on the same counter and feel oak and her '68 was just as real, the homestead already staked, the well already dug, and she had memories of a drought that year that cracked the earth into a mosaic she sometimes still dreamed about. And Porter could put his hand on the same wood and feel mesquite and he knew, with the certainty of a boy who has not yet learned to distrust his own recall, that none of this was old, that the town was a new thing and Dunstan had built it the way God builds things — by speaking, by deciding, by walking into a stretch of nothing and declaring it a place.

Both answers were materially true. The wood showed grain patterns from two different trees. If you cut it open I believe you would have found two kinds of heartwood nested inside each other like the rings of a planet that had formed twice.

Dunstan was already the law here. He had a badge. It was a five-pointed star made of tin, and it sat on his chest with the calm authority of a thing that had always been there. No one could say who gave it to him. No one thought to ask. He walked the single street of Caliche in the evenings and the people nodded to him and the nods were real — real respect, real deference, real gratitude — because in every version of the town's founding, Dunstan had done something necessary. He had shot the right man, or dug the right well, or arrived at the right moment with the right words, and the rightness of him was layered so thickly it had become indistinguishable from truth.

Asa saw the town and tried to solve it. This is what I loved and grieved about him in equal measure — his mind reached for the puzzle. He walked the street and noted the contradictions the way a watchmaker notes the misalignments in a broken movement: *here* the hitch rail was old, weathered to silver-grey, and *there* it was new, still oozing sap. *Here* the church had a bell and *there* it had an empty tower and *there* it had no tower at all, just a peaked roof and a wooden cross that leaned slightly east as if straining toward a sound only it could hear. He cataloged these things. He made a ledger in his mind: original, revision, revision of the revision. He believed that if he could identify the sequence, he could locate the first change, and if he could locate the first change, he could reach Dunstan's logic, and if he could reach Dunstan's logic, he could reason with it.

He thought the town was a problem to solve.

I could see, from where I stood in the lake, that it was not. It was a condition. A way of being. The layering was not damage — or it was damage, but it was damage the way a canyon is damage, the way a river carves a valley: the destruction *is* the shape, and the shape has become the truth, and the truth does not remember the flat plain it replaced. Asa wanted to undo what Dunstan had done. But undoing is just another kind of doing. Subtraction leaves its own mark. A word erased still dents the page.

I stood in the lake and I watched him and I could not help him and this was the beginning of the part that does not end.

## IV.

A man named Carver died. He was a rancher or a deputy or an idler who sat on the general store porch and whittled sticks into points for no reason. He was minor. He was the kind of man whose death, in a town with a single coherent history, would merit three lines in the county record and a brief disruption in the rotation of who owed whom a drink. But Dunstan was making revisions — small ones, adjustments, the way you'd tune a piano by flattening this string and sharpening that one until the chord you wanted resolved — and each revision changed the shape of Carver's dying.

Asa attended the funeral. Then Asa attended the funeral. Then Asa attended the funeral. Then Asa attended the funeral.

The first time, Carver had been shot in a card game at a table behind the saloon. The cards were still on the table — a pair of sevens showing — and the man who shot him was sitting in the jail and the widow was in black bombazine and the hymn was "Shall We Gather at the River" and the preacher was an old man with white eyebrows who spoke about the valley of the shadow and meant it, genuinely meant it, and Asa stood at the back and held his hat and grieved the way one grieves a stranger: politely, and with the right amount of distance.

The second time, Carver had been thrown from a horse. The horse was a blue roan with a scar on its left hip and it stood in the corral behind the livery looking unbothered, the way horses look when they have done something enormous. The widow was in dark grey. The hymn was "Abide With Me." The preacher was the same old man but younger by a few years, or older, it was hard to tell, his eyebrows were not quite as white. Asa stood at the back and held his hat and felt the first vibration of something he did not yet identify as horror.

The third time, Carver had died of fever. Three days of shaking and delirium, his wife boiling willow bark, the doctor arriving too late from a town that may or may not have existed in the previous version. The widow was in dark blue, almost black, a dress that had been dyed from something lighter and still held the ghost of its former color in the deeper folds. There was no hymn. Someone read from Ecclesiastes. *To every thing there is a season.* Asa stood at the back and did not hold his hat because in this version he had not brought a hat because in this version he had arrived in Caliche by different means, or at a different time, or the hat had never existed, and his bare head felt obscene among the covered ones, a single blank face turned up to a sky that had no opinion.

The fourth time, Carver had been hanged. The crime was unclear — horse theft, or murder, or a debt owed to a man who existed in only one timeline and had been owed the debt in another. The widow was not at the funeral. She had left town, or she had never been his wife, or he had never married. There were seven mourners. The preacher was a different man entirely, young, nervous, reading from notes. The hymn was one Asa did not recognize. The coffin was pine and it was the wrong size.

Asa laughed.
