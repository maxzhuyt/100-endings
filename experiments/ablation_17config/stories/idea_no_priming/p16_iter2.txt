# The Borges Codex

## I.

Marta Voss had been retired for eleven years, which in her profession meant she had been forging nothing larger than her own signature, and even that she did with a hesitation that would have embarrassed her younger self. She lived above a framing shop in Montevideo. She drank coffee that cost exactly what coffee should cost. She was sixty-one years old and had not committed a crime since the Eisenstein affair, which this narrator will not describe because it has been described too many times already and always wrong, and because Marta herself remembered it only as a sequence of hotel rooms in descending order of quality, the last of which had a window that opened onto a wall, which she had stared at for eleven hours while waiting for a passport that would save her life and which arrived, as passports do, smelling of glue and someone else's name.

She was, in the parlance of people who rank such things, the third-greatest forger of the twentieth century.

She would have been the first, but the first was dead, and the second had done something in 1987 that redefined the criteria in a way Marta found personally insulting and technically indefensible, and so she had settled into third place the way one settles into a window seat on a long flight: with a bitterness so practiced it had become comfort.

She made seven phone calls in three days.

This narrator — who knows what was said on every call and will share almost none of it — can report that each conversation followed the same arc: the long pause after the specialist recognized her voice, the even longer pause after she explained the target, and then the agreement, which came not at the end of the conversation but slightly before it, in the gap between Marta finishing her pitch and the specialist asking the first practical question, a gap that should have contained an objection and contained instead a silence shaped exactly like one.

The first call was to Fen Chadwick, who held locks in a regard most people reserve for the dying: with tenderness, with attention to mechanism, and with the unshakable belief that every locked thing wanted, in its secret iron heart, to be opened. He answered from Kraków. His workshop was immaculate. His fingernails were not. He said yes before Marta mentioned the fee, which she mentioned anyway, because she was a professional, and because the fee was Fen's only reliable evidence that the world outside his workshop was real. His hand, when it set down the phone, remained on the receiver for four seconds longer than the setting-down of a phone requires.

The second call was to Luisa Farrow, who did with explosives what poets claim to do with language — she made controlled destruction feel like revelation. She was in Lisbon, dismantling a condemned parking structure for a municipal contract so far beneath her talents that it constituted its own form of hiding. She listened to Marta's description of the library with her eyes closed. She asked two questions, both about load-bearing walls. She did not ask whether the walls in question obeyed Euclidean geometry, which was either an oversight or an act of profound and willful faith. She had, that morning, rewritten a letter to her sister for the sixth time. The letter was in her jacket pocket. It was sealed. It was not, by any reasonable standard, a will, except in the way that all letters written by people who expect to die are wills, which is to say in every way that matters.

The third call was to Konstantin Babić, who could replicate any document, any surface, any texture of human administrative authority. He was the best counterfeiter alive, which Marta knew because she was the best forger alive, and the difference between counterfeiting and forgery is the difference between impersonating a person and impersonating a person's soul, and Konstantin had the decency never to attempt the latter. He was in Dubrovnik, eating an apple, reading a newspaper whose headline he would not remember in an hour but whose typeface — *Neue Haas Grotesk*, poorly kerned, the lowercase "a" fractionally too close to the "t" in "Croatia" — he would remember for the rest of his life. He said yes. He set the apple down. He did not pick it up again.

The fourth was to Jun Xu, who could enter any building in any city on earth and exit it holding something that had not been his when he entered, and who referred to this talent, without irony, as "collection." He was in Shanghai, between jobs, which meant he was spending thirteen hours a day in a public library reading novels about heists, because Jun believed — not incorrectly — that the genre contained more practical intelligence than any security manual. He had read, that week, a novel in which a thief steals an unstealable painting, and he had admired the author's ingenuity while privately noting three structural errors that would have resulted in the thief's arrest, imprisonment, and eventual apology. He told Marta he was interested. He did not say he was willing, but Marta had known Jun for twenty years and understood that "interested" was his word for the particular feeling a person has when they step to the edge of a rooftop and find that the urge to jump and the urge to step back are, for one vertiginous moment, the same urge.

The fifth was to Nadia Restrepo, who could defeat any electronic security system built after 1970 and most built before, and who regarded the digital world with the calm superiority of a veterinarian examining an animal that does not yet know it is sick. She was in Bogotá, at her mother's house, repairing a washing machine with tools that could have defeated the security at the Banco de la República and that she was using, at this moment, to tighten a hose clamp. She asked Marta how a library beneath Buenos Aires could have electronic security if it predated electricity. Marta said it didn't predate electricity; it predated certainty. Nadia tightened the hose clamp, straightened up, and told her mother the washing machine would be fine. Her mother, who had survived three decades of not asking what Nadia did for a living, did not ask what the phone call had been about, and Nadia, who had survived three decades of not answering, felt, for the first time, that the silence between them was not discretion but prophecy.

The sixth was to Tomás Herrera, the driver, whose particular genius was not speed but spatial intuition — the ability to construct, in the theatre of his mind, a three-dimensional map of any city and then to drive through it as though the streets were suggestions he was free to edit. He was in Buenos Aires already. He had been in Buenos Aires for six years. He knew the city the way a surgeon knows a body: not with love, but with the intimate, proprietary attention that comes from having opened it up and seen what's inside. He met Marta in a café on Calle Defensa, and she showed him a map of the streets above the library, and he studied it for eleven minutes without speaking, and then he said, "These streets don't close." Marta said no, they don't. Tomás folded the map along a crease that was not there when he received it and that would not be there later when he unfolded it, and this was not magic; it was Buenos Aires.

The seventh was to Yael Aronov, the scholar, the only member of the team who was not, strictly speaking, a criminal, and whose presence on a heist crew was either the most prudent or the most reckless decision Marta had ever made, depending on whether you believed, as Marta claimed to believe, that the library could be understood, or whether you believed, as this narrator does, that understanding and the library had parted ways several centuries ago and were now involved in a custody dispute over the concept of sequence. Yael was an emerita professor of library science at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, and she had published, in 2011, a monograph on non-orientable cataloguing systems that had been read by eleven people, three of whom had gone mad, one of whom was Delacroix, but we are not yet discussing Delacroix, because Delacroix is a structural problem that this narrator intends to introduce at the moment of maximum inconvenience, which is how Delacroix himself preferred to arrive. Yael said yes. She said it quietly, the way you say yes to a diagnosis.

The team assembled in Buenos Aires on a Tuesday in March. They met in the back room of a restaurant in San Telmo that served nothing after ten and asked nothing before it. Marta stood at the head of a table that was slightly too small for eight people, which meant that everyone's elbows touched, which meant that everyone was aware, at every moment, of every other body in the room, which was either a mistake or a tactic, and with Marta these categories had been indistinguishable for thirty years.

She told them about the Codex.

She told them it was a manuscript, hand-lettered, approximately 900 pages, bound in a material that three separate analyses had identified as leather, vellum, and a substance that did not correspond to any known animal, and that a fourth analysis — conducted by a chemist who subsequently became a shepherd in Patagonia and refused all further correspondence — had identified as "the resolved form of a previously theoretical polymer that should not exist for another two hundred years, if it intends to exist at all."

She told them the Codex was located in the deepest level of a library beneath Buenos Aires, a library that had been discovered in 1939 by a municipal worker repairing a sewer line and that had been, in the decades since, the subject of eleven government inquiries, four academic expeditions, two exorcisms, and a zoning dispute that was still, technically, in litigation.

She told them the library rearranged itself.

She said this the way one says "the building has stairs" — as a structural fact, a logistical consideration, one challenge among several. She did not say that the rearrangement was continuous, that it was responsive, that the library's architecture was not a fixed space through which one moved but a linguistic process through which one was *read.* She did not say this because she was withholding it, or because she did not know, but because — and this is the interpretation this narrator finds most beautiful and most damning — she had decided that certain facts were better encountered than described, the way certain animals are better met in the wild than in photographs, because the photograph does not bite.

She told them the Codex was worth, to the buyer she had already secured, eleven million dollars, split eight ways.

She did not tell them what the Codex contained.

Or rather: she told them it contained a narrative, handwritten in a Spanish that predated the standardization of Spanish, and that the narrative concerned, among other things, a theft. She presented this detail as a curiosity — a thematic irony, a selling point, almost — and the room received it as such, with the small appreciative exhalations that professionals make when a job has an elegant shape. No one asked what kind of theft. No one asked whether the narrative had an ending. No one asked whether the ending was happy.

Every member of the team agreed. It must be said plainly, because the story will later make it complicated: every member agreed. Fen shook Marta's hand. Luisa nodded once, which was her version of a standing ovation. Konstantin asked about timeline. Jun asked about entry points. Nadia asked about the electrical grid. Tomás asked about the streets. Yael asked about the cataloguing system, and Marta said there wasn't one, and Yael asked again, slower, as though Marta had misheard, and Marta said, again, that there was no cataloguing system, and Yael blinked three times in rapid succession and said "Good," in a tone that meant the opposite of good, in the way that a doctor saying "interesting" means the opposite of interesting.

The room was warm. The elbows touched. The enthusiasm was unanimous and, like all unanimous things, was composed entirely of people who had mistaken the silence of others for conviction.

No one hesitated aloud.

## II.

Now: a word from the management.

This narrator has been patient. This narrator has described the assembly with all due respect for the conventions of the genre — the brilliant crew, the impossible job, the charismatic leader whose past is a series of elegant scars. This narrator has allowed the reader to feel what the reader is supposed to feel at this stage: admiration, anticipation, the vicarious pleasure of watching competent people agree to do a difficult thing competently. This narrator has earned, through patience, the right to tell you what is actually happening in this story.

Not one member of this crew wants to be here.

Let us be precise.

Fen Chadwick, who said yes before the fee was mentioned, had spent the three days between Marta's call and his arrival in Buenos Aires redesigning his workshop in Kraków — not packing it, *redesigning* it, rearranging his tools in a new and more efficient layout that would be waiting for him when he returned, which was an act of either optimism or valediction, and he could not determine which, and the inability to determine which was itself the answer. He had calculated the odds of success at roughly the odds of threading a needle through another needle. He had told no one. He assumed the others knew something he didn't.

Luisa Farrow had rewritten the letter to her sister a seventh time on the flight from Lisbon. The seventh version was shorter than the first six. It said, in its entirety: *I am going to do something that an intelligent person would not do, and I am doing it because I am surrounded by intelligent people who appear to believe it can be done, and I have never been able to distinguish between courage and the fear of being thought afraid.* She sealed it. She unsealed it. She added a postscript: *The cat prefers the blue bowl.* She sealed it again. It was in her jacket pocket now, in the restaurant in San Telmo, pressed against her ribs, which meant that every time she breathed, the letter moved, which meant that every time she breathed she was reminded that she had written it, which meant that she could not breathe without rehearsing her own death, which she experienced not as terror but as a kind of persistent, low-grade annoyance, like a song stuck in her head.

Konstantin Babić had spent the flight from Dubrovnik studying the typefaces of Argentine government documents, which was his version of preparation and also his version of prayer. He had not eaten since the apple. He kept thinking about the apple. Specifically, he kept thinking about the moment he set the apple down — the precise trajectory of his hand, the placement on the table, the angle of the stem — and he could not stop thinking about it because it had been, he was increasingly certain, the last act he had performed that was entirely his own. Everything since — the packing, the flight, the arrival, the handshake — had been dictated by a momentum he had not chosen and could not now resist without making a scene, and Konstantin had survived forty-three years in the counterfeiting trade by never, under any circumstances, making a scene. He assumed the others had reasons. Their reasons were not his business. His business was surfaces.

Jun Xu, in the restaurant, had assessed every exit. There were two. One led to the dining room. One led to an alley. The alley was, he had confirmed during a visit to the restroom that was not a visit to the restroom, 2.4 meters wide, unobstructed, and connected to Calle Defensa via a passage between two buildings. He had this information. He did not intend to use it. The assessment was involuntary, like blinking, and equally useless, because the exit he needed was not from the restaurant but from the commitment he had just made, and that exit did not connect to any street. He had read, in the heist novel he finished on the plane, a scene in which a thief abandons a crew two days before the job, and the author had rendered this as cowardice, and Jun had thought: *No. That is the bravest scene in the book. The coward is the one who stays because leaving would require an explanation.*

Nadia Restrepo had, upon arriving in Buenos Aires, purchased a voltage meter, two signal analyzers, and a broadband spectrum scanner from an electronics supplier in Microcentro, and she had spent three hours sweeping the streets above the library's known perimeter for electromagnetic signatures, and she had found nothing, which was not the same as finding that there was nothing, and the difference between "I found nothing" and "there is nothing to find" was the difference between an unanswered question and an impossible one, and Nadia was not a woman who tolerated impossible questions, which is why she was here, and also why she should not have been here.
