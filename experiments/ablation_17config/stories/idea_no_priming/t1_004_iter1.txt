# Love Yourself Out of the Labyrinth

---

## Chapter One: Hi, I'm [Redacted], and I Need You to Believe Me

Before we begin this journey together, I want you to do something for me.

I want you to take a breath. Not a branded breath—not the 4-7-8 method or the box breath or the one with the hand on the belly that every therapist learned from the same weekend seminar in 2019. Just a breath. One that belongs to you. One that doesn't have a trademark pending.

Good.

Now I want you to write down two names.

First: the name of the person who taught you what love is.

Second: the name of the person who taught you what love *costs*.

Take your time.

I'll go first.

The first name is Maren.

The second name is also Maren.

---

That is how the book was supposed to open. I wrote those words six weeks ago, in a rented apartment in Lisbon that I paid for with the last reliable deposit from my publisher, who at that point still believed I was writing a book called *Love Yourself Out of the Labyrinth: A Self-Guided Journey from Toxic Patterns to Radical Wholeness*. Two hundred and eighty pages, trade paperback, pub date March, blurbs from two therapists and a podcast host with a following in the mid-six figures. The kind of book that gets a cardboard display in the self-help aisle at airports, which is where it would reach you, dear reader, somewhere between your gate and your gate-adjacent Auntie Anne's, at a moment when you were vulnerable enough to believe a stranger's promise that pain has a structure and structures can be escaped.

I still believe that. I need you to know that I still believe that.

I am writing this at Gate B-47 of an airport I will not name, laptop balanced on my knees, boarding pass tucked into the manuscript binder, carry-on between my feet. My flight boards in twenty minutes. The man next to me is asleep with his mouth open and his phone face-down on his thigh, and I am grateful for his unconsciousness because I am typing very fast and I do not want to be perceived.

Eleven minutes ago, a push notification appeared on my phone.

The notification was from TikTok, an app I deleted in January but which persists in my phone's architecture the way certain people persist in your nervous system—formally evicted, functionally resident. Someone had tagged my account, or rather the frozen husk of my account, in a video. I should not have opened it. Chapter Four of this book is about the neuroscience of compulsive checking, and I can tell you with professional authority that the thumbnail preview is designed to exploit the orienting response, the brainstem's ancient inability to ignore a novel stimulus. I opened it.

The video was Maren.

She was filmed at golden hour—the forty minutes before sunset that turn skin into something benedictory, something that makes you want to believe in the person wearing it. She was sitting on what looked like a clifftop, though I couldn't identify the location. Her mascara was tracked deliberately down both cheeks in two symmetrical lines, the kind of crying that has been rehearsed in a ring light, and I say that not to be cruel but because I helped her rehearse it, once, for a campaign about "radical vulnerability" for a skincare brand, and I remember the specific brush she used to paint the tracks because I bought it for her, and it was not waterproof, which was the point. In the video, she was speaking directly to the camera in the voice I used to love—low, unhurried, a voice that made you lean in—and she was saying my full legal name. Not the name on this manuscript. My actual name. The name my mother gave me, which I had kept off every platform, every byline, every bio, for three years, because Maren and I had agreed early on that one of us should remain unsearchable, should keep a self that the internet could not hold, and I had volunteered because I believed, then, that disappearance was a form of devotion.

The caption read: *I'm finally ready to tell the truth about what happened to me.*

The hashtag was #SurvivingTheLabyrinth.

The video had 1.4 million views.

It had been posted eleven minutes ago.

I will say this calmly, because calm is the only professional register available to me, and because this manuscript is the only structure I have left, and because I need you to read these words and decide, in the quiet courtroom of your own attention, whether I am a person telling the truth or a person performing the telling of truth, and I cannot help you make that distinction because I am no longer sure I can make it myself:

Maren is not Maren.

Maren has not been Maren for almost two years.

And the thing that wears her face has just told 1.4 million people my name.

---

I know how that sounds. I know because I have spent twelve years as a relationship coach, workshop facilitator, and author of two previously published books (*Boundary as Birthright* and *The Cartography of Enough*), and in that time I have sat across from hundreds of clients who have said some version of "you don't understand, they changed, something *happened* to them, they became someone else," and I have nodded with compassionate authority and guided them toward the clinical language: traumatic bonding, idealization-devaluation cycles, cognitive dissonance as a feature of coercive control. I have said, in workshops, to rooms of people who traveled hours to hear me, that the feeling of "this isn't the person I loved" is the psyche's way of metabolizing the unbearable recognition that it *is* the same person, that the cruelty was always there, latent, waiting, and that the project of healing is the project of integrating those two images into one. I wrote Chapter Six about this. It's called "The Stranger Was Always in the House."

I believed it when I wrote it.

I am not sure I was wrong.

But I am sure—the way you are sure of gravity, or theeli taste of pennies right before a migraine, or the specific silence of a house where someone is awake who should be sleeping—that whatever is operating Maren's body, voice, face, and following is not a personality disorder, a trauma response, or a particularly effective social media strategy. It is something older. It is something that found, in the infrastructure of platform culture, a host environment so perfectly suited to its needs that the question of whether it is *supernatural* or *systemic* has become meaningless, because the system has achieved everything a supernatural entity would want, and it has done so using only Wi-Fi, parasocial attachment, and the human inability to distinguish between being seen and being loved.

That is what I am trying to write about.

That is what the book is about.

Before we begin this journey together, I need you to do one more exercise. This one is not in any of my published work. This one I invented just now, at Gate B-47, with my hands shaking and my boarding group about to be called and 1.4 million strangers learning my name.

The exercise is this:

Think of the last time someone told you a story and asked you to believe them. Not believe *in* them—believe *them*. The distinction matters. Believing in someone is an act of devotion. Believing someone is an act of investigation. The first asks you to close your eyes. The second asks you to open them.

Open them.

I am going to tell you what happened.

---

They are calling my boarding group. I am going to close this laptop and walk down the jetway and sit in a seat that costs more than I can afford and fly toward a place I will tell you about when I arrive, and during the flight I will not have Wi-Fi, which means for five hours and forty minutes nothing that Maren—nothing that *it*—posts can reach me, and that window of silence is the most expensive, most precious commodity I currently possess.

Five hours and forty minutes of being unreachable.

Long enough to write you a chapter.

Long enough, maybe, to make you believe me.

---

## Chapter Two: What We Bury in Beautiful Places (A Site Visit)

The chapel sits on the edge of the caldera like a tooth left in a jawbone. Whitewashed, blue-domed, barely larger than a hotel bathroom, clinging to the cliff face with the resigned tenacity of a barnacle. The kind of building that has been painted so many times the paint is structural—strip it and the walls might sigh and collapse. When Maren and I came here in 2021, September, the first year, when we were still negotiating the boundaries between her public life and our private one, we had to hike forty minutes from the main road because Maren insisted on arriving without a scooter, without a rideshare, without anything that would let her skip the approach. "I want to earn it," she said. She was always saying things like that then, things that now read either as genuine spiritual longing or as copy, and I have given up trying to determine which, because the answer might be that there was never a clean distinction, and that thought is the one I cannot afford to have, because if Maren was already performing when I believed she was praying, then I have no baseline, no "before," no version of her that the thing replaced, only a woman who was always, in some sense, available for habitation because the interior had already been staged.

Chapter Two of the book is about sacred spaces. "Identify a place where you felt held," the prompt says. "Return to it in your mind. What made it safe? Was the safety in the place, or in who you were when you were there?"

I wrote that prompt. I also, apparently, followed it here.

The chapel is different now.

The first thing I noticed, climbing the last switchback in the 9 a.m. heat with my bag cutting into my shoulder and the manuscript binder leaving a faint rectangular bruise on my lower back, was the line. Eight people, standing single-file along the narrow stone path, phones out, waiting. This is not unusual. Santorini in shoulder season is a managed hallucination—every sightline has been scouted, every sunset is a scheduled event, and the algorithm has performed a kind of site selection on the island's geography, elevating certain vistas to canonical status while others, equally beautiful, remain unvisited and therefore, in the platform's taxonomy, unreal. The chapel had been "discovered" in 2022, I later learned, by a travel influencer with eleven million followers who posted a reel of herself lighting a candle and weeping with the caption *some places just hold you, you know?* The reel got forty-seven million views. The chapel got a QR code.

The QR code is etched into the stone doorframe. I stood in front of it for a long time, touching the grooves with my index finger, feeling the depth of the cut. Someone had used a rotary tool. The stone was old—seventeenth century, I'd been told in 2021, by a local woman who sold candles from a basket at the trailhead and who is no longer there. The QR code links to an app called SacredSpace, which offers guided meditations, sponsored prayer intentions, and a "light a virtual candle" feature that costs $2.99 per candle, with bulk pricing available. I downloaded it. I don't know why I downloaded it. Professional curiosity. The same impulse that made me take notes at our couples' retreat while Maren slept on my shoulder—extracting, always extracting, making experience legible by making it useful. I am writing about this. I am always writing about this. This is part of the problem that this chapter is about, and this chapter is part of the problem, and there is no outside to the problem, which is the entire thesis of the book I am trying to write, and I am aware that this paragraph is spiraling, and I am going to let it spiral for one more sentence because the spiral is the shape of the thing I am describing, and the book is called *Love Yourself Out of the Labyrinth*, and a labyrinth is just a spiral with a minotaur.

Inside the chapel.

Smaller than I remembered. The icon on the wall—Panagia, the All-Holy, Mary holding the child with the enormous sad adult eyes—was the same, though someone had hung a string of fairy lights around the frame, battery-operated, warm white, the kind you can buy in bulk from Amazon for content shoots. They were off. The batteries had died or no one had bothered to replace them, and the dead lights framing the Mother of God struck me as the most honest religious image I had ever seen.

The ring light was bolted to the wall opposite the icon.

It was professional-grade, eighteen inches, with an adjustable color temperature and a cold shoe mount for a phone. Someone had drilled into the plaster—into the seventeenth-century plaster—and attached a bracket, and the ring light hung there like a halo that had been bought for $34.99 plus expedited shipping. It was positioned so that anyone standing in front of the icon would be lit from the front, evenly, forgivingly, the way ring lights are designed to light: erasing shadows, erasing texture, creating a face that the camera can love because it has been stripped of everything the camera cannot sell.

I did not editorialize. I am not editorializing. I sat down in the back pew, which was not a pew but a wooden bench with a foam cushion that had been duct-taped at the seam, and I opened my laptop, and I began writing, and the act of writing in a chapel is an act of devotion or an act of extraction and I no longer know which and the not-knowing is the opening theme of Chapter Two, which is called "What We Bury in Beautiful Places," and which begins:

*Dear reader, identify a place where you were loved without performance. Go there. If it has changed, notice the change. Do not assign blame. The change is information.*

The queue moved one couple at a time. Each couple entered, stood before the icon, positioned themselves in front of the ring light (which was off, but they carried their own—portable ones, phone-mounted, clip-on), filmed for ninety seconds to three minutes, and exited. Their faces, illuminated. The Mother of God behind them, dark except for the dead fairy lights. Some of them crossed themselves. Some of them kissed. One woman stood alone and whispered something I couldn't hear and then checked the footage and whispered it again, adjusted her angle, and checked again, and I watched her loop three times through whatever private prayer she was trying to preserve, and I felt such tenderness for her that I had to close my eyes, because the tenderness was real and real feelings are dangerous when you are trying to maintain the observational distance required to write a self-help book in a chapel where the sacred and the commodified have merged so completely that the building itself seems confused about its purpose, like a church that has been rented out for so many weddings it has forgotten it was built for God.

I was writing. I was present. For twenty minutes, I was close to something—not peace, but a rhythm. Chapter Two was taking shape. The self-help prompts were aligning with the experience, the experience was feeding the prompts, and for a stretch of paragraphs I could almost believe that the book was going to work, that the framework was sound, that I could write my way out of the labyrinth by mapping it.

Then the woman in the queue said Maren's name.

She was talking to her partner, a man in linen shorts holding a gimbal stabilizer, and she said it the way you say the name of someone who has given you permission to feel: softly, with ownership, with the gratitude you reserve for people who have suffered publicly so that you might suffer less privately.

"Maren posted about this place," the woman said. "She said it was the last place she felt safe before everything."

She said the word *everything* the way a congregant says *amen*—a container word, a word that holds whatever you need it to hold.

Before everything.

Maren posted about this place.

Of course she did. Of course it did. The thing wearing Maren had found this chapel in Maren's memory the way you'd search a hard drive, and it had posted the coordinates, the story, the emotional charge, and 1.4 million people had absorbed the coordinates, the story, and the emotional charge, and some of them had traveled here, to this cliff, to this bench where I once sat with a woman who might have loved me, and they had brought ring lights, and the ring light bolted to the wall might as well have been a flag planted by a colonizing force that had arrived before me, that would always arrive before me, because it traveled at the speed of content and I traveled at the speed of a body.
