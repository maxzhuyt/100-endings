# Vostok Craniotomy

## I.

The kettle had calcified again. Yelena could hear it in the whistle — a choked, mineral rasp instead of the clean scream it had produced in March, back when the water still ran clear through the filter and the sun still grazed the horizon for a few hours each afternoon. She lifted it from the electric plate and poured through a square of cheesecloth folded twice. The water came through yellow-tinged. She drank it anyway. Everyone drank it anyway. The filtration cartridges had been on the resupply manifest for November, and November was a word that did not mean anything at Vostok. November was a date on a calendar pinned to the mess wall. Outside the corrugated steel the temperature was sixty-eight below zero and the sky had been dark since April and would remain dark until the middle of September, and the word *November* was a small printed square you could cover with your thumbnail.

She carried the cup back through the corridor that connected the galley module to the dormitory annex. The corridor walls sweated frost in fine crystalline patterns that no one scraped anymore. In the first weeks of winter, Gennady Orlov — the glaciologist, who was meticulous about surfaces in the way that men who studied ice tended to be — had gone through each morning with a putty knife and a rag, shaving the frost from the rivets and wiping down the steel. By June he had stopped. The frost was the corridor now. It narrowed the passage by perhaps a centimeter on each side, and the twelve winterers passed through it with their shoulders canted slightly inward, a posture so habitual it had migrated into how they sat, how they stood, how they held themselves even in the wider galley module where there was room to spread their arms if anyone had wanted to.

No one wanted to. The body learned to be small at Vostok.

Yelena's bunk was the third on the left in the women's section, which was the women's section only because she slept there and she was the only woman. The module had four bunks. The other three held boxes of veterinary supplies, core-sample bags she was storing for Orlov, and a sleeping bag she kept rolled and ready for reasons she did not examine. Her own bunk was made with the sheet tucked under the mattress at hospital corners, a habit from veterinary school that had outlasted every other habit from that period of her life. Beside the bunk, bolted to the wall, a shelf held seven books, a framed photograph she had turned face-down sometime in May, and a case of tissue slides she was cataloguing for the biology division in Mirny. The slides contained cross-sections of Weddell seal liver, each one labeled in her handwriting with a date and a four-digit specimen number. She had been cataloguing them for three months. She was in no hurry. The work gave her hands something to do under the lamp in the hours between dinner and sleep, and at Vostok the hours between dinner and sleep were the dangerous ones, the hours when the dark outside pressed against the module walls and the generator's diesel hum took on the quality of a living thing breathing.

She set her cup on the shelf and picked up the slide she had been examining before the kettle called. Specimen 3371. Weddell seal, adult female, liver cross-section stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Under the pocket microscope the hepatocytes arranged themselves in neat cords radiating from the central vein, the sinusoidal spaces clean and regular, the architecture of a healthy organ doing its work in the dark cold water beneath the ice. Yelena noted the absence of pathology on her log sheet, wrote the date, set the slide in the completed tray. She reached for 3372.

From the common room, two modules away through the frost-narrowed corridor, she could hear the murmur of the radio playing something orchestral — Borya Chesnokov's evening selection, which alternated between Shostakovich and a Beatles cassette he had carried twelve thousand kilometers from Leningrad. Tonight it was Shostakovich, the Eighth Quartet, which meant Borya was in one of his moods. Yelena had learned to read the winterers the way she had once read the animals in her clinic in Arkhangelsk: by small behavioral signatures, by patterns of movement and sound, by what they chose and what they avoided. Borya played the Eighth when he was homesick. Orlov stopped scraping frost when he was depressed. The station commander, Platonov, ate standing up when he was anxious. Kolya Denikin, the diesel mechanic, played chess with himself when he could not sleep.

Kolya's chess game had been in progress on the mess table for four days. She had passed it that morning on her way to the galley — the board with its pieces in a Sicilian Defense she recognized from the notation sheet he kept beside it, his careful Cyrillic recording each move as though the game were being played between two real opponents. He played both sides with equal seriousness. He kept score. She had watched him once, sitting across from the empty chair, his elbows on the table, his jaw on his fists, staring at the board with the same furrowed intensity he brought to the diesel generator when its timing slipped. He had not noticed her standing in the doorway. She had not interrupted. There were courtesies at Vostok that had no names, that had never been discussed, that everyone observed the way the body observed the narrowed corridor: by instinct, by the accumulated knowledge of proximity.

She knew Kolya's hands. This was not a remarkable thing. She knew everyone's hands. The veterinarian's diagnostic instinct began and ended with hands — the shape of them, the condition of the skin, the way they moved, what they revealed about the body's interior state. Kolya's hands were large and square-knuckled, permanently darkened with diesel grease in the creases despite the pumice soap he used each evening. The left ring finger had been broken and healed without setting, sometime before Vostok, and it angled slightly outward so that when he held a chess piece he gripped it between thumb and middle finger. His cuticles were cracked from the cold. He wore a steel watch with an expandable band that caught his wrist hair when he twisted it, which he did often, a self-soothing mechanism she had catalogued without judgment. His felt valenki boots were the largest pair at the station, size forty-seven, and they stood propped against the heating pipe in the corridor each night, salt-stained and creased across the toe where he rocked forward on his feet while working.

The valenki were there now. She had passed them on her way from the galley. They were the boots of a man who had settled in for the night.

She catalogued specimen 3372 — healthy, no pathology, the Weddell seal's liver an exemplary organ — and reached for 3373. The generator hummed. The Shostakovich ended and was replaced by silence and then the click-hiss of Borya rewinding the tape to play it again. Frost crept along the rivets above her head. She adjusted the pocket microscope's focus knob, a movement so practiced her hand performed it the way a pianist's hand finds an octave — without looking, without thought, with the accumulated certainty of ten thousand repetitions.

## II.

She found him in the corridor between the communications module and the latrine.

He was on the floor, and the floor was the first wrong thing, because the floor at Vostok was minus-fifteen even with the heating pipes and no one lay on it voluntarily. His left leg was extended and his right was drawn up against his chest in a flexion that the deeper part of her brain — the part that had spent nine years reading animal bodies — registered before the rest of her caught up. Decerebrate posturing. No. Decorticate. The right arm was pulled inward, wrist flexed, fingers curled. The left arm was at his side. The posture was asymmetric, and the asymmetry was a sentence she could read: this was happening on one side.

She was on her knees beside him before she had consciously decided to move. The frost of the floor bit through her wool trousers at the kneecaps, and she registered this in the same peripheral file where she registered the hum of the generator and the distant Shostakovich and the fact that the corridor's overhead bulb was flickering at the end of its filament life. Her hand went to his throat. Carotid pulse: present, bounding, slower than it should be. She counted against the second hand of her own watch. Fifty-two beats. Bradycardia. She tasted something metallic in the back of her mouth that was not a real taste but a cascade of adrenaline reaching her salivary glands.

"Kolya."

His eyes were open. This was the second wrong thing. The right eye tracked — it moved toward her voice, or toward the light, or toward some stimulus she could not identify — but the movement was slow, deliberate, decoupled from conscious intent. The left eye did not move. The left pupil was fixed, blown wide, a black disk that had swallowed the iris. She held her penlight — the small aluminum one she carried in her trouser pocket out of a habit from the clinic, a habit that had survived the journey from Arkhangelsk to Mirny to Vostok because habits are the last things the body surrenders — and clicked it on and swept the beam across both eyes. The right pupil constricted, then dilated. Consensual response intact on the right. The left pupil did not respond. It stayed wide and black and fixed, a hole that looked through her.

She drew her thumbnail along the sole of his left boot — she had to pull the valenki away, and it came off with the stiff reluctance of frozen felt, and his foot beneath was in a thick wool sock that she peeled back far enough to expose the sole. She drew her thumbnail hard from heel to toe. The great toe extended upward. The other toes fanned. Babinski sign, positive. She replaced the sock. She did not replace the boot.

Her fingers moved to his scalp. She palpated with the flat pads of her fingertips, beginning at the occiput and moving forward over the parietal bone, pressing with the measured five-hundred-gram force she had been taught in her first anatomy course — the force that would depress a dog's skin deep enough to feel the bone beneath without distorting soft tissue. On the left temporal region, above and anterior to the ear, she found it: a boggy swelling, a fluctuance beneath the skin where the scalp had separated from the periosteum. He had struck something, or something had struck him. The blood was pooling between bone and tissue in a raised, tender mound the size of her palm.

She sat back on her heels.

In practice, in the clinic, she had drained epidural hematomas in four dogs that she could remember. Three had survived. The fourth had died on the table because the hematoma had been arterial — a meningeal artery torn by the fracture itself — and the bleeding had outpaced her ability to evacuate it. She remembered the fourth dog with the specificity that failure always conferred: a Caucasian Shepherd, eight years old, hit by a delivery truck on Troitsky Prospekt. The owners had carried him in on a blanket. She had shaved the temporal region, trephined the skull, lifted the bone flap, and found the dura taut as a drum skin over a lake of dark arterial blood that welled faster than she could suction it. The dog had died with her fingers inside its skull. She had felt the brain swell against her gloved hand as the pressure overwhelmed the last compensatory mechanism, and the heartbeat — which she could feel through the tissue, transmitted up through her fingertips — had slowed, and slowed, and stopped.

In horses she had done it twice, both times successfully, both times with the animal standing and sedated, the trephine applied to the frontal bone at a site she could locate in her sleep, the bone thinner than people imagined, the dura beneath it gray and glistening and alive.

She knew the anatomy. She knew the procedure. She knew the feel of the drill biting through the outer table, the change in resistance as the diploë gave way to the dense inner table, the critical moment when the last millimeter of bone yielded and the drill tip kissed the dura and you had to stop — absolutely had to stop — because a millimeter more was the brain itself, the cortex, the tissue that held everything a being was.

She knew this in horses. She knew this in dogs. She knew the equine skull's thickness at the frontal bone: eight to twelve millimeters. She knew the canine temporal bone: two to four millimeters. She did not know — not with her hands, not with the memory that lived in her fingertips — the human temporal bone. She knew it from textbooks: thin, two to three millimeters in places, thinner than a dog's in the squamous portion, the thinnest flat bone in the human calvarium. She knew the middle meningeal artery ran in a groove on its inner surface, and that a fracture through this groove was the most common source of epidural hemorrhage, and that the hemorrhage was arterial, and that arterial meant fast, and that fast meant the hematoma was expanding now, this moment, each heartbeat driving another pulse of blood into the space between bone and dura, compressing the left temporal lobe, pushing the medial structures toward the midline, and when the uncus of the temporal lobe herniated through the tentorial notch it would compress the brainstem and the brainstem was the structure that told the heart to beat and the lungs to breathe.

He had an hour. Perhaps less.

His breathing changed as she knelt there. It assumed a pattern: deep breaths, then shallow breaths, then a pause — five seconds, eight seconds — then deep again. Cheyne-Stokes respiration. The brainstem was beginning to feel the pressure. The clock was not metaphorical.

She placed her hand on his chest. Through the wool sweater she could feel the rise and fall, the deep and shallow, the pause. Her hand rose and fell and paused with him. Her own breathing synchronized to his, a mammalian reflex she could not suppress, the veterinarian's body tethered to the patient's body through the palm. She felt his heart beat beneath her hand. She felt it slow.

She looked at his face. Kolya Denikin, forty-three years old, diesel mechanic from Novosibirsk, a man who played chess against himself with absolute seriousness and wound his watch and rocked forward on his size-forty-seven feet while adjusting fuel injectors, whose hands were square and grease-darkened and whose broken ring finger angled outward in a way she had observed a hundred times across the mess table without ever asking how it had happened. His face was slack. The left side drooped slightly — the corner of the mouth pulled down, the nasolabial fold smoothed flat. The face of a man whose brain was being slowly crushed from the outside in.

Her hand on his chest rose and fell and paused.

## III.

Platonov was at the radio when she reached the communications module. He was standing — he was always standing when the static was bad, as though height could improve the signal — with the headset clamped over one ear and his free hand pressing the transmission key in rhythmic, hopeful bursts. The receiver produced a sound like ice fracturing deep in a glacier, a low splintering crackle overlaid with a higher hiss that swelled and receded in waves. Ionospheric storm. The charged particles pouring out of the sun — invisible, unstoppable, a weather system that existed a hundred kilometers above the stratosphere — had turned the radio into a machine that received nothing but its own noise.

Platonov looked at her. She could see in his face that he already knew something was wrong. He had the commander's instinct for emergency, an animal wariness that narrowed his eyes and brought his weight forward onto the balls of his feet when the equilibrium of the station shifted. She told him. She told him in the fewest possible words because the words were not the thing that mattered — the thing that mattered was in the corridor with one pupil blown and Cheyne-Stokes breathing — and because she had learned in the clinic that the fewer words she used the more precisely the information landed. Epidural hematoma. Expanding. Brainstem compression beginning. Without intervention, death within the hour.

He asked the question she had already answered in herself. Could she do it.

She did not answer. She crossed to the radio set and put the headset to her own ear.
