# The Thing in the Drawer

## I.

She checks the thermostat at 7:14 every evening. She has done this for nineteen years. Before her it was her father who checked the thermostat, and before that it was his father who had the Kelvinator unit installed in 1961 — a broad, wheezing appliance that died in 1987 and was replaced with a Bohn walk-in compressor that hums at a frequency of sixty hertz, which is the frequency of the American electrical grid, which is the frequency some studies have associated with unease in mammals. She does not find the hum uneasy. She finds it correct. Forty-one degrees Fahrenheit. She notes this on a clipboard that hangs from a nail her father drove into the drywall in 1979, the year she was born, the year he stopped being a man who had no children and became a man who did. The clipboard holds a log going back fourteen months. The thirteen months before that are in a filing cabinet. The years before that fill two banker's boxes in the storage room. The temperature has never been wrong. She checks it every night anyway, because the routine is not about temperature. The routine is about the touching of the clipboard, the uncapping of the pen, the passage of her body through the doorframe of the preparation room at 7:14, the way her fingers brush the wood of the frame without her knowing they do this. Her father's fingers wore a groove in the same wood. Hers has begun to deepen it.

The preparation room is twelve feet by sixteen. Fluorescent lighting, two tubes, one of which has buzzed since March. She will not call an electrician. Electricians require conversation — the greeting, the explanation of the problem, the small talk while the man works, the settlement of the bill. She could write it down, of course. She has a pad. But the pad invites questions. *Why don't you talk? Were you always like this? What happened?* The questions are not asked with cruelty. They are asked with the same dumb, hydraulic curiosity that makes a person peer into an open casket. She would rather work in a buzzing light for the rest of her life than answer the questions, and so the tube buzzes, and she has arranged this, and the arrangement is so ordinary that it is invisible, which is how she prefers her arrangements.

She inventories the embalming fluid. Four bottles of arterial chemical — Permaglo, twenty-eight index, the salmon-colored one that her father favored and that she has continued to order from the same supplier, not out of loyalty but because the supplier's ordering system is online and requires no phone call. Three bottles of cavity chemical. One jug of Dis-Spray. She notes the levels. She will need to reorder the Permaglo within two weeks. She opens the supplier's website on the desktop computer in the office — a machine so old it takes forty-five seconds to load a webpage — and places the order. The confirmation email will arrive in her inbox, which she checks once daily at 6:00 a.m. She has not spoken to another human being on the telephone in six years. The last time was the plumber, and she had dialed the wrong number first and sat for eleven seconds listening to a stranger say *hello? hello?* before she placed the handset back in its cradle with the care of a person setting down a detonator.

She is fifty-one years old. Her name is June. The name will not matter for some time.

Three cadaver drawers are occupied tonight. She confirms each latch. Drawer one: Eleanor Purcell, eighty-seven, cause of death cerebrovascular accident, viewing postponed from Thursday to Saturday due to family travel. Drawer two: Gerald Ash, ninety-one, cause of death congestive heart failure, services pending coordination with a daughter in Arizona. Drawer three: John Doe, approximate age forty to fifty, transferred from the county morgue eleven days ago. Unidentified. Unclaimed. He will be cremated eventually, when the county completes its statutory waiting period. She has kept him at temperature. She has not begun preparation — there is no one to prepare him for — but she has maintained the cold chain with the same diligence she affords the Purcells and the Ashes of her professional life, because the dead are in her care, and care does not require an audience.

She latches the third drawer. Her hand rests on the steel an extra half second. Not because of the John Doe. Because the drawers were her father's purchase — heavy-gauge stainless, Frigid Fluid Company, 1974 — and the steel holds the memory of his hand the way the doorframe holds the groove of his fingers, and she collects these contacts the way a person collects, without deciding to, the exposed surfaces of a loved one's absence. Her father has been dead for nineteen years. He occupies no drawer. He was buried at Greenwood, three miles east, in a plot he purchased in 1970, next to her mother, who died in 1982, when June was three, too young to retain anything but the shape of the loss, the outline where a thing had been.

She climbs the stairs to the apartment.

The apartment is six rooms — kitchen, living room, bedroom, bathroom, a room that was her childhood bedroom and is now used for storage, and a room her father called the study, which contains a desk, a chair, and four hundred books she has not read. The apartment is clean in the way that places are clean when only one person lives in them and that person has no visitors. Not sterile. Lived-in, but lived in by one body only, so that the wear patterns are narrow — a path from the bed to the bathroom, a path from the kitchen to the living room, a path from the top of the stairs to all of these. The couch cushions are compressed in one spot. The bedroom carpet is worn on one side. Sometimes she stands in the doorway of a room and sees the fossil record of her own movements and thinks nothing about it, because to think about it would be to see it, and she has organized her life around not seeing.

This is the matter of the mirrors, which should be stated plainly. There are no mirrors in the apartment. There are no mirrors in the funeral home. There have not been mirrors in either space for approximately fifteen years, which is not the amount of time she has wanted them gone but is the amount of time it took her to remove them all, because some of them were large and bolted to walls, and some of them were small and hidden — the chrome of a faucet, the glass of a framed photograph, the black screen of a television — and the project of eliminating every reflective surface from a building is not a dramatic undertaking but a slow, practical, almost bureaucratic one, like filling out tax forms or repainting a hallway: she did it in stages, over years, the way a person rearranges furniture around a bad knee. She does not think of it as a project. She does not think of it at all. The bathroom mirror was removed first, replaced with a painted board cut to the same dimensions. The preparation room mirror — used for cosmetic work on decedents — was replaced with a magnifying lamp on an adjustable arm, which is in fact a better tool, which allowed her to frame the removal as a professional improvement. The hallway mirror, which had been her mother's and which her father had kept because he kept everything of her mother's, went into the storage room and then into a dumpster during a night when the reasons felt sufficient and the morning when they didn't had not yet arrived. She planed the reflections out of her life the way a woodworker planes a surface smooth— not in a frenzy but with patience, with craft, with the steady removal of material until what remained was a world in which she never encountered herself by accident.

She makes a microwave meal. Salisbury steak. The plastic film peels off with a sound she has heard perhaps four thousand times. She eats standing at the counter. She does not sit at the table. The table has two chairs. The second chair has never been occupied in nineteen years. It could be removed. It has not been. This is the one inconsistency in her arrangement, the one object that argues against her thesis, and she has never examined why it remains, and the narrator, who sees everything, who has followed her through every room and registered every removed mirror and every solitary path worn into the carpet, will note the chair and move on, because the narrator understands that some objects are left in place precisely because to move them would be to admit what they mean.

She washes the plastic tray. She dries it. She places it in the recycling bin. She brushes her teeth — without a mirror, by feel, the way she has done everything for fifteen years, by feel, navigating her own body and her own building and her own life the way a person navigates a familiar room in the dark, which is to say: competently, but only because the room never changes, only because she has made certain the room will never change.

She sets out tomorrow's cosmetics kit on the preparation room counter before she retires, descending the stairs one final time: foundation in three shades, lip color, setting powder, the fine brushes she orders from a theatrical supply company in Chicago. The kit is arranged with surgical precision — each item in its place, each brush laid parallel. The work she will do tomorrow, if the weather permits the Purcell viewing, is the work of making a dead woman look like herself. This is what mortuary cosmetics is: the reconstruction of a living appearance on a body that no longer lives. It requires studying photographs of the deceased, matching skin tones from life, rebuilding the illusion of sleep or peace on a face that has been vacated. She is, by any measure, very good at it. She has been told so in the thank-you cards that arrive sometimes — brief, stunned notes from families who say *she looked just like herself,* as if this were a miracle and not a technical achievement, as if the dead woman had collaborated in the effort. She keeps the cards in a drawer. She reads them sometimes. They are the closest thing she has to performance reviews, to being seen, to evidence that her work matters to someone other than herself. She does the cosmetics with the same focus a painter brings to a portrait, and the narrator observes — without stating, without underlining — that she has spent her adult life perfecting the art of making dead people look alive while performing on herself the opposite procedure.

She goes to bed. The building is quiet. The Kelvinator hums.

She does not know that this is the last ordinary night.

## II.

The forecast called for four to six inches. By 10:00 p.m., the National Weather Service has revised the estimate to fourteen. By midnight, the revision is meaningless because the storm has exceeded all models, and the county emergency broadcast — which she receives as a text alert on the cell phone she keeps charged but rarely touches — reads BLIZZARD WARNING / STAY INDOORS / TRAVEL IMPOSSIBLE UNTIL FURTHER NOTICE. She reads this in bed. She sets the phone down. She is not alarmed.

The physics of the storm proceed as follows: wind from the north-northwest at forty-seven miles per hour, gusting to sixty-three. Temperature dropping to negative four Fahrenheit. Snowfall rate: three inches per hour. The funeral home is a two-story brick building constructed in 1952. It has survived worse weather but not by much. By 1:00 a.m., the snow has packed against the ground-floor doors to a depth of two feet. The windows, single-pane originals her father discussed replacing and never replaced, ice over from the outside in — first a lacework of frost, then an opaque sheet that turns each window into a cataract. The building creaks. The wind finds the gaps in the mortar. She lies in bed and listens to the building absorb the storm the way a body absorbs a blow — not silently, not without cost, but with the structural patience of a thing that was built to stand.

At 2:17 a.m., the power fails. She knows the interval because she hears the silence — the cessation of the refrigerator upstairs, the overhead lights clicking off, the various small electrical murmurs that constitute the voice of a functioning building — and then, nine seconds later, the generator engages. It is a propane unit, installed in 2009, housed in a concrete enclosure on the north side of the building. It powers the essential circuits: the Kelvinator compressor, the preparation room lights, a single outlet in the apartment kitchen, and the emergency lighting, which is amber-colored and dim and which turns the funeral home into something that looks like a photograph of itself, sepia-toned and shadowed. The generator hums at a different frequency than the compressor — lower, more guttural, a mechanical throat clearing that repeats every four seconds. She has heard it before, during outages. It is unremarkable.

She gets up. She goes downstairs. Professional obligation: confirm the compressor is running, confirm the cold chain is intact, confirm the three bodies in her care are being maintained at temperature. The stairwell is dark except for the amber emergency sconces, which throw long shadows upward. The funeral home at night, in emergency lighting, looks the way all buildings look when their primary systems fail — like a place pretending to be itself, the architecture intact but the animating principle withdrawn.

She enters the preparation room. The compressor hums. The temperature reads forty-one degrees. She checks each drawer.

Drawer one: Eleanor Purcell. Latched. She opens it, confirms the body bag, closes and relatches. Drawer two: Gerald Ash. Latched. Confirmed. Drawer three: John Doe. Latched. Confirmed.

She stands in the preparation room, in the amber light, and listens to the storm. The wind is a physical presence — she can feel it in the vibration of the walls, in the way the building sways imperceptibly on its foundation. The snow is not falling; it is being driven, horizontal, packed against every surface. She is sealed in. She understands this without drama. The last time the roads were impassable for more than twenty-four hours was 2011. She has food for four days. The generator has fuel for three. She will be fine.

She climbs the stairs. She returns to bed. She sleeps.

At 3:41 a.m. — and the narrator knows the time because the narrator knows everything, including the things she will not learn until later — a sound begins in the preparation room. It is the sound of steel sliding against steel: a drawer opening. The runners are old and unlubricated and they produce a specific noise, a low metallic groan that travels through the building's bones, through the floor joists and plaster walls, up through the floorboards of the apartment and into the frame of her bed, where it enters her spine as vibration before it enters her ears as sound. She wakes. She lies still. The sound continues — the long, slow exhalation of a drawer reaching its full extension — and then stops.

She knows the sound. She has heard it ten thousand times. She knows which drawer, too, because each one has a slightly different voice — the runners worn to different tolerances, the weight of each body producing a different pitch. This is drawer three, the John Doe. Heavier than Purcell, lighter than Ash. The sound is unmistakable.

She gets up. She does not turn on the overhead light, because the overhead light is not on the generator circuit. She takes the flashlight from her bedside table — a heavy Maglite, aluminum, the kind that doubles as a weapon, which is not why she keeps it but is why her father bought it — and descends the stairs.

The preparation room door is open. She left it closed. The amber light from the sconce in the hallway reaches the first few feet of the room and stops. Beyond it, darkness, and the hum.

She turns on the flashlight.

Drawer three is open. Fully extended. The body bag is unzipped — not torn, not damaged, but unzipped, the zipper pull resting neatly to one side as though someone had opened it with care. The bag is empty. The John Doe is not inside. The bag retains the impression of his body — the wrinkled outline where shoulders and hips had pressed the vinyl — but the body itself is gone.

She sweeps the flashlight across the room. The beam catches the counter, the cosmetics kit she laid out hours ago, the magnifying lamp, the steel table, the instrument tray. Everything is in its place. The room is empty.

She steps forward. She checks drawers one and two. Latched. Occupied. She touches her own arms — a self-inventory, a grounding gesture, the kind of thing a person does when reality has shifted and the body needs to confirm its own continuity. She is standing in the preparation room in flannel pajamas and rubber-soled slippers, holding a flashlight, looking at an empty drawer that should contain a man.

Then she turns toward the chapel.

The chapel is through the next doorway — a room of eight pews, a lectern, a space designed for thirty mourners and rarely filled. In the amber half-light, the pews throw ladder-shadows across the floor. And in the fourth pew, sitting upright, hands resting on its thighs, is a figure.
