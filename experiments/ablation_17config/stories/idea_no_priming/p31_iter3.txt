The mop moved in arcs. Not the wide theatrical arcs of someone performing work for an audience but the short, efficient, slightly lopsided strokes of a woman who had calculated, through three years of repetition, the precise expenditure of energy required to clean 240 linear feet of corridor tile in the forty-seven minutes between checkpoint scans, leaving eleven minutes of the hour for wringing, bucket refill, and the walk to the next section, during which she could, if she kept her pace at what she privately thought of as her *thinking speed*—not fast, not slow, a speed that did not invite questions from the roving security detail or the other Corbin Services staff, most of whom she avoided because conversation cost time and time was money and money was the surgery—review the numbers again.

$14,200. The balance. Not the total—the total had been $61,340 before insurance, which she was required by federal law to have and which she had, through her Corbin contract, at a premium of $387 per month for the family plan, the family plan being necessary because Emeka's ears—both of them now, the left one first and then the right, the right being the one the ENT at Johns Hopkins called "more urgent though not yet emergent," a distinction she had come to understand meant *we will not operate today but we will hold you responsible if you wait too long*—required coverage that the individual plan did not provide. $14,200 after insurance. After the $4,000 deductible. After the co-insurance split. After the first $900 payment she had made in January from the savings she had been building for two years by not replacing the Corolla's tires, which were bald now, bald in a way that the man at Jiffy Lube said was "past worn, ma'am, that's cord showing," so that driving to Fort Meade in rain felt like steering through oil, the car drifting on 295 at 5:15 a.m., the wipers inadequate, her knuckles white. $14,200. She could break it into payments of $200 a month. At $200 a month it would take seventy-one months. Five years and eleven months. By which time Emeka would be twelve and Ada would be nine and the interest—

She stopped.

The mop stopped.

She stood in the silence of Corridor E-9 at 5:41 a.m. and listened to the sound that Fort Meade made when it believed it was alone: the hum. Not a single tone but a braided, layered, almost living vibration that came from behind the closed doors and through the walls and up from the floor—the sound of machines processing signals, running algorithms, sifting the communications of the world through filters she would never see and could not name. She had read, once, in a magazine in the waiting room of Emeka's ENT, that the NSA intercepted and stored more data in a single day than had existed in the entire world in 1990. She did not know if that was true. She did not know what a room full of that data would look like, or sound like, or whether the hum she felt in her molars was the vibration of all that captured language pressing against the building's structure, trying to get out.

She did know that the doors on either side of E-9 were closed and would remain closed and that her badge—the blue badge, the one the security detail glanced at and immediately dismissed, the one that said CORBIN and not NSA and not USA, the one that opened nothing—would not open them.

She dipped the mop. She wrung it. The water in the bucket was gray.

$14,200.

If she took a second job—weekends, something with tips, the Nigerian restaurant on University Boulevard was hiring but the owner was a man from Owerri who her mother's cousin said had trouble with his hands, meaning he put them on women, meaning no—or if she found a different kind of job, a night shift somewhere in addition to this night shift, something she could do between 2:00 a.m. when she finished here and 6:30 a.m. when the children woke—but the children could not wake alone, she had made this rule and would not break it, she would be in the apartment when their eyes opened even if her own eyes were closing, even if the exhaustion was a kind of weather system that moved through her body on a schedule she could predict, the fog rolling in at 3 p.m. regardless of how much coffee she drank—

She mopped.

To her left, a door she had never seen open bore a placard that read, in small letters: **TS/SCI — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY — NO LONE ZONE**. She had cleaned in front of that door for three years. She had never wondered what was behind it for longer than the time it took the thought to arrive and depart, because wondering was not useful, because the door was not for her, because she was not, in Fort Meade's taxonomy of human relevance, a person who warranted a door.

The cameras in E-9 were positioned at either end of the corridor, angled toward the access points. She had noticed, in her first month, that the cameras tracked the suited personnel who badged through the doors—followed them, adjusted, recorded. When she passed beneath them, they did not move. She had tested this once, early on, feeling foolish, walking slowly beneath the nearest camera and then stopping and then waving, actually waving, at the small dark eye in its ceiling housing. It had not moved. She was not in the system's field of concern. She was part of the corridor. She was the mop and the bucket and the gray water, furniture that happened to breathe.

$14,200.

If she calculated it differently. If she took the tax refund in February—$2,100, maybe, if the EITC came through, if the preparer on Oakford Road didn't make the mistake he'd made last year that triggered the review that delayed the refund by eleven weeks—she could pay $2,100 in February and then $200 a month and then it would be—

She moved her lips in the silence of E-9.

Sixty months and a half. Five years. Emeka would be eleven. Ada would be eight.

She mopped, and the building hummed around her, full of other people's secrets that she carried past without hearing, or so everyone believed, or so she herself believed, which was almost the same thing.

She heard about the study from a flyer pinned beneath a business card for a tax preparer and above a torn sheet advertising a 2004 Honda Civic, and what she saw before she saw the words was the number. **$200 for 3 hours.** The number was printed in a font larger than the rest, which meant the people who made the flyer understood what she understood: that the number was the message and the rest was just the mechanism for earning it.

*Brain Imaging Study. Healthy adults 30–55. No history of neurological disease. Three hours, one session. Earn $200 for participating in cutting-edge neuroscience research.*

She tore off a phone number tab. She called from the parking lot of the laundromat while Ada slept in the car seat and the Corolla's engine ticked. $200 was the copay she had deferred. $200 was the space between the checking account balance on the 28th of the month and the rent on the 1st. $200 was not nothing.

---

The machine was louder than she expected.

They had told her it would be loud. The intake coordinator, a young woman with a badge on a lanyard and the particular bright exhaustion of a graduate student, had said: "It's going to sound like a jackhammer inside a garbage truck, basically, but you'll have earplugs and you can listen to music if you want." Miriam had declined the music. She did not want distraction. She wanted to lie still and do whatever they asked her to do and think about nothing and get the card and go.

They slid her into the bore. The ceiling of the machine was close—eight, maybe ten inches from her face—and white, and she could feel the magnetic field like a weather, a pressure that was not a pressure, a hum that was not the Fort Meade hum but was, she thought, its cousin: the sound of a machine that wanted to see inside things. A coil enclosed her head like a loose cage. She held the squeeze-ball they'd given her in her right hand—"Squeeze this if you need to stop at any time, for any reason"—and she breathed and she was still.

The first task was simple. Watch the screen. A small mirror angled above her eyes reflected a projection: arrows pointing left or right. Press the left button if the arrow points left. Press the right button if the arrow points right. She pressed. Her accuracy, she would later learn from a file she was not meant to see, was 99.2%. Her reaction time was unremarkable. She was, in the language of the study, "a compliant and attentive participant."

The second task was also simple. Close your eyes. Think of nothing. Rest.

She closed her eyes. She did not think of nothing. She thought of the $200, and then of the balance, and then of Emeka's ear, the left one, the way he tilted his head toward her like a bird when she spoke to him from his right side because his right ear was the bad one, the one the surgery was for, and how she had never mentioned this tilt to him because she did not want him to know that she watched him the way she watched him, with the specificity of terror, monitoring him for signs that the world was taking things from him that she could not afford to replace.

In the adjacent room—the control room, she had seen it briefly when they walked her past, a dim space full of monitors and the blue glow of displays showing what she assumed were images of brains—a research assistant named Paul Osei sat in a rolling chair and watched the scan populate in real time and thought about his wife.

He was not thinking about his wife deliberately. He was supposed to be attending to the scan parameters, checking the slice prescriptions, confirming the TR and TE values, and he was doing these things, but underneath the doing—in the substrate of his attention, the part of the mind that runs its own programs regardless of what the foreground demands—he was thinking about the argument they'd had that morning about the lease. The lease on their apartment in Hampden was expiring in October and Anna wanted to renew for two years because the rent increase on a one-year renewal was $150 more per month, but a two-year renewal locked them in, and Paul didn't want to be locked in because he was applying for a postdoc at UCL and if he got it they'd need to break the lease and the early termination fee was two months' rent, which was $4,600, which they did not have, and he had said this to Anna in what he believed was a reasonable tone, and Anna had said, "You are always planning for a future that doesn't include the one we actually have," which had struck him as both unfair and—this was the part that was circling in his mind as he watched Miriam Oke's resting-state scan populate—accurate. He felt the accuracy of it like a small burn. He was thinking: *She's right. I am always leaving.*

Miriam, inside the scanner, felt the burn.

She did not identify it as someone else's feeling. It arrived the way such things had always arrived: as a sudden, sourceless shift in emotional weather, like stepping from sunlight into the shadow of a building she hadn't seen. One moment she was thinking about Emeka's ear. The next moment she was inhabited by a guilt that had a different texture than her own guilt—thinner, more abstract, laced with a specific image she couldn't quite resolve, something about a window and a woman's back turned toward it and rainlight—and she thought: *I am always leaving.* And then the thought was gone, or not gone but absorbed, folded into the general noise of being alive in a body in a loud machine thinking too many things at once, and she returned to the question of the balance, which was $14,200, which was where she always returned.

But the machine saw.
