# The Laundromat at the End of Everything

## I.

Machine 3 has a trick where, nine minutes into the spin cycle, it hitches—Loss of rhythm, half-second stutter, like a heart skipping—and then catches itself and goes on like nothing happened. You wouldn't notice unless you'd been listening to it five nights a week for three years running, which I have, so I do. I notice it every time. I could set a watch by it, if I owned a watch, which I don't, because the only time that matters in here is dryer time, and that's printed right on the dial: sixty minutes for a dollar fifty, except Machine 8 runs hot and will scorch your towels at forty, and Machine 2 runs cold and takes ninety to do what the others do in sixty, and the regulars know this, and the new people learn it the hard way or they learn it from me, standing behind the register with my inventory of small mercies.

The register, by the way, doesn't open. Hasn't opened since before I started. Mr. Villanueva—he owns the place, lives in Summerlin, comes by on the first and fifteenth to collect from the coin traps and leave me an envelope with a check in it—he said he'd fix it in 2021. It is not 2021. The register sits there like a little locked mouth, which is fine because nobody pays me for anything out of a register. They pay the machines. I am scenery. I am the person who refills the detergent vending panel and mops the linoleum and tapes the OUT OF ORDER signs to whatever's broken this week, and if that sounds like loneliness, I need you to understand: it isn't. Or it wasn't.

The linoleum is a shade of green that does not occur in nature. I have tried to identify it. Not sage, not olive, not seafoam, not mint. It is the green of a thing that wanted to be another color and got talked out of it. There are fourteen stains I can identify from memory: the ancient bleach ghost by Machine 1 that looks like a map of Italy if Italy had a tumor; the coffee stain near the folding table shaped like a dog's ear; the one under Machine 12 that is either rust or blood and which I have chosen not to investigate because there are questions a night-shift attendant learns not to ask if he wants to keep sleeping.

The vending machine sells exactly one product now. It used to sell Coke, Sprite, Dr Pepper, and water. Something happened to its internal mechanism—probably around the same time the register gave up—and now every button, regardless of label, produces a room-temperature grape soda. Grape Fanta, specifically. The can drops into the retrieval slot with a sound like a small, resigned bell. Seventy-five cents. It is the most reliable transaction available in the building.

The fluorescent lights—there are eighteen of them, recessed panel fixtures in a dropped ceiling that's water-stained in three places—they buzz. Not constantly. They warm up. From midnight to about 1:30, they're nearly silent, just a faint electrical presence you feel more in your teeth than your ears. But around 1:47, give or take, the ballast in the third fixture from the door hits its stride, and there's this low, resonant hum that turns the whole room into the inside of a tuning fork. It used to bother me. Now it's the sound of the middle of the night, which is the sound of the world leaving you alone, which is the sound of peace.

I am aware that I'm describing a laundromat on East Tropicana in the voice of a man describing the Sistine Chapel. I know that. I know what this place looks like from the outside at 2 AM—the stuttering OPEN sign, the fogged-up windows, the parking lot with its one shot streetlight and its constellation of cigarette butts. I know what I look like in it: thirty-one, sitting on a plastic chair behind a register that doesn't open, reading a water-damaged paperback or staring at the dryers like they're a fireplace. I know how that reads.

But here's the thing about a room where you spend enough nights: it stops being a room. It becomes the dimensions of your life. The twenty-six steps from the door to the back wall. The eleven machines on the left, the twelve on the right, the folding table in the middle like an altar. The air that smells like detergent and warm lint and, faintly, grape. You learn a place the way you learn a body—by repetition, by attendance, by being there when no one else wants to be.

The regulars: there's Dave, who comes Tuesdays and Thursdays around 11 PM with a single sleeping bag he washes on hot, who served in Fallujah and doesn't talk about it but who will talk about baseball with an encyclopedic recall that makes me think he carries the stats around as a kind of ballast against other memories. There's the woman I think of as Mrs. Nguyen, though we've never exchanged names, who comes Monday mornings just before my shift ends, always with a rolling cart, always with six loads sorted by color with a precision that borders on liturgical. There's the kid—maybe nineteen, maybe twenty-two, impossible to tell—who comes irregularly, always at the last possible hour, always washing the same oversized hoodie and sitting on the floor with earbuds in, not looking at anyone, radiating the specific misery of a person who does not have a washing machine because they do not have a fixed address.

And there's Maren.

Maren, who has never once brought laundry.

I don't know when she started coming. That's not true—I do know, approximately, the way you know when a season changed: not the day, but the feeling of before and after. It was over a year ago. Maybe fourteen months. She walked in around 2 AM on a nothing Tuesday, looked around the way you look around a place you've ducked into to avoid weather, and sat on the folding table. Not a chair. The table. Legs dangling, boots not quite reaching the floor. She had short dark hair and hands that wouldn't stay still and a face that looked like it was always in the middle of deciding whether to be amused or furious.

She didn't have laundry. She didn't explain not having laundry. She sat on the table and looked at the machines spinning and said, "This is the most boring aquarium I've ever been to."

I said, "The fish are in the back. These are just the filters."

She looked at me—really looked—and her mouth did something that was almost a smile, and she said, "Do you have anything to drink in this place?"

I pointed her to the vending machine. She pressed the Sprite button. The grape soda dropped. She opened it, drank it without comment, stayed until 3:15, and left.

She came back the next night.

And the next.

Not every night. Maybe four out of seven, sometimes five. Always around 2 AM, always laundryless, always sitting on the folding table like it was a dock and she was waiting for a ship. She'd drink the grape soda—she never pressed the grape soda button; she always pressed a different one, Coke or water or Dr Pepper, and accepted the grape Fanta with what I came to understand was a kind of philosophical commitment to receiving whatever the universe handed her—and she'd talk.

Or we'd talk. The distinction dissolved early. She'd say something and I'd say something and the machines would hum and the fluorescents would buzz and the hours would pass the way hours only pass when you're not watching them, which is the only way worth passing them.

I learned things about her in the way you learn things at 2 AM, which is sideways, in fragments, through details that aren't framed as revelations. I learned she worked at one of the casinos off the Strip—not the big ones, one of the mid-tier places with a loyalty program and a steakhouse that's always running a two-for-one. She was a blackjack dealer. I figured this out not because she told me but because of her hands. When she talked, her hands moved in this specific way—lateral, fluid, precise—and one night I realized she was miming the motions. The deal, the sweep, the flip. Shuffling air.

"You deal," I said.

She looked at her hands like she'd been caught at something. "Eight hours a day, five days a week, you become the job." She wiggled her fingers. "These don't belong to me anymore. They belong to third base and the shoe."

"That's bleak."

"That's Tuesday."

I learned she lived alone—apartment, east side, landlord who thought smoke detectors were decorative. I learned she didn't cook, survived on casino cafeteria food and gas station coffee. I learned she read—not a lot, but specifically: true crime, planetary science, one Edith Wharton novel she'd stolen from a hotel lobby and reread every year. I learned she had a sister in Tucson she called twice a month and a mother she didn't call at all.

I didn't learn these things in one night. I learned them the way sediment builds: layer by layer, night by night, each detail settling onto the last until there was a topography.

What I gave her in return: jokes. That was my currency. Not good jokes—not polished, not rehearsed. The kind of jokes that only work at 2 AM in a laundromat, which is to say the kind of jokes that are really just observations made at a slight angle. I'd tell her about Dave's baseball statistics, about Mrs. Nguyen's color-sorting system, about the time Machine 7's drum light exploded and I thought, for one genuine second, that I was being raptured. She'd laugh. Not every time. She had standards. But when she laughed, she laughed with her whole torso, leaning back on the folding table with her palms flat behind her, and the sound filled the room the way the ballast hum filled it—not loud, but *present*, vibrating in the walls.

I want to describe one night. This is the night I'm calling Night One, even though it wasn't the first night—it was maybe the hundredth—because it's the night I keep returning to, the night I measure everything else against. It's a Wednesday. She's on the table. I'm behind the register. The only other person in the place is a man asleep in the chair by the window, his laundry long finished in Machine 5, and we've both silently agreed to let him sleep because where else is he going to go.

She's telling me about a player she dealt to that night, a guy who bet the table minimum for four hours straight and never changed his expression. "He wasn't winning," she said. "He wasn't losing. He was just... there. Sitting. Playing. Like the cards were a form of breathing."

"Maybe they were."

"That's depressing."

"It's not depressing. Some people breathe by running. Some people breathe by painting. Some people breathe by playing blackjack at a mid-tier casino on a Wednesday."

"Some people breathe by sitting in a laundromat at 2 AM talking to the attendant."

She said it while looking at Machine 9, not at me, and her voice was the same voice she used for everything—level, dry, faintly amused—and I understood that she had just told me something enormous and that the only appropriate response was to not treat it as enormous, because treating it as enormous would change the composition of the air in the room, and the air in the room was, at that moment, perfect.

"The attendant breathes by being sat with," I said. "It's symbiotic. Like those fish that clean sharks' teeth."

"Which one of us is the shark?"

"You. Obviously. I'm the little fish. I just hang around and eat scraps."

"Scraps," she repeated, and she was smiling, and the fluorescent at the far end flickered because its ballast was dying, and the sleeping man by the window shifted in his chair, and Machine 3 did its nine-minute hitch, and the room held all of us in its green linoleum palm, and I did not say what I was thinking, which was: *I would live inside this moment if the architecture of time allowed it.*

What I said was: "You want another grape soda?"

"Hit me," she said, and the dealer hands flipped an invisible card.

I'm telling you about this night because I need you to know what this room was. Not what it looked like—I've told you what it looked like, the stains and the machines and the dropped ceiling—but what it *was*. What it was to me, and what I believe it was to her. It was the one room. The room where nobody was paying for anything except dryer time. The room where nobody was performing, or owing, or calculating. The room where a man could sit behind a broken register and a woman could sit on a folding table and the distance between them—eleven feet, I've measured, from the edge of the register counter to the near edge of the table—could be the exact right distance, not too close, not too far, the distance at which two people can tell each other the truth in the guise of jokes and the truth will travel the air between them and arrive intact.

I'm telling you about this night because I need you to love this room before I tell you what happened to it.

The fogged windows. The hum. The grape soda. Machine 3's skipped heartbeat. Maren's laugh and Maren's hands and the eleven feet of air between us that held, each night, the shape of everything I didn't say.

I'm telling you about this room the way a man describes a house that's already on fire.

## II.

She didn't come on a Thursday, which wasn't unusual. Thursdays were uneven—sometimes she worked late, sometimes the casino ran a tournament and dealers pulled doubles. I'd learned not to assign meaning to her absences the way I'd learned not to assign meaning to Minnesota Twins statistics, which is to say I'd learned the *discipline* of not assigning meaning, which is a different thing from actually not assigning it.

It was 12:40 AM. The place was empty. I'd mopped. I'd restocked the detergent panel. I'd checked that the OUT OF ORDER sign on Machine 4 was still taped straight because it bothered me when it peeled at the corner and hung crooked, which is the kind of thing that bothers you when the alternative is sitting still with your own thoughts.

Machine 4. The big industrial unit in the back corner, the kind you'd use for comforters, sleeping bags, things that don't fit in the standard drums. Its door had broken three weeks earlier—the latch mechanism failed, so the door wouldn't seal, so the drum wouldn't engage, so it sat there, mute, a large metal box pretending to be a washing machine. Mr. Villanueva had said he'd send a repair guy. He had not sent a repair guy. I'd taped the sign. OUT OF ORDER. I'd taped it neatly because I take a stupid amount of pride in the things I can control, which is tape and signage and nothing else.

I don't know why I opened it. That's not true. I opened it because I opened everything, because checking the machines was something to do with my hands, because I was the kind of person who straightened the OUT OF ORDER sign and wiped the lint traps and pushed the chairs into alignment against the wall—a person who maintained, who tended, who circled the room like a monk circling a cloister. I opened Machine 4's door because it was a door and I was a person who opened doors and looked inside.

The duffel bag was sitting in the drum.

Black. Nylon. The kind of bag you'd bring to a gym if you went to a gym, which I don't, which most people who use laundromats at midnight don't. One zipper, top-loading, no branding. It sat in the stainless-steel drum like something placed there carefully—not tossed, not stuffed. Placed.

I looked at it for what felt like a long time.

Then I looked behind me, at the empty laundromat, at the fogged windows, at the door that no one was walking through.

I unzipped the bag.
