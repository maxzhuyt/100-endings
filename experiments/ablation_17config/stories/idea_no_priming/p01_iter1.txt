# The Alkali Deed

## I.

The land was white. That was the first correction. I had carried green in my mind for eleven hundred miles—green the color of the little park on Eutaw Street, green the color of the word *valley* when Ephraim read it aloud in the room above Kessler's shop, his finger tracing the listing as if the ink were a road he could already walk—and the land was white. White as a stripped bone. White as the inside of a skull. I thought: no. I thought: that is salt. I thought: salt means water below, salt means a lakebed dried, and lakebeds hold richness. But the white was not salt. The white was alkali. The chalk earth of a place that had never been a lake, had never held anything, had been scoured to its mineral confession by a wind that—

The wind. I must describe the wind, because the wind was the second correction. In Baltimore the wind had edges. It came off the harbor and hit brick and broke into smaller winds that curled around corners, that carried the smell of creosote and tar and the oyster shells banked along the wharves. A wind with an architecture. Here the wind had no edge because it had no obstruction. It did not blow *past* us. It blew *through* the space we occupied as if we were not in it. As if we were the least interesting objects the wind had ever encountered, and after eleven hundred miles of encountering nothing, we were still not enough to make it turn. The wind went from the northwest to the southeast and it had been doing this, I understood all at once, since before there was anything to call northwest or southeast. Since before there were words.

So I looked again. I made my eyes look again.

Six hundred and forty acres. A section. Ours—the word catches, even now, like a bone in the throat—*ours* by the terms of a deed I carried in the cedar box beneath the second-best quilt, a deed by which the government of the United States transferred to Ephraim James Hollis and Clara Vass Hollis, his wife, the right to work this land and to call the working a life. Six hundred and forty acres of alkali flat stretching in every direction to a horizon that shimmered and lied, because the shimmer looked like water but there was no water, the shimmer was heat on chalk, the shimmer was the land making promises the land could not keep—

No.

I must be precise. The *listing* made promises. The land made nothing. The land lay there as it had always lain, indifferent as a face that does not know it is being watched, and the shimmer was physics, was light bending through heat, and it was my mind—*my* mind—that insisted on reading water into the bending, because my mind had carried green for eleven hundred miles, and the green had a creek in it, and the creek had willows, and the willows dropped shade onto a porch where I would—

Ephraim turned to me. His face. I need to say what his face was doing, but I find I cannot separate what his face was doing from what my face must have been doing, because his expression was a mirror held up to mine—no. His expression was a *correction* of mine. Whatever my face showed, his face was already answering: the jaw set, the eyes narrowing not against the wind but against the possibility that I might speak first and say the thing he could see I was thinking. He spoke before I could.

"The well will want digging deep," he said.

This was the third correction. Not the land, not the wind, but Ephraim's voice already building the future tense around the blank white fact of the present. *Will want.* As if the well were a creature with desires, as if depth were a matter of want and not of geology, as if the forty feet between the surface and whatever the surface concealed could be crossed by the same stubbornness that had carried us from a rented room above a butcher shop to this white, treeless, wind-scoured corner of Nebraska Territory.

I said nothing. The wind said nothing. The land said nothing as loudly as it could.

## II.

The taste of the dust. That was what broke the seal.

It was alkaline and chalky and it coated the inside of my lips within minutes of stepping down from the wagon, and the taste was—the taste was like the paste Mrs. Beckham had spread on the wallpaper in the upstairs hall of the boardinghouse on Pratt Street where I had waited for Ephraim to finish his accounts three evenings a week, wheat paste, the particular adhesive of *putting one thing onto another thing and hoping it holds*—and the memory opened before I could close it, the way a door opens when the latch has not caught and the wind finds the gap.

The room above Kessler's. November. The lamp with the cracked chimney that threw a line of shadow across the ceiling like a crack in the plaster, and Ephraim sitting on the edge of the bed with the *Baltimore Sun* open on his knees, the land office listings running in a column as dense and small as scripture, and his finger—his right index finger, the nail split from the press at Harwood & Sons where he set type ten hours a day for a wage that covered the room and the lamp oil and nothing else—his finger stopped.

"Clara. *Clara.*"

I was mending. I remember this because I was mending his other shirt, the gray one, and the needle was in my mouth and I had to take it out to answer, and the needle was warm and tasted of metal, and this taste—the needle's copper-bright sharpness—is the opposite of the alkali, is the taste of *before*, and yet the two tastes now occupy the same space in my mouth as if the eleven hundred miles between them had collapsed into a single swallow.

"Six hundred and forty acres," he read. "Platte River valley. Rich bottomland. Creek on the western section. Timber. Government deed. Forty dollars and proof of habitation."

He looked up. And here is the thing I cannot— Here is the thing I have circled and circled and not yet landed upon, the thing that sits at the center of every subsequent night and every swallowed word—

His face, reading it, was the face I had married. Not the face worn thin by the press and the lamp oil and the calculation of what we could not afford. The face from before. The face of the man who had stood in the doorway of my father's shop on Lexington Market and said, *I have an idea about our life*, and the idea had been so large and so strange and so beautiful that I had followed it out the door and into a marriage that fit like a shoe one size too small—not painful, not yet, but present, always present, the awareness that something was not quite— That the life above Kessler's was not the life he had meant, and that the distance between the life he had meant and the life we were living was a distance he felt in his body, in the slump of his shoulders over the press, in the silence after supper when he sat with his split-nailed hands open on his knees as if waiting for someone to place something in them.

But that night. The listing. *Platte River valley.* His voice reading it had the sound of a man who has been holding his breath for four years and has just been told he may exhale.

And I—

I looked at the listing. I read it over his shoulder. The words were small and smudged and the column was dense and the type was the same type Ephraim set every day at the press, ten-point Roman, and I knew—I *knew*—how easily a word could slip in the compositing stick, how *bottom* could lose its second *t* and become *botom*, how *river* could become *rises*, how the space between what was printed and what was true could be as narrow as a single letter and as wide as—

Did I see it? Did I see the way the listing said *Platte River valley* without specifying which section, without naming the creek, without any particular that would bind the promise to a plot? Did I notice that *rich bottomland* is a phrase and not a survey, that *timber* is a word and not a tree?

I have asked myself this for every mile of eleven hundred miles. I have asked myself at the bottom of every loop my mind makes in the dark. And the answer is: I do not know. I do not know whether I saw and said nothing or whether I did not see because I was looking at his face. Because his face, reading it, was the happiest I had ever seen it. Because the face was more legible than the listing and I chose to read the face.

He stood up. He paced the narrow room—four steps to the window, four steps back, the floor creaking on the third step, always the third—and he began to list what we would need to do.

"The furniture," he said. "We sell the furniture."

And we did. The oak table that had been his mother's, the one with the cigarette burn from his uncle Amos shaped like the state of Maryland. Fourteen dollars. The chairs, five for the set. The bed frame, though we kept the mattress until the last night and then sold it to Kessler's wife for three dollars that she later said was two too many. The press, his share of the press—Harwood bought him out for sixty dollars which was half what it was worth and Ephraim knew this and did not argue because arguing would have cost a day and a day was a day further from the listing.

My mother's clock. I must write it plainly or I will circle it forever. My mother's clock, the brass-and-walnut mantel clock with the chime that sounded on the quarter hour, the chime I had heard in my sleep every night of my girlhood, the chime that was indistinguishable from the sound of *safety*—sold to a man on Hanover Street for nine dollars. I wrapped it in the same muslin I had used to wrap it when we carried it from my mother's house after she died. I set it on his counter. He wound it, checked the chime, nodded. He did not haggle. Nine dollars was what it was worth and not a fraction of what it cost.

The books. Seventeen volumes, including the Shakespeare Ephraim had bought me the first Christmas, the one with the red binding and the gilt-edged pages that caught the lamp light and threw it back in small gold coins across the quilt. Four dollars for the lot. The buyer did not even open them.

Ephraim's good coat, the dark wool, the one he had worn the day we married. Two dollars. His razor. Fifty cents. My sewing box, the one my father had built with the compartments fitted so precisely that each spool sat in its well without rattling. I could not sell this. I packed it in the cedar box with the deed.

Ninety-seven dollars, all told, once you subtracted the deed fee and the wagon and the mule and the provisions. Ninety-seven dollars which had been, the day before, a life. A small life, a life that fit like a shoe one size too small, but a life with walls and a clock that chimed and a Shakespeare with gilt pages and a husband whose face, though tired, was a face I could see by lamplight every night across a table that had belonged to his mother.

We burned Baltimore to the ground. We burned it bill by bill, and the ashes bought us a piece of paper in a cedar box, and the piece of paper said we owned six hundred and forty acres in the Platte River valley, and the Platte River valley said nothing back, because the acres were not in the valley. The acres were here. On the flat. In the white. In the wind.

## III.

He began the morning after we arrived.

I should say: he began before I woke, which means he began in the dark, which means he lay beside me under the canvas we had strung between the wagon bed and a stake he drove into soil soite it rang, and at some hour I cannot name he untangled his body from mine—here I felt it, the withdrawal of warmth along my right side as specific as a blade drawn from a sheath—and he rose and went out into the dark and began to cut the land into bricks.

I found him at first light. He had used the spade and the hand-saw and he had cut eighteen blocks of sod, each one roughly two hands wide by three hands long by one hand deep, and he had stacked them in a line that described— I did not understand until the fourth day what the line described. I thought he was building a wall. He was building a *room*.

The alkali soil was not soil. I know this now but I did not know it then; I knew only what I saw, which was that when the spade struck the surface it did not sink the way a spade sinks into earth. It skidded. It rang. The top two inches wereite chalk,ite calcium fused by a hundred years of wind and no rain into something closer to calciumite than to dirt, and cutting it required not the smooth downward stroke of a man turning a garden but the angled, repeated, chipping strike of a man splitting stone. Each block took—I timed it, because my mind needed a task and my hands had none—each block took between eleven and fourteen minutes and left a white gash in the ground like a wound that would not bleed.

By the second day his hands had opened. The chalk powder found every crease in his palms and then, with the patience of a thing that has no need to hurry, *widened* the creases into splits. Not cuts—cuts are clean, cuts have edges. These were fissures, like creek beds in miniature, following the lines of his hands as if the chalk were reading his fortune and disagreeing with it. The blood that came was thin and pale because the chalk absorbed the red and left a pinkish paste in the cracks of his skin.

I said: "Let me wrap them."

He said: "They'll toughen."

They did not toughen. Each morning the chalk reopened what each night's rest had begun to close, and by the third day the fissures had deepened to a point where I could see, beneath the crusted pink, the wet red of the underlayer, and I understood that the chalk was not merely drying his skin but *replacing* it—calciumite powder filling the cracks and fusing with the raw tissue beneath until his hands were not entirely flesh and not entirelyite— until his hands were becoming the land's material, as if the house he was building required not just his labor but his *substance*.

I watched. I cataloged. I did not look away because looking away felt like a lie—a refusal to see what my silence had purchased. Eighteen blocks. Then forty. Then a hundred and ten. He stacked them with a precision that I recognized from the press, from the way he set type: each block aligned to the block beneath it by feel, by the nerve-memory in his ruined fingertips, by a rightness he could not explain and did not try to. The walls rose. One foot. Two feet. Knee-height. Waist-height. The gaps between blocks he filled with a slurry of alkali dust and water hauled from the creek six miles southeast—not the creek from the listing, not the creek that was supposed to be on our western section, but a thin, seasonal rivulet that tasted of chalk and required a twelve-mile round trip with the mule.

On the fourth day I understood the shape. It was not a rectangle. It was a rectangle with an *extension*—a three-sided projection on the eastern wall, open to the sky, roofed by nothing because there was nothing to roof it with, three low walls of sod forming a space approximately six feet wide and four feet deep.

A porch.

The porch faced east.

I stood behind him—he was fitting the last block of the porch's southern wall, pressing it into place with both bloodied palms, adjusting it by fractions—and the recognition came not as a thought but as a physical event, a contraction in the center of my chest as if a hand had closed around my heart and *squeezed*, because I remembered. I remembered the evening on Pratt Street, the evening I had waited for him outside Harwood & Sons, and the boarding house across the street had been lit by the last of the sunset and a woman was sitting on the porch with a cup in her hand watching the light go, and I had said—

I had said it to no one. I had said it to the air, or to myself, or to the evening, the way a person says a thing that is not a request but a description of a life not lived, a life glimpsed through a window in a city where porches are common and sunrises are blocked by buildings—

"I should like to sit on a porch some morning and watch the sun come up with nothing in the way."

He had been beside me. He had heard.
