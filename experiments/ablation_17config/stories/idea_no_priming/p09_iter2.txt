# The Last Cartography of Dara Keane

---

## I.

You need to understand something before I go any further. This is not a story I am telling comfortably. This is not a story I am telling from a desk, or from memory, or from any of the warm safe distances a person puts between herself and the things she has survived. I am telling this from the third floor of St. Erasmus Memorial Hospital, east wing, last shuttered ward, room 309, where the overhead fluorescents have been off for — I want to say eleven months. I want to say that with confidence. I have tallies. I will show you the tallies. They are on the wall behind the bed, scratched into the paint with the edge of a ballpoint pen cap, and they do not add up to eleven months, but I will show them to you anyway because I need you to see how carefully I've been trying.

My name is Dara Keane. I am — I was — a registered nurse. ICU credentialed, neuro-specialty, twelve years. I have been alone in this building for the duration of those tallies, however many they are, and I have been watching over a comatose patient named Eli Vargas, and I can see the inside of his mind.

I don't mean metaphorically. I don't mean I've grown attuned to his expressions or learned to interpret his vitals with unusual sensitivity. I mean that when I sit beside his bed and look at him — at his body, at the specific geography of his face — something opens, and behind his face there is a city, and I can see its streets. I can see its buildings. I can see the weather systems of his dreaming, the way fear condenses and falls as a kind of rain, the way memory accumulates in drifts against the walls of structures his subconscious has built and will not stop building.

I do not understand how this works. I have not understood it for a long time, and I have stopped expecting that I will.

I need to tell you this now, before the telling changes, because I am no longer certain this story is coming from where I think it is. There is a possibility — I am going to state it plainly, the way I was taught to state a patient's prognosis when the family asks and the family deserves the truth — there is a possibility that what you are receiving is not a human account transmitted from a real place to a real reader. There is a possibility that this narration is itself an artifact of the system I am about to describe to you, generated inside the architecture I believed I was only observing. I don't know. I have no way to verify from in here. But you should know the provenance of this document is in question. You should know that before you decide to trust any of it.

I'm telling you anyway. I'm telling *you* — not an abstract anyone, not a future investigator, not a sympathetic void. You. Whoever is holding this. Whoever is reading or hearing or processing these words. I need a witness. I think I am disappearing, and the specific way I am disappearing requires that someone on the outside know it happened, even if the someone is uncertain and the outside is theoretical. Even if you conclude, at the end, that I never existed.

Stay with me. I'll be as honest as I can. That qualifier — *as I can* — is the one I need you to hold in both hands for the rest of this. It is doing more work than it looks like it's doing.

---

## II.

The year the Quiet Plague started is the year I stopped eating breakfast, which is a stupid way to timestamp a civilizational catastrophe but is the only honest anchor I have for it. I stopped eating breakfast because I was arriving at St. Erasmus for the six a.m. shift and the intake ward was already full by the time I clocked in, and there was no time, and in those first weeks there was a specific smell — not decay, not infection, not any of the smells that training teaches you to associate with crisis. It was a clean smell. That was the wrongness. The patients smelled like nothing. Like absence. Like a room where a person had been standing and then simply wasn't. I would gag on it, that clean nothing, and breakfast became impossible.

They came in calm. That was the other wrongness. You prepare — your body prepares, your adrenal architecture prepares — for screaming, for blood oxygen crashes, for the frantic metabolic theater of bodies fighting to stay alive. None of that. They walked in or were walked in and their vitals were textbook. Heart rate nominal. Respiration nominal. Temperature a clean 98.6, every one of them, as if even their thermodynamics had been standardized. They sat on the edge of the examination table and they looked at you and they answered questions, but the answers did not correspond to the questions, and the answers did not correspond to each other, and after a while you understood that the person in front of you was not experiencing the same room you were experiencing. They were *somewhere*. They were perceiving *something*. But it was not here, and it was not shared, and when you put two patients in the same room and asked them to describe what they saw, they described two irreconcilable worlds with the same calm, steady certainty.

The term the media settled on was *perceptual divergence*. The term the nurses used among ourselves, in the break room, in the thirty seconds between one impossible intake and the next, was *gone*. They're gone. Not dead. Not unconscious. Just — departed, into private universes that had no exit and no common border.

You want to know the specific face of it? I'll give you one. A man named Gerald, sixty-two, retired electrician, brought in by his wife who said he'd stopped recognizing their house. He was sitting up. He was lucid, verbal, cooperative. I asked him where he was. He said he was standing in a wheat field and the wheat was made of static and there was a dog watching him from the horizon line and the dog was the size of a cathedral. He said this the way you'd say "I'm in a hospital." He was calm. His pulse was sixty-eight. His wife was crying behind the curtain and he was describing, with the measured patience of a man giving directions to a lost tourist, the dimensions of an impossible dog in an impossible field, and when I asked him if he could see *me*, he said, "Of course. You're the woman in the static. You're the one the dog is waiting for." He smiled. It was a kind smile.

That was day nine. By day forty there were more Geralds than there were beds. By day ninety there were more Geralds than there were cities organized enough to count them.

The Loom was the answer. I'm going to tell you about the Loom now, and I need you to understand that the word is not a metaphor. It is an acronym — Linked Oneiric Override Matrix — but no one used the full name, because the full name made it sound like what it was, and what it was was too frightening to say in clinical settings. What it was, stripped of the procurement language and the emergency-authorization jargon, was this: a neural-interface infrastructure, deployed at scale, designed to enter a divergent mind and impose a shared perceptual framework over its private hallucination. A scaffold. A standardized consensus architecture laid over the wild, idiosyncratic terrors of individual psychosis like a grid over unmapped territory. The idea was that if you couldn't bring the patients back to shared reality, you could at least build a *new* shared reality inside them, a stable common space where treatment could occur, where therapeutic contact was possible, where they could *see each other* again even if they could no longer see us.

I remember the night they activated it at St. Erasmus. I was on the fourth floor, charting, and I heard it through the walls — not a mechanical sound, not a hum exactly, more like a resonance, the way you can feel a very large engine through the soles of your shoes even when you can't hear it. The lights browned. Not flickered — *browned*, the color temperature dropping, as if the building's electrical capacity had been redistributed and the surplus was going somewhere invisible. I went down to the ward. I stood in the doorway. I watched the Loom take hold.

There is a specific expression a face makes at the moment the interface engages. I have seen it on more faces than I can count but I have never found the right clinical language for it. The closest I can get is this: it looks like watching someone step backward off a pier. The surprise is there — the eyes widen, the jaw slackens — but the body doesn't fight. The body *accepts*. There is a half second where the face holds everything — the terror and the relief and the surrender — and then it goes smooth, and the person behind it is elsewhere, inside the common architecture, and what remains in the bed is a body running at minimal autonomic output while the mind is housed in a consensual infrastructure it did not build and cannot leave.

They told us it was temporary. They told us it was treatment. They told us the scaffold would stabilize the divergent minds and then the minds would be extracted, reintegrated, reintroduced to baseline reality like divers returning slowly from depth. That was the language: *controlled ascent*. It implied a bottom and a surface and a body that wanted to rise.

What they did not tell us — what took months to surface, what I think some of them knew and could not bear to know — was that the scaffold had fused. The Loom's shared architecture was not a temporary framework overlaid on damaged minds; it had *integrated*. The patients' subconscious processes had colonized the scaffold the way root systems colonize soil, threading through every strut and joint until the structure and the psyche were indistinguishable. Removing the Loom would not free the patient. Removing the Loom would collapse the only coherent reality the patient's mind still recognized. The scaffold had become the ground they stood on. The cage had become the world.

After that, the facilities began to close. Not because the crisis was over but because the definition of *crisis* had quietly shifted. The patients were stable. The patients were alive. The patients were, inside the Loom, experiencing a shared environment that registered, on every metric the Loom's monitoring systems could produce, as *real*. They were not suffering — or rather, their suffering could not be measured by instruments calibrated for the old reality. They were elsewhere. They were housed. They were *handled*. And the infrastructure required to maintain them in that state could be automated, distributed, reduced to server racks and remote monitoring and the thin pulse of a fiber-optic connection to a maintenance hub that itself required fewer and fewer human beings to operate.

One by one, the wards shut down. Patients were transferred to home Loom-support systems — black boxes the size of a suitcase, connected to the central network, placed beside the bed where the body lay. Families were given instructions. Helpline numbers. Pamphlets with titles like *Living with Loom-Dependent Care: A Guide for Home Providers*. Some families kept their people. Some families didn't. Some patients had no families. Those patients stayed in the facilities until the facilities closed, and then they were transferred, and then the question of *transferred where* became a question no one was staffed to answer.

St. Erasmus was the last to close. I know this because I was there when the notice came — printed, taped to the break-room door, institutional language that managed to say *we are abandoning this building and the patients inside it* without any of those words appearing in the text. Essential operations cessation. Facility decommission. Managed transition protocol.

The staff left. I don't blame them. There were no paychecks coming. There was no institutional authority left to not-come-to-work for. They went home, or they went to other cities, or they went to wherever people go when the structure that organized their daily existence dissolves and they are suddenly just a person standing in a parking lot with a badge that no longer opens any doors.

I stayed.

I need to be careful here because I am going to tell you the reason I stayed, and the reason is true, but it is not the whole truth, and I know it is not the whole truth because there is a part of my motivation that I cannot access. It sits in me like a stone in fruit — I can feel its shape, I can feel the way the rest of me has grown around it, but I cannot see it and I cannot name it and I am not certain it is mine.

The reason I stayed: Eli Vargas, room 309, the last patient whose Loom-interface I could not transfer to home support because he had no home and no family and no suitcase-sized box ready to receive him. His body was here. His mind was in the Loom. The generator still ran. The interface still functioned. Someone had to maintain the line between his body and his elsewhere.

That is the reason I have. It is clean and it is dutiful and it is not enough. There is something else — something that has to do with what I saw when I first looked at him and the city opened behind his face — but I cannot reach it yet, and I will not fabricate access I don't have. You will have to accept the incomplete version for now. So will I.

## III.

Let me tell you about the body first. You should see the body first because the body is what is real, or what I believe is real, or what I have been using as my anchor for the word *real* for as long as I've been in here, and if I start with the other thing — the city — I am afraid I will not come back to the body at all, and the body deserves to be come back to. The body is what I'm *for*.

Eli Vargas. Age at admission: thirty-four. Occupation, listed on his intake paperwork in his own handwriting, in a script that was careful and precise and had the particular character of someone who drew for a living: *cartographer*. He made maps. I want to sit with that for a moment. He made maps. He recorded, for professional purposes, the shape of the actual world — its contours, its distances, the spatial relationships between things that exist in shared, measurable space. He was a person whose vocation was the *accurate representation of reality*. I need you to hold that.

In the bed in room 309, after eleven months — however many months — Eli Vargas weighs approximately fifty-two kilograms. I estimate this because the scale broke in month four and I subtract based on visible wasting. His muscle mass has atrophied in the pattern specific to long-term immobility: the large locomotive groups first — quadriceps, gluteals, deltoids — reducing the limbs to a strange, attenuated architecture, the bones increasingly prominent beneath skin that has thinned to a quality I can only describe as translucent. Not transparent. Translucent. Light passes into him but does not pass through. He glows faintly in the mornings when the sun comes through the window, and the glow has a bluish cast, as if the blood beneath has cooled to a different spectrum.

His skin has taken on the texture of something not quite biological. It is too smooth. It is the smoothness of something that is not being used — a surface that receives no friction, bears no weight, encounters no weather. It is, I think, slowly becoming *decorative*, the way the skin of fruit in a still life is decorative: present, pigmented, technically accurate, and profoundly uninhabited.

There is an NG tube for the enteral nutrition I prepare and hang every twelve hours from supplies that have lasted longer than they should, which is a fact I am noting and setting aside for now.
