# THE TRUE BIOGRAPHY OF THE HORSE CALLED COMANCHE

## Being a Personal Monograph by Gustave Korn, Late Farrier-Sergeant, Seventh United States Cavalry, Written at His Own Expense and for No Man's Benefit but the Animal's

---

## I.

I have put this off for the better share of eleven years.

There are men who set down their remembrances of the frontier service like coins laid in a row, each one bright and of a fixed denomination, so the reader may take them up and know at once what they are worth. I have read several of these books. I have read General Custer's *My Life on the Plains*, which is a very handsome volume. I have read Captain Benteen's letters to the newspapers, which are less handsome but which have the virtue of being composed by a man who was present at the events he describes and not dead at the time of writing. I have read what the correspondents wrote. I have read what the Congressmen said. I have read the proceedings of the Reno Court of Inquiry from start to finish, all one thousand and three hundred pages of it, in the reading room of the Kansas City Public Library over the course of nine winter Saturdays, and I can say without reservation that there is not a single true sentence in the lot concerning the subject of this monograph.

The subject of this monograph is a horse.

I say this plainly because I know how it sounds. A man of my station does not customarily produce monographs. A monograph is the province of the educated man, the scientific man, the man of letters. I am a farrier. I shoe horses. I trim and dress hooves. I treat the spavined, the foundered, the lame. I pare the frog and clean the cleft and set the nail at such an angle that it emerges through the hoof wall at the proper height and clinches flat, and I have done this work ten thousand times or more in my life, by rough computation, and I will tell you that there is no work on this earth that more closely joins one living creature to another. You cannot shoe a horse at a distance. You stand with the animal's leg clamped between your thighs and his hoof laid open in your hands like a book, and you read what is written there — the wear, the cracks, the bruises in the sole, the heat or absence of heat in the tissues — and if you are a man of any feeling at all you cannot help but know something of the animal himself. Not merely his soundness. His mind. His temper and his history and his dread.

I served in the Seventh United States Cavalry from October of 1874 to April of 1881. I was present at the Washita in the capacity of apprentice farrier. I rode the Yellowstone Expedition of '73 and the Black Hills Expedition of '74. I was attached to the pack train under Captain McDougall during the engagement at the Little Big Horn River on the twenty-fifth and twenty-sixth of June, 1876, and I was among the first party to reach the field where Custer's five companies had fallen. I was afterward assigned to care for the horse Comanche, the sole surviving creature of that field, from the day of his recovery to the day I left the service. During those years I fed him, groomed him, treated his wounds and their recurring afflictions, trimmed and shod his hooves, walked him when he could walk, sat with him when he could not, and knew him as well as any man has ever known any animal, which is to say imperfectly, but with a fidelity that officers and correspondents do not possess and cannot manufacture from the safe vantage of their desks.

I will be accused, I expect, of writing above my station. I accept the accusation. I will be accused of sentimentality, of anthropomorphism, of attributing to a dumb brute a complexity of perception he did not possess. This I do not accept. I have heard men of education speak of the horse as a machine of bone and muscle, admirably constructed, incapable of thought beyond the instinct for grass and the avoidance of pain. These men have not held a horse's leg. They have not felt the tremor that passes through the cannon bone when the animal scents something that the man cannot see. They have not watched a horse's eye in the moment before battle, when the pupil opens so wide that you can see your own face reflected in it, and what looks back at you is not blankness, not mechanism, but something so close to understanding that the only honest word for it is *communion*.

I have carried what I know about Comanche for a very long time. It has sat in me like a stone in the mouth, too large to swallow and too hard to spit, and I have rolled it over with my tongue in the dark hours more nights than I can reckon. I do not write this for the Army. The Army has its reports. I do not write this for history. History has been told already, by men of higher rank, and history has been told wrong. I write this for the horse. I owe him the attempt. That it will fail — that language is a poor shoe to fit over so strange a hoof — I know from the outset. But a farrier does not refuse the work because the foot is difficult. He picks up the foot. He begins.

This is the true biography of the horse called Comanche, as known to me, Gustave Korn, farrier, who held him.

---

## II.

He was a bay gelding of mixed blood, standing fifteen hands and two inches at the withers, with a deep chest and clean legs and a white star on his forehead the size of a silver dollar that had been rubbed to no particular shape. He had been purchased by the Army in St. Louis in the spring of 1868, out of a lot of forty-one horses bought from a dealer named Brughière who dealt in animals of Western breeding, mustang crosses mostly, horses with Spanish blood in them that showed in the density of the bone and the hardness of the hoof. His age at that time was judged to be four years or near it. He had no name. He was designated only by a brand on the left shoulder — U.S. — and a regimental number that I have forgotten and that does not matter.

Captain Myles W. Keogh of Company I selected him personally from the remount string at Fort Leavenworth in the autumn of that year. I was not present for the selection — I had not yet joined the regiment — but I have been told by men who were that Keogh walked the line of horses twice, slowly, with his hands behind his back and his kept tilted at that angle he favored, which was the angle of a man who believes himself to be observed. Keogh was an Irishman, a Catholic, a veteran of the Papal Wars, and a man of considerable personal vanity, which he carried with such ease and good humor that it became a kind of charm rather than a defect. He wore a medallion of Agnus Dei around his neck at all times, even in the field, even in the bath. He had the Irishman's instinct for a good horse — not the cavalryman's instinct, which is a trained thing, but the older instinct, the one that lives in the hand and the eye before the manual of horsemanship has had its say.

He chose the bay. He chose him, according to Sergeant Bustard, because the horse stood quieter than the others in the line, not with the dull quietness of a broken animal but with what Bustard called "a watchful quality," as though the horse were measuring the man who measured him. Keogh named him Comanche. The reason for the name has been disputed. Some say it was because the horse had been captured from Comanche Indians, but this is false; he came from a dealer in St. Louis. Others say it was because the horse screamed like a Comanche warrior when he was first shot, at the engagement on the Cimarron in the fall of '68, a bullet through the hindquarter that he survived with minimal lameness. I believe this second account is closer to the truth, though I suspect the name was also simply a name that appealed to Keogh's romantic Irish ear, being savage and musical and appropriately Western for a horse he intended to ride into history.

Fort Leavenworth in those years was the finest cavalry garrison on the frontier, which is not to say it was fine. It was a post built of limestone and ambition, set on a bluff above the Missouri River, with barracks that leaked in the spring rains and stables that were better constructed than the barracks, as was proper, since the horses were more expensive than the men and more difficult to replace. The stable assigned to Company I was a long, low building of hewn timber with a cedar-shake roof and stalls sufficient for forty-two animals, each stall separated by a plank partition and bedded with prairie hay. The smell of such a stable is not what the civilian imagines. It is not foul. It is dense, complicated, alive. There is the sweetness of hay and the sharpness of urine and the warm, heavy, bakery smell of horse sweat, which is different from human sweat in that it carries a vegetable undertone, almost like fresh bread, and beneath all of this there is the iron smell of the shoes themselves, the tang of hoof parings, the whiff of coal smoke from the forge that never fully leaves the hair of any man or animal who lives near it. I have been in churches that smelled less holy.

The daily routine of a cavalry horse at a garrison post is regimented to a degree that would astonish the civilian. Reveille sounds and the stable guard begins. Each horse is inspected in his stall — eyes, nostrils, legs, hooves. Each horse is watered, each is fed his ration: twelve pounds of hay and twelve pounds of oats per day for a horse in regular service, fourteen of each for an officer's mount in hard training. The oats are measured by a scoop of known volume and poured into the manger, and the horse eats, and while he eats the trooper assigned to him enters the stall and begins the grooming. The curry comb first — a toothed oval of iron that is drawn across the coat in circles to loosen the dirt and dead hair. Then the dandy brush, stiff-bristled, to sweep the loosened filth away. Then the body brush, softer, for the finer work — the face, the belly, the inside of the legs where the skin is thin and tender. Then the hooves, each one lifted in turn and cleaned with a pick, the sole inspected, the shoe checked for looseness, the frog examined for thrush. A conscientious trooper performs these tasks in thirty minutes. A negligent trooper performs them in ten, and his horse develops skin disease, and his farrier knows it, and writes the man's name in the book of his memory.

Keogh groomed Comanche himself. This was unusual for an officer, who would ordinarily assign the work to his orderly or to a trooper of the company, but Keogh made a practice of it, at least in garrison. Not every day. But often enough that the horse knew his hand, and I believe — though I was not yet his farrier, and must rely on what others have told me and what I later observed of the animal's temperament — that this was the foundation of the bond between them. A horse does not love as a dog loves. He does not seek to please. But he learns the hands that touch him, and he assigns to each hand a quality — safety, danger, competence, indifference — and he orders his world accordingly. Keogh's hand was competent and vain and affectionate, and Comanche learned it early, and what he learned was something close to trust, if trust is the right word for the condition of an animal who has not chosen his master and cannot leave his stall.

Fort Leavenworth is also where I first encountered the animal, though I did not yet know what he would become to me. I arrived at the post in October of 1874, a young man of twenty-three, newly enlisted, with a farrier's certificate from a shop in Cincinnati and a German father's conviction that no work is respectable unless it leaves marks on the hands. I was assigned to the regimental forge under the supervision of Chief Farrier Helmuth Brand, a Prussian with forearms like fence posts and an absolute faith in the superiority of the German method of shoeing, which emphasizes the natural balance of the hoof and does not permit the excessive paring of the sole that the American method endorses. Brand assigned me to Company I on my first day. I protested — not because I had any objection to the company, but because I had been told the company already had a farrier, a man named Sweeney, and I did not wish to intrude upon another man's work. Brand looked at me with the expression of a man who has heard something very stupid and is deciding whether to mention it.

"Sweeney," he said, "is a drunk and a butcher. He could not shoe a rocking horse. You are his replacement, and you will begin with Captain Keogh's mount, which Sweeney has shod so badly that the animal is beginning to forge."

Forging is a gait defect in which the hind hoof strikes the bottom of the front hoof, producing a clicking sound at the trot. It is caused, most often, by a failure to trim the hind hooves short enough or a failure to set the front shoes back far enough. It is a beginner's error. Sweeney had been farrier to Company I for three years.

I went to the stable and found the horse in his stall. He was eating oats. He turned his head when I opened the stall door and regarded me with one eye — the left — and I remember that eye with the clarity you reserve for things that mark the start of what your life will actually be, as opposed to what it had been. It was a dark eye, nearly black, large and mild and not quite empty. There was something behind it. Not thought, as men think. Something older than thought and less confused. An attention. A quality of presence that I have encountered in very few animals, and in fewer men, and that I do not attempt to explain here because explanation would diminish it and because the whole of this monograph is my attempt to show what that eye contained, or rather what I believe it contained, which may not be the same thing, though I have spent eleven years and more trying to close the distance between the one and the other.

I spoke to him. I said, "I am going to pick up your foot now." I always spoke to the animals before I touched them. This was not sentiment. It was practice. The voice is a tool, like the rasp, and it works upon the animal's nerve the way the rasp works upon the hoof wall — it shapes the surface it addresses. I put my hand on his shoulder and ran it down his leg, and he shifted his weight off the near fore and let me take it up, and there, in the bright autumn light that came through the stable door, I examined for the first time the hoof that I would come to know better than I know my own hand.

The shoe was an abomination. Sweeney had used a number two keg shoe — a machine-stamped shoe, an insult to a horse of Comanche's quality — and had nailed it so far forward that the heels of the shoe extended a full inch beyond the heels of the hoof, and the toe was left long and unrounded, so that the breakover point was pushed forward and the horse was forced to lift each foot with more effort than nature required. The sole had been pared to the thickness of a penny. The frog had been trimmed until it barely contacted the ground, which is a mutilation, since the frog is the organ of circulation in the foot and must bear weight to function. I set the hoof down and stood up and said several words in German that my father would not have approved of, and the horse turned his head and looked at me again with that same eye, and I will tell you that what I saw in it then was not surprise, because a horse does not feel surprise at the discovery that he has been maltreated, any more than a fence post is surprised by the rain. He had felt the wrongness in his feet and had carried it without complaint because he had no mechanism for complaint, and the wrongness had become the way the world was, and he had adjusted to it as horses adjust to everything, by bearing what must be borne.

I pulled all four shoes that afternoon. I trimmed each hoof to its natural shape, taking care to leave the sole its full thickness and the frog its full substance. I heated four hand-forged shoes in Brand's forge — bar stock, not keg iron, shaped on the anvil to follow the outline of the hoof as the hoof wished to be, not as a lazy man with a factory shoe had insisted it should be. I nailed them on with six nails per shoe, each nail driven at a precise angle, each clinch laid flat and smooth with the clinching block. The whole operation took something over three hours.
