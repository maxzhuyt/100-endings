# The Natatorium

## I.

You smell it before you see it. That's the first thing. You're still in the parking lot, standing on the cracked asphalt behind the Cascade Country Club — or what was the Cascade Country Club before the bankruptcy filing, before the auction that sold the dining room's chandeliers piece by piece, before the groundskeeper stopped coming and the boxwood hedges grew into each other like old couples leaning together in sleep — and the smell finds you. Chlorine. Which shouldn't be possible, because the building has been condemned for eleven months, and condemned buildings do not smell like chlorine. Condemned buildings smell like mildew and raccoon urine and the particular sweet rot of water damage in plaster walls, which is what this one also smells like, underneath. But the chlorine is there: sharp, institutional, alive.

And then, beneath the chlorine, menthol.

I stood there with my bag over my shoulder — the canvas tote with the resistance bands and the clipboard and the waterproof case for my assessment forms — and I almost didn't walk in. Not because of the building. I've worked in worse. I spent two years at a rehab facility in Tacoma where the pool was an above-ground Intex in a converted storage unit, and my best clinical outcomes happened in that pool, including Gerald Mack, seventy-one, post-stroke, left-side hemiplegia, who relearned the word *beautiful* in that water during his eighth session while I supported his head and his mouth moved like a fish trying to learn air, and the word that came out was not the word he was reaching for — he was reaching for *cold*, because the water was always too cold in the Intex, we could never get the heating right — but the word his brain offered, dragged up from whatever undamaged corridor still held language, was *beautiful*, and he said it looking at the corrugated metal ceiling, and I have thought about that every single day for eleven years. I was not afraid of the building.

I was afraid of the menthol, because menthol meant someone had already applied muscle rub, which meant someone had prepared for me, which meant someone was expecting something I hadn't agreed to yet.

But I walked in.

The natatorium was at the back of the club, past the locker rooms with their mosaic tile floors — tiny hexagons in cream and maroon, half of them loose, clicking underfoot like worried teeth. The hallway was dark. Someone had run an extension cord from a working outlet near the emergency exit all the way to the pool deck, where it powered two construction-site flood lamps aimed at the ceiling, so the light came down diffused and strangely golden, the way light looks in paintings of annunciations.

The pool itself was Olympic short course — twenty-five meters, six lanes, the lane dividers long gone. The Art Deco tilework on the walls was extraordinary even in ruin: geometric waves in turquoise and cobalt and gold leaf, half of them furred with black mildew, the gold leaf flaking in patches that looked like countries on a map of nowhere. Above the deep end, a rusted electronic scoreboard still displayed the results of a 1987 Pacific Northwest Invitational: Lane 4 in first, Lane 2 in second, times frozen in amber-orange dots, some of them burned out so the numbers read like redacted documents. The diving platforms rose at the far end — one-meter, three-meter — their surfaces chalky with disuse, their bolts bleeding orange rust down the concrete pylons.

The pool was full.

Not full the way a maintained pool is full, with its water line kissing the gutter system and the surface skinned flat and reflective as a blade. This water was four inches below the lip, slightly cloudy, and you could see, if you looked — and I looked, because I have spent my professional life reading water — the ghost of a garden hose's circular current still turning in the deep end. Someone had filled this pool by hand. Later, Ruthie would tell me it took three days. Three days of rotating shifts, two women at a time, running a hose from the utility spigot behind the men's locker room, watching it fill inch by inch in the construction-lamp light while the building settled and groaned around them like a body turning in sleep.

The water was warm. I could feel that from the deck, from the humidity that pressed against my bare forearms and the way my glasses fogged the instant I crossed the threshold. Someone had solved the heating problem, too — I would eventually learn it was an array of livestock water heaters, the kind used for horse troughs, sunk at intervals along the pool bottom, powered by the same daisy-chain of extension cords and surge protectors that any fire marshal would have called a war crime.

And the echo. A natatorium is an instrument. I have always known this. The hard tile, the water surface, the vaulted ceiling — any sound you make is caught and repeated and layered on itself, so that a whisper becomes a chorus, and a cough becomes a detonation, and seven women laughing in the shallow end become the memory of every crowd that ever filled this room, plus the ghost of every crowd that will never fill it again. The space was designed for cheering. For the slap of hundreds of palms on thighs, for the percussive bark of coaches, for the starting pistol's crack and the answering roar. Now it held the sound of water moving against tile and the murmur of seven women who had decided that a condemnation notice was a suggestion.

The mildew said: *abandoned.*
The chlorine said: *and yet.*

I stood on the pool deck with my canvas bag and my clipboard and my twelve-session contract from a woman named Ruthie Azarian, who had called my office three times in one day — each message more precisely worded than the last, ascending from request to proposal to what I can only describe as a grant application, complete with a stated objective, a timeline, and a budget — and I understood none of what I was looking at, except that it was the most stubborn thing I had ever seen.

## II.

Let me tell you about the bodies.

That is, after all, what I was there for. The contract said *aquatic physical therapy*, and aquatic physical therapy begins with assessment, and assessment begins with the body in front of you: its history, its mechanics, its particular dialect of pain. I am a translator. That is the work. A body speaks in restriction and compensation, in the angle at which a shoulder climbs before it catches, in the tremor of a quadriceps under load, in the almost imperceptible flinch — a fractional hesitation, less than a blink — when a joint moves past the frontier of what it can do without cost. I read these. I have read them for twenty-three years. I have read them in hospital pools and home bathtubs and the Pacific Ocean at low tide and, once, in a hotel hot tub in Reno where my patient, an eighty-four-year-old retired pipe fitter named Amos Cheng, insisted on doing his hip abduction exercises during his granddaughter's bachelorette weekend. People live in their bodies the way they live in their houses: some with meticulous maintenance and quiet pride, some with the blinds drawn and the mail piling up, some with a combination of structural neglect and bizarre, lavish renovation — a rotting foundation but a gleaming new porch — that tells you everything about what they value and what they have chosen to ignore.

My clipboard had seven names.

**Maxine Ostroff**, eighty-one. Bilateral knee replacements (2011, 2014), left rotator cuff repair (2019), lumbar stenosis. When she moved her left arm through abduction, the cuff clicked at forty degrees — a specific, almost musical click, like a lock tumbling open — and then again at ninety, and she moved through both clicks without pausing, without acknowledgment, the way you step over a crack in a sidewalk you walk every day. Her hands were enormous. Pianist's hands, I thought at first, and then I watched her grip the pool gutter and hoist her body weight plus the water's resistance with the casualness of someone pulling open a screen door, and I revised: mechanic's hands. Her spine curved left at the thoracic, a scoliosis she'd had since adolescence, and she'd built her entire movement vocabulary around it — every rotation, every extension carried that leftward lean like an accent she'd stopped hearing.

**Evelyn Park**, seventy-eight. Right hip replacement (2017), osteoporosis (T-score -3.1, which is the kind of number that makes younger orthopedists suck air through their teeth and start talking about what you should *stop* doing), cervical radiculopathy. Her bones were glass. I say this without hyperbole. Her bones were the thing I woke up thinking about for the entire twelve weeks. A fall, a bad entry, even an aggressive twist against the water's resistance — any of these could have broken her. She knew this. She entered the water with a precision I have never seen in any patient, before or since: a controlled, almost ceremonial lowering, one hand on the ladder, one hand on the wall, each foot placed with the deliberateness of someone crossing a frozen river and testing every step. And then she was in, and the water held her, and her face did something I cannot describe except to say that I have seen it once before, on a veteran named Darya — a former Army medic, thirty-nine, bilateral below-knee amputee, who, the first time I got her into a therapy pool, went absolutely still and then said, *I'm tall again* — and what Evelyn Park's face did when the water took the weight of her ruinous, magnificent, glass-boned body was the same thing Darya's face did. It was the face of someone remembering a country they'd been deported from.

**Ruthie Azarian**, eighty-three. The logistics officer. Degenerative disc disease, L4-L5. Both hands gnarled with rheumatoid arthritis, the knuckles swollen and angled like river stones, the fingers performing their daily mutiny against the brain's instructions. She had compensated so thoroughly that her grip strength tested normal — she'd simply rerouted every task through her palms and the heels of her hands, bypassing the fingers' rebellion entirely, the way a river reroutes around a landslide and eventually forgets it was ever straight. Ruthie did not make small talk. Ruthie made plans. She met me on the pool deck that first day with a laminated schedule — the lamination was perfect, the edges sealed, the document warm to the touch as if it had just come from the machine — and the schedule made no sense. Eight hours a week, split into four sessions, two of them beginning at 4:45 a.m.

"Is that a problem?" Ruthie said, and the natatorium's echo made the question larger than she meant it, or perhaps exactly as large.

"It's early," I said.

"So is death," Ruthie said. "We don't get to pick the schedule for that either. Tuesday and Thursday, pre-dawn. Saturday and Sunday, mid-morning. Do you need parking validation?"

The building was condemned. There was no parking validation. I would later understand that this was Ruthie's only form of humor.

**Dolores Katz**, eighty. The hands. That was my first note, my first clinical impression, the thing I wrote on the intake form before I wrote anything else: *hands — remarkable stability, fine motor, minimal tremor.* Cervical spondylosis, moderate. Right knee: bone-on-bone medial compartment, no replacement, refused replacement, refused it three times according to Ruthie, who told me this as if reporting a military commendation. Dolores was the one who smelled of menthol. BenGay, the original formula, the smell that is also the smell of my grandmother's kitchen in Gresham — not the kitchen itself but the threshold, the place where you'd stand and the warmth would reach you and the menthol would reach you and you'd know, before you saw her, that she was in the room and her shoulders were hurting and she'd been up since five and there was coffee on the stove and the world was comprehensible. Dolores's neck had the posture of a woman who had spent decades balancing something on her head — not a book, not a deportment exercise, but something heavier, something that required vigilance. When I palpated her cervical spine, she held so still that I could feel my own pulse in my fingertips, and hers was slower than mine, and steady, steady, steady.

**Sylvia Gutierrez-Morrison**, seventy-nine. Fibromyalgia. Which means: everything hurts, all the time, in ways that do not photograph, do not image, do not appear on any scan, and therefore, in the estimation of a not-insignificant number of medical professionals, do not exist. Sylvia's body was a country whose suffering had been denied diplomatic recognition. She moved through the water with a defiant fluidity that cost her — I could see it costing her, the slight lag at the end of each stroke where the pain caught up and she waited for it to pass, the way you wait for a wave to go over you — and she never once mentioned it. What she mentioned was lunch. And what her daughter said to her on the phone last Thursday. And the specific way the light hit the pool at 5:15 a.m. in November, which she described to me with the attentiveness of a landscape painter, and which I can confirm was accurate, because I saw it myself, and it was the color of a peach at the moment you realize it is perfectly ripe and you have perhaps twelve hours before it isn't.

**Helen Bychkov**, eighty-two. Parkinson's, early to moderate. The tremor was in her right hand, and it was a small companion, a bird nesting in her palm. In the water, the tremor quieted. I have seen this before — the water's proprioceptive input, its constant pressure on every surface of the skin, seems to recalibrate the nervous system's feedback loop, as if the body, surrounded by resistance, finally finds something solid enough to push against. Helen on land walked with the characteristic Parkinsonian shuffle: small steps, forward lean, the feet suspicious of the ground's intentions. Helen in the water was a different person. Helen in the water had amplitude. She swept her arms through full extension, she rotated her trunk with a freedom that her land-based body had forgotten or been told to forfeit, she moved with a largeness that, when I first saw it, made me look away, not because it was wrong but because it was too much — too much evidence of what was being taken from her, inch by inch, on dry land, every day.

**Bette Kopelow**, seventy-seven. The youngest, and the one the others watched. Bette was the captain, or had been, or would always be — there were tenses in this group that I never fully understood, hierarchies established in decades I wasn't present for. Bilateral shoulder impingement, chronic. Mitral valve prolapse, monitored, stable. Her body was the most conventionally athletic of the seven: broad shoulders, long arms, the kind of natural buoyancy that comes from a lifetime in chlorinated water. She moved in the pool the way certain musicians play — with the technical precision so deep it has become invisible, so that what you see is only expression, only intent, only the music. Bette was the one who called the formations.

And they swam.

Not paddling. Not the gentle, therapeutic bob-and-stretch that my contract described. They *swam*. Bette counted — she had a counting voice that was low and percussive and carried across the water like a metronome — and the seven of them moved into a formation that I first mistook for a warm-up drill. It was not a warm-up drill. It was a synchronized figure: a hybrid, something between the old school of precise geometric patterns and a more contemporary fluidity, and they performed it in the golden light of the construction lamps with the mildewed tiles watching and the dead scoreboard presiding and the only audience a woman with a clipboard whose intake forms suddenly looked like a joke.

Maxine's left shoulder clicked at forty degrees. Evelyn entered her back layout with the controlled descent of a lunar module. Helen's right hand steadied itself against the water's embrace and executed a scull so clean it barely left a ripple.
