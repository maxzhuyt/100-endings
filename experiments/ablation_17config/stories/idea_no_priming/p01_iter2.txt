# DUNMOUTH

---

## I. The Disembarkation

A proper confidence begins the way a sonnet does: with the terms of its contract laid before you, pristine as a tablecloth, so that you might admire the linen and forget to check the bill.

I arrived in Dunmouth on a Tuesday, which is the most dishonest day of the week. Monday makes no pretense of itself—Monday is blunt and ugly and will rob you with its hands. But Tuesday smiles. Tuesday wears a better coat. The ferry from Kingsport had groaned through four hours of Channel fog so dense it might have been upholstered, and when the hull kissed the pilings of Dunmouth harbor, the town materialized around me like a rumor someone had finally committed to print.

It was smaller than I'd been told. They always are.

My name—for those who follow the ruin of promising careers the way some follow cricket—is Beaumont Crisse, formerly of the Third Directorate, formerly styled Deputy Marshal of the Southeastern Fraud Division, formerly in possession of a pension, a modest reputation, and a set of rooms on Chancery Lane with a landlady who understood that a gentleman does not explain the hours he keeps. I say *formerly* with the practiced ease of a man who has rehearsed the word until it sounds less like a wound and more like a title. Formerly. There is real music in it if you listen: an iamb and a whisper, the stress falling on the act of having been.

What happened—and I will say this once, with the compression the subject deserves—is that I identified a network of fraudulent marine insurance claims operating out of the Liverpool exchange, traced its architecture to three clerks and a harbormaster's deputy who had been generating phantom cargo manifests for the better part of a decade, constructed a prosecution so meticulous it could have been set to music, and presented it to my superiors, who were at that time being entertained, at a club in Pall Mall, by the brother-in-law of the chief clerk's chief patron. The network was not prosecuted. The architecture was preserved. What was dismantled, with bureaucratic efficiency that I confess I might have admired under other circumstances, was me.

I was not discharged. Nothing so operatic. I was *repositioned*—a word that means, in the vocabulary of the civil service, that your desk has been moved to a room without windows and your name has been removed from the circulation list for everything except fire drills. I lasted nine weeks in that room. On the tenth Monday, I wrote my letter of resignation in iambic pentameter—a flourish no one at the Directorate would have recognized, which was precisely the point—and walked out into a London that had already forgotten I existed.

So. Here I was. Repositioned once more, this time by my own hand, to a town whose principal industries appeared to be fog, oysters, and the ancient art of failing to mind one's own business. Dunmouth. Population: enough to sustain two criminal syndicates and not enough to sustain three, which is the essential demographic fact of the place and the fulcrum on which everything I am about to describe would turn.

The syndicates. Yes. Let me lay the table.

On the east side of the harbor, operating from a warehouse complex that had been a sardine cannery in some more honest era, the Morvaine family conducted its affairs. Aldric Morvaine—patriarch, widower, owner of a face assembled from scrap iron and disappointment—controlled the movement of contraband spirits through Dunmouth's eastern quays with the organizational precision of a man who believed God was primarily interested in logistics. His men were quiet, professional, and dressed in the kind of dark wool that suggests violence is a salaried position. The Morvaine operation was not large, but it was *finished*, in the way a good cabinet is finished: no rough edges, no visible joints, nothing extraneous. Aldric had been running spirits for thirty years and had refined the process to something approaching civic infrastructure. His boats came in on the tide. His men unloaded in the dark. His product moved through a chain of pubs and private clubs that stretched as far as Bath. It was, in its way, admirable.

On the west side, from a public house called The Greengage that smelled of tar and pork fat and ambitions slightly too large for their container, the Sullivane family offered its counterargument. Emmet Sullivane—patriarch, raconteur, owner of a laugh that could be heard through walls and a grudge that could be heard through decades—ran a complementary but competing operation in smuggled tobacco, counterfeit duty stamps, and what he called, with the wounded dignity of a man who has looked up the word in a dictionary, *import consultancy*. Where Aldric was a watchmaker, Emmet was a bonfire. His men were loud, numerous, and dressed in whatever they had been wearing when he telephoned. His operation worked not because it was efficient but because Emmet had the kind of charisma that makes inefficiency feel like generosity—barrels fell off trucks, money wandered between accounts, shipments arrived on the wrong day at the wrong dock, and somehow the whole enterprise lurched forward on the fuel of Emmet's personal conviction that the universe owed him a good time.

They hated each other. This is not unusual among men who share a harbor and a trade, but the specific texture of the Morvaine-Sullivane hatred had a pettiness so baroque it bordered on the devotional. I had done my research. Six months of letters, clippings, interviews with men who had left Dunmouth and men who wished they had, and I had traced the feud to its origin with the care of a lepidopterist pinning a moth:

Nineteen years ago, at the wedding of a municipal clerk named Pettifer, both Aldric Morvaine and Emmet Sullivane had been invited to offer a toast. It was a small wedding. There was punch. There was a string duo playing something by Handel. And Emmet Sullivane, rising to his feet with a glass of champagne that he had personally smuggled as a gift to the happy couple, delivered a toast so beautiful, so unexpectedly tender, so precise in its observations about love and partnership and the sacred daily compromise of two lives choosing each other again and again that the room went silent, and then wept, and then burst into the kind of applause that means *you have said the thing we did not know we needed to hear.*

Aldric Morvaine rose next.

And delivered the same toast.

Not similar. Not inspired by. *The same.* Word for word. Because both men had, independently, copied it from the same book—a Victorian compendium of wedding oratory that had been circulating in Dunmouth's lending library for decades. Aldric had checked it out first. Emmet had copied the toast from the copy that Aldric's wife, then still living, had transcribed in her own hand and left in the church pew where Emmet found it. The provenance was disputed. The humiliation was symmetrical. And what could not be resolved was the unbearable question of *possession*—who owned the words, who had felt them first, whose sincerity was real and whose was theft.

The punch bowl was overturned. The string duo fled. Poor Pettifer never spoke to either man again. And for nineteen years, Aldric and Emmet had conducted their criminal enterprises in a state of mutual loathing so operatic that the smuggling itself had become, I suspected, largely a vehicle for the grudge—the revenue secondary to the pleasure of denying the other man a monopoly on anything, including the right to have meant what he said at a wedding.

This was the game board. These were the pieces. And I—disgraced, pensionless, brilliant in ways that no one who mattered had ever been willing to confirm—had come to Dunmouth to play them against each other.

The scheme was not complicated. The best schemes never are. The complications are for other people. I would present myself to each patriarch as a useful outsider—a former government man with knowledge of enforcement patterns, shipping regulations, ways to tighten an operation or loosen a rival's. I would offer my services to both, committing to neither, and through a series of calibrated betrayals—each man fed just enough intelligence about the other's operations to provoke escalation, never enough to provoke war—I would drive them toward a crisis so carefully managed that when it arrived, both organizations would be overextended, exposed, and vulnerable. At which point I would broker the peace. Take my commission from both sides. And leave Dunmouth richer, vindicated, and possessed of a story so elegantly constructed that my exile from the Directorate would come to seem not like a disgrace but like the prologue to a finer career.

The scheme had rhythm. It had symmetry. It had the quality I prize above all others in a plan: it was *amusing.*

I collected my bag from the ferry's deck, where it had acquired a film of condensation that made the leather look like it was sweating, and descended the gangplank. The dock was a lattice of dark wood and guano. Three fishermen were mending nets at the far end, their hands moving with the automatic grace of men who could do this work asleep and probably had. A customs officer in a coat two sizes too large was examining a crate of something with the theatrical suspicion of a man who had been told to look busy. The fog had the quality of damp gauze, and through it the buildings of Dunmouth arranged themselves in receding layers of grey—grey stone, grey slate, grey plaster—as though the town had been painted by someone who had heard of other colors but considered them a matter for larger cities.

It was perfect. A stage set for a man who worked in fog.

I paused at the end of the dock to orient myself—east toward Morvaine, west toward Sullivane, and straight ahead, up the cobbled rise of Harbor Street, toward the inn where I had reserved a room under a name that was almost but not quite my own. The customs officer glanced at me and returned to his crate. The fishermen did not look up.

But one of them said something. Not to me. To the net, or to the water, or to the habit of saying it:

"She was out again last night."

The second fisherman grunted.

"Brighter this time. Lit up the whole south channel. Billy Tench saw it from the breakwater—said the water went green, pure green, for half a minute. Like it was *lit from inside.*"

"Billy Tench drinks."

"Billy Tench has eyes."

The third fisherman said nothing. He was looking at the water, and his hands had stopped.

I noted this with the part of my mind that notes everything and files most of it under *local color.* Every harbor town has its phantom. Every fisherman has his luminous thing in the deep—a kraken, a ghost ship, a mermaid with generous proportions and no interest in marine biology. It is the folklore of men who stare at dark water for a living. The mind furnishes what the sea withholds. I had no use for it. My business was with men, not mantas. Or whatever bioluminescent jellyfish had wandered into the Channel this season and given old Billy Tench a story to nurse alongside his evening pint.

The fog thickened. I turned up Harbor Street. Behind me, the third fisherman was still looking at the water with his hands held motionless in the net, and the water, I noticed—though I did not pause, and I did not mark it, and I certainly did not allow it to interrupt the iambic progress of my thoughts—was, in fact, faintly green.

A trick of the fog, of course. Algae. Phosphorescence. The mundane luminescence of organic decay, which is all that any light is, finally, when you hold it to the lens.

I walked on, and the green faded behind me, and I thought of nothing but the toast at Pettifer's wedding and the two proud men who had both stolen the same words about love.

## II. The First Breach

The first contact between syndicates was, like all good first contacts, an accident that I had spent four days engineering.

A Morvaine man named Gull—long-jawed, competent, possessed of the watchful stillness of a heron waiting for a fish to make a mistake—had been running a consignment of Calvados from a French trawler that anchored, every third Thursday, behind the eastern breakwater. I knew this because I had, by the fourth day, made myself useful to Aldric Morvaine in the way that a new tool makes itself useful: by being visibly sharper than the old ones. I had corrected an error in Aldric's tide charts. I had identified a redundancy in his lookout rotation that was costing him two men per night for no gain. I had done this quietly, without flourish, with the professional modesty of a man who wishes it to be understood that he is accustomed to much larger operations than this but does not consider it beneath him to help. Aldric had said little. He said little about everything. But he had begun to leave his door open when I knocked, which among men who trust no one is the equivalent of a sonnet.

I knew the Calvados would arrive Thursday night. I knew Gull would be on the dock with two men and a handcart. And I knew—because I had, through a separate channel of cultivated usefulness, made myself plausible to Emmet Sullivane's lieutenant, a florid woman named Bridie Kell, who managed Emmet's dockside logistics with the ruthless precision her employer lacked—that a Sullivane tobacco shipment would be landing, by pure coincidence, at the next wharf over, at the same hour.

Not close enough for confrontation. Just close enough for *sighting.* Each crew would see the other. Each would report back. Each patriarch would learn that the other was operating within stone's-throw of his sacred territory on the same night. The insult would be geometric, not violent—a matter of proximity, of *presumption*—and it would land precisely on the bruise of nineteen years' resentment. The plagiarized toast. The stolen words of love. Each man would see in the other's presence not a logistical inconvenience but a *repetition of the original crime:* someone else, too close, saying the same thing, claiming the same right.

I positioned myself at the foot of the eastern breakwater at half past ten, because a conductor should be in the pit when the overture begins. The fog was obliging—a dense, low marine layer that muffled lamplight to a haze and reduced the harbor to its sounds: water against wood, rope against iron, the distant mechanical complaint of a winch. I could see Gull's crew at the Morvaine wharf, three dark shapes moving with the efficiency of men who do not need to speak. Across the water, just visible as a smear of lantern-light, the Sullivane crew was arriving.

The timing was exquisite. I permitted myself the small, private pleasure of admiring it—the way a clockmaker admires the moment before the chime.

Gull's men saw the Sullivane lantern first. There was a hitch in their movement—a pause, a recalibration, the silent semaphore of men deciding whether what they are seeing requires a change in posture. One of them reached into his coat. Not drawing. Just locating. Confirming that the geometry of hand to weapon remained what it had been that morning.

Across the water, the Sullivane lantern went still. They had seen, too.

Good. *Good.* The two crews, frozen in mutual awareness, each waiting for the other to blink. And tomorrow, two patriarchs would receive reports that would taste, on their tongues, like the old humiliation made fresh. I would be there to receive both reports. I would say very little. I would let the silence work. Silence is the most expensive rhetoric—it costs you nothing and charges the listener everything.
