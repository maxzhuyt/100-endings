# Ekpyrosis

## I.

On the morning of her fortieth birthday, Sable Achour knelt on the rooftop and pushed her thumb into the soil around the lemon tree and felt it give the way it always gave — warm,ite, faintlyite, holding the memory of yesterday's watering the way good soil holds everything you offer it. The tree was nineteen years old. She had planted it the spring after her daughter was born, when her hands needed something to do that was not holding, not checking breath, not the soft terror of new motherhood but something slower, something with roots. Nineteen years later the trunk was thick as her forearm and the lemons came in every October, too many for the household, and she carried the extras down to Kostas at the corner bakery in a cloth bag that had once been her mother-in-law's, and he made a tart that people on Amethyst Street talked about until December.

She pruned the dead growth first. Always the dead growth first, her mother had taught her, though Sable could not remember the lesson — only the knowledge, lodged in her hands like a splinter grown over. Her mother had died when Sable was four. What remained was not memory but practice: the way Sable held the shears, the way she spoke to the tree under her breath, the way she pressed her face into the leaves after cutting and breathed. These were inherited gestures. She performed them the way a church performs its liturgy, for reasons older than the practitioner.

Below, New Thessaloniki was waking in the amber light it always woke in, the light that came over the eastern hills at an angle that made the limestone walls glow as if they had been soaked in honey overnight. The baker — Kostas, always Kostas, who had taken over from his father who had taken over from his mother — was pulling the first trays from the oven, and the smell came up through the streets the way water finds its level, filling the low places first, the alleys between apartment blocks, the courtyard where the old men played tavli before the heat. There was bread, and there was dust from the hills, and beneath and through everything the faint salt presence of the sea, which lay beyond the harbor wall, which caught the light in the mornings and threw it back, which was blue in the way that a word is blue when you have said it so many times it stops being a description and becomes the thing itself.

The sea did not exist. It had never existed. The harbor wall contained no water. The hills to the east generated no sunrise because they were not hills but render boundaries, the edges of a consensus environment projected by 211 interconnected processing cores across the midsection of the generation ship *Ekpyrosis*, which had been drifting without engine power for approximately ninety-three years through a corridor of interstellar space so empty that the word *empty* embarrassed itself. The ship held 4,217 living souls and the complete biological and cultural record of a planet that had burned six centuries ago — not in fire, exactly, but in a cascade of systemic collapses so interdependent that historians aboard the first generation had given up trying to sequence them and simply called it the Ekpyrosis, the old Stoic word for the conflagration at the end of a cosmic cycle, when everything returns to fire so that everything might begin again. The ship had been named either in hope or in irony. Three hundred years of argument had not settled which.

The population of New Thessaloniki did not know any of this. They had voted — their grandparents had voted, in a referendum so agonized it lasted eleven months — to forget. Not to suppress. Not to classify. To *forget*, collectively, chemically, architecturally, to restructure the simulation's cultural layer so thoroughly that the ship became not a secret but an absence, a thing that had never been, the way a word in a dead language is not hidden but simply no longer528 spoken. The referendum passed by sixty-three percent. The dissenting thirty-seven percent had included Sable's mother, who was among the last to undergo the transition, who had stood on this same rooftop on the night before her integration appointment and looked up at a sky that she still knew was a projection and wept for a set of stars she would not remember in the morning.

But that was thirty-six years ago, and this is Sable's fortieth birthday, and the lemon tree needs pruning, and we have always known how this story ends.

*

On her seventh birthday there had been a paper crown. Yara, her mother-in-law — who was not yet her mother-in-law but simply the large warm woman from the apartment downstairs who watched Sable while her father worked the evening shift at the desalination plant — had made it from an old grocery bag, cutting the points with kitchen scissors, coloring them gold with a marker that bled through on every stroke. Sable wore it until it tore. She kept the largest piece under her pillow for a week. She could still feel the weight of it if she closed her eyes on her birthday morning, that particular paper gravity on her brow, how it made her someone other than herself for eight hours — someone with authority over a kingdom of the kitchen table, the courtyard, the rooftop where the lemon sapling her father had planted two years prior was barely taller than she was.

On her twenty-third birthday she had felt the baby kick for the first time. She'd been sitting in the courtyard below with Davit, who was not yet her husband but was already the person she turned to when something happened that she needed to say out loud, and she had grabbed his wrist and placed his palm against her belly and said *there*, and his face had done something she still could not describe, a collapse inward and an opening outward at the same time, like a door blown open by a wind that came from inside the house.

On her fortieth birthday she finished the pruning, gathered the clippings into a pile, wiped the shears on her apron, and went downstairs to fight with Yara about the cake.

The fight was annual and ceremonial and conducted with the seriousness of treaty negotiation. Yara held that the cake must be walnut, as it had been walnut every year since Sable's seventh birthday, since walnuts were what Yara knew, and what Yara knew was not open to discussion. Sable held that forty was a different number than seven and might permit, say, orange, given that the tree was producing and it seemed a waste. Yara held that using fresh oranges in cake was an offense against oranges. Sable held that Yara's position was based not on culinary principle but on the fact that she had already bought the walnuts. Yara conceded nothing.

"You'll eat it," Yara said, "and you'll remember you like it."

"I always remember I like it," Sable said. "I just thought —"

"Forty is not so old that you need to think this much."

The kitchen smelled of butter warming. Yara's hands, knuckled and large and mapped with the particular topography of seventy years of use, cracked the first walnut against the counter with a sound like a small, definite truth.

Sable kissed her cheek and went to walk her daughter to school.

*

Eleni was nineteen and did not, strictly speaking, need to be walked to school. She was studying mathematics at the polytechnic on the far side of the agora, a twenty-minute walk she had been making alone since she was fourteen. But on Sable's birthday, by a tradition neither of them had ever named or discussed, Eleni appeared at the door at 7:40 with her bag over one shoulder and her hand extended — not her full hand, but two fingers, the index and middle, held out the way she had held them when she was three and too proud for full hand-holding but too uncertain for none. Sable took the two fingers. They walked.

The streets of New Thessaloniki were narrow and paved in a stone that looked like it had been worn by centuries of foot traffic, though no foot had ever touched it — the wear was designed, coded with algorithmic attention to the way limestone erodes under Mediterranean weather patterns that had been extrapolated from a planet's worth of geological data and rendered with such fidelity that even the imperfections were perfect. Sable's sandals made a sound on this stone that her body recognized below the threshold of thought: the sound of home, of walking, of every morning she had ever set out into the city and felt it receive her.

They passed the bakery. Kostas waved from behind the glass with a flour-white hand. They passed the courtyard where the old men were already assembling the tavli board, and Mr. Petridis, who was eighty-one and had never forgiven Sable for beating him at cards when she was sixteen, nodded with the grave, compressed warmth he reserved for people he held grudges against. They passed the school where Eleni had gone as a child, its blue door open, the sound of younger children reciting something — a poem, a multiplication table, a list of the regions of the country and their capitals, geography of a land that existed only in the simulation's educational database, which had been compiled from the cultural archive with care that bordered on devotion.

"Happy birthday," Eleni said, when they reached the polytechnic gate.

"Thank you."

"Are you going to let Grandmother make the cake again?"

"I never let her. She simply does."

Eleni smiled. It was Davit's smile — the same structural event, the same sudden redistribution of the face's weight — and it landed on Sable the way it always landed on her, like a stone thrown into water that was already still.

"I'll be home by six," Eleni said, and went inside, and Sable stood on the street with the light on her face and the smell of bread and dust and sea-salt around her like a sentence she had been living inside for forty years, and she felt what she always felt on her birthday, which was the feeling of being alive in a place that knew her, and she turned, and she went home, and the light was amber, and the city hummed, and the sea — which was not a sea, which was a carefully modeled compression artifact drawing from bathymetric data of the Thermaic Gulf as it had existed in the twenty-first century — held its salt smell in the air like a promise it had no authority to make.

## II.

At noon she went back to the rooftop to check on the clippings.

This was not unusual. She always checked. The clippings needed gathering into the compost bin at the roof's edge, and the compost bin's lid had a habit of catching in the wind if it wasn't weighted, and there had been an incident three years ago when the wind had carried it into the Kostopoulos family's laundry line and Yara had been forced to apologize, which she had done with the air of a head of state conceding a minor territorial dispute.

Sable gathered the clippings. She weighted the lid. She stood.

The sun was directly overhead, or nearly so — it was June, or what New Thessaloniki called June, and the light at midday came down steep and white and compressed the shadows of everything into tight dark pools at their bases. The lemon tree's shadow lay on the rooftop tiles in a shape she had seen a thousand times: the trunk's narrow bar, the canopy's irregular oval, the gap on the western side where she had pruned a major branch two years ago and the new growth had not yet filled the space.

The shadow fell six degrees too far north.

She did not know, in the moment of seeing it, that it was six degrees. She knew only that it was wrong. The knowledge came in the same channel as the knowledge of how to prune — not thought but recognition, the body's long ledger of what a thing looks like when it looks right, and the silent alarm when it doesn't. She had pruned this tree at noon in June for twenty years. The shadow at noon in June fell *here* — along the edge of the fourth tile from the planter, bisecting the hairline crack that had been there since before she moved in. Today it fell *there* — past the crack, into the fifth tile, angled northward by a margin that anyone else would have called nothing.

She looked at it. She looked at the tree. She looked at the sun, which she could not look at, which forced her gaze down again, back to the shadow, which was still wrong.

She looked away.

She crouched and pulled a few weeds from the base of the planter. She repositioned the compost bin a centimeter to the left, for no reason. She brushed soil from her knees.

She went to the small shelf beside the rooftop door where she kept the gardening supplies — the shears, the twine, the watering can — and beneath the watering can there was a piece of chalk that Eleni had left there years ago, part of a set she'd used to draw on the tiles when she was small. Sable picked it up. The chalk was yellow, the yellow of the streetlights, of the limestone at sunrise, of everything in New Thessaloniki that was warm.

She drew a line on the tile at the edge of the shadow. A short line. Five centimeters. The kind of mark that could have been anything — a child's scribble, a stain, a meaningless artifact of a woman puttering on her rooftop on her birthday.

She put the chalk back under the watering can. She went downstairs.

*

That evening Yara presented the walnut cake with a single candle — "One is enough, you're not a stadium" — and Sable blew it out while Davit held his phone up to take a photo and Eleni sang the birthday song in a key that was enthusiastic and structurally unsound. They ate on the terrace. The light was different in the evening, softer, the amber going to copper, and the hills to the east — the render boundaries, the edges of everything — were dark against a sky that was performing sunset with the commitment of a fresco.

"The light felt different today," Sable said. She said it to the terrace railing, to the evening, to no one in particular. But Davit heard.

"Different how?"

"I don't know. On the terrace. At noon. Like it was coming from somewhere slightly — " She stopped. She did not have the word. The word was *wrong*, and she did not want to say *wrong* about the light that had held her entire life.

"It's your birthday," Davit said. He leaned across the table and kissed her forehead. His lips were warm and tasted faintly of walnut and powdered sugar. "Everything feels different on your birthday."

"That's true," she said.

"The cake turned out fine after all," Yara said, from inside, where she was wrapping the leftovers with the satisfied precision of someone whose position had been vindicated. "You'll have a piece for breakfast."

"I will," Sable said.

She sat on the terrace with Davit as the sky went dark, and his hand was on her knee, and from somewhere below came the sound of Mr. Petridis's radio playing the evening news — weather (clear), harbor report (calm), cultural calendar (a ceramics exhibition opening Thursday) — and everything was warm and known and shaped by thirty years of living in the same four blocks with the same two hundred people, and the light on the terrace was copper going to blue, and the sea held its last line of brightness along the horizon like a held breath, and Sable thought about the chalk line on the roof, and said nothing, and went to bed beside her husband, and lay in the dark listening to him breathe.

At age twelve, on a Tuesday in March, Sable had heard a bird outside her bedroom window sing a song that was beautiful and complex and lasted approximately forty-five seconds, and then, exactly forty-five seconds later, she had heard the bird sing it again — not a similar song, not the same species' generic call, but the *identical* song, every note, every pause, every micro-ornament reproduced with a fidelity that was not birdlike but mechanical. She had sat up in bed. She had listened for a third repetition. It did not come. She told her father at breakfast and he said birds repeat themselves, it's what birds do, and she had accepted this because she was twelve and her father was her father and the world was the world.

At thirty-one, on a Sunday evening in November, she had been standing at the kitchen window watching rain, and the rain had stopped. Not tapered. Not wound down. *Stopped* — every drop in mid-trajectory, a freeze-frame lasting perhaps a quarter of a second, and then resumed as if nothing had interrupted it. She had blinked. She had leaned closer to the glass. The rain was falling normally. She thought: I'm tired. She thought: The light was odd. She thought: Rain does strange things in the wind. She did not mention it to Davit.
