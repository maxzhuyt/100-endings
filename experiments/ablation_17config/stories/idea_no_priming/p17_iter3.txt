# Lemon-Light

## I.

The pillowcase smelled of lavender starch and that was the end of the intake and the beginning of everything else.

The nurse handed her the linens — two sheets, one pillowcase, one thin blanket the color of lake ice — and said something about meal schedules, but the lavender had already opened its door and Céleste was not in the hallway anymore. She was in the kitchen on Rue des Acacias, Thursday, autumn, 1996 or 1997 — no, 1996, because the calendar on the wall showed a photograph of Lake Annecy and grandmother had bought that calendar the September after her hip surgery, which was August 1996, and the iron was hissing on the board and her mother's wrists were turning, turning the collar of a white blouse with that particular practiced rotation, left hand pulling the fabric taut while the right hand pressed forward, and the steam rose and smelled of lavender starch — this lavender, this exact lavender, the molecules identical or near enough that her olfactory cortex could not distinguish now from then — and the kitchen window was open and the specific temperature of the air on the back of her seven-year-old neck was fourteen degrees Celsius and the light was the gray-gold of early October in Lyon and her grandmother was humming in the next room.

The melody: D, F-sharp, A, G, F-sharp, E, D. A folk song. Auvergnat. *Là-haut sur la montagne.* Second verse. Her grandmother's voice was mezzo-soprano run ragged by decades of Gauloises and she never sang the first verse, always began at the second — *l'herbe n'y pousse guère* — and the timbre had a catch on the *guère,* a little glottal friction that Céleste could reproduce now, could pour forth from her own throat with such fidelity that her mother would have gone gray hearing it, because her grandmother had been dead eleven years and the catch on the *guère* was alive in Céleste's mouth like a bird preserved in amber, warm and detailed and fluttering its tiny accurate wings —

"Room 16," the nurse said. "End of the corridor. Shall I show you?"

Céleste was holding the linens. Her fingers had not moved. The entire kitchen — its temperature, its steam, its dead woman humming — had occurred in the space between receiving the pillowcase and the nurse's next sentence. Four seconds. Perhaps five.

"Yes," Céleste said. "Thank you."

They walked. The corridor was white and very clean, lit by windows on the left that gave, at each passing, a frame of the sea below the cliffs. The sea was the color of — she refused it. She pressed her teeth together. The sea was the color of the sea.

The nurse walked ahead. Her shoes were soft-soled and made a sound against the tile that was not quite a squeak and not quite silence but a particular compressed friction, rubber against glazed ceramic, a sound like — she refused it. A sound like shoes on tile, only that, she would allow it to be only that.

The door to Room 16 had a brushed-steel handle and an electronic lock that clicked when the nurse held her keycard to the sensor. The click was bright and mechanical, a small decisive sound, middle C if C were made of metal, and it opened a second door that was not made of steel.

Her grandmother's jewelry box. The clasp. The same click, or close enough — within a quarter-tone, within the range of slippage that her auditory memory could not dismiss as mere similarity because her auditory memory did not deal in mere similarity, it dealt in identity, and the click of the lock was the click of the clasp, was her grandmother's bedroom, was the smell of sandalwood and old silk, was the box opening to reveal the cameo brooch and the garnet earrings and the thin gold chain with the cross that Céleste now wore under her blouse because it had been left to her in the will, the metal warm from her own clavicle, and her grandmother's fingers had been cool and dry and papery when they closed the box and the click when it closed was not the same as the click when it opened, the closing click was a semitone lower, more damped, because the latch engaged differently in the catching direction than in the releasing direction, and she knew this with a certainty that went beyond memory into a kind of molecular witness, as though she had been not a child in the room but an instrument, a recording device, a machine that happened to be wrapped in the skin of a seven-year-old girl who loved the woman whose fingers closed the box —

"Here you are," the nurse said. She had a wide face and patient eyes and her name badge read MATHILDE. "Dinner's at seven. Dr. Morel will see you in the morning."

Céleste stood in the room. The bed was narrow and white. The window was tall and showed the cliff's edge, the scrub grass, the huge indifferent sea. There was a desk with a lamp. A wardrobe. A chair.

She put the linens on the bed. She stood very still and waited until the only thing in the room was the room.

It took a long time.

## II.

The family came in pieces, the way families do when you cannot stop hearing them.

On her third morning, she was walking to the dining hall and the sun came through a window at a particular low angle — southwest-facing, approximately nineteen degrees above the horizon, the light strained through marine haze — and the angle was identical to the angle of light through the dining room window of her parents' house in December, and it was December, and it was dinner, and her mother was telling Aunt Vivienne about the holiday in Nice.

"It was the year the hotel had that plumbing issue, remember? And we had to move rooms twice, once on the second day and once on — was it Thursday? Thursday, I think —"

"It was Wednesday," Céleste said. She was nine. She was sitting at the end of the table. She had not meant to speak. "We moved rooms on Wednesday at four in the afternoon. The porter's name was Grégoire and he had a mustache that curved upward on the left side more than the right and he smelled of tobacco and verbena. You told him the bathroom faucet had been leaking and he said, 'Madame, they all leak, it is a feature of the building,' and Papa laughed but you didn't laugh, you said, 'Then it is a feature we would prefer to live without,' and your voice was the one you use when you are polite but mean something sharp underneath, and then we moved to room 408 which faced the courtyard instead of the sea and you were upset about losing the sea view but didn't say so directly, you said, 'Well, the courtyard has its own charm,' but your mouth did the thing it does when you're lying, the left corner pulls down approximately two millimeters —"

Her mother's face. Céleste could not refuse her mother's face. It surfaced with the rest: the color leaving the skin in a wave from forehead to jaw, the particular shade — not white, not pale, but a gray-rose, the shade of bloodlessness, of shock, as though Céleste had produced not a memory but a weapon. Aunt Vivienne's fork halfway to her mouth. Her father's hand on the tablecloth, the fingers opening and closing once around the linen, a gesture of helpless seeking that she would see again nine years later when he signed the commitment papers.

The sun moved past the window in the sanatorium corridor. The angle shifted. The light became simply light.

Céleste kept walking.

Other pieces came other ways. The pen.

On the fifth day, in Dr. Morel's office — a calm room, books, a window angled away from the sea as though the patients needed protecting from the excessive beauty of it — the doctor uncapped a pen and began writing in her file, and the sound of nib on paper, the faint rasp and glide, was close enough.

A different office. Fluorescent. Three doctors in a row like a tribunal, though they smiled. Her mother and father seated in plastic chairs that were the particular orange of public institutions, molded polypropylene, circa 2003. Her sister Agathe was not present. Agathe had said she wouldn't come. Agathe had said, on the phone, in a voice Céleste could still hear with every frequency intact: "I can't watch them do this to you. I'll never forgive them. But I can't stop them, and if I come I'll say something I can't take back, and Céleste — Céleste, I know you'll remember it. You'll remember exactly what I say. And I can't — that's the thing, isn't it? You'll *keep* it. Whatever ugly thing comes out of my mouth, you'll carry it forever, perfectly, and I can't put that in you."

The doctors spoke. Hyperthymesia, they said. Hyperthymestic syndrome, though the terminology was still contested. Involuntary autobiographical recall of such density and fidelity that normal functioning — the word *normal* doing its quiet violence in the sentence — had become impossible. They recommended residential care. A new facility. Progressive, they said. Holistic. On a cliff above the Mediterranean, as though the location were itself a form of treatment, as though beauty administered to the eyes could quiet what was happening behind them.

Her father's hand opened and closed on his thigh. The same gesture. The seeking.

Her mother said: "If it will help her. If she can — if they can make it so she can live." And the pause before *live* was a quarter-second, and in that quarter-second, Céleste heard everything her mother could not say: *live with us, live near us, live in a way we can bear, because we cannot bear this, this daughter who is a recording of us, who carries our every failure in high fidelity, who can reproduce the exact pitch of every argument we thought had been forgotten, who makes us know that nothing we have ever said or done has been lost, and we are not large enough to be loved by someone who remembers us completely.*

They signed the papers. Céleste sat in her chair and did not sign anything because by then she had been deemed — that word too, *deemed*, how it arrived in a doctor's mouth like a stone dropped in still water — she had been deemed incapable of determining her own best interest. Her memory was evidence against her. The very totality of her recall, which should have proved the staggering competence of her mind, had been reframed as the staggering dysfunction of it.

She came back. Dr. Morel was writing. The pen moved on the paper.

"Céleste?" he said gently. He had noticed. They were trained to notice.

"I'm here," she said. *Here* was a word that meant different things to her than it meant to him. She was always here. She was always everywhere she had ever been.

And Agathe's wedding. This memory came unbidden, surfacing during a group session on the seventh day when another patient — she didn't know his name yet — shifted in his chair and the cheap fabric of the cushion released a ghost of gardenia from whatever detergent was used, or whatever air freshener occupied the room before them, and the gardenia was enough.

The garden of the restaurant where Agathe married Paul. June. The gardenias were real, potted, lining the stone path. Céleste was twenty-three and wearing a green dress and holding a glass of champagne and the light was — it doesn't matter what the light was. The light was the light. The gardenias were breathing their thick perfume into the warm air and Céleste felt the catch, the trip, the interior lurch that meant a cascade was beginning, and she tried — she always tried — to hold on to the present moment, to Agathe's face, to the music, to the clink of glasses, but the gardenias had already built their bridge and she was four years old and standing in her grandmother's garden in Clermont-Ferrand and the gardenias there were smaller, wilder, and a bee was crawling into one of the blossoms and her grandmother said, "Watch, *ma puce,* watch how it disappears," and the bee entered the flower and the petals half-closed around it and her grandmother's hand was on her shoulder, the left hand, the one with the gold ring that was always slightly too loose and made a faint ticking sound against her collarbone when she moved —

And Céleste, standing at Agathe's wedding, began to speak. Not deliberately. It was as though the memory, arriving with such force, needed a mouth. She described the garden in Clermont-Ferrand. She described the bee. She described the precise weight and temperature of her grandmother's hand, the ticking of the ring, the smell of the soil that was different from the restaurant soil — moreite, moreite? No — *micaceous,* that was the word her adult mind supplied for what her four-year-old senses had recorded, a mineral glitter in the dark earth — and the guests had fallen silent and Agathe's face was the thing Céleste carried with more pain than any other face in her collection of faces: not anger, not embarrassment, but a terrible, exhausted pity. As though Agathe, in her white dress, on the day of her greatest happiness, was watching her sister drown in a sea she could not see, and had understood at last that no one could pull her out.

In the group session, Céleste pressed her hands flat against her thighs and breathed. The gardenia faded. The other patients waited. The therapist, a young woman with careful eyes, offered water.

Céleste drank the water. The glass was plastic. It made no sound worth following.

## III.

She met Noémie on a Tuesday, because Tuesday smelled different from the other days.

"Meatloaf," Noémie said, seating herself across from Céleste in the dining hall with the authority of someone claiming a country. She was small, North African, perhaps fifty, with cropped silver hair and hands that moved constantly, as though conducting an orchestra only she could hear. "The Tuesday meatloaf is burnt sienna with an undercoat of regret. Wednesday is better. Wednesday they do the fish, which is a passable cerulean, though lately they've been heavy-handed with the dill, which pushes it toward chartreuse, and I do not care for chartreuse in my protein."

Céleste looked at her.

"Noémie Khedira," the woman said. "Synesthetic perfumer. Formerly of Grasse, formerly of Guerlain, formerly of my own mind, which they tell me has been improperly wiring my senses since birth, as though the wiring diagram they prefer were anything more than a consensus of the unimaginative." She inhaled deeply over her plate. "There. You see? Someone sat in your chair at breakfast who was wearing — not wearing, *had recently applied* — a citrus eau de toilette, drugstore grade, bergamot top note, synthetic musk at the base. And they were anxious. You can always smell the cortisol. It's a kind of copper. The chair has kept it." She looked at Céleste. "You're new. You have the look."

"What look?"

"The *still listening* look. As though you haven't yet learned to stop hearing things. Most people arrive here having spent years trying to stop. The ones who are still listening — they're the interesting ones." She took a bite of meatloaf and her face registered complex, layered displeasure. "Definitely burnt sienna."

Noémie, as it turned out, could map a room the way a surveyor maps terrain — but her coordinates were olfactory. She knew who had visited the common room and in what order by the layers of scent they'd left, oldest at the bottom, freshest at the surface, each carrying emotional weather. She had been a master perfumer, the youngest *nez* ever hired by a major house, and her creations had been extraordinary precisely because she experienced fragrance as color, texture, sound. A perfume, to Noémie, was not a scent but a landscape. She built worlds in bottles.

The problem — the reason she was here, in this beautiful building on this beautiful cliff — was that she could not stop. She smelled everything, always, and everything she smelled became a synesthetic environment so rich that she would sometimes stand frozen in a doorway for twenty minutes, overwhelmed by the sheer topography of what her nose was telling her. She had been fired, gently, from Guerlain. She had been fired, less gently, from two subsequent firms. She had struggled in a series of increasingly small apartments in increasingly remote towns, trying to find a place that smelled of nothing, which is to say a place that did not exist.
