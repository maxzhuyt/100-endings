# The Toast

## I.

The argument Dale Xu was having with himself when the world ended was about time — specifically, whether two minutes and thirty seconds or two minutes and forty-five seconds was the correct duration for reheating lentil soup in a 1200-watt microwave. He'd set it at 2:30. He was regretting this. I know because he was murmuring about it, the way Dale murmurs about everything, which is to say at a volume precisely calibrated to be inaudible to anyone who is not standing directly behind him waiting for the microwave, which I was.

I should note for the record that I did not need the microwave. I was heating water for tea, which I could have done at my desk with the electric kettle I keep in my bottom drawer, which HR has asked me twice to remove and which I have twice not removed. But the kettle makes a sound like a cat being startled, and I'd been on a call with the Galveston office for forty minutes, and I wanted to stand somewhere that was not my desk and listen to a sound that was not Todd Armitage from Galveston explaining, for the third time, why the fiscal year should be restructured to begin in March. The microwave hummed. Dale murmured. These were honest sounds. I needed them.

"The viscosity issue is really a surface-temperature problem," Dale said to the microwave, or to God, or to the lentils. "If I'd let it sit at room temp for ten minutes prior, the thermal gradient would be—"

The microwave said 0:04. Then 0:03.

Then something happened to the floor.

I want to be precise about this because precision is the only tool I have left, and I am not going to let it go. The floor did not open. It did not vanish. What happened was more like a crease. Imagine you are standing on a piece of paper and someone folds it — not tears it, not crumples it, *folds* it — and you are caught on the fold. The fluorescent lights of Trellick & Associates contracted to a seam. The microwave was a line, then a point, then a memory of a point. My stomach did not drop. It *preceded* me somewhere, arriving at our destination a full half-second before the rest of my body, so that by the time I materialized on the stone floor of the great hall, my gut was already there, already unhappy, already intimately familiar with the local gravity.

The stone was green marble veined with gold. I noticed this because my face was very close to it.

Dale was standing. Of course Dale was standing. Dale had materialized upright, holding an imaginary bowl of lentil soup, his hands still cupped in the exact posture of a man who has just opened a microwave, and the court of Verdalia was watching him.

It was a court. There is no other word for it. The hall was vaulted — stone ribs arching overhead like the interior of some enormous animal — and hung with banners in shades of green and ivory and deep, oxidized gold. The smell hit me before the visuals fully resolved: floral, dense, almost narcotic, like standing inside a lily at the moment of its fullest bloom, the instant before rot. There were people on either side of a central aisle, hundreds of them, in layered formal dress that I did not have a cultural framework for, and every single one of them was looking at Dale with an expression I initially interpreted as reverence and later revised to *appetite*.

At the far end of the hall, on a raised dais, sat a woman in a chair that was trying very hard not to be called a throne. She was pale, dark-haired, and still in the particular way that large predators are still. A crown of woven branches sat on her head. Her eyes were green. Not poetically green. *Operationally* green, the way a radar screen is green — active, sweeping, painting everything they touched with information.

Someone was tying a sash around my chest.

I looked down. Two attendants — or functionaries, or whatever the Verdalian word is for "people who dress you without asking" — were fastening an elaborate sash across my torso. It was emerald silk, edged with tiny gold birds, and it was already half-secured by the time I processed that it was happening. I had the distinct impression it had started being tied before I'd arrived, as if my body had been expected at these coordinates and the sash had been waiting.

"Her Eminence," said a voice to my left — a tall man in a high-collared robe, holding a staff that appeared to be a living branch with its leaves still on — "the Foreign Dignitary of the Outer Reach."

He was talking about me.

"The Outer Reach has sent its tributary," the tall man continued, and the court murmured — a single, coordinated exhalation, like a congregation responding to a liturgy. "And with the Dignitary comes the Voice." He turned to Dale. "The Voice will now deliver the Toast to Her Illuminated Majesty, the Empress of the Verdant Throne, the Keeper of the Ninefold Garden, the Still Water at the Root of the World."

Dale was still holding invisible soup.

"I'm sorry," he said. "The toast?"

"The Toast."

I could hear the capitalization. So could Dale. He looked at me. I was on the floor with a sash being tied around me by two strangers. I was not in a position to help. I shook my head, which was the most honest communication I could manage. *I don't know either. I am also here. I am also not sure what here is.*

Dale turned back to the court. He licked his lips. He was computing. I could see it happening — the rapid, slightly too-visible cognitive processing that made Dale both good at his job and difficult at parties. He was trying to identify the procedure.

"Your… Majesty," he said.

The court inhaled.

"I'd like to say, on behalf of—" He paused. Looked at me again. I was upright now, the sash secured, the attendants retreating. "On behalf of the, uh, Outer Reach—"

The tall man handed Dale a goblet. It was gold. It was already full of something that caught the light like a liquid emerald. Dale took it with both hands, which I would later learn was the only thing he did correctly in this entire attempt.

"I'd like to toast the Empress," Dale said. "To her health. And to the, uh, the continued prosperity of the—" He gestured vaguely with the goblet. A single drop of green liquid arced through the air and landed on the marble floor.

The silence that followed was not an absence of sound. It was a *presence* of silence — a positive, three-dimensional quiet that pushed against my eardrums like pressurization. The court was not merely not speaking. The court was performing an act of coordinated non-speech that had weight, texture, and what I can only describe as *directional intent*. The silence was aimed at Dale.

Dale looked at the drop on the floor. Then at the goblet. Then at the Empress.

The Empress's left eyebrow moved approximately two millimeters.

The tall man with the branch-staff closed his eyes, and when he opened them, his face bore the expression of a veterinarian about to explain that the prognosis is not good.

"The Voice," he said quietly, "has Spilled."

The court gasped. Not individually. In unison. The same gasp, the same pitch, the same duration, starting and ending at the same instant, as if conducted.

"I didn't mean—" Dale started.

"The Spilling negates the Toast," the tall man said. "The Dignitary's tributary bond is unpaid. The cycle must—"

And then the fold happened again. The green marble became a crease, the court became a line, the Empress's green eyes became two points of light that lingered a beat too long, and I was on the floor of the break room at Trellick & Associates, and Dale was standing in front of the microwave, and the microwave said 0:01, and then it beeped.

Dale opened the microwave. He looked at his soup. He touched the side of the container with one finger.

"It's still warm," he said.

"Dale," I said. I was sitting on the break room floor. Karen from Accounts Receivable was looking at me from the doorway with the detached interest of someone who has seen stranger things in this break room and is not going to engage.

"Yeah," Dale said.

"What just happened?"

Dale picked up his soup. He peeled the lid back. Steam rose. He looked at the steam for a long time, as if it might contain instructions.

"I think," he said carefully, "that I need to do that better."

## II.

He was filing 1099s. That was the second time. A Tuesday — I remember because I'd just come from the Tuesday staff meeting, where Todd Armitage from Galveston had been piped in via speakerphone to continue his argument about the fiscal year, and I was carrying a legal pad with the words *MARCH IS NOT A BEGINNING* written on it in block letters, which was either a note about Todd's proposal or a thesis I was developing about my life. I walked past Dale's cubicle and saw him reach for a tax form and the fold took us both mid-stride.

This time, Dale was ready. He'd brought notes. Not on index cards — not yet; the index cards came later — but on a Post-it note stuck to his palm. I saw it as he raised the goblet (both hands; he'd remembered). *Her Illuminated Majesty. Verdant Throne. Ninefold Garden.*

"Your Illuminated Majesty," Dale said, "Keeper of the Ninefold Garden—"

"The Still Water at the Root of the World," I muttered from my position on the dais steps, where I had been re-sashed with disconcerting efficiency.

Dale did not hear me. Dale was committed.

"—I bring greetings from the Outer Reach and offer this toast to your eternal…" He checked his palm. "…flourishing."

The word *flourishing* hit the room like a brick through a window. I didn't understand why at first — it seemed perfectly benign, the sort of word you'd find on a sympathy card — but the court's reaction was immediate and architectural. The front row physically recoiled. The tall man with the branch-staff gripped it so hard a leaf fell off. The Empress's stillness, which I'd previously categorized as predatory, shifted to a register I can only describe as *geological*: the stillness of tectonic plates in the instant before they decide to move.

Later — many loops later — I would learn that *flourishing* in Verdalian court vocabulary is an indelicate reference to uncontrolled growth, and to wish it upon the Empress is roughly equivalent to saying, "I hope your body is consumed by vines." It is the worst thing you can say to a sitting monarch whose entire cosmology is organized around *controlled* growth, *ordered* cultivation, the precise and deliberate stewardship of living things. Dale had wished botanical cancer on the Empress of Verdalia and he'd done it with the confident delivery of a man who has checked his notes.

We were folded back. Dale's soup, microwaved the previous day, was in the office fridge. He ate it cold, standing in the break room, reading his Post-it note with the expression of a grad student who has received unexpected feedback.

"I need better data," he said.

The third time, he was parallel parking.

I was walking to lunch. Dale's Civic was attempting to nose into a space on Heller Street that could not have accommodated a Civic if the Civic had been made of liquid. I paused on the sidewalk — not to help, but because watching Dale parallel park was one of the small, reliable pleasures of working at the same firm as Dale. He approached it like a proof. Each adjustment of the wheel was a logical step. Each correction referenced the previous correction. He could parallel park a car into any space, given enough time, but the amount of time he required made it an event.

The fold took us between his second and third correction.

This time, Dale brought index cards. He'd done research — God knows where; I don't know what databases cover Verdalian court protocol — or perhaps he'd simply worked from first principles, reverse-engineering what he could from the phrases the tall man (whom I had by now titled, in my notes, the Master of Ceremonies, though his actual Verdalian title translated to something like the Branch That Announces the Wind) used in his introductions.

Dale made it further. He got through the salutation. He correctly identified the Empress's three primary honorifics and two of her four secondary ones. He raised the goblet with both hands, at what he estimated was the correct height — his notes said "sternum-level, tilt fifteen degrees toward dais" — and he began the body of the toast.

"May the roots of your reign drink deep," he said.

This was good. I watched the court. They were leaning in. The Duchess in the second row — the one who would later renounce her title — had her hands clasped. The Master of Ceremonies had relaxed his grip on the branch-staff. Even the Empress herself had tilted her head approximately three degrees, which in her emotional vocabulary appeared to be the equivalent of a standing ovation.

"May your canopy shelter the—"

Dale raised the goblet with his left hand.

He switched hands. He didn't even realize he'd done it — it was a natural gesture, a conversational adjustment, the sort of thing a person does when one arm gets tired. His right hand released the goblet. His left hand caught it. Easy. Seamless.

The Duchess in the second row stood up, removed her ceremonial circlet, placed it on her chair, and announced in a clear, carrying voice that she was no longer the Duchess of Thornwall and that her lands, title, and bloodline were hereby dissolved in the sight of the Verdant Throne.

Dale stared at her.

"What did I—"

But the fold was already happening, and the last thing I saw was the Empress's face, and her expression was not angry. It was *patient*. The way a teacher is patient. The way a cat is patient with a door it knows will eventually open.

*

The fourth time. The fifth. The sixth.

I am going to compress now because Verdalia did not compress; Verdalia delivered each repetition at full fidelity, with orchestral scoring and banquet-quality refreshments, and each one was an eternity inside a minute, and if I narrate them all at the pace they occurred, you will experience what I experienced, which is a flattening of time into a paste.

Fourth attempt: Dale mispronounced *chelvarath*, which is the Verdalian word for the bond between a ruler and her subjects. What he actually said was *chelvareth*, which is either an obscure verb meaning "to ferment improperly" or a very old challenge to mounted single combat, depending on regional dialect. The court interpreted it as the latter. A knight in green-lacquered armor stood and drew a ceremonial sword. Dale was folded home before anyone could clarify whether he was expected to fight the knight or the knight's horse.

Fifth attempt: Better. Significantly better. He'd brought a pronunciation guide on laminated card stock. He made it through the salutation, the honorifics, the body of the toast, and was within sight of the closing benediction when he committed what Verdalian etiquette refers to as the Premature Breath — he inhaled at a point in the toast where the speaker is expected to sustain silence for a count of four, a silence called the Third of the Seventeen Silences (the Silence of the Soil Before Rain). His inhale was small. It was involuntary. His lungs needed air. But the Third Silence is sacred, and filling it with the sound of a human body's respiratory requirements was, in the court's estimation, a declaration that material need supersedes spiritual obligation. It was, essentially, atheism by inhalation. A minor ecclesiastical crisis unfolded. Dale was folded back.
