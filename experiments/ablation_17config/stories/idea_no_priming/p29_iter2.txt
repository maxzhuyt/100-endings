# The Centerline

## I.

The desk was 121.9 centimeters wide. This was the standard municipal allotment for a Grade III Tax Exemption Processor — 15.2 centimeters narrower than a Grade II desk but 7.6 centimeters wider than the desks assigned to data-entry clerks in Annex B, who were not, technically, processors at all, and whose workspace dimensions fell under a separate memorandum (Administrative Circular No. 2019-0041, "Revised Space Allocations for Non-Processing Personnel"), which Lien had read once, in its entirety, during a period when the exemption queue had been cleared for twenty minutes and there was nothing else within arm's reach.

The desk was steel-framed, laminate-topped, the laminate a shade the procurement catalog described as "Sahara." It was not the color of any desert Lien had seen, which was none, but she accepted the description as she accepted all catalog descriptions — as a category, not a claim. The surface bore a ghost ring from a coffee mug that had belonged to her predecessor, a woman named Gloria who had retired to Bulacan and whose mug had left this single archaeological trace, permanent as a petroglyph, positioned four centimeters from the upper-right corner. Lien placed nothing on the ring. She placed nothing on most of the desk. Her materials occupied a corridor down the center: stamp pad (red, Bureau of Internal Revenue standard), date stamp (adjustable, currently reading APR 08 2024, though it was April ninth and she had not yet noticed), pen holder (municipal issue, beige — a different beige than the desk, a more defeated beige), and the stack.

The stack was the morning's exemption applications — Form 2307s, BIR Form 1601-EQs, and a particular local variant, the Municipal Real Property Tax Exemption Request (Form MRPTE-7), which had been designed in 1997 by someone who believed that thirteen signature lines were insufficient and that the word "hereby" could never appear too frequently. The forms arrived in Lien's intake tray in a loose sheaf, sometimes damp from the hands of the applicants who had filled them out in the lobby where the air conditioning had been broken since January, and she processed them in order — first checking field completeness, then cross-referencing the property identification number against the digital registry (which loaded in eleven seconds on good days, forty on most), then stamping.

She stamped.

The stamp met the pad. The pad gave its ink. The stamp met the form. The form took the impression. She lifted the stamp. She checked the impression. The impression was legible, round, red, perfect.

She stamped the next.

And the next.

At 12:15, without rising, she opened the Tupperware she had placed in the shallow drawer beneath the stack. The lid resisted — the left clasp had warped, or the container had warped, or there was some fundamental misalignment between lid and base that had developed over the years of daily use, and which she managed each day by pressing the left side down with her thumb while pulling the right side up with two fingers in a motion so practiced it no longer registered as effort. Inside: rice, compacted into the shape the container imposed on it, and a portion of monggo, the mung beans soft and brownish-green, the liquid mostly absorbed during the morning hours so that what remained was less a soup than a paste. She ate with a spoon from the drawer. She ate at the desk. She chewed in a way that took up no more space than necessary — her jaw working in small, vertical motions, her elbows held close, her body a contained operation, a process within the process, fueling itself.

The fluorescents above were the 6500K daylight variety, which the General Services Office had installed in 2021 as part of the Workplace Luminosity Improvement Initiative (Memorandum Order No. 2021-0163). They emitted a bluish-white light that flattened everything beneath them — faces, paper, laminate, hands — into the same overexposed plane, so that Lien's fingers on the forms appeared the same pale noncolor as the forms themselves, and there were moments, not frequent but not rare, when the boundary between her hands and her work achieved a kind of visual dissolution, her body continuous with the documents it processed, as if she were not handling the forms but extruding them, as if the forms were a secretion of her fingers, which existed for this purpose, which had no other demonstrated purpose, which were — she picked up the stamp — instruments.

APR 08 2024.

She stamped.

APR 08 2024.

She had processed fourteen forms with yesterday's date before a memo from Records (a handwritten note, actually, on a torn quarter-sheet, from Maritess, who handled the filing and who noticed everything and mentioned everything and whose threshold for leaving a note was approximately zero) informed her that the stamp was wrong. The note read: *Lien — FYI date today is APRIL 9 not 8. Pls check ur stamp. Tks — M.* The note had a smiley face. The smiley face had lashes.

Lien adjusted the stamp. She retrieved the fourteen forms. She took from the second drawer a bottle of correction fluid — not the municipal-issue brand, which was watery and gray, but a small bottle of the Japanese kind she had bought herself, dense and white and precise — and she painted a small rectangle over each incorrect date, waited for each rectangle to dry to a smooth opacity, and re-stamped. APR 09 2024. The process took twenty-two minutes. She did not sigh. She did not, strictly speaking, feel frustration, or if she did, the feeling was indistinguishable from the low, persistent hum of the fluorescents, which was always there, which was not precisely a sound but a condition, and which she had long ago stopped distinguishing from the baseline texture of being here, at the desk, which was 121.9 centimeters wide.

At 3:40, Rodel from Assessment reached across her desk for the stapler.

His elbow touched hers. Fabric against fabric, a brush — the cuff of his polo shirt against the cuff of her blouse, less than half a second of incidental contact, the kind that occurs a thousand times daily in offices where desks are 121.9 centimeters wide and human bodies are wider than their allotted space, meaningless, accidental, nothing.

Her arm pulled in against her ribs. Her shoulders curled. Her hand, which had been mid-reach toward the next form, retracted and hovered, fingers drawn slightly inward, the motion fast and involuntary and total, her whole right side seizing toward her center as though the contact had been a voltage.

Rodel said, "Oh — excuse me." He took the stapler. He returned to his desk, which was the same width as hers, 2.3 meters away.

Lien placed her hand back on the form. Her fingers resumed.

The day concluded at 5:00. She logged the completed forms. She entered the count in the daily output sheet: forty-seven processed (fourteen re-stamped). She returned the Tupperware to her bag. She returned the correction fluid to the second drawer. She powered down the monitor, which displayed a pale blue screen for three seconds before going black, and in those three seconds the screen held a faint reflection of her face — a face she did not examine, because the screen was not a mirror, it was a monitor, and its purpose was to display the property registry, and her purpose was to read it.

She walked home.

Left on Rizal Street, past the pharmacy with the sun-bleached cardboard display of multivitamins and the hand-lettered sign that had read OPEN since 2016 and no longer signified a state but merely an identity. Past the corner where two tricycles were always parked at this hour, their drivers on plastic stools, their radios playing different stations simultaneously so that the intersection produced a small zone of harmonic chaos — a ballad overlapping with an advertisement for glutathione supplements. Across the pedestrian lane at the junction of Rizal and Bonifacio, where the traffic aide was sometimes present and sometimes not and where the painted lines had faded to the point where their function was memorial rather than directive — they indicated not where to walk but where someone had once believed people should walk. Right on Bonifacio. Past the DMV building, which occupied the ground floor of a larger structure whose upper floors were ambiguously tenanted — Legal Aid on the second floor, a dance studio on the third that Lien had never seen open, and above that a series of windows that were sometimes lit and sometimes not and in which, occasionally, she had seen a figure moving in ways that might have been exercise or might have been agitation.

She did not look up.

She had taken this route for four years. The streets had long ago ceased being places and become a sequence — a syntax of turns and crossings that her legs performed while her mind, if it could be called that (she sometimes thought of it more as a registry, a continuously refreshing database of tasks pending and tasks completed), moved ahead to the evening's schedule: dinner (rice, something from the freezer), the electric bill (due Friday), a load of laundry if the machine was free (it was shared with the other tenant on her floor, an elderly man named Mr. Quiambao who washed his clothes on an unpredictable schedule and who communicated his use of the machine by leaving his rubber slippers in front of it, which Lien checked for each evening with the same procedural attention she gave to the property identification numbers in the registry).

She turned into the alley behind the DMV building. It was a shortcut — forty-four steps from mouth to mouth, she had counted once, or rather her legs had counted while her mind was elsewhere, and the number had persisted, stored in the body's own filing system, beyond retrieval or deletion. The alley smelled of motor oil and sampaloc and, on certain evenings, the sweet chemical residue from the print shop whose back door opened onto the passage.

Rain began. Not suddenly — it had been organizing itself all afternoon, the clouds accumulating in gradients of gray that Lien had not observed because the tax office had no windows on her side of the room, only on the Assessment side, where Rodel sat and sometimes looked out and once had said, to no one in particular, "Looks like rain," which was not information Lien could process because weather was not within her jurisdiction — but now, at 5:23 p.m., as she entered the alley, it arrived. Fat, warm, unhurried drops. The kind that fell individually at first, each one a distinct event on her shoulders, her hair, the back of her hand — and then, within seconds, collectively, so that the individual drops lost their identity and became rain, undifferentiated, total, soaking the alley and the motor oil and the sampaloc leaves and the back of Lien's blouse and
the forty-four steps, of which she had taken eleven.

## II.

She had taken eleven steps when the hand closed over her mouth.

It came from behind and to the right — the fingers spreading wide across her jaw, the palm pressing flat against her lips so that her breath, which had been a quiet, metronomic thing, was suddenly re-routed through her nose in a sharp involuntary jet. The shock was not the hand. The shock was the *completeness* of it — the way another person's body could, in an instant, reorganize the fundamental operations of her own. Her breathing, her balance, her direction. The hand took all of them.

She was pulled backward, off-axis, her weight shifting to her heels in a way that made her arms rise involuntarily — not to fight but to stabilize, the way a person on a tilting boat reaches for nothing. A second arm wrapped around her torso, pinning her arms at her sides, and she was being moved. She was being *carried* — though not efficiently. The man — she could feel that it was a man, the chest behind her flat and hard, the breath at her ear fast and shallow and smelling of instant coffee and something herbal, a medicinal sharpness — was half-dragging, half-guiding her toward the far wall of the alley where a fire escape descended in a zigzag of rusted metal.

He lost his grip.

Her left arm came free, and she twisted, and for a moment they were face to face — her eyes wide, his eyes wider, his face narrow and weathered and deeply lined, Filipino, fifties or sixties, the rain collecting in the grooves around his mouth and dripping from eyebrows that were disproportionately thick, theatrical almost, like two caterpillars staging a confrontation across the bridge of his nose. He wore a faded black T-shirt with characters she didn't recognize — Chinese, maybe — and track pants with a stripe down the side, and his grip on her was rearranging itself, searching for a better hold, his fingers opening and closing against her shoulder like a man trying to find the right chord on an instrument he hadn't played in years.

"Sorry, sorry," he said.

Then he had her again, both arms, and they were going up. The fire escape shuddered under their combined weight. The treads were grated steel, and through the grates Lien could see the alley floor receding — the oil slick, the sampaloc leaves, her bag, which had fallen, which lay on its side with the Tupperware half-emerged from the open zipper like a small animal cautiously exiting a burrow. They climbed. The man's breathing was ragged and percussive. On the third landing he stopped.

"Knee," he said. Just the word. He released her right arm to grip the railing and bent slightly, adjusting something in his left leg — rotating the joint, testing its range — and in that pause, which lasted perhaps five seconds, Lien could have descended. Three landings. Forty-four steps to the mouth of the alley. The route home. The registry of tasks. She stood on the landing in the rain, her blouse soaked through, her mouth still feeling the pressure of where his palm had been — the salt of it, the calluses, the specific topography of a stranger's hand printed on her lips — and she did not descend.

She did not descend because her legs did not descend. Her mind, which was already composing the police report (5:23 p.m., alley behind the DMV, male Filipino, 50s-60s, black T-shirt, track pants), had issued no instruction to her legs, and her legs, in the absence of instruction, did not move, and in the gap between the mind's failure to command and the body's failure to act, the man straightened, took her arm again — gently this time, almost a guide's grip, the kind a museum docent might use to steer a visitor toward the next exhibit — and they continued up.

The roof.

It was not so much a roof as an archaeology of half-finished intentions. Satellite dishes — three of them, different sizes, aimed at different quadrants of the sky like a grove of aluminum sunflowers — anchored to concrete blocks with bolts that had rusted into permanent fusions of metal and mineral. Between the dishes, a platform of plywood planking, warped and buckled from seasons of rain, its surface spongy underfoot, strewn with: a tarp (blue, insufficient, tethered at two of its four corners); a plastic chair (monobloc, white, the kind that populated every funeral and fiesta in the country, here serving as furniture for nobody); several pairs of focus mitts in varying states of decomposition; a wooden dummy — a *mook yan jong*, though Lien did not know this word yet — planted upright in a concrete-filled tire, its three arms jutting out at angles that suggested a saint in some heretical icon, or a traffic sign in a place where every direction was wrong.

And a cleared space. Perhaps three meters by four, marked at its borders by strips of electrical tape on the plywood — red tape, faded to pink, precise in its geometry, forming a rectangle that was the only evidence of deliberation amid the rooftop's chaos. Within the tape lines: nothing. The plywood here was worn smoother than elsewhere, polished by the friction of years of shuffling feet.

He released her. He stepped into the rectangle. He turned.

"Sit," he said, pointing to the monobloc chair.

She sat. The rain was steady now, and the tarp covered roughly one-third of the cleared space, creating a zone of relative dryness that included the wooden dummy and one of the satellite dishes but not the chair. She sat in the rain. She was aware of the following things: she was on a roof; she did not know which building; a man had brought her here against her will; the man was now pacing the edges of the taped rectangle muttering in a combination of Tagalog and what might have been Fukien; her blouse was transparent with rain; somewhere below, her bag was on the ground with the Tupperware exposed; tomorrow was Thursday; she had seven MRPTE-7 forms in the priority queue.
