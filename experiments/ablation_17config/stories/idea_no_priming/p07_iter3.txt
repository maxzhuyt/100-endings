# The Bout

## I.

The girl's left hook had been a clean, sharp thing all night, the kind of punch you build in a gym over years and nobody gives you credit for because it doesn't look the way it does in movies. It looked like work. Three rounds of it, and Marta Sobczak had counted the scoring punches the way she counted everything — on her fingers, silently, behind her back where the cameras the armory didn't have wouldn't have caught it anyway. She had Danielle up two rounds to one and the third round clear enough that even the generous read, the one she'd give to the other corner out of professional charity, still kept the fight where it belonged.

The ring announcer was a man named Phil Agostino who sold flooring in Dunmore and had been doing this for fifteen years with the same rented tuxedo that no longer closed at the waist. He held the envelope. The crowd in the Scranton armory — maybe three hundred people, half of them families who'd driven hours — settled into the particular silence of people who do not know they are about to be lied to.

Marta's hands were bad tonight. They were bad most nights now, the knuckles swollen into a topography she could read like weather, and the tape scissors were still in her right hand because she'd been cutting the wrap off Danielle's left glove when the scorecards came back. She stopped cutting. The scissors stayed open in her grip.

Split decision.

Not for Danielle.

The sound the crowd made was not a single thing. It started as a held breath, a communal inhale that lasted a half-second too long, and then it broke apart — one section of the bleachers groaning, another shouting, a woman Marta didn't turn to look at screaming the word *bullshit* in a voice that would be hoarse tomorrow. The Scranton contingent. Marta's people, some of them, or at least people who knew what a clean fight looked like and could count.

In the ring, Danielle's arm was not being raised. The other girl's arm was. Keisha Odom, from Philadelphia, who had fought a strange and careful fight, was standing with her coach's hands on her shoulders, and she was not smiling either. That detail snagged somewhere in Marta's attention and stayed there, a thread she would pull later.

Marta did not throw a chair. She did not shout. She set the scissors on the apron of the ring where they would be found the next morning by a janitor who would throw them away, and she climbed through the ropes to where Danielle was standing very still, like a girl who had been told something in a language she almost spoke but not quite. Marta took both of Danielle's hands and finished cutting the tape. She unwound the wraps with the same motion she had used ten thousand times, the rotating pull at the wrist, the peel across the knuckles, and neither of them spoke. She rolled the wraps into two tight cylinders and put them in the pocket of her jacket — a men's Carhartt, brown, bought at a Goodwill in 2014, too warm for the armory but she'd worn it for every fight since and was not interested in discussing why.

She drove Danielle home. The car was a 2017 Hyundai Elantra with 140,000 miles and a passenger-side window that didn't go all the way up, and the drive from the armory to Danielle's mother's house in South Side took eleven minutes, during which Danielle said four things and Marta said two of them back and none of it mattered. What mattered was the quality of the silence between the sentences, which was the silence of two people who had lost something together and had not yet decided whether to be angry at the same target.

She walked Danielle to the door. She told her to ice both hands and drink water, not Gatorade, water. She got back in the car. She drove to the gym — her gym, Wojcik's Boxing & Fitness, the name a vestige of a dead man's optimism — and parked in the lot behind the building, next to the dumpster that the city had been promising to replace for three years.

She turned the engine off. The October air came in through the window that didn't seal, and it was the kind of cold that doesn't announce itself, that just settles into the metal of the car and waits.

Her phone was in the cupholder. She picked it up and navigated, with what a younger person would have recognized as practiced clumsiness — the reading glasses were in her glove box and she would not put them on even alone in a dark parking lot — to the video someone had already posted. A wide shot from the bleachers, shaky, the ring a bright white square against the dark surround. The resolution was bad. The three rounds played out in miniature on the cracked glass of her screen.

She watched the whole fight once through. Then she went back to the beginning and started again, pausing, advancing frame by frame with her thumb, which was the worst digit for this kind of work but the one she'd always used. She was not looking for evidence. Evidence was a word for people who needed convincing. She was building something else — a structure, a sequence, a case so thorough that any honest person confronted with it would have no room to pretend they didn't see what she saw.

But there were no honest people. She knew that. She had known it before the fight, before the drive to Scranton, before the weigh-in that morning that had felt wrong in ways she hadn't let herself name at the time. She had known it the way her left knee knew rain was coming — not through reason but through the accumulated record of damage, the body's own filing system, which was more reliable than any commission's.

The car engine ticked as it cooled. The taste of the armory's coffee — burned, from a machine that predated the Clinton administration, fifty cents a cup from a Styrofoam stack Marta knew was paid for by the commission's petty cash fund, which also paid the officials, which was the kind of detail that only mattered if you were the kind of person who noticed how money moved — sat on the back of her tongue like a residue, and she swallowed against it.

She did not call anyone. There was no one to call. The people who would understand were the people she'd see at the gym in the morning, and the people she might have called for comfort were people she'd burned years ago or buried or simply let drift away in the manner of a woman who had organized her entire life around a single room with a canvas floor and heavy bags that needed re-chaining every six months.

On the screen, in the second round, Danielle threw the left hook. It landed. Marta could see it land even in this garbage footage, could read the impact in Keisha Odom's shoulders, which flinched in the specific way that indicated the punch had arrived on the jaw and not the guard. A scoring punch. A clean, unarguable scoring punch.

She paused the video. The frame held: Danielle's fist against Keisha's face, a moment of physics that the judges had somehow not seen or had seen and not counted or had counted and then subtracted through whatever arithmetic the evening's arrangement required.

Marta Sobczak was fifty-three years old. Her shoulders were thick from decades of holding mitts, and her left knee had been bad since 2009, when she'd stepped wrong on the gym's front step — the one she'd been meaning to fix — and torn something she'd never had the insurance to get imaged. She wore a size eleven shoe, men's. Her hands, which had wrapped more than ten thousand sets of knuckles by any reasonable estimate, were becoming the hands of an old woman faster than the rest of her, the joints swelling and stiffening and curling inward as if trying to make a fist one final time. She carried reading glasses in every bag she owned and wore them in none of the places she went. She owed $4,200 on the Elantra. She owed more than that on the gym. The lights in the parking lot — there was one, municipal, on a timer — clicked off at midnight, and it was now 12:07, and the dark was total.

She played the video again. Frame by frame. She was not grieving. Grief was for things that surprised you.

She had been expecting this her entire life.

## II.

What Danielle Ruiz remembered about the second round was the feeling in her knuckles after the jab that opened the combination — the one Marta had drilled into her so many times it had stopped being a technique and become a reflex, and then stopped being a reflex and become a thing her body did the way it breathed, without consultation — and the feeling was wrong. Not in her hands. In the air around the other girl.

Keisha Odom was supposed to be a pressure fighter. That was what the tape showed, the videos Marcus Webb posted on his gym's Instagram page: a girl who came forward, who smothered, who made the ring small. Danielle had prepared for that girl. For three months she had prepared, with Marta holding the mitts and backing her toward the ropes, making her work off the back foot, making her find the angle, and Danielle had hated every second of it with the passionate hatred of a nineteen-year-old who knows she is being improved against her will.

But the girl in the ring was not that girl. Keisha fought the first round aggressively enough — she pressed, she threw, she came inside the way the tape said she would — and then, in the second round, something shifted. The pressure eased. Not dramatically. Not in a way that a crowd would notice or even that a ringside judge, watching the overall picture, might flag. It was subdermal. It was a rhythm change, the kind of thing you only feel if you are inside the fight, if your skin is the instrument receiving the data.

Keisha started fighting at range. She circled. She threw single shots — a jab, a jab, a right hand that didn't commit — and when Danielle came forward to close the distance, Keisha moved. Not away. Laterally. *Carefully.* As if the ring had been divided into zones that Danielle couldn't see, and Keisha was following a map.

The word Danielle thought, though she didn't think it in language — she thought it in the body's own grammar, which has no words but has syntax — was *choreography*. The other girl was following a choreography Danielle hadn't been given.

She'd tried to tell herself, between rounds, that she was imagining it. Marta was in the corner, compact and certain, saying the things she always said: *Hands up. Double the jab. She's giving you the right side.* And Marta's voice was steady, which usually meant everything was fine, because when things were not fine Marta's voice didn't change, which was either the most reassuring thing about her or the most frightening, depending on whether you were the kind of person who needed your fear confirmed to believe it.

But there was something else Danielle saw. Between the second and third rounds, while Marta pressed the enswell under her left eye where a jab had opened a small mouse, Danielle had looked across the ring to Keisha's corner. Marcus Webb was leaning into his fighter's ear the way coaches do, and just behind him, half-hidden by the ring post, a man in a gray blazer was talking. Not to Marcus. Near Marcus. Close enough that his mouth could be heard. The man was short, white, heavy in the face, and he wore a lanyard that read PASAC — the state athletic commission credential. He said something. Marcus didn't look at him. Marcus didn't respond. But Danielle saw Marcus's back change — saw the muscles between his shoulder blades tighten in a way that was not the posture of a man ignoring someone but the posture of a man receiving an instruction he didn't want.

The enswell pressed her skin. The cold of it made her blink. When she opened her eyes, the man in the gray blazer was gone, and Marta was saying, *Double the jab.*

And before all of it, that morning, the weigh-in. In a cinder-block room behind the armory's main hall, with a digital scale that looked like it belonged in a veterinarian's office and a man operating it who read the numbers the way a priest reads liturgy — by rote, without looking. Danielle had stepped on the scale and the man had said "One-eighteen" and written it on his clipboard and said "Next" before Danielle had stopped moving, before the digital readout had finished its own deliberation. She weighed 117 and had weighed 117 every morning for a week, but 118 was within the limit and it didn't matter, except that Keisha had weighed in after her and the man had done the same thing — called the number before the scale settled, written it, moved on — and Keisha, who was supposed to make 122, might have made it or might not have, and no one in that room, including Marta, had said a word.

Danielle remembered this in the dressing room after, while Marta peeled the wraps off in silence. The dressing room was not a dressing room. It was a storage area behind the bleachers, with folding chairs and a utility sink and exposed pipes that sweated condensation onto the concrete floor. Danielle sat on a chair that wobbled, and Marta knelt in front of her working on the wraps, and the sounds of the armory — the crowd dispersing, the ring being broken down, someone's car alarm in the parking lot — came through the walls thinned into ghosts of themselves.

Danielle looked at Marta's face. She had been looking at this face for five years, since she was fourteen and had walked into Wojcik's because a girl at school said you could hit things there and no one yelled at you for it, and she knew its vocabulary better than her own mother's. Marta's face angry was a stone. Marta's face happy was the same stone in slightly different light. Marta's face when a girl did something beautiful in the ring was the stone with a crack in it that let something through briefly before sealing itself back up.

But this was none of those. This was Marta's face *recognizing* something. The way you look at a person you've been expecting at your door. Not shocked. Not outraged. Resigned in the muscular way of a person whose resignation is not passivity but a preparedness so thorough it has burned all the surprise out of the body.

Marta had known. Before the bout. Before the scorecards. Before the weigh-in that didn't look at the numbers and the man in the gray blazer who spoke into Marcus Webb's silence. Marta had known, or had suspected, or had understood the probability well enough that the result was not new information but a confirmation, and she had put Danielle in the ring anyway.

The wrap came off the left hand. Marta wound it into a cylinder. The room's fluorescent light buzzed at a frequency Danielle could feel in her teeth.

"Did you know?" Danielle said.

Marta didn't look up. Her hands — those old, swollen-knuckled hands — continued working, rotating Danielle's right wrist, peeling the gauze layer by layer, as deliberate and unhurried as if this were any other night.

"Know what," Marta said.

It was the right answer. It was the answer that sounded like a clarifying question, that performed ignorance of the premise, that offered Danielle a door through which she could retreat into specificity — *know about the scoring, know about the official, know about the weigh-in* — any one of which Marta could deny individually without ever addressing the architecture. It was a deflection so well-constructed it could have been mistaken for honesty.
