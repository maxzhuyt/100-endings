# The Partition of Delia Morrow

## I.

The petition crossed the filing window at 2:47 p.m. on the fourteenth of September, which was a Friday, which meant it would not be docketed until Monday but would receive a Friday stamp, which meant the twenty-day answer period under Section 15-61-10 *et seq.* of the South Carolina Code would begin to run over a weekend when no respondent could reasonably be expected to receive service. This was not unusual. Petitioners' counsel frequently filed on Fridays. It was sound practice if you wanted to compress your opponent's calendar. The clerk noted it the way he noted everything — not as strategy, not as gamesmanship, but as a fact of the docket.

*In re: Partition of Real Property Known as the Morrow Family Tract, TMS# 049-00-00-117, Beaufort County, South Carolina.*

He read the caption twice. Filed the original and two copies. Assessed the filing fee at $150 pursuant to the civil action schedule and recorded payment by trust account check drawn on Yancey, Calvert & Spiers, LLC, 1422 Main Street, Columbia, South Carolina 29201. He did not recognize the firm. This meant nothing. Firms appeared and dissolved like weather systems over the civil docket. He had been processing filings in the Beaufort County Clerk of Court's office for nineteen years, and in that time he had watched at least four Columbia firms reorganize under new letterhead without changing so much as a comma in their standard interrogatories.

He assigned the case number: 2001-CP-07-02438. He recorded it on a four-by-six index card — his own system, parallel to the courthouse database, maintained since 1988 in a metal drawer under his station — and noted the case type as Partition Action – Forced Sale. He noted the number of respondents: twenty-three. He noted the statutory basis: S.C. Code Ann. § 15-61-10 through § 15-61-100. He initialed the card in the lower right corner with the mechanical pencil he kept in his shirt pocket, the only pencil in the office that was always sharp, because he sharpened it each morning at 7:26 a.m. before unlocking the filing window at 7:30.

The petition itself ran eleven pages. It was cleanly formatted, double-spaced, with the standard recitals. The petitioner was Lowcountry Atlantic Partners, LLC, a Delaware limited liability company registered to do business in South Carolina, and the petition alleged that Lowcountry Atlantic held an undivided one-seventh fractional interest in the subject parcel, acquired by quitclaim deed from one Reginald Morrow of Brooklyn, New York, recorded in the Beaufort County Register of Deeds at Book 1094, Page 302, on April 4, 2001. The petition alleged that the parcel comprised approximately 387 acres on St. Helena Island, that the property had been held in common by the heirs and descendants of one Solomon Morrow since 1879, that no formal partition had ever been effected, that the cotenancy was unmanageable, and that a partition sale under Section 15-61-50 was the only equitable remedy.

The twenty-three respondents were listed on Exhibit A. The clerk read the names the way he read all exhibit lists — scanning for anomalies, for addresses that might complicate service, for deceased parties who would require substitution. He found the usual features of an heirs' property case: respondents scattered across four states, several listed at the same St. Helena Island address, two listed as "address unknown — last known address" followed by notations that were clearly more than a decade old. He found one respondent listed as an infant, by and through his mother, which would require appointment of a guardian ad litem under Rule 17, SCRCP. He noted all of this on the index card in his compressed shorthand. None of it surprised him.

Heirs' property cases came through the Beaufort County docket with the regularity of tides. The clerk had processed — he knew the number, because he kept meticulous counts of case types by year, by category, by outcome, on a separate set of index cards in the same metal drawer — thirty-seven partition actions involving heirs' property since he began his parallel records in 1988. Of those thirty-seven, thirty-one had resulted in commissioner's sales. Of those thirty-one, twenty-eight had resulted in purchase by the petitioning party or the petitioning party's affiliate. The pattern was not hidden. It did not need to be. It operated in plain sight, within the statutory framework, at every stage compliant with the Code.

These cases came in waves. After Hurricane Hugo, there had been a cluster — damaged properties, unpaid tax assessments, out-of-state heirs who did not know they owed taxes on land they did not know they owned. In the mid-nineties, the resort development boom on Hilton Head had expanded to the Sea Islands, and a second wave began. The clerk had processed nine heirs' property partitions in the past three years alone, eight of which involved Sea Island tracts originally granted during or immediately after Reconstruction. In seven of those eight, the acquiring party at the commissioner's sale had been a development entity. In six of those seven, the development entity had been represented by Columbia counsel. The courthouse processed these cases. The courthouse did not comment on patterns.

In the lower portion of the filing — below the prayer for relief, below the signature block for petitioner's counsel, below the verification — the clerk found the property description. It referenced, as such descriptions do, the chain of title back to the original conveyance. And there it was:

*...by deed of the South Carolina Land Commission to Solomon Morrow, dated March 12, 1879, recorded in the office of the Register of Mesne Conveyances for Beaufort County in Deed Book C at Page 114, conveying four hundred (400) acres, more or less, of the Sea Island Tract, St. Helena Parish...*

1879. The clerk had seen the date before. Not this specific conveyance, not this specific grantor, but the era, the instrument type, the granting authority. The South Carolina Land Commission: the Reconstruction-era agency that sold confiscated and tax-forfeited land to freedmen on credit, in parcels of 25 to 200 acres, at prices the Commission itself set and that the purchasers frequently could not sustain, leading to forfeitures, leading to resales, leading to the long attritional process by which the land that was supposed to be the material foundation of Black freedom in the Lowcountry was, acre by acre, returned to other hands. Except sometimes the purchasers did sustain. Sometimes they paid off the Commission and received fee simple title, and the land stayed. For a generation. For two. For four. Until the heirs multiplied and the interests fractionated and no one filed a formal partition and the property taxes went delinquent because no single heir could compel the others to contribute, and a stranger purchased a fractional interest at a tax sale or from a distant relative for a price that bore no relation to the land's development value, and then the stranger petitioned for partition by sale, and the statute — as the clerk understood the statute, as every clerk in every coastal county in South Carolina understood the statute — did not ask the court to weigh history. The statute asked the court to determine whether the property could be fairly divided in kind, and if it could not, to order a sale. That was all. That was the mechanism.

The clerk looked at the date on the deed reference. March 12, 1879. He calculated. One hundred and twenty-two years. He did not calculate the weight of those years. He processed the filing.

He was reaching for the next document in the intake stack when he noticed a separate filing clipped beneath the petition — a single page that had been submitted simultaneously, or nearly so, at the same window. It was a Notice of Appearance, pro se, filed pursuant to Rule 11(a), SCRCP, on behalf of Respondent Delia Morrow, and on behalf of Respondents Clara Morrow Jenkins, Theresa Morrow Odom, Robert Morrow Jr., Anita Morrow, James Solomon Morrow, and Lucille Morrow Baptiste, all of St. Helena Island, South Carolina, for whom Respondent Delia Morrow had attached individual authorizations to appear.

The clerk read the signature block.

*Delia S. Morrow, Esq.*
*South Carolina Bar No. 70214*
*Attorney Pro Se and for Named Respondents*

She had the same surname as the tract. As the 1879 grantee. The clerk had been at this window long enough to recognize when a name on a filing carried more than its alphabetical weight. He knew what it meant for a Morrow to be defending a Morrow tract. He knew, further, that "Esq." after a respondent's name in an heirs' property case was rare — not unprecedented, but rare enough that he could recall only two prior instances in his nineteen years, and in both of those cases the attorney-respondent had retained outside counsel rather than appearing pro se.

Delia Morrow had not retained counsel. She was representing herself, and six family members, against a Columbia firm, in a partition action that the clerk — based on his thirty-seven prior cases, his metal drawer of index cards, his comprehensive private knowledge of the Beaufort County civil docket — already understood would proceed along its statutory trajectory with the same institutional momentum that carried every other filing from intake to disposition.

He initialed the index card a second time, below and to the left of the first initial, which was his notation for a case he would follow.

He closed the filing window at 5:00 p.m. He locked the index card drawer. He went home. The petition entered the docket the way water enters a pipe: without resistance, without memory, in the direction the system required it to go.

## II.

The twenty-day answer period was a procedural interval. It existed in the Code as a courtesy, a window of due process, the statutory system's gesture toward the principle that no person should lose a right without notice and opportunity to be heard. In practice, in heirs' property cases, the twenty days served a different function that no statute described. They were the period in which the respondents — the heirs, the descendants, the family members who may or may not have known they held fractional interests in a piece of land they may or may not have visited in decades — were supposed to absorb the news that their inheritance was being converted to cash. Twenty days. The clerk had observed, across his thirty-seven cases, that this period was often the only period in which the respondents existed within the system as anything other than names on an exhibit list. Once answers were filed, the case became motion practice. Once motion practice began, the case became counsel's argument. The respondents, as people, receded.

On the sixth day of the answer period — a Thursday, September 20 — the clerk went to the basement.

The basement records room of the Beaufort County Courthouse was not, strictly speaking, part of the clerk's jurisdiction. It was maintained by the Register of Deeds. But the clerk had keys because the clerk had always had keys, because the previous clerk had given them to him in 1982 when he started as a filing assistant, and because no one had ever asked for them back. The records room held originals — deeds, plats, mortgages, satisfactions — dating to 1711, when the Lords Proprietors established the precinct. The room was climate-controlled to the extent that a 1974-vintage dehumidifier and a single temperature gauge, which the clerk checked every time he descended, could be said to constitute climate control. The gauge read seventy-one degrees. The humidity was, as always, higher than the manual recommended. This was the Lowcountry. Humidity was not a condition; it was the medium in which everything existed.

He went down to pull the original deed for the case file. This was, technically, unnecessary. A certified copy would suffice for the court's purposes, and a certified copy could be ordered from the Register of Deeds through the regular channel, which involved a request form, a three-day processing time, and a per-page fee. But the clerk preferred to see originals. This was not sentimentality. It was professional practice. In his experience, original documents occasionally contained information that certified copies did not reproduce — margin notes by prior clerks, stamps indicating prior actions on the same parcel, physical evidence (water damage, insect damage, tears along fold lines) that could be relevant to chain-of-title issues. He had, on two occasions in nineteen years, identified recording errors by comparing originals to certified copies. He considered this due diligence.

The records room was organized by book and page number, which in Beaufort County meant that deeds recorded before the twentieth century were filed in large folio volumes stored on reinforced shelving along the east wall. The clerk located Book C. It was bound in faded brown leather, warped slightly along the spine, with a handwritten label on the front board: *Deed Book C — 1876–1882.* He carried it to the reading table — a metal folding table that someone had placed in the center of the room at some point before his tenure — and opened it to Page 114.

The deed was there.

He read it slowly, not because the language was difficult — it was standard conveyancing language of the period, dense but intelligible — but because original documents from this era required a particular kind of attention. The paper was heavy, rag-based, in better condition than documents fifty years younger. The ink had faded to brown but remained legible. The hand was a recording clerk's formal script — not the original drafter's hand but the hand of whoever had transcribed the deed into the record book, which was standard practice before the widespread adoption of photographic recording methods.

The deed read:

*Know all Men by these Presents, That the South Carolina Land Commission, by authority vested in it under the Act of the General Assembly of the State of South Carolina approved March 27, 1869, entitled "An Act to provide for the appointment of a Land Commissioner, and to define his powers and duties," and the several Acts amendatory thereto, for and in consideration of the sum of Three Hundred Dollars, lawful money of the United States, to it in hand paid by Solomon Morrow, a freedman, of St. Helena Parish, Beaufort District, the receipt whereof is hereby acknowledged, hath granted, bargained, sold, aliened, released, and confirmed, and by these Presents doth grant, bargain, sell, alien, release, and confirm, unto the said Solomon Morrow, his heirs and assigns, all that certain piece, parcel, or tract of Land, situate, lying, and being on the Island of St. Helena, in the District of Beaufort, in the State of South Carolina, containing Four Hundred Acres, more or less, and bounded as follows, to wit: On the North by lands formerly of the Estate of John Fripp, deceased; on the East by the Combahee Marshes and the public road leading from the Ferry to Beaufort; on the South by lands of Neptune Small; and on the West by Chowan Creek and the Marsh—*

The metes and bounds continued with compass headings and chain-lengths that the clerk did not attempt to reconstruct spatially. He had seen enough nineteenth-century plats to know that these measurements were approximate and that the boundaries they described bore only a rough relationship to modern survey coordinates. What he noted was the language preceding the description. *Solomon Morrow, a freedman.* The designation was common in Land Commission deeds. It was a status marker — a legal identification that located the grantee within a particular historical category. The clerk had encountered it in at least a dozen documents in this room. It told the recording clerk, and anyone who subsequently read the record, that this purchaser was a formerly enslaved person acquiring land for the first time. It was a legal fact. It was also, the clerk understood, a form of witness. The deed was the only document in the Beaufort County records that testified to Solomon Morrow's existence, his freedom, and his act of purchase. The clerk did not dwell on this. He noted it.

He continued reading. The habendum clause:

*To Have and to Hold all and singular the Premises before mentioned unto the said Solomon Morrow, his Heirs and Assigns forever. And the said South Carolina Land Commission doth covenant and agree to and with the said Solomon Morrow, his Heirs and Assigns, that it is lawfully seized in Fee of the aforesaid Premises; that they are free of all Encumbrances; that it has good right to sell and convey the same to the said Solomon Morrow, his Heirs and Assigns; and that it will warrant and forever defend the same unto the said Solomon Morrow, his Heirs and Assigns, against the lawful Claims and Demands of all Persons whomsoever.*
