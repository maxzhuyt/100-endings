# The Detainee League

## I.

Let me tell you about the hallways first, because the hallways are the argument.

CoreCivic Southwest Processing Center — Facility 34-Alvarado, if you're reading the invoice, which you aren't, which is the point — runs its main corridor on a northwest axis, 340 feet from intake to the cafeteria, fluorescent-lit around the clock with the particular blue-white frequency that a 2019 study linked to reduced melatonin production but that a different 2019 study, funded by the GEO Group, linked to "improved alertness and compliance outcomes." The walls are painted the color of skim milk. Every forty feet there's a stencil, dark blue, sans-serif, Futura Bold if you care, and I do, because I used to care about fonts for a living:

**YOUR PROCESS IS YOUR PROGRESS.**

**STRUCTURE IS FREEDOM.**

**EVERY DAY YOU ARE CLOSER.**

Closer to what. The sentence doesn't finish. That's the design.

The commissary sells ramen at $1.15 a packet, deodorant at $7.40, phone minutes at fourteen cents per. A twenty-minute call to an immigration attorney in Tucson costs $2.80. A twenty-minute call to Accra costs $6.20. These numbers matter because they are the facility's actual language, the one spoken beneath the stencils. I know this because I spent eleven years in sports management — not the inspirational kind, not the *Jerry Maguire* epiphany kind, the real kind, the kind where you learn that every institution communicates its values through its price sheet and everything else is décor.

My name is Dariush Shirazi. I was a licensed agent with the National Soccer League Players Association from 2011 to 2022. I was disbarred for commingling client funds, which sounds worse than it was and was worse than it sounds, two things that can both be true. I had a residence in Scottsdale. I had a residence in La Jolla. Now I have a bunk, a case number, and an immigration hearing that has been rescheduled four times because Judge Martha Kessler of the Phoenix Immigration Court processes asylum claims from detained Iranians at the nationally competitive rate of one every eleven weeks, and there are forty-seven of us in the pipeline.

I am forty-four years old. My BMI is not what it was. I manage a soccer team I did not volunteer to manage, in a league I recognized as a marketing operation within ninety seconds of its announcement, and I am telling you this story because I am the wrong person to tell it, and every other candidate is either deported, still detained, or Kevan Asante, who will not tell it himself because Kevan Asante does not narrate — he *acts*, and then he leaves you to deal with what you saw.

I first saw Kevan in the yard on a Thursday in October. I know it was Thursday because Thursdays the yard opened thirty minutes early on account of a staffing overlap that no one in administration had bothered to correct, and I used those thirty minutes to walk the perimeter alone, which is to say, to perform the muscle memory of a man who once walked the sidelines of MLS stadiums with a credential around his neck and an opinion about every body on the field.

He was in the southwest corner, near the cracked section where the concrete buckled from a drainage issue no one had repaired since, I'd estimate, 2016. He wore the standard-issue slides — the gray ones, the ones that cost $3.50 at manufacture and are billed to the federal per diem at $11 — and he was doing something that made me stop walking.

He had no ball. He was doing footwork drills on cracked concrete in slides, alone, for no one. Step-overs. Feints. A Cruyff turn so clean it made the air move differently around his ankles. The movement quality — I need you to understand this — was not recreational. It was not exercise. It was the specific, unconscious fluency of a body that has been professionally trained, has competed at a level where footwork is not a skill but a *dialect*, and has retained that dialect the way a poet in exile retains syntax: automatically, involuntarily, as a matter of biological identity.

I watched for four minutes. I timed it because I still had my watch then, before I traded it to Gustavo Linares for six weeks of phone minutes and a tube of antibiotic cream.

Four minutes. In four minutes I could tell you: right-footed dominant, comfortable on the left. Probably a ten — an attacking midfielder — from the way he shifted weight before the imaginary pass, already seeing the geometry of a field that wasn't there. Late twenties, maybe thirty. Sub-Saharan African — Ghanaian, as I'd learn — with the build of someone who had once been fed for performance and was now being fed for cost-per-unit compliance. He'd lost fifteen, maybe twenty pounds off what would have been his playing weight. The facility does that. The calorie count is technically adequate the way a motel Bible is technically literature.

I watched him and I felt the thing I have always felt when I see quality in a context that doesn't deserve it. I felt my professional apparatus activate — the scouting reflexes, the mental spreadsheet, the automatic calculation of market value, positional scarcity, narrative packaging potential. And then I felt the sick undertow of knowing I was doing it here, in this yard, in this place, and that the apparatus itself was the disease.

Because here is what I knew, standing on that cracked concrete, that Kevan Asante did not yet know, or knew and had not yet been forced to articulate:

Quality, in a place like Facility 34-Alvarado, is the most dangerous thing you can possess.

Not because quality is punished. It isn't. It's worse than that. Quality is *seen*. And in a system designed to process you into anonymity — to convert your specific, irreducible human particularity into a case number, a bed-night, a line item on a reimbursement invoice — being seen is not liberation. Being seen is *inventory*.

I knew this. I knew this the way I knew the commissary price sheet and the hallway fonts and the precise angle at which the security cameras were mounted to capture the maximum square footage of the recreational yard: not because I am intelligent, although I am, but because I had spent my entire professional life inside the machinery that converts human talent into content, and I could hear it humming here, at a different frequency, toward a different product, but with the same fundamental engineering.

I went back to my bunk. I lay down. I said to myself: *Don't.*

Which is, if you've spent any time around agents — the real ones, not the movie ones — the exact word we say right before we do the thing we were always going to do.

## II.

Let me introduce you to the squad.

Gustavo Linares, my watch's new owner, was a forty-one-year-old Salvadoran bricklayer who had played exactly zero minutes of organized soccer in his life but possessed the lower body of a man who had carried ninety-pound loads up scaffolding for two decades, which translated, on a soccer pitch, into the ability to hold a defensive position with the immovability of a bollard. He could not pass. He could not dribble. He could, however, stand where you put him and remain there with a conviction that bordered on the geological. Kevan made him our center-back. Gustavo's case had been pending nineteen months. His attorney had stopped returning his calls in month fourteen.

Omar Bah — Gambian, twenty-three, formerly of the Gambian second division, a winger with pace that made your eyes lag behind his body — had crossed into Texas eighteen months earlier and been transferred twice, ending up in Alvarado after a paperwork error that his pro bono attorney was attempting to correct through a process that, Omar told me, "moves like a river that has forgotten it is a river." Omar could run. God, Omar could run. What he could not do was stop running, which meant his positional discipline was nonexistent, which meant Kevan spent three weeks calmly, methodically, with a patience that I found almost medically unsettling, teaching Omar to *not* run — to hold his width, to wait, to let the ball find him rather than chasing it like a dog after a car. Omar, by the end of those three weeks, could hold his position for up to eleven seconds at a stretch, which Kevan treated as a victory on the order of the Treaty of Westphalia.

Arjun Mehta, Indian, thirty-six, software engineer by training, who had overstayed an H-1B after his employer's sponsorship collapsed and found himself, through a sequence of bureaucratic misfortunes that he could narrate with the precision of a man who had debugged code for a living, in a detention facility in the Arizona desert playing left midfield in slides. Arjun had played cricket. Only cricket. He understood *nothing* about soccer except — and this was the thing that made Kevan's eyes narrow with interest — he understood *angles*. He understood the geometry of where a moving object would be in two seconds, because he had spent his whole life tracking balls off a bat, calculating trajectories. Kevan put him in midfield and told him to forget everything about his feet and think only about triangles. "You are not a player," Kevan told him. "You are a mathematician who happens to be standing on a pitch." Arjun scored twice over the course of the season, both times looking genuinely surprised to find a ball at his feet and a net behind it.

Jean-Pierre Kubwimana, Burundian, twenty-nine, who had played professionally for a club in Bujumbura whose name I will not attempt to spell from memory and who, of everyone on the team, required the least instruction and the most management, because Jean-Pierre's skill level was real and his temperament was an undetonated ordnance. He had received three yellow cards in preseason scrimmages that did not technically count, which gives you the picture. His asylum claim involved things he did not discuss and that Dariush Shirazi, narrator of this account, will not speculate about, except to note that Jean-Pierre flinched at certain sounds and did not sleep in the way that a person with an untroubled history sleeps, and that on the pitch his aggression had the quality not of competitiveness but of *release*, which Kevan understood and managed by giving Jean-Pierre very specific, very narrow tasks — "Win this ball, find me, then rest" — that channeled the energy into discrete, controllable bursts. Jean-Pierre's case had been pending four years.

He was the one. I should tell you now.

There were others. Seventeen on the full roster: Hondurans, Guatemalans, a Cameroonian, two Haitians, a Mauritanian whose name was Sidi and who played goalkeeper with the serene detachment of a man who had decided, at some point, that nothing the world sent at him could be worse than what it had already sent, and who was, as a consequence, genuinely excellent in goal, because fearlessness, it turns out, covers a multitude of technical sins.

Kevan organized them. That is the verb. Not coached — *organized.* He looked at each man the way I imagined he had once looked at a football pitch: as a system with assets, deficits, and possibilities that existed between the two. He did not raise his voice. I never heard him raise his voice. He spoke at a consistent volume and with a diction that carried the faint residue of British coaching — he'd spent time, I later learned, at an academy in Manchester — and when a man could not do what was asked, Kevan did not repeat the instruction louder; he redesigned the instruction around the man's capability. This is a vanishingly rare quality. I have worked with coaches who earned seven figures and could not do it.

Practice was held in the yard, four days a week, 3:00 to 4:30, on cracked concrete with a ball that had been inflated optimally exactly once, during its manufacture, and had been in a state of gradual metaphysical decline ever since. The goals were constructed from PVC pipe and cargo netting by a man named Tomás whose case I never learned the details of because Tomás did not speak, not out of inability but out of what appeared to be a considered philosophical position, and who expressed himself exclusively through acts of structural engineering that were, I will say, better than the facility deserved.

It was during the third week of practice. Jean-Pierre — four years pending, the midfielder, the one with the flinch — was running a pattern Kevan had drawn on the concrete with a wet finger. The pattern was simple: diagonal run from the left channel, check back, receive to feet. Three other players were involved. The ball was supposed to arrive at Jean-Pierre's feet at the moment he turned, so that the turning and the receiving were one motion, a single breath.

Kevan was at midfield. He looked up, saw Jean-Pierre begin the check, and released the ball.

I have seen — in my professional life, in stadiums, on screens — thousands of passes of varying quality, intent, and consequence. I have seen passes that changed the score. I have seen passes that changed careers. What I saw Kevan do on that cracked yard was neither of those. It was something more radical. The ball arrived at Jean-Pierre's feet at the exact moment his body turned, with the exact weight, at the exact angle, so that it was not a *pass* in the athletic sense — it was a *declaration*. The ball said: *I knew you would be here. I knew your body would be in this position at this moment. I trust your existence in this space.*

Jean-Pierre received it. First touch, clean. And then he stopped.

He stood there, ball at his feet, and his face did something I will not describe fully because I do not have the right. His shoulders came forward. His jaw moved. And he began to weep with the specific, total helplessness of a man who has not been assumed competent — has not been assumed *present* — by another human being or by any system in years.

Kevan walked over. Slowly. No urgency. The way you approach an animal that has been hurt and does not yet know whether you are the next thing that will hurt it.

I turned away.

I turned away, and I am telling you that I turned away so that you understand there is a moment of this story I chose not to witness, and that the gap in my telling is not an artistic decision but a confession. I can describe commissary price sheets. I can describe hallway fonts and camera angles and the precise mechanics of a league designed to convert detained men into donor engagement metrics. I can describe these things because they are the architecture of exploitation and I have spent my life inside that architecture and I understand its load-bearing walls.

What I cannot describe — what I refused to look at — is what it looks like when one human being, through the simple act of threading a ball to exactly where another human being is, says: *I see you, I know where you are, and I expect you to be there.*

I heard Kevan's voice, low, and Jean-Pierre's breathing. I did not turn around. The practice resumed after four minutes. Jean-Pierre completed the drill. No one mentioned it.

That night, on my bunk, I stared at the ceiling and thought: *You should have watched. You owed him that.*

But I am a man who spent eleven years selling athletes to cameras, and I knew — I *knew* — that some things, the moment you observe them, become content. And I would not do that to Jean-Pierre Kubwimana. Not even in the privacy of my own narration.

I am doing it now, of course. I know that. Don't think I don't know that.

## III.

The assembly was held on a Wednesday in the cafeteria, which had been reconfigured with the speed and efficiency of a production team that had done this before — folding chairs in rows, a podium with a microphone, two standing banners flanking a pull-down screen. The banners read **DETAINEE LEAGUE: YOUR GAME, YOUR STORY** in the same Futura Bold as the hallway stencils, except in a friendlier blue, a blue that had been — I promise you this — *focus-grouped*, a blue that said *nonprofit* and *impact* and *we are the good ones* in a single Pantone value.

The warden's name is not relevant.
