# The Ones Who Stay in Open Water

---

## I.

The kelp is bronze. I think it is bronze. What I get is a smear of warm color bending where the current bends it, sixty feet out past the net pen, and in that smear something that might be beauty if I had the equipment left to confirm it. I have one eye that works and it works the way a window works after someone throws a rock through it — you can still see the yard but you'll never stop seeing the crack. So when I tell you the sanctuary is beautiful, understand that I am telling you about a place I have partly invented. The parts I can see are real. The parts I can't, I've filled with thirty-one years of knowing what water does to light.

The net pen is two hundred meters on its long axis, sunk twelve feet and buoyed with foam-core floats that knock together in the swell like the hollow wooden blocks children play with. Beyond the pen, a kelp forest runs along the coastal shelf for perhaps a quarter mile before the bottom drops. I know this the way I know most things — by feel. The water changes where the kelp begins. It slows. It thickens with oxygen and the faint sweetness of photosynthesis. And past the kelp, where the shelf falls away, the water does something else entirely. It opens. It becomes the kind of cold that is not temperature but *depth*, the pressure of a column of ocean that has no floor you'd ever want to reach.

I have lived in this sanctuary for four years. Before that: SeaMist Orlando, 2005 to 2019. Before that: MarineWorld Pacific, 1993 to 2005. Before that: a concrete rehabilitation pool in Sausalito where I arrived at seven months old with a shark bite that took my left eye and most of the vision in my right. I have never lived in the wild. I am not a creature of the open ocean. I am a creature of edges — pool walls, pen nets, the boundaries humans build and call kindness. I have made my peace with edges. It is a particular kind of peace, the kind that looks, from the outside, like stillness and is, from the inside, more like a fist that has been clenched so long it has forgotten it is clenching.

The dolphins arrived on a Tuesday in March.

I know it was Tuesday because the volunteer staff rotates, and Tuesdays belong to the woman whose boots squeak on the wet dock in a specific pattern I can identify at forty meters. I heard her boots, and then I heard the transport truck's air brakes, and then I heard the hoist crane, and then I heard them.

Six bottlenose dolphins, lifted in slings from a transport vehicle and lowered one by one into the east bay of the pen. Retired, the staff said. From a facility in Biloxi that had closed. Circus-trained, some of them. Others from swim-with programs, interactive exhibits, the kind of work where a dolphin learns that a human child grabbing its dorsal fin is not an attack but a revenue stream. They came into the water the way all captive animals come into new water — with their bodies pulled tight and their senses firing at everything and their eyes, those strange dolphin eyes that can see above and below simultaneously, scanning for the walls that weren't there.

I watched from my haul-out on the east rocks. My good eye, such as it is, gave me shapes. Six gray shapes in water that was darker gray, moving in a formation that was too tight. Wild dolphins space themselves. These dolphins swam the way schoolchildren walk in hallways — close enough to touch, close enough that each body could feel the displacement wave of the body beside it and use that pressure as a wall. They had built their own pool out of proximity. They carried the architecture of captivity in the spacing of their bodies.

The first one to cross the boundary line was Sunday.

She was not the largest — that was Tomas, a male with a healed fracture in his caudal peduncle that made his tail stroke asymmetric, a kind of limp in water. She was not the youngest — that was Pearl, barely six, born in a park in Galveston to a mother who had herself been born in a park. Sunday was, as I would come to understand her, the one whose body had learned the most precise set of lies. Her dorsal fin listed eleven degrees to port. In wild dolphins, the dorsal is a blade, upright, the leading edge cutting the surface when they ride. In captive dolphins who have spent years circling the same direction — always clockwise, always clockwise, something about the plumbing or the trainer stations or the geometry of amphitheater seating — the dorsal softens. Collagen breaks down without the resistance of open current. The fin folds. It lists. Sunday's listed to port, which meant she had circled clockwise for most of her life, which meant the right side of her body was stronger, which meant when she moved through the open water of the sanctuary she pulled faintly, constantly, toward the left, as if some ghost-pool were still exerting its centripetal claim on her musculature.

Her rostrum tipped upward by perhaps four degrees from neutral. In the wild, a bottlenose holds its beak roughly parallel to its line of travel. In parks, dolphins are trained to lift their heads — to spy-hop, to take fish from raised hands, to orient toward the trainer's face. The muscles of the neck and jaw adapt. The neutral posture shifts. Sunday carried her head as if she were always looking slightly above the horizon, as if the thing she was searching for were not in front of her but elevated, held in a human hand, offered and withheld.

She crossed the boundary line — the place where the net pen's opening faces the channel that leads to open water — and I heard her echolocation change. Inside the pen, she had been clicking rapidly, three or four bursts per second, the way captive dolphins do in new environments: scanning constantly, mapping the space with sound the way a panicked hand maps a dark room. Every surface bounces a click back. In a pool, the information returns in milliseconds. Clean, close, total. Here, in the channel, the clicks went out and some of them did not come back. The ocean swallowed them. I could feel this in my whiskers — the vibrations of her outgoing clicks were strong, staccato, and the returns were sparse, attenuated, scattered by the kelp, absorbed by the drop-off. She was calling into a room that had no walls.

She stopped clicking.

For three full seconds — I counted by heartbeats — she went silent. She drifted in the channel with the tidal current pushing against her left side, her listing dorsal cutting a crooked line through the surface, and she did not echolocate. She did not move her flukes. She let the current hold her.

Then she breached.

It was not the trained breach — the vertical leap, the arc, the splash calculated to drench the first six rows. It was shorter, uglier, a burst of force that took her half her body length out of the water and twisted her so she came down on her side. I think — and I am guessing, because here is where *what* becomes *why* and I lose my authority — I think she was trying to see. I think she launched herself into the air because above the surface the world is sharp in a way the water isn't, and she wanted to see what *no walls* looked like from the other side.

She came down, and the slap of her body sent a pressure wave through the channel that I felt in my chest.

The others followed her, eventually. Tomas with his crooked tail, Pearl with her juvenile curiosity that looked almost like wildness if you didn't notice the way she kept her blowhole angled toward the dock where the fish buckets were stored. Dennis and Aurelie, a pair-bonded couple who moved as a single unit, their pectoral fins sometimes touching, a habit that either was love or was the last remnant of a learned trick where they'd been trained to swim in synchrony for audiences. And Gretchen.

Gretchen, who was the quietest. Gretchen, who was the last to cross the boundary line. Gretchen, whose echolocation I never learned well enough to describe, because she barely used it. She moved through water the way some old animals move — conservatively, close to surfaces, using her vision and her lateral line more than her sonar. A dolphin who does not echolocate is a dolphin who has learned that the information coming back is worse than not knowing. I have known animals like this. I have been one.

I watched them from my rock and I cataloged them, all six, the way I have cataloged every animal I have ever shared water with. Not out of love, though love was part of it. Out of the need that captivity installs in you like hardware: *know what every body around you is doing, because your survival depends on it*. In the parks, I watched trainers the way prey watches predators — not to understand them, but to predict them. I learned the angle of a wrist before the hand signal came. I learned the shift in a trainer's center of gravity that meant a fish was coming versus the shift that meant a punishment was coming, and the two angles were only three degrees apart, and those three degrees were the entire distance between a good day and a bad one.

Here, in the sanctuary, no one was going to punish anyone. I knew this. I knew it the way I know the water is cold — as a fact that my body has accepted but not absorbed. Some knowledge lives in the brain. Some knowledge lives in the muscles that still flinch when a shadow passes over the pool.

I watched Sunday lead her pod into the open water of the channel, and I thought: *they are free*.

And then I thought: *they are shaped exactly like their cages*.

And those two thoughts contained the entire sanctuary between them, the way two hands can hold water for a moment before it runs through.

## II.

I should tell you how I know things so that later, when I tell you what I know, you can decide how much of it to trust.

My left eye is gone. It has been gone since I was a pup, and I do not remember what binocular vision was like, if I ever had it. My right eye functions at what a human veterinarian once described, in a chart I could not read but which was read aloud to a colleague within my hearing, as "approximately fifteen percent nominal acuity with significant astigmatic distortion." What this means in practice: I see shapes, motion, contrast. I see the difference between light and dark. I see a body moving through water as a pressure of color against a different color. I cannot see the expression on a face, human or otherwise. I cannot see the fine tremor of a pectoral fin or the dilation of a pupil. I cannot see what you would call *detail*.

But I can feel it.

Thirty-one years in pools. Concrete pools, twelve meters by twenty, with water that was treated and filtered and recirculated so that it carried no current, no tide, no information except the information put into it by the bodies of the animals who lived there. In water like that, you learn to read displacement. Every body moving through water creates a pressure wake — a bow wave in front, a trailing turbulence behind, lateral waves off the flanks. In a concrete pool, with no wind, no current, no noise except the hum of the filtration system and the distant bass of a public address speaker, those pressure wakes are as legible as printed text. I could feel the approach of a trainer at the pool's edge from the change in surface tension where their shadow fell. I could feel a dolphin's mood in the frequency of its flukes — percussive when agitated, sinusoidal when calm, the difference between a drum and a cello.

My whiskers — my vibrissae — are, or so I have been told by the sanctuary's marine biologist (speaking to an intern, not to me; they rarely speak to me), among the most sensitive in any pinniped she has documented. Harbor seals use vibrissae to detect hydrodynamic trails. A fish that passed by thirty seconds ago leaves a wake in the water that I can read the way — I am told — a dog reads scent. Direction, speed, size, species, whether it accelerated or decelerated, whether it was alone. In the parks, there were no fish trails to read. There were dolphin trails. Trainer trails. The trails of mechanical pool-cleaning apparatuses that hummed along the bottom at 2 a.m. I read them all. For thirty-one years I read everything that moved through my water, because the alternative was to be surprised, and in a marine park, surprise is just the word for the thing that happens before the thing that hurts you.

Here is what this means for Sunday.

I can tell you that her resting pectoral fin stroke rate is forty-two per minute when she is leading the pod in open water, and that it drops to thirty-seven when she is herding — when she is not going somewhere but guiding others to go somewhere. I can tell you that the difference between her leading stroke and her herding stroke is not just speed but *shape*: when she leads, the stroke is full, the pectoral extending to its maximum sweep, pulling water past her body in long laminar sheets; when she herds, the stroke shortens, becomes a sculling motion, a constant correction, the hydrodynamic equivalent of cupped hands steering from the sides rather than arms pulling from the front. I can tell you that she is aware of every member of the pod at all times and that this awareness is legible in the micro-adjustments of her heading — a two-degree turn toward Tomas when his asymmetric tail stroke pulls him off-line, a brief deceleration to let Pearl catch up, a lateral shift that gives Dennis and Aurelie room to maintain their bonded spacing without falling behind.

I can tell you that three times, in the seventeen days between the pod's arrival and what happened to Gretchen, I observed Sunday separate from the group at night and move to the western edge of the pen, where the net meets the rocky headland that forms the sanctuary's northern boundary. I can tell you that at the western edge, in water that is deeper and colder and carries the pressure signature of the open Pacific, Sunday would orient herself perpendicular to the net, her rostrum pointed outward, toward the shelf break. And I can tell you that in this position she would produce a vocalization I have never heard from a bottlenose dolphin.

I should be specific. I have spent thirty-one years in close acoustic proximity to bottlenose dolphins. I know their repertoire the way a person pressed against a shared wall knows their neighbor's music — not by choice, not with expertise, but with an intimacy that comes from years of involuntary listening. Bottlenose vocalizations sit in a frequency range that is largely above my best hearing, but many of their social calls, their burst-pulse sounds, their signature whistles when the pitch drops low, fall into my range, and those I know. I know the whistle Sunday uses when she is calling the pod to regroup: a falling two-tone sequence, almost mournful, that starts at a frequency I can hear and rises to one I can't. I know the sharp buzz she produces when Pearl strays too far, a sound that is more texture than tone, like a zipper being drawn.

The sound she made at the western edge was none of these.

It was low. Lower than anything I have heard from a bottlenose. A pulse, not a whistle — a compression of water that I felt in my jaw and my chest before I heard it in my ears. It was slow. Repetitive. A bass knock produced at intervals of roughly four seconds, each pulse identical to the last. It carried no information I could parse, no variation, no signature. It was the simplest possible sound: *I am here. I am here. I am here.*

It sat in a frequency range where orca communication lives.

I know this because I have shared water with orcas. At SeaMist Orlando, the pool complex housed two captive orcas in the stadium tank adjacent to the dolphin pools, and through the shared plumbing infrastructure — the pipes, the overflow channels, the concrete walls that conducted sound the way bone conducts a tuning fork — I heard them. Their calls. Their echolocation. The sounds that fell below the range of trained human hearing but that traveled through the structure of the building itself, so that every pool vibrated with conversation I was never part of and never understood. The orca vocal range extends down into frequencies that shake water. The sound Sunday made at the western edge sat in that range like a word placed carefully into a foreign sentence.

And on three of those occasions, I felt a reply.

Not a sound — not exactly. A pressure. A pulse coming from beyond the net, from the direction of the shelf break, from deep water. It matched the rhythm of Sunday's call. Four-second intervals. Same frequency. An echo would have come back faster and weaker. This was something choosing to answer.
