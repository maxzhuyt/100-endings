# The Annotated Collapse of Colonel Bernard Fitch, U.S. Army (Ret.)

---

## I.

Let us begin with the specimen.

Colonel Bernard Fitch, United States Army, retired — though he would not use that word, *retired*, not without adding qualifiers that reframed departure as reassignment and reassignment as a lateral expression of ongoing purpose — held, at the time of the events we are about to assemble for your inspection, the following credentials: seventeen years of active service, the bulk of them spent in logistics, which is the military's word for making sure the right things arrive at the right places before the wrong things happen; a deployment to the Persian Gulf in 1991, where he coordinated supply convoys across four hundred miles of road that was sometimes road and sometimes a suggestion the desert made about where road might eventually go; a Bronze Star, the citation for which praised his "tireless organizational initiative under conditions of extreme operational tempo," which is the military's way of saying he did not sleep and things did not explode; an honorable discharge in 1995; and a current appointment — effective fall semester, 1987 — as Assistant Professor of Military Science at Ridgemont State University, a land-grant institution in central Indiana that the U.S. News & World Report college rankings had, the previous year, placed at number 174 out of the 204 schools surveyed, a fact the admissions office chose not to reproduce in its brochures but which the campus newspaper extracted and printed under the headline WE'RE NUMBER 174!, which Fitch had clipped and taped to the inside of his filing cabinet, though he would not have been able to tell you why.

We should see his office. It tells you everything you need, and more than he'd want you to have.

Room 307-B of Harker Hall, second floor, east corridor, a space roughly twelve feet by nine with a window that overlooked the parking lot of the Sigma Chi house. The commendations on the wall — seven of them, various sizes, variously framed but uniformly matted in navy blue — had been hung with a torpedo level. You could run a laser line across their bottom edges and weep with satisfaction. The desk was organized into quadrants, each serving a declared function: upper left for active correspondence, upper right for reference materials, lower left for the week's cadet evaluations, lower right for personal effects, which amounted to a single framed photograph of Fitch shaking hands with a brigadier general whose name even Fitch had begun to forget and a coffee mug bearing the words MISSION FIRST in stencil-style lettering. The mug was ceramic. It was cream-colored, or had been once; it was now the color of a creek bed in a region with heavy tannin content. Fitch had never washed it. He did not believe in washing coffee mugs. He had once explained this to a colleague as a matter of "seasoning, like cast iron," and the colleague had looked at the mug and then at Fitch and decided not to press the point.

It is a Tuesday morning in October. The year, again, is 1987, which those of you performing arithmetic will note makes certain dates we'll discuss later impossible, and you would be right to notice this, and wrong to think it matters. The leaves on the quad maples have turned the particular orange-brown of a manila envelope left in the sun. The air smells like cold dirt and someone's malfunctioning catalytic converter. Fitch is in his office at 0547, thirteen minutes before he is required to be anywhere, because Bernard Fitch does not arrive on time. Bernard Fitch arrives *early*, and the distinction is a load-bearing wall in the architecture of his selfhood.

Now. We should be transparent with one another, you and I, because the alternative is that I pretend to a neutrality I do not possess, and you pretend to believe me, and we proceed through this account in a state of mutual dishonesty that would serve neither of us. I find Colonel Bernard Fitch both pitiable and hilarious. I find him pitiable because he has constructed, over the course of fifty-one years, a life whose every surface communicates competence, order, and masculine self-possession, and I know — as you will shortly know — that by Thursday afternoon of this same week he will be lying face-up in the cafeteria salad bar of the Ridgemont State University student union, draped in surplus parachute silk, with ranch dressing in his left ear and a garbanzo bean lodged in a location he will not permit the paramedic to name in the incident report, and that from this posture of absolute indignity he will look up at the fluorescent lights and experience something that is either a cardiac event or the arrival of the first genuine emotion he has had in six years. And I find him hilarious because — well. You'll see why. You'll see the geometry of it. You'll see how every load-bearing wall turned out to be drywall, and how every drywall panel turned out to be painted paper, and how the paper tore.

Understanding how he got from this Tuesday morning to that Thursday salad bar is the only reason we are being assembled.

I trust you're comfortable. I trust you understand what's being asked of you. We are going to watch a man come apart at the joints, and we are going to find it very funny, and then we are going to find it something else, and the fact that I'm telling you this now — handing you the map before the territory — doesn't diminish the journey. It makes you responsible for taking it with your eyes open.

He is in his office. The mug is full. The commendations are level. The desk is in quadrants. The man is in one piece.

We should get started.

---

## II.

The practice field behind Harker Hall was, in a previous institutional life, a women's field hockey pitch, and the ghost of its old line markings still surfaced through the grass every spring like memories the university couldn't fully repress. Fitch had requisitioned its use for ROTC drill formations on Tuesday and Thursday mornings from 0630 to 0745, and the registrar's office had granted this without argument because no one else wanted it, because the drainage was poor, because the aluminum bleachers on the east side — a six-row set installed sometime during the Ford administration — tilted visibly to the left, and because the nearest bathroom was a Porto-John that the athletics department had abandoned after a football tailgate in September and whose interior condition had become the subject of a campus legend that freshmen told each other in the dining halls with the solemnity of a ghost story.

Eighteen cadets stood in formation at 0630. They were freshmen, all of them, because Fitch had been assigned the freshman cohort, a fact that Fitch understood as a temporary staging position and that the department chair, Dr. Leland Pryce, understood as a containment strategy. They wore PT uniforms — gray shirts, black shorts — and most of them shivered because it was forty-one degrees and most of them had not yet learned that the Army does not recognize temperature as a valid experience.

Fitch stood in front of them. He wore his BDU jacket ironed to specifications that exceeded regulation, boots polished to a state that one cadet later described as "almost wet-looking, like they were alive," and the expression of a man who believed that the next forty-five minutes were the most important forty-five minutes these eighteen human beings would experience today, this week, possibly this semester, and who was not entirely wrong, though for reasons he could not yet imagine.

"Good morning," he said, and his voice carried the way voices carry when they have been trained to treat open air as an auditorium. "Today we are going to execute a basic column-to-line formation shift, and we are going to execute it *correctly*, which will make it the first thing some of you have executed correctly since you arrived at this institution."

A few cadets smiled. Most didn't. In the second rank, fourth from the left, Cadet Anaya Reyes did neither. She stood at attention with her eyes on the middle distance and her weight evenly distributed across both feet and her expression set at a frequency that registered, if you were looking closely — and Fitch was always looking closely, which was both his talent and his first, structural problem — as *alert but unconcerned*. It was the face of someone who had already read the manual, understood the drill, and was waiting for the performance to begin. Not her own performance. His.

Reyes was twenty years old. She was five-foot-six with a runner's build and dark hair pulled into a regulation bun so tight it looked structural, like it was holding something together underneath. She had transferred to Ridgemont State from a community college in Killeen, Texas, at the start of the fall semester. Her transfer paperwork listed her previous ROTC participation as "one semester, incomplete," without elaboration. She spoke precisely, infrequently, and only when the question required more than compliance.

Fitch gave the order. "Column to line — *march*."

And here is where we must slow down, because what happens next takes four seconds in real time but contains, compressed within those four seconds, the entire mechanism of what will eventually put this man in a salad bar.

Seventeen cadets moved. They moved with the herky-jerky confidence of freshmen who have drilled just enough to know the footwork but not enough to feel it, a kind of ambulatory sight-reading. They pivoted. They sideslipped. They established the line. It was not beautiful. It was not even particularly good. But it was *synchronous* — the steps landed within the same approximate rhythmic window, close enough to read as unison from a distance.

Reyes moved too. She performed every element of the maneuver with textbook fidelity — the angle of the pivot was correct, the length of the sideslip was correct, her spacing in the established line was correct. But she arrived a half-beat late. Not a full beat. Not a stumble, not a delay that could be called hesitation or incompetence. A *half-beat*. She was a thirty-second note behind the rest of the formation, the way a jazz musician drags behind the downbeat to create a pull, a tension, a sense that the rhythm is not *wrong* but is being *commented upon*. It was the kinetic equivalent of putting a word in italics. The maneuver said *march*. Reyes said *march*.

Fitch felt it.

He could not have explained what he felt. If you had asked him — and no one would, because Fitch was not a man to whom people addressed questions about his feelings — he would have said the formation was "sloppy," or that "someone" was "dragging," and he would have scanned the line and found nothing to correct, because there was nothing to correct. Reyes was in position. Reyes was in compliance. But Fitch's nervous system had registered a disruption, the way a man who has tuned pianos for thirty years can hear a string that is technically within tolerance but *not right*, and the disruption had landed in his body, in his sternum, as something that was not quite irritation and not quite anxiety but sat in the territory between them, vibrating.

He called the formation to attention. He called it again. He began to pace the line, his boots leaving crisp prints in the damp grass, and his voice rose — not in anger, exactly, but in volume, because volume was the tool Fitch reached for when precision failed, the way a man with a hammer treats everything he encounters as an insufficiently impacted nail.

"The formation shift is executed *on the beat*, not *near* the beat, not *around* the beat, not *in the general neighborhood* of the beat. *On* it. The United States Army does not syncopate."

This was, though Fitch did not know it, probably the most perceptive thing he would say all week.

"I will demonstrate," he said, and here is where the local geography enters the causal chain because Fitch, who wanted to show the maneuver from an elevated vantage — who wanted, though he would not have phrased it this way, to *rise above* the formation, to re-establish the vertical axis of authority that Reyes's half-beat had subtly tilted — turned and walked to the aluminum bleachers on the east side of the field.

The bleachers. Let us look at them. Six rows, bolted frame, galvanized aluminum over a steel substructure that had not been inspected since the Carter administration. The left-side support struts had been slowly torquing for three years due to uneven ground settlement. The bolts along the third row were not so much fastening the planks as *suggesting* a relationship between plank and frame, a relationship both parties had grown ambivalent about. The bleachers were, structurally, an argument waiting to lose.

Fitch climbed to the third row. He planted his boots — a combined weight of approximately two hundred and fourteen pounds of man, boot, and unexamined emotional investment in the concept of elevation — on a plank that had been, unknown to him or anyone else, migrating slowly away from its left-side bolt for the better part of a year.

"Column to line. Watch the *feet*."

He stepped forward to gesture. His left boot came down on the plank's unsupported end.

What followed took approximately 1.6 seconds, but we will take it slowly because it deserves the attention.

The plank tilted. Not dramatically — it dipped perhaps four inches to the left, which was enough to send Fitch's center of gravity past the point of recovery but not enough for him to understand, in the first fraction of a second, that recovery was no longer an option. His body did what bodies do: it tried to correct. His right foot shot sideways to find purchase on the second-row plank, which was also compromised, and which responded to this new lateral load by torquing free of its remaining bolt with a sound — a high, shearing *shriek* of aluminum on steel — that carried across the practice field like the scream of a mechanical animal.

Fitch went through the bleachers.

Not over them. Not off the side. *Through*. His left leg dropped between the second and third rows. His right hip struck the frame rail. His arms, reaching for anything solid, found only a plank that came free in his hands like a lever disconnected from its function. He landed on the ground beneath the bleachers in a posture that defied skeletal convention — his left leg still through the gap above, his torso twisted, his right arm behind him at an angle that suggested his shoulder had briefly entertained the idea of dislocation before thinking better of it. The plank he'd pulled free clattered down beside him with a second metallic shriek, a coda.

The bleachers, having expressed their opinion, settled into a new configuration and were still.

On the field, eighteen cadets stood frozen. The formation had dissolved into a loose cluster of individual humans performing the same rapid calculation: *Is he hurt? Is it bad? Am I allowed to react?* Cadet Morrison, front rank, let out a sound — a single, involuntary bark, half gasp, half laugh, the kind of noise the body makes when it doesn't know which protocol to run — and then clapped his hand over his mouth as though he could push it back in. The sound hung in the October air for a moment, visible almost, like breath.

From the ground beneath the bleachers, Fitch could see the sky through the gaps in the rows above him. The sky was the pale, noncommittal blue of a region that has not yet decided whether it's going to rain. A bolt lay near his head. It was rusty. He stared at it with the expression of a man who has not yet updated his internal model of the world to include the events of the last two seconds, who is still, in some part of his nervous system, standing on the third row giving a demonstration.

One cadet moved.

Reyes left the formation without being told and crossed the field at a jog — not a sprint, not the headlong rush of panic, but the measured, purposeful pace of someone who had been expecting to be needed. She knelt beside the bleachers, one knee in the mud, and extended her hand.

"Colonel. Are you hurt?"
