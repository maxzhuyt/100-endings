The rational agent accepts.

**2. Fordham**

Lord,

I know I said I wouldn't do another panel. I know I said the panels were a vanity and that the last one — with Al Mohler and that woman who kept calling me "Reverend" even though I explained twice that I am not ordained, I hold a *doctorate in systematic theology from Tübingen* — was the final straw. You heard me say that. You were there.

But this isn't a panel. This is a *retreat.* The word itself — You see it, don't You? — carries the monastic echo. Retreat. Withdrawal from the world. The desert mothers did this. Macrina did this. Granted, Macrina did not have a television crew, and the desert mothers were not housed in a Titan II missile silo in western Kansas with a libertarian economist who once referred to the Sermon on the Mount as "an early example of market-distorting altruism" on a podcast that, I confess, Lord, I listened to the entirety of while pretending to proofread galleys.

The money is significant. I should say that plainly because You already know it and there is no dignity in pretending otherwise. Adjunct salaries at two institutions plus the book advance for *Kenosis and the Keno Hall* (which Eerdmans has described as "ambitious but unlikely to earn out") do not sustain a person, and my insurance situation is —

But that isn't why.

Here is why: Vikram Patel will be there. The transhumanist. The man who said, at the Aspen Ideas Festival, that "consciousness is a software problem and death is a hardware failure," and the audience *applauded.* Nine hundred people applauded the abolition of the soul. I watched the clip eleven times. I have the applause memorized. It is a specific sound — enthusiastic but not ecstatic, the clapping of people who have been told something that flatters them. That is different from the sound of genuine praise, which I think You know better than anyone.

I need to be in a room with that man. I need to be in a room with all of them. Not because I have anything to prove but because — Lord, I am going to say this badly, I always say it badly — because the arguments I make in books are too *clean.* They exist in a space where I always win because the other voices are paraphrased, pruned, set up to fall. I have never tested my faith against a living, breathing interlocutor who genuinely believes that the human person is an optimization problem. I have only tested it against my notes on what such a person might say, which is a form of shadow boxing, which is a form of pride.

I need to be humiliated a little. That is the monastic instinct. You know this.

I also need the $350,000. You know this too.

I have already sent the RSVP. I am writing this prayer after the fact, which is not ideal — I recognize the structural irony — but prayer is not exclusively petition. Sometimes prayer is the honest accounting that comes after the decision has already been made by the part of oneself that does not pray.

Forgive me for the order of operations.

Amen.

P.S. — Surely Osei will decline. She called the Whitmore Foundation "a laundromat for oligarchic guilt." 2019. I have the tweet saved. Why do I have the tweet saved.

**3. Osei**

**DRAFT — The Silo and the Spectacle: Notes Toward a Critique of Civilizational Nostalgia**

*Nadia Osei*

*[UNPUBLISHED — DO NOT SUBMIT — this is not an essay, this is thinking out loud, stop formatting it like an essay, Nadia]*

The invitation arrived on Whitmore Foundation letterhead, cream-colored, the paper weight alone communicating endowment. "Civilization Rescue Retreat." I want to sit with that phrase, which carries the entire pathology of the contemporary bourgeois intellectual class in five syllables. *Civilization* — whose? *Rescue* — from what? *Retreat* — to where? The answers, respectively: theirs, themselves, and an ICBM silo in Kansas, which is so on-the-nose it ceases to be metaphor and becomes simply *architecture.*

Seven public intellectuals. Six months underground. Television cameras. The premise is that if you put enough credentialed minds in a bunker, the bunker becomes a think tank. This is the fantasy of the PMC apocalypticists — that catastrophe is a seminar, that collapse is a curriculum, that the appropriate response to civilizational failure is a well-curated panel. In this fantasy, the missile silo is not a relic of the same military-industrial death drive that produced the conditions requiring "civilizational rescue" in the first place. It is simply a *venue.*

I should refuse.

I am going to refuse.

I am drafting the refusal now, and it will be the most precisely worded email of my career, because even refusal, for people like us, is a publication. Meachum will read it. Fordham will read it. They will know I was above this, that I saw through it, that I named the structural function of the spectacle before consenting to be spectacularized.

*[Here the draft breaks off. Below, in a different color of ink, smaller handwriting:]*

The advance for *Surplus Personhood* was $14,000. Suki starts at Dalton in September if she gets off the waitlist and the tuition is — it doesn't matter what the tuition is. What matters is that I am now calculating tuition against principle, which is exactly the calculus the Whitmore Foundation depends on. They don't need you to believe in the project. They need you to need the money. The invitation is not a philosophical proposition. It is a financial instrument.

*[Smaller still:]*

If I go, I can write about it afterward. The critique from inside. The participant-observer. I've built a career on exactly this method — embedding in the structures I study, metabolizing them into analysis. The silo is simply another field site. I am not capitulating. I am *gathering material.*

*[In the margins, barely legible:]*

Tell yourself whatever you need to, Nadia. You have been telling yourself things for forty-three years and the telling has never once prevented the doing. You will go. You will go because the $350,000 is Suki's tuition for four years and because your last three op-eds were rejected and because you have not been invited to anything in nine months and because loneliness, which you have theorized so beautifully in print, turns out to be — in practice — banal. You will go because they asked. That's the whole of it. They asked.

**5. Crane**

Douglas Crane did not write down his deliberations. Douglas Crane, the Straussian political philosopher who had taught at the Committee on Social Thought for twenty-two years and who believed that the history of Western civilization was the history of approximately forty books, did not keep a journal, did not draft memos to himself, did not speak into recording devices. Douglas Crane sat in his study on Woodlawn Avenue, in the leather chair his first wife had chosen and his second wife had tried to replace, and stared at the invitation for six evenings. On the seventh evening — and he was conscious of the biblical symmetry, and embarrassed by his consciousness of it, and further embarrassed by his embarrassment, which struck him as a form of inverted piety — he called the Whitmore Foundation's number and said, "This is Crane. I accept."

What he did not say, to the Foundation or to anyone, was that his doctor had used the phrase "early-stage" in a sentence the previous Tuesday, and that the six months in the silo would either be the last significant act of his intellectual life or a ludicrous waste of the time he had left, and that he could not determine which, and that this inability to determine which was itself a kind of answer, because Douglas Crane had spent forty years arguing that the examined life required the willingness to submit one's certainties to conditions that might destroy them, and he had never once meant it literally until now.

He also did not say — would not have said, would have considered it beneath expression — that he was, in the simplest and most animal sense, afraid to be alone with the diagnosis.
