# What the Well Gave Back

## I.

The bucket came up wrong.

I knew before I saw it. The rope was heavy in a way that wasn't water-heavy. It was thick-heavy, like pulling something up that didn't want to come. The crank fought me. I set my feet on the stone apron and leaned back and hauled, hand over hand, the way Callum taught me back when I was nine and the handle still came up to my chin.

The bucket cleared the lip. The water was black.

Not muddy. Not silted. Black like a bruise under a fingernail, with an iridescence that moved when I tilted the pail. It smelled sweet and wrong, the way the compost bins smell in August when someone throws meat in by mistake. I set the bucket on the stone. I looked at it. I looked at it for a while.

It was June. The wells on the east side had been low since April. Harlan Goss's stock pond went dry on the fourteenth of May, and by the end of the month the Kellner family was driving their jugs to Enid to fill them at the Walmart spigot. But this well — the big communal one by the meeting hall, the one that went down ninety feet into the limestone aquifer — this one had never failed. Petra said so at every meeting. She said the founding families had dowsed for it in 1987 and that the aquifer was a gift and that gifts do not leave.

Three flies landed on the surface of the black water and did not move again.

I poured the bucket out onto the dirt. It left a stain that looked like a hand.

Then I saw the marks.

They were cut into the stone lip of the well, on the south side, down low where you wouldn't see them unless you were crouching the way I was crouching. Not scratched. Cut. Whoever made them had used something sharp and had pressed hard and had known what they were making. Three symbols in a row. The first looked like two triangles overlapping with a line through both. The second was a circle with marks radiating inward, not outward, like a sun drawn backward. The third I couldn't describe then and can't describe now. It sat wrong in my eyes. I looked at it and my vision slid off it the way water slides off wax, and I had to force myself to look again, and when I did my stomach dropped like I'd missed a step on a staircase.

I didn't tell anyone.

I don't mean I made a decision not to tell anyone. I mean my body walked to Callum's cabin and my mouth told Callum. That was what my body did when something was wrong. It had been doing it since I was three and Callum was nineteen and already too old for what I needed from her but giving it anyway.

She was on the porch, sharpening a skinning knife on a whetstone. She did this more than she needed to. I think she liked the repetition, the long stroke, the sound of metal finding its edge. She was wearing a shirt with the sleeves cut off and I could see the scar on her left deltoid where the shrapnel had gone in and the two smaller scars on her forearm where it had come out the other side, because nothing in Callum's body went in a straight line, not even metal.

I said, "The well's gone bad. There's marks on the stone."

She didn't ask what kind of marks. She put the knife down and the whetstone down and she looked at me and I watched something cross her face.

It was not fear.

I knew Callum's fear. I had seen it at three in the morning when she came out of a dream shouting in a language that wasn't English and wasn't any language, just sound, just the noise a person makes when their body is somewhere their mind won't follow. This was different. This was still. This was like watching someone open a letter they'd been expecting.

Recognition.

She looked at me for a long time. She looked at the ridge to the east where the tree line was brown and withdrawing, every blackjack oak pulling its leaves inward like fists, conserving. She looked back at me.

"Show me," she said.

We walked to the well. She crouched. She ran her thumb over the symbols. She stayed like that for two full minutes, and I counted because I didn't know what else to do.

She didn't say a word about what the symbols meant. She stood up and her knee popped and she pressed her hand flat against the well's stone lip like she was taking its temperature.

"Don't drink this water," she said. "Tell people the pump's fouled. Tell them to go to the Kellners' standpipe until I figure this out."

"Figure what out?"

She picked up the bucket I'd left on the ground. A residue of black coated the inside. She held it up to the light and looked through it the way she looked through everything — like it owed her information and she intended to collect.

"Go home, Bree."

I went home. I didn't tell anyone about the marks on the well. I didn't tell anyone what Callum's face had done.

It was the first thing I kept for her, and it was not the last.

## II.

The Ottinger barn came down on a Wednesday.

Nobody heard it happen, which was strange because the Ottinger barn was forty years old and twelve thousand square feet and held sixteen head of cattle and you'd think a structure like that meeting the earth would make a sound you could hear from the road. But Karen Ottinger said she was in the kitchen and she heard nothing and when she looked out the window the barn was flat. Like it had been flat always. Like the standing version was the aberration and the fallen version was the correction.

Callum was there within the hour. I was with her because I'd been at her cabin when the call came through on the radio, and she didn't tell me to stay and I didn't offer.

The smell hit us at the fence line. Sweet rot, already, in the heat, and under it something sharper, like scorched hair or the smell of a hot electrical wire. The temperature was a hundred and four. The sky was white. Everything had edges.

Callum climbed the wreckage the way she did everything physical — with a competence so deep it looked like indifference. She placed her feet without looking down. She balanced on a collapsed truss and surveyed the field of debris and I saw her eyes move in a pattern I recognized from the times she'd taken me hunting, the same grid search, the same systematic sweep left to right, near to far.

"Hand me that pry bar," she said.

I handed her the pry bar from the truck bed.

She worked for three hours. I helped where I could. She levered boards and corrugated tin and I dragged them clear and we found the cattle underneath, one by one, and none of them were right.

The first had been dead long enough that its belly was distended and its legs were rigid, splayed at angles that a falling roof would not produce. Its head was bent backward. Not broken backward. Bent, like something had taken the skull and rotated it past the point where the spine should have stopped things and the spine had simply allowed it. Callum rolled the animal over with her boot and her knee and her hands and she looked at its belly and she didn't say anything.

The second was worse. I won't say how.

The third had the marks.

They were cut into the fence post nearest the carcass. The same three symbols. The overlapping triangles, the inverted sun, the third one my eyes couldn't hold. Callum crouched and I watched her hands — her torn-up hands, her knuckles bleeding now from the pry bar work, her fingers precise the way they always were, the way I imagined they'd been when she packed wounds in Helmand or wherever she'd been that she did not discuss.

She had a scrap of paper from the truck's glove box. She drew the symbols. Her lines were exact.

She drew more. She'd found others I'd missed — carved into the foundation stones where the barn had met the ground, cut into the underside of a feed trough, scratched into the concrete pad where the water tank had stood. She mapped them. She noted positions. She worked the way I'd seen her work on the engine of her truck when something was wrong, which was the way she'd worked on Danny Farrow's broken arm last winter, which was the way she moved through every problem: with her hands, with her body, with a silence that was not empty but full, full of a competence she had no interest in performing.

Her limp was bad by the second hour. She didn't mention it. She never mentioned it. I knew only what I'd assembled over the two years since she'd come home — the discharge papers she'd left on the kitchen table once, the prescription bottles in her medicine cabinet that I read when she was out (trazodone, naproxen, something I couldn't pronounce that I later looked up and found was for nerve damage), the way she set Danny Farrow's arm without hesitating while Danny screamed and his mother held him and Callum said, "It's clean, it'll set straight, stop moving," and it did.

She'd been a combat medic. 68 Whiskey, which was a designation I'd found on her DD-214. She'd deployed twice. She'd come home and she'd come here and she didn't talk about the middle part. Some nights I heard her through the wall. Some mornings there were bottles in the recycling that hadn't been there the night before. Some afternoons she sat on the porch with the whetstone and the knife and she sharpened and sharpened and her eyes were somewhere I couldn't reach.

Now she was crawling through a dead barn in heat that made the air shimmer and she was reading marks in stone and wood and she was not surprised by them.

"Callum."

She looked up.

"What are they?"

She sat back on her heels. Her shirt was soaked through. There was blood on her forearms where the corrugated tin had got her. She looked at the scrap of paper with its constellation of symbols and positions and she looked at the dead cattle and she looked at the way the wood of the barn had splintered.

The wood had splintered wrong. I had noticed this but had not said it because I didn't have the words. The boards hadn't broken the way wood breaks under weight, cracking along the grain, splintering outward from the point of impact. They had broken inward. Toward themselves. As if the pressure had come not from above but from inside the wood itself, as if every board had tried to become smaller than it was and had torn apart in the effort.

"Binding marks," Callum said. She said this the way she said everything, which was once and with certainty.

"Binding what?"

She folded the paper and put it in her back pocket. She picked up the pry bar and got to her feet and her knee nearly buckled and she caught herself on a beam and I pretended I hadn't seen.

"I don't know yet," she said. "But someone here knows what they're doing. And they've been doing it for a while."

We drove home. She didn't speak. I watched her hands on the steering wheel — the torn knuckles, the steady grip, the old burn scar on her right hand that she said was from an engine block and that I believed was from something else. Callum's body was a document written in a language I was still learning to read. Every scar was a sentence. Every unmentioned pain was a paragraph. I was fourteen and I had been reading her my whole life, and I was not finished.

That night I lay in my bed in the cabin we shared and I listened to her move around the kitchen. She was looking at something. I could hear pages turning. Not a book — something stiffer, like a manual, or printed sheets. She was up until two. I know because I watched the light under the door disappear at 2:07 a.m. and I had been counting the minutes the way I'd counted them at the well, because counting was what I did when I was afraid and did not yet have permission to say so.

In the morning the paper with the sigil map was pinned to the wall above her desk, next to a printout of something I couldn't read, covered in her handwriting.

She had circled the third symbol — the one I couldn't look at — and beside it she had written a single word.

I didn't read the word.

I wish I had.

## III.

The birth I remember is the Cortez baby.

It was March, before the wells went bad, back when the drought was just the drought and not yet the other thing. Maria Cortez was nineteen and it was her first and she labored for twenty-two hours in the cabin she shared with Jesse while half the women of the collective rotated through in shifts, bringing broth, bringing ice chips, bringing the steady presence of people who had done this before and believed it would be fine.

Petra came at the end.

She always came at the end. This was understood. Petra was not a midwife — Lena Farrow was the midwife, competent and brisk and unsentimental in the way that Callum was unsentimental, which was the way of people who have seen enough of the body's reality to stop decorating it. Petra's role was different. Petra came after.

Maria delivered at 4:16 a.m. I was there because I'd been there all day, because I was the one who ran between cabins with messages and supplies, because I was small and fast and nobody noticed me, which was the condition I preferred. I was in the corner of the room when the baby came, and I saw Lena catch it and clear its mouth and lay it on Maria's chest and I saw Maria's face, which was a face I will not describe because it belonged to her and not to me.

Then Petra came in.

She filled the room the way she always filled rooms — not with size, because she was small, and not with volume, because she was quiet, but with a warmth that was almost physical, a radiance that made people lean toward her the way plants lean toward windows. She was fifty-three. Her hair was gray and long and she wore it braided with red thread woven through. Her hands were small and quick and always busy — adjusting a blanket, touching a shoulder, tucking a strand of hair behind someone's ear. She touched people constantly and people let her, wanted her to, reached for her.

She crossed the room to Maria and she put her hands on Maria's shoulders and she said, "You did beautifully. Look what you've made."

Then the blessing.

Everyone knew it. Everyone had received a version of it, either at their own birth or at the birth of their children. Petra spoke the words. I don't remember all of them. I remember the shape — an invocation of the land, the water, the collective, the bonds between people who had chosen to live together in a place they'd built from nothing. She spoke about roots and about holding and about the ties that keep a person safe. She spoke about belonging. Her voice was low and steady and the room settled into it the way a held child settles into the arm beneath it.

She laid herbs on Maria's belly. I knew some of them — sage, cedar, sweet grass, which were common. And others I didn't know. Small dark leaves that looked like they'd been dried a long time. A sprig of something woody and almost black. She arranged them in a pattern on the cotton sheet that covered Maria's stomach and her fingers worked quickly and I watched because I was always watching.

Here is what I saw.
