## Editor's Note on Conventions

Throughout this monograph, dates are given in the Old Territorial Style (O.T.S.) unless otherwise noted. Acreages refer to the Modified French Arpent as surveyed by the Beaumont-Delacroix expedition of 1809 and should not be confused with the Standard Congressional Acre adopted after the Reorganization. Sums denominated in *livres territoriales* have been converted to United States dollars at the fixed rate of exchange established by the Provisional Comptroller's Office in Baton Vermillion, though the author acknowledges, as he must, that this rate was never honored by any merchant, banker, or flesh-and-blood human being west of the Trestle River.

Territorial Franco-Creole legal terminology has been transliterated according to the Thibault-Marchand system (rev. 1842), with certain modifications necessitated by the unique orthographic customs of the French Badlands district clerks, who were not, as a class, celebrated for their spelling. Where a term exists in both the metropolitan French legal tradition and the Territorial Franco-Creole dialect, preference has been given to the latter, on the grounds that the metropolitan term, however precise, was never once uttered in the rooms where these agreements were made.¹

## CHAPTER I

### Provenance of Riparian Deed No. 1173-F, with Preliminary Remarks on the Hydrology of the Alkaline Flats

---

The document that is the subject of this monograph — Riparian Deed No. 1173-F, hereafter "the Deed" — is a single sheet of rag-linen paper, 34 cm × 47 cm, bearing the watermark of the Toulouse Royal Paper Mill, dated in the hand of Territorial Notary Gaspard Michaud on the eleventh day of March, 1819, and filed with the Provisional Comptroller's Office in a cedar box that had once, if the staining on the interior lid is any guide, held Andalusian cigars. It grants to its holder dominion over the waters of the Deux Fourches watershed: the creek, the creek's tributaries, the seasonal runoff channels, the subsurface springheads, and — in a clause whose legal creativity has been insufficiently admired — "all moisture, whether flowing, standing, seeping, or condensing, that may presently or in future be found within the boundaries herein established."²

The Deed passed through four holders before arriving, by inheritance through the maternal exception codified in Territorial Statute 7-14(b), to Clementine Darrow, née Marchaud, in the year 1831. Of these earlier holders, three died without issue and one, the surveyor Arnaud Chasseriau, was last noted in the public record when he purchased a one-way passage on a flatboat heading south and told the dock clerk, according to the embarkation log, that he was "finished with dirt."³

Mme. Darrow enters the archive — and it is the archive, not the mythology, that concerns this monograph — as Item 7714 in the property index of the French Badlands Administrative District: *"Darrow, Clementine M. — Propertied, femme sole, 2nd desig. — Holder, R.D. 1173-F — Deux Fourches watershed, all riparian rights — Acreage: see attached survey (survey not attached)."*

There is no birth record. There is no death record.⁴ Between these two absences, there is the Deed, the ledgers, the correspondence files, and the collected testimony of ninety-one years of rainfall data, which latter the compiler now presents.

### §1.1 — Rainfall Data for the Alkaline Flats, 1803–1847

The significance of Riparian Deed No. 1173-F cannot be apprehended without reference to the hydrology of the surrounding territory. The compiler therefore presents the following table, drawn from the combined observations of the Beaumont-Delacroix survey team (1803–1811), the Provisional Comptroller's rainfall gauges at Forts Duchesne and Sainte-Geneviève (1812–1833), and the Darrow Estate's own rain barrels as measured by the estate clerk — that is, by the present compiler — each morning at six o'clock for the period 1836–1847.⁵

| Year(s) | Avg. Annual Rainfall (inches) | Characterization |
|---|---|---|
| 1803–1811 | 11.4 | Marginal |
| 1812–1818 | 8.7 | Poor |
| 1819–1824 | 6.1 | Critical |
| 1825–1830 | 4.3 | Severe |
| 1831–1835 | 2.9 | Emergency Declared (P.C.O. Gazette, 12 May 1833) |
| 1836–1840 | 1.6 | — |
| 1841–1844 | 0.8 | — |
| 1845 | 0.3 | — |
| 1846 | 0.1 | — |
| 1847 | Trace | — |

The reader will observe that the column labeled "Characterization" ceases to contain entries after 1835. This is because the Provisional Comptroller's Office, which was responsible for issuing official characterizations, closed its French Badlands substation that year, the clerk having resigned his post to seek employment in a climate he described, in his letter of resignation, as "compatible with the continued existence of human life."⁶

The trend is unambiguous. By the time Mme. Darrow assumed possession of the Deed in 1831, the alkaline flats were in the fifteenth consecutive year of decline. By 1840, the year the first leaseholder presented himself at the Darrow property seeking terms, rainfall had fallen to a quantity that the Beaumont-Delacroix expedition's chief meteorologist would have classified, had he been available for consultation, as "functionally nonexistent." He was not available. He had died of thirst in 1827.⁷

What remained, in 1840 and every year thereafter, was the Deux Fourches watershed: one creek, spring-fed, subterranean in origin, indifferent to rainfall, flowing at a constant rate through land that was otherwise becoming powder. The deed to that creek was in Clementine Darrow's cedar box. The cattle on the surrounding ranches were dying.

The ranchers came.

### §1.2 — On the Nature of the Darrow Holding

A word is necessary on the character of the property itself, apart from its hydrological significance.

The Darrow holding comprised not merely the riparian rights codified in the Deed but also a physical parcel of forty-seven arpents situated at the junction of the two forks of the creek from which the watershed takes its name. On this parcel stood a house: two stories, cypress frame, galleries on three sides, built in the Creole plantation style by an earlier holder whose optimism regarding the district's future was not, in the event, rewarded by history. The house was large for the territory. It had rooms that were not strictly necessary. One of these rooms, situated on the second floor at the eastern end of the gallery, had been fitted — at some point before the compiler's tenure, by a hand he cannot identify — with a lock of notable quality, curtains of a dark velvet that admitted no light from the exterior, a writing desk of the type used by court clerks, and a single straight-backed chair positioned at the center of the room upon a floor that had been, for reasons the compiler will address in due course, reinforced with additional joists beneath.

The room is listed in the property inventory as "the study."⁸

This monograph will have occasion to refer to it frequently.

## CHAPTER II

### Article III: Mechanisms of Counter-Signature, with Especial Reference to the Darrow Method of Clause Ratification

---

The French Badlands Territorial legal code, assembled piecemeal by a succession of provisional administrators none of whom had formal legal training and at least two of whom were, by their own admission, barely literate, contains numerous provisions without parallel in any other jurisdiction known to the compiler. Among these is Article III of the Compact on Leasehold Ratification (Territorial Statute 22-7, ratified, if "ratified" is the word, in 1814 by a quorum of three men in a room above a trading post), which provides that a contract between a propertied *femme sole* of the second designation and any leaseholding party shall be considered binding only upon the completion of the following:

1. Presentation of terms by the petitioning leaseholder, in person, at the holder's property.
2. Review and attestation by a licensed fiduciary clerk.
3. *Ratification of each clause through the physical instrument and personal hand of the holder, witnessed by the attending clerk, each clause to be individually ratified in sequence.*

It is the third provision that requires explanation.

The Territorial code does not specify the nature of the "physical instrument." It does not define "personal hand." It does not describe the form that "individual ratification in sequence" must take. These omissions were, in the context of the early Territorial legal apparatus, unremarkable; the code was written by men who assumed that the holders of major deeds would be other men, and that ratification would involve a handshake, a glass of whiskey, and perhaps a rifle fired into the air. The framers did not anticipate Clementine Darrow.⁹

What Mme. Darrow devised — or what had been devised before her and merely continued under her hand, the provenance is unclear — was a method of clause ratification so precise in its choreography that the compiler, who witnessed every session from behind the velvet curtain in his capacity as attending clerk, can reconstruct each one from the ledger without recourse to memory, which is as it should be, memory being unreliable and the ledger being the ledger.¹⁰
