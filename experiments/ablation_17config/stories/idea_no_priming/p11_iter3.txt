# The Stubborn Idiocy of Human Desire

## I.

I have maintained the pipes of the Loma Seca Desert Resort & Conference Center for forty-one years. In that time I have pulled from drains the following: eleven wedding rings, a prosthetic hand, six handguns (three loaded), one Bible in Portuguese with all the passages about forgiveness razored out, a child's shoe, a wig, a length of intestine that turned out to be surgical tubing and not, as I had knelt there praying, a reason to call the police, and enough hair to knit a second human being, though not one you would want to meet. I have replaced the east wing's sewage main three times. I have soldered copper in the dark by feel alone. I have spoken to pipes the way other women speak to saints, and with better results.

My name is Dolores Puente Alcántar. I am seventy-eight years old. My knees are finished. My hands are the only part of me that has gotten better with time, and this is because I have asked nothing of them except honest work.

I am standing now in the mechanical room beneath the east wing, and the water is at my shins and receding. Somewhere above me, the smoke is thinning. I can tell because the air has shifted direction in the ventilation shaft — pulling upward now instead of pressing down — which means someone has opened a door or a wall has given way. There are sirens, but they are the distant kind, the kind still deciding whether to bother with us. The last fluorescent bulb in this room is flickering at its terminal frequency, the death-rattle of a ballast I replaced in 2011 with a part that was already used.

Last Saturday, seven former soldiers arrived at the Loma Seca to celebrate a wedding. By Sunday morning, four of them were dead, a sewage main that I had held together with prayers, calcium deposits, and my unwillingness to retire had erupted through the ballroom floor with the enthusiasm of a geyser that had been waiting its entire geological life for an audience, and I had seen — right here, in this room, by this light — the thing I had spent four decades waiting to see.

Proof that love is a catastrophic plumbing failure nobody has the dignity to call a plumber for.

You will want to know about the deaths. You will want to know about the men with guns, and the sewage, and the thing Alondra said to Carver in the tunnel when she thought no one else could hear but I could hear because I have always been able to hear through pipes, it is the only gift God saw fit to give a woman He otherwise treated as an afterthought. You will want to know about the ceviche.

I will tell you all of it. But I will start where water starts. At the highest point. Before it falls.

## II.

The Loma Seca Desert Resort & Conference Center was built in 1957 by a man named Harold Fisk who believed that the desert outside Alamogordo would become "the Palm Springs of the thinking class." Harold Fisk was an optimist of the most dangerous variety — the kind with capital. He built an eighty-room resort in a place where water had to be pumped from a depth that would embarrass most oil wells, and he connected every room with a plumbing system so ambitious in its scope and so criminal in its engineering that I have spent my entire career in a state of furious astonishment that the building still stands. The pipes run through the walls like veins through a body that should have died in infancy. They cross where they should not cross. They rise where gravity forbids. In the east wing, a hot water line passes through a cold air return in a way that suggests Harold Fisk's plumber was either a surrealist or a drunkard or both, and the result is a single room — 114 — where the shower produces a mist that is neither hot nor cold but *confused*, a temperatureless fog that I have come to think of as the most honest weather the building makes.

I came to the Loma Seca in 1983. I had been married for six years to a man named Octavio who sold air-conditioning units in Las Cruces and who believed that my profession was an embarrassment to him at dinner parties, a position he maintained with increasing vehemence until the night he told me that a real woman's hands should not smell like pipe dope, and I told him that a real man should be able to unblock his own toilet, and the marriage concluded in the way that most marriages conclude around here, which is to say slowly, and then with a single door slamming so hard it cracked the casing.

I took the job at the Loma Seca because the Loma Seca would take me, and because the man who interviewed me — the old maintenance chief, Gallegos — said to me: "The building is dying. I need someone too stubborn to let it." And I thought: *I know what that is. I have been that for a man. I can be that for a building instead.*

Forty-one years. I have watched this resort decline from a minor destination to a regional embarrassment to a place where the county government holds mandatory trainings because the rate is low enough to justify with taxpayer money. The pool has been drained and refilled and drained again so many times that the tiles have forgotten their original color. The conference room's ceiling leaks in a pattern I have mapped to a failed boot flashing on the roof that no one will authorize me to replace because the roof itself is scheduled for replacement in a fiscal year that never arrives. The west wing has been closed since 2014. I keep its pipes from freezing in winter out of principle, the same way you might keep visiting a relative in a coma.

But the plumbing endures. Beneath every renovation, every coat of paint applied over mold, every new manager's affected optimism, the pipes remain — the resort's truest autobiography, written in copper and iron and PVC and, in the oldest sections, clay.
