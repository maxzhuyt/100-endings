# The Sigurdsson Defections

## I.

They came into Jericho Falls by three separate conveyances, which told you everything.

Henrik arrived first, on the morning stage from Redfield, sitting with his bag between his boots and his hands on his knees like a man reporting for sentencing. He was the eldest and the largest — wide through the chest and fair-haired in a way that belonged to the northern timber countries, to the Swede who'd fathered him in a logging camp outside Duluth and whom he had never met. He stood on the platform and watched the dust lift off the street in coils and thought about how a man could die of thirst in a place like this and no one would remark upon it. He did not think about his mother. He had trained himself out of that.

Julien and Nessa shared a rented dray that pulled in an hour later, its axle groaning, its horse indifferent to the apocalyptic heat. That they had traveled together meant nothing warm. They had simply departed from the same city — San Francisco — and Julien had calculated that splitting the fare was more efficient than paying it twice. He was like that. He was lean and dark, with the bones of his Creole father arranged prettily in his face, and he had a way of stepping down from a conveyance that made you feel he was arriving at a party held partially in his honor. Nessa climbed down after him without assistance, a small fair woman of twenty-three whose resemblance to Henrik began and ended with her coloring. Where Henrik was a monument, Nessa was a needle. She had her English father's narrow face and his physician's habit of looking at people as though she were diagnosing them. She diagnosed Jericho Falls in nine seconds and found it terminal.

Cass came last and on foot, which he would have done even if someone had offered him a horse. He came from the south, walking the rail line with a canvas pack and a carbine he made no effort to conceal. He was twenty-six and red-haired, freckled, wiry — the Cork father expressed in every tendon — and he vibrated at a frequency the others could feel before they could see him. He rounded the corner near the land office and stopped, seeing Henrik on the porch, and for four full seconds neither brother moved. Then Cass spat in the dirt, adjusted the carbine on his shoulder, and crossed the street toward him with the unhurried gait of a man who has never once avoided a thing he should have avoided.

"Henrik."

"Cass."

"She here yet?"

"Inside."

Cass studied the door of the land office the way he might study the mouth of a cave that was rumored to contain either gold or a bear. "Good for her," he said, and went in.

Marta Sigurdsson was already at the counter when all five of her children assembled behind her, and she did not turn around. She was fifty-one years old and had been beautiful in a way that had shaped the trajectory of her life and the lives of five men and the lives of the five children those men had left behind, and she was still handsome, but the beauty had curdled into something more tactical, a face that knew precisely what it could do and no longer bothered to do it unconsciously. She stood with her weight on her right leg because her left was bad — a break that had healed unevenly after a fall from a second-story window in Abilene eight years prior, a fall that had also ended her partnership with a man named Crowther and begun her acquaintance with morphine. She held her traveling case against her ribs with both arms, the way a woman holds a child she suspects someone will try to take. She was sweating. The office was hot, certainly, but not as hot as all that.

The clerk was a young man with a receding chin and the apologetic demeanor of someone who delivers bad news professionally. His name was Partlow, and he had been in Jericho Falls for three years, which was long enough to have watched the town curdle. He had the deed spread on the counter and a hand near it, as though he might pull it back.

"The parcel is just under forty acres," he said, "running from the creek — which is dry, ma'am, I should be clear about that, she's been dry since April — running from the creek northeast to the ridge. Used to be grazing land."

"Used to be," Marta repeated, not as a question.

"Still could be, I suppose, if you brought water in. But the issue isn't the water, ma'am." Partlow looked past her at the five bodies arranged in no particular formation behind her — Henrik by the door, Julien near the window, Nessa against the far wall, Cass leaning on nothing in the center of the room, and Tomás, the fifth, standing where he always stood, which was wherever it was easiest to watch everyone else without being watched himself. Tomás was the middle child and the quietest, a dark-eyed young man of twenty-eight who had his father's mestizo complexion and his mother's skill at looking like he wasn't paying attention. In his vest pocket, a watch ticked against his chest.

"The issue," Partlow continued, "is that the parcel sits on the line between Frank Crowley's operation to the north and the Delacroix outfit to the south. And those two families have been —" He paused. He looked again at the five strangers, at the woman with the limp, at the traveling case. "There's been violence. Regular violence. Over the trade road that runs through that exact corridor. Opium, they say, though Crowley'll tell you it's about grazing rights, and Mrs. Delacroix won't tell you anything because she doesn't care what you think. Two men dead this spring. One last fall. The land your uncle left you, ma'am, is the exact piece of ground they're killing each other to control, and neither of them is going to be happy to hear a third party's put a claim on it."

He said all of this with the earnestness of a man who believed in the power of information, in the basic rationality of people who receive warnings. He was young enough, still, for that.

And each of Marta's children heard the warning, and each metabolized it differently, because they were five people whom an accident of blood had made into a family and whom everything else — geography, temperament, the specific quality of each father's absence — had made into strangers.

Henrik heard the word *violence* and began calculating exit routes and timelines. If they left now, they could be back in Redfield by Thursday. There was work in Redfield. There was always work, for a man built like he was built, who did not complain. He had spent his life not complaining. The not-complaining had become a kind of religion, quietly practiced, whose central tenet was: *expect nothing from her, and nothing is what she'll cost you.*

Julien heard *opium* and leaned slightly forward. Not with greed, exactly — Julien's avarice was subtler than greed, more architectural. He heard the word and saw a structure: supply lines, distribution, territory. He saw the shape of money, and he admired the shape even before he calculated whether any of it was his. He had his father's gift for admiring systems. His father, a card mechanic in the French Quarter, had admired the system of poker so thoroughly that he'd spent thirty years inside it without ever once considering leaving. Julien had inherited the admiration and the myopia.

Cass heard the warning and felt the heat in his hands that he associated with the approach of something interesting. He did not think in terms of risk. He had never developed that faculty. Risk was a concept for people who intended to live long enough for consequences to matter, and Cass — although he could not have articulated this, although a frontier in 1884 did not furnish the vocabulary — had made his peace with brevity at fifteen, when he'd stabbed a man in Cheyenne who'd been beating a horse. The man had lived. Cass had not particularly cared either way. What he cared about was whether things moved. Things here looked like they were about to move.

Nessa heard the warning and was already composing, in the quiet surgery of her mind, the letter she would write to the man in San Francisco who had asked her to marry him and whom she had left without answering. She had come to Jericho Falls because Marta's summons had arrived the same week as the proposal, and Nessa, who understood herself with the pitiless clarity she applied to everyone else, recognized that she had chosen the summons precisely because it was the worse option. She always chose the worse option, when the better one would have required her to be happy. Happiness was the territory whose border she patrolled from the outside, taking notes.

And Tomás heard everything.

Tomás always heard everything. He heard the withdrawal in Marta's breathing — the shallow pulls that meant her body was counting the hours since its last administration. He heard the lie in her posture, the way she held the traveling case: not protectively but possessively, the way an addict holds a supply. He heard the clerk's warning and registered it fully, without translation, and he also heard the thing beneath the warning, which Partlow himself did not know he was saying: *This land is worthless except as a provocation.* And Tomás thought: *She knows that. She knew that before we came.* He felt the watch tick against his chest. *For M, who deserved better than me.* His father's hand, in miniature, in copperplate. A man who'd wept the one time Tomás had met him, in a hotel in Santa Fe, who had pressed the watch into an eleven-year-old's hands and said, *Give this to your mother,* and whom Tomás had looked at and known, with the X-ray certainty of children, was a defeated man. Tomás had kept the watch. He had not given it to Marta. He had never told her about the meeting.

He watched her now from behind.

"Ma'am," Partlow said, "I'd encourage you, strongly, to consider —"

"Show me where to sign," Marta said.

She signed. Her hand was steady, which told Tomás she had dosed herself that morning — enough to function, not enough to dull the gears. The traveling case held the supply. He was certain of it. How much, he did not know.

They walked out into the street, all six of them, and the light was so bright it had the quality of an interrogation. Marta turned and faced her children. She looked at them the way a carpenter looks at lumber: measuring, grading, assessing what each piece could bear.

"There's a boarding house on Guthrie Street," she said. "Two rooms, I've arranged it. Henrik, Cass, Julien in one. Nessa and Tomás with me."

"The hell I'm sharing with him," Cass said, pointing at Henrik, who said nothing.

"Two rooms," Marta said, "or the street. I don't care which."

She turned and walked up the thoroughfare, and her limp was worse in the heat, and her children followed her at five separate distances.
