# The Pottsbury Nativity

## I.

The envelopes made a satisfying grid: four across, three down, arranged by creditor and then by date of final notice, which happened also to be—Edmund noted with the detached appreciation of a man who has passed through panic into something colder—an arrangement by ascending consequence. The top row was merely unpleasant. The bottom row was the end of the world.

He squared the corners with his fingertips. The kitchen table had a wobble that no amount of folded cardboard had ever cured, and when he leaned forward the whole spread shifted a quarter-inch to the left, so he held very still and looked at his work the way a chess player looks at a lost position, not because there is a move to find but because the geometry of defeat has its own austere beauty.

From the bedroom: Helen's breathing, shallow and irregular in the way it had been since November, when the breathing had become the thing she did instead of asking him questions. She had stopped asking questions not because she trusted him but because she had understood, with the animal clarity of a woman who had married a clerk and watched him remain one, that the answers would not help.

The stockings hung from the mantelpiece by drawing pins. Three of them: Arthur's, Lily's, and the baby's—though the baby was two and a half now and had a name, which was George, but Edmund still thought of him as the baby because the baby had arrived at roughly the same moment as the first letter from Partridge & Whittle, Solicitors, and the two events had become inseparable in his mind, twinned like figures in an allegory he could not read.

He had presents for none of them. He had sixpence in his trouser pocket and a tin of condensed milk that was meant for Boxing Day tea and a plan that, when he examined it in the silence of midnight on the twenty-fourth of December, seemed no more and no less insane than the situation that had produced it.

He washed his hands. He dried them on the towel that hung from the oven rail. He put on his coat, his hat, his gloves—the left glove had a hole in the index finger, which he had repaired with black thread that did not match—and he picked up a small parcel wrapped in brown paper. Inside the parcel was a book he had bought secondhand for Gerald three years ago and never given, a volume of Ruskin that had cost him two shillings at a barrow in Farringdon and was, he supposed, worth roughly one shilling now, which made it the only asset in the household whose depreciation he could view with indifference.

The streets were empty and white. Snow sat in the gutters in a way that would be filthy by morning but was, at this hour, still clean enough to pass for beautiful. He walked to Gerald's house because he did not own a car and because the buses had stopped running and because the walk, twenty-two minutes through Kensington at a brisk pace, was exactly the right length to rehearse a lie without having enough time to talk himself out of it.

Gerald's house was lit from every window. The party was audible from the pavement: a piano, laughter, the particular roar of voices that meant champagne had been open for at least two hours. The front steps had been swept and salted by someone who was paid to sweep and salt things, and the door knocker was a brass lion's head that Gerald had brought back from Florence in the same year that Edmund had brought back from the hospital a baby he could not afford to feed.

He did not knock. He went around to the servants' entrance, which he knew would be open because tonight the caterers would be using it, and he waited in the basement corridor until the kitchen traffic produced one of those brief intervals—sixty seconds, perhaps ninety—in which no one was coming or going and the corridor belonged to no one.

The study was at the end of the hall, past the cloakroom, past the small lavatory that Gerald's guests used when they did not wish to go upstairs. It was locked, but it was locked with the kind of lock that Gerald had not bothered to upgrade since purchasing the house, because Gerald did not think in terms of locks. Gerald thought in terms of walls, and the walls were his, and who would need to lock a room inside his own walls?

Edmund had the key because he had always had the key. He was the brother-in-law who handled small tasks—collected dry cleaning, returned library books, held the spare key to the study for those occasions when Gerald mislaid his own, which was often, because Gerald was the kind of man who could mislay a key to his own study and find this charming rather than alarming, because there was always someone like Edmund to hold the spare.

The room smelled of leather and pipe tobacco and something else, faintly chemical—the preservative that had been applied to the display case, perhaps, or merely the smell of money at rest. The Nativity set occupied the center of Gerald's desk, which was itself the size of Edmund's kitchen table and did not wobble. It sat on a cloth of green velvet. It was lit, even now, even during the party, by a small brass lamp that Gerald left burning through the Christmas season, as if the figures required light even when no one was looking, as if they might grow afraid in the dark.

They were beautiful.

Edmund had known they were beautiful. He had seen them every Christmas for eight years, since the year Gerald married Margaux, and every year he had stood in this room with a glass of Gerald's whisky and looked at the figures and felt something that he would have called reverence if he were a different kind of man, and that he called instead appreciation, which was the same feeling with the embarrassment left in.

The Madonna was perhaps nine inches tall. Her robe was blue edged with gold, her face turned down toward the manger, and her expression—this was the thing about the set, the thing that made it supposedly worth a fortune—her expression was not serene. She looked worried. She looked like a woman who had just been told something she did not entirely believe by someone she was not sure she trusted, and who was sitting with the knowledge the way one sits with a toothache, unable to forget it, unable to make it mean what it should mean. She looked, Edmund thought, like Helen.

He set the Ruskin on the corner of the desk. He took from his coat the three dish towels he had brought from home—clean, pressed, folded in thirds, because he was not a man who could bring dish towels anywhere without pressing them first—and he spread the first towel flat on the velvet.

He picked up the Madonna.

She was lighter than he expected. He did not know what he had expected. He had never touched her before. He placed her on the dish towel and folded the cloth around her, tucking the edges under the way one tucks a blanket around a child who is already sleeping, with the particular care that comes from knowing the child will not notice but that the gesture matters anyway, the way all gestures that no one sees matter, which is to say not at all, or absolutely.

Joseph next. Joseph was taller, thinner, painted in browns and reds, holding a staff that was no thicker than a matchstick and that Edmund handled with the held-breath delicacy of a man defusing an explosive. The staff did not break. He wrapped Joseph in the second towel, adjacent to the Madonna but separated by a fold of fabric, which seemed correct without his being able to say why.

The Christ child was the size of his thumb. He held it in his palm for a moment and felt the precise weight of it, which was nothing, which was almost nothing, and then he wrapped it in the corner of the third towel and placed it between its parents.

The magi came next, and here he slowed, because there were three of them and they were the most elaborately painted—Caspar in ermine, Melchior bearing a gilded vessel, Balthasar in robes of deep improbable green—and their gifts were rendered in such fine detail that Edmund could see the individual brushstrokes on the lid of the myrrh box, and he thought, wrapping each figure with the focus of a surgeon, that whoever had painted these gifts had understood something about the act of giving that Edmund was only now beginning to learn, which was that the gift is always too small for the occasion, and the occasion is always too large for the room.

The shepherds were simpler. Two of them, rough-featured, kneeling. He wrapped them together in the remaining fabric.

The animals: an ox, a donkey, and three sheep of diminishing size, which he nested inside each other's wrapping like Russian dolls because there was no more cloth, and he was improvising now and hating it, because improvisation is what happens when the plan meets the world and the world wins.

The angel he wrapped last, in his own handkerchief, because the angel's wings were fragile and extended and would not survive the hard corners of a dish towel. The angel's face, unlike the Madonna's, was serene. The angel was the only figure in the set who seemed to know what was happening.

He placed them all in the leather valise that he had bought for this purpose at a shop in Camden for four shillings—his last four shillings, an investment in his own crime, and he felt, as he closed the clasps, the specific nausea of a man who has crossed a line and cannot see it behind him anymore.

The case was on Gerald's desk. The velvet cloth was empty. The lamp still burned over nothing.

He walked out through the servants' entrance. No one saw him. The snow received his footprints with the absolute indifference of snow, and behind him the party made sounds like happiness, and above the party, in a room he had never entered, Gerald's children slept in beds that were paid for, in a house that was warm, and Edmund Pottsbury—clerk, husband, father of three, thief—walked into Christmas morning carrying everything sacred and none of it his.

## II.

Gerald kept his car—an Austin 10, green, three years old, spotless—in a mews two streets south of the house. Edmund had the key to this as well, for the same reason he had the key to the study: someone had to drive the thing to the mechanic when Gerald could not be bothered, and someone had to park it properly when Gerald left it at angles that offended the mews, and that someone was always Edmund, because Edmund was the kind of man who could be relied upon to do small things well and who had been, without quite noticing, conscripted into a lifetime of small things so that Gerald would not have to notice them either.

He opened the driver's door. He placed the valise on the back seat with the care of a man placing a child in a crib. He sat down. He adjusted the mirror. He placed his hands on the steering wheel.

"You'll want to let her warm up a bit. She's been sitting since Tuesday."

Edmund did not scream. He did not, in fact, make any sound at all, which was its own kind of scream—the scream of a man whose body has decided that silence is more survivable than noise.

Margaux was in the passenger seat. She was wearing her fur coat—the good one, the one Gerald had given her for their fifth anniversary, which was sable and which she wore with the unapologetic ease of a woman who understood that the coat was beautiful, that she was beautiful in it, and that neither of these facts required an apology. Under the coat, visible at the collar and cuffs, was a dress Edmund had seen earlier that evening through the study window: dark green silk, cut in the way that expensive things are cut, which is to say in the way that makes the cutting invisible. Her hair was pinned. Her lipstick was recent. She held a cigarette in her left hand and in her right a small glass of champagne that she had apparently carried from the party through the snow and into the car without spilling, which struck Edmund as the single most remarkable act of the evening, including his own.

At her feet was a valise. Not his valise—hers. Smaller. Tan leather, with a clasp.

"Margaux."

"Edmund."

"What are you doing in the car."

"Sitting."

"Why are you sitting in the car."

She drew on the cigarette. The tip flared orange in the dark interior, and for a moment her face was lit from below, which made her look like a figure in a painting, which was how she often looked, because Margaux had a face that painters and a certain kind of man responded to in the same way: with the suspicion that beauty of that precision must be doing something deliberate.

"I know what you've taken," she said.

Edmund's hands did not leave the steering wheel. "I haven't taken anything."

"You're a dear man and a miserable liar. You left the lamp on. An empty display case lit like a chapel. Gerald won't notice until morning because Gerald doesn't notice anything before morning, but I noticed because I went looking for you, and you weren't at the party, and you don't go to parties to enjoy them, you go to parties to stand in Gerald's study and look at things you can't afford, and tonight the things you can't afford were gone and so were you."

"Margaux—"

"I don't care why."

"—this is not—"

"I know why. I've known about the money since September. Helen telephoned me. She telephoned me because she had no one else to telephone, which tells you everything you need to know about what your pride has cost her."

The snow was falling harder now. It landed on the windscreen in patterns that were almost, but not quite, regular, and Edmund watched them because they were easier to watch than Margaux.

"The plan," he said, carefully, "requires me to reach York by morning. Alone. I know a dealer there. The set is insured for—Gerald told me—he told me at dinner three years ago, quite drunk, quite pleased with himself—it's insured for—"

"Eight hundred pounds."

"Eight hundred pounds, yes. The dealer will give me half that, perhaps less, but enough—more than enough—to settle everything, and I'll send the money to Gerald anonymously, over time, when I can, with interest—"

"Edmund."

"—and I need to go alone because if anyone sees us together it will look—"

"Like what?"

He turned to her. She was watching him with an expression he could not catalogue—not amused, not pitying, not angry, but something from which all three of those had been removed, leaving a residue that was simply attentive. She was paying attention to him in a way that no one had paid attention to him in years, and it was unbearable.

"Like something it isn't," he said.

"Get the car started. We'll freeze."

"You cannot come."

"I agree. It's a terrible idea."

"Then—"

"I sat here for forty minutes waiting for you. My coat is sable but it is not heated. I have a champagne glass and nowhere to set it down. I am wearing shoes that cost more than your monthly rent, which I know because Helen told me your monthly rent, and the snow has already ruined the left one. I am not going back inside."

"Margaux, please—"

"Start the car."

He tried once more. He tried with a speech that was— he felt it even as he spoke—a clerk's speech, organized in numbered points, each point leading logically to the next, the whole structure elegant and airtight and exactly the kind of argument that persuades no one who has not already agreed. Point one: Gerald might check the car. Point two: her absence from the party would be noticed. Point three: the dealer in York was expecting a single seller, not a couple. Point four: if they were seen together on the road, at night, on Christmas Eve—

Margaux opened the champagne hand and let the glass drop neatly into the footwell, where it landed on her valise and did not break. She put both hands in her coat pockets. She looked straight ahead through the windscreen into the falling snow. She was, Edmund understood with the calm of total defeat, not going to respond to his points. She was not going to respond to anything. She was going to sit in the passenger seat of Gerald's car until the car moved or the sun came up, and in either case she would be there, and in neither case would it be Edmund's decision.

He started the car.

The engine caught on the second try, and the sound of it in the mews was enormous, and Edmund thought of the Nativity figures in their towels on the back seat, and of the Nativity itself—the actual one, the one in the Bible, which he had heard read every Christmas of his life and which was, now that he considered it, also a story about a man with a plan that had already gone wrong, setting out at night with a woman he could not control, toward a destination that might not receive them.

He did not think this consciously. He was not the kind of man who made allusions. But the shape of the thing was there, underneath, like the pattern in a carpet that you only see when you are lying on the floor, which is where Edmund felt he approximately was.

They drove north.
