# The Census of a Dying Dream

## I.

District Eleven, West Threshold. Population at last count: 4,223. Population at present count: 4,197. Net loss: 26. Cause of attrition: 12 to age, 6 to emigration toward the Midward gates, 4 to vocational reclassification (see: District Fourteen, East Threshold, supplementary rolls), 3 to a quarrel over the rightful ownership of a singing dog, 1 to the slow and unremarkable erosion of conviction that one exists.

Moll recorded this last entry without editorial comment, as was proper. The census did not distinguish between causes of departure. A body removed from a district was a body removed from a district, whether by hearse or by ontological doubt, and the appropriate column was Column Sigma: Attrition, Unspecified. She made the mark. She moved to the next house.

The eleventh district of the Kingdom of Brelle unfolded behind her in its customary grid: streets named for their compass headings and ordinal positions (Third North, Seventh East, First Diagonal), buildings numbered in a spiral sequence originating from the district's central well, each doorframe painted in one of eleven approved municipal colors corresponding to the structure's zoning classification. Residential: pale blue. Commercial: a dark and sensible umber. Religious: the color of goldenrod, which the Bureau of Spiritual Affairs had selected by committee in the year 1164. Moll knew the year because the Bureau's founding charter was a census document, and all census documents were, in a sense, her kin.

She climbed the steps of 4-Seventh-East. Residential. Pale blue, though the paint had cracked in a pattern that suggested either poor weather or indecision on the part of the underlying wood. She knocked. She waited. She held the ledger against her forearm in the Bureau-approved posture—spine braced against the wrist, pages open to the current entry field, stylus in the right hand, left hand free for gestures of civic greeting—and when the door opened, she said what she always said, which was nothing improvised.

"Census of Brelle. Season of the Millstone, year 1212. I am Enumerator Moll, credentialed under the Bureau of Categorical Completion, Registry Mark 1378-South. I am here to count you."

The woman in the doorway was perhaps fifty. Stout. A dress the color of boiled beets, faded to a gentler blush at the elbows where the dye had surrendered to use. Her hair was dark and pulled into a practical knot from which three individual strands had escaped in an arrangement that Moll would describe, in Column Theta: Physical Presentation, as "minor asymmetric disarray, non-indicative." The woman held a piece of bread in her left hand. She was not eating it. She was holding it the way one holds a thought one is not yet ready to abandon.

"Afternoon," the woman said.

Moll recorded: *Household 4-Seventh-East. Occupant 1 of (pending). Female. Approximate age: sixth quartile. Dress: faded beetroot. Bread in left hand (wheat, partial). Threshold greeting: "Afternoon." Posture: receptive, slightly guarded, leaning eleven degrees from frame center. Time of interview: three-quarter bell.*

"How many in the household?" Moll asked.

"Just myself and my daughter. My husband's been reclassified to Fourteen."

"The singing dog quarrel?"

"He didn't even like the dog."

Moll noted this in the margin—not because it was relevant, but because the Bureau's Addendum to Guideline 9 encouraged enumerators to capture "contextual flavor" where it did not impede the count. Contextual flavor, per the Addendum, enriched the kingdom's self-knowledge without compromising its self-organization. Moll had always appreciated this permission. It was the Bureau's single concession to the possibility that people were more than data points, and it was housed, appropriately, in a footnote.

She recorded the daughter. Age: second quartile. Hair: black, braided. Occupation: student of letters, which placed her in Vocational Category 7 (Symbolic Trades), Subcategory C (Pre-Professional). The daughter stood behind her mother and looked at Moll's ledger with the cautious fascination of someone watching a mirror that might not reflect her back.

"Am I in there?" the daughter asked.

"You are now," said Moll.

The daughter smiled. Moll did not record the smile except in the sense that all smiles were recorded by the silence that followed them, which is to say: she turned the page.

---

There was a fact about the Kingdom of Brelle that appeared in every census ledger, on page one, in a box bordered by a double rule, beneath the seal of the Bureau of Categorical Completion and above the enumerator's oath of fidelity. It was printed in the Bureau's standard typeface—a seriffed and unimaginative font called Permanent—and it read as follows:

*The Kingdom of Brelle exists within the recurring dream of the god Somn, whose body rests at the world's edge in a state of terminal decline. All features of the kingdom—including but not limited to its geography, its citizens, its weather, its physical laws, its history, and this ledger—are products of the Dreamer's oneiric activity. The Bureau of Categorical Completion acknowledges this datum and files it under Column Omega: Cosmological Context. Current status of the Dreamer: declining. Estimated remaining dream-cycles: see Bureau advisory, updated seasonally.*

Moll read this box at the start of every census season the way a sailor reads a weather almanac: with respect for its authority and no particular expectation that it would change her route. The dream was the dream. The count was the count. Column Omega existed to hold the fact, and the fact, being held, did not spill into the other columns. This was the Bureau's great achievement, perhaps its only genuine one: it had given the end of the world a place in the spreadsheet, and having placed it there, it had rendered the end of the world *administrative*.

The seasonal advisory, posted in every district office on paper the color of weak tea, currently read: *Dreamer's cognitive output: diminishing. Rate of oneiric decay: 0.7% per cycle, consistent with a forty-cycle trend. Projected total cessation: not within the current enumerator's career span. Enumerators are advised to continue standard operations.*

Moll continued standard operations. She moved through District Eleven with the steady, lateral patience of a typewriter carriage, house to house, threshold to threshold, recording the kingdom one person at a time. She counted the baker on First Diagonal (male, flour-dusted, spoke the word "rosemary" as his threshold greeting, which was unusual but not unprecedented). She counted the locksmith's household on Ninth North (four occupants, one of whom was a baby whose vocation was listed as Pending with a subsection note: *"Shark," per the mother, who reports the child resembles one*). She counted the small, quiet woman on Second East who opened her door, said nothing at all, held up three fingers to indicate her household size, and closed the door with a firmness that bordered on civic commentary.

Moll marked them all. Column Alpha through Column Omega. The count accumulated. The kingdom, in the act of being tallied, became more solid, more present, more defensibly real—as if each entry in Moll's ledger were a stitch pulled tight in a fabric that might otherwise sag. This was the Bureau's operational theology, though it would never have used that word: *that which is counted, persists*. The census was not merely a record of the kingdom. It was, in some functional and never-fully-articulated sense, the kingdom's ongoing argument for its own existence. Each entry said: *This person is here. This person is distinct. This person has a name and a trade and a garment color and a word spoken at the threshold, and these details matter, and their mattering is the substance of the world.*

Moll believed this. Not as ideology. As practice. The way a mason believes in plumb lines.

By evening she had counted 211 households. She had filled forty-six pages. She sat in the district's enumerators' quarters—a narrow room with a cot and a desk and a lamp that burned with the steady, unemotional light that the Bureau provided to all its field agents—and she reviewed her entries. Everything balanced. Every column tallied. District Eleven was, as of this evening, a known quantity, which was the closest thing to a saved one that the Bureau's vocabulary allowed.

She closed the ledger. On its cover, the Bureau's motto was embossed in Permanent: *To Count Is to Keep.*

The lamp burned. Outside, the wind carried the sound of the kingdom arranging itself for sleep—a clatter of shutters, a dog's half-committed bark, the distant chime of some municipal bell marking an hour that would, if the seasonal advisory was correct, continue to exist through at least the next enumeration cycle. Moll made no entry for any of this. Some things the ledger did not need to hold. She slept.

## II.

District Twelve, South Threshold. Day six of the Season of the Millstone.

The woman was standing in the doorway of 7-Fourth-West, and she was wearing a dress the color of boiled beets, faded to a gentler blush at the elbows. Her hair was dark, pulled into a practical knot. Three individual strands had escaped. She held a piece of bread in her left hand—wheat, partial—and she was not eating it. She leaned eleven degrees from the door frame's center.

"Afternoon," the woman said.

Moll's stylus stopped.

She looked at the woman. She looked at her ledger. She looked at the woman again. The face was not similar. It was not reminiscent. It was the face she had drawn in shorthand six days ago on page fourteen of the District Eleven section, the face that belonged to 4-Seventh-East, the face she had cataloged with the quiet competence of someone who had cataloged nine thousand faces before it and expected to catalog nine thousand more. The same face. The same dress. The same bread, held at the same angle, interrupted at apparently the same moment of the same meal.

Moll turned back to page fourteen. The entry was there. Ink undisturbed. *Household 4-Seventh-East. Occupant 1 of 2. Female. Approximate age: sixth quartile. Dress: faded beetroot. Bread in left hand (wheat, partial). Threshold greeting: "Afternoon."*

She turned forward to the blank page that awaited 7-Fourth-West in District Twelve.

The Bureau's guidelines were clear on the matter of recording. Guideline 3, Section 1: *An enumerator records what is present at the threshold. The enumerator does not editorialize, interpolate, or adjust the observed datum to fit prior entries. Each threshold is a new act of observation. Each observation is sovereign.*

Moll recorded what was present at the threshold.

*Household 7-Fourth-West. Occupant 1 of (pending). Female. Approximate age: sixth quartile. Dress: faded beetroot. Bread in left hand (wheat, partial). Threshold greeting: "Afternoon." Posture: receptive, slightly guarded, leaning eleven degrees from frame center. Time of interview: three-quarter bell.*

She wrote each word with a care that was not diligence but something closer to dread. Each stroke of the stylus confirmed what the stylus was not designed to confirm: that two entries in two different districts, separated by sixty pages and six days of road, were identical. Not merely alike. Not parallel. Not the product of coincidence or the limited palette of a dying god's imagination. *Identical.* Down to the degree of the lean and the partial state of the bread and the specific three strands of escaped hair, which Moll now saw fell across the woman's brow in the same arc—left temple to right cheekbone—that she had noticed but not quite recorded the first time, a detail she had carried in her body rather than her ledger, and which now returned to her with the sickening precision of a debt she had thought she didn't owe.

"How many in the household?" Moll asked.

"Just myself and my daughter. My husband's been reclassified to Fourteen."

"The singing dog quarrel?"

"He didn't even like the dog."

The daughter appeared behind the mother. Age: second quartile. Hair: black, braided. She looked at Moll's ledger.

"Am I in there?" the daughter asked.

"You are now," said Moll.

She turned the page. She did not look back. The woman closed the door, and Moll stood on the step and experienced something that the Bureau's vocabulary could not accommodate and that her ledger could not record, which was the sensation of a tool encountering the thing it was never built to measure. An accountant once told Moll that the most terrifying number in mathematics was not infinity but *two*, because two identical things in a system that guarantees uniqueness means the system is lying about what it guarantees.

Moll had thought he was being dramatic.

She walked to the next house. She knocked. She asked her questions. She recorded the answers. She did not flip back to page fourteen. She did not need to. The two entries sat in her ledger like a pair of matched weights on a scale that was supposed to hold only one, and the scale, having no mechanism for this, simply trembled.

That night, in the District Twelve enumerators' quarters, she opened the ledger to the current page and the page from District Eleven and laid them side by side on the desk. She read each entry aloud, one after the other, and they were the same sentence spoken twice by the same mouth, and the lamp's steady light—that unemotional Bureau-issue light—illuminated them both with the same impartial clarity, because the light, like the ledger, did not know how to distinguish between a single truth and a truth that had said itself again.

She wrote beneath the District Twelve entry, in the narrow space the Bureau allocated for fieldwork annotations:

*Duplicate observed. Cause: unknown. Column: none.*

There was no column for this. The Bureau of Categorical Completion had designed a ledger with a place for everything, including the death of the god whose dream made the places possible, and it had not designed a column for the possibility that the dream had begun repeating itself. That the raw material of persons—the faces, the dresses, the words, the gestures, the eleven-degree leans—was running out, and the Dreamer, in its terminal sleep, had started reusing what it had.

Column: none.

Moll closed the ledger. She did not sleep. Outside, the wind carried the same sounds as the night before, and she could not be certain whether this was because the nights in the Kingdom of Brelle reliably sounded alike, or because there was, now, only one night, being dreamed over and over by a god who had forgotten it had already dreamed it.

## III.

She met the girl on a road between districts, which is to say: she met her in the space the Bureau classified as Non-Enumerable Territory, per Guideline 11 ("Roads, rivers, and liminal thresholds connecting categorized zones are not themselves subject to census and should be traversed without recording"), which meant that Moll was, for the forty-minute walk between District Twelve and District Thirteen, technically off-duty, her ledger closed, her stylus pocketed, her identity as Enumerator in a state of administrative suspension.

She was, therefore, simply Moll—a woman walking, unburdened of the obligation to count—when the river beside the road stopped flowing.
