# The Chapel of Returning

## I.

These are the hands that were given to me. These are the ten inadequate instruments set at ten and two upon the wheel, and the wheel is neither hot nor cold, and the engine has been off for eleven minutes, and the condensation on the windshield is doing what condensation does, which is to say: it is making the world outside impressionistic, which is to say: it is lying to me in the manner I prefer.

I prefer my lies soft-focused. I prefer them in watercolor. I have been, if we are honest — and we are not honest, you and I have never been honest, but let us pretend toward honesty the way one pretends toward prayer, which is to say with the body first and the conviction never — I have been sitting in this car long enough that the dispatcher has called twice and I have answered twice and both times I said *en route* which is a French phrase meaning *I cannot make my hands release this wheel*.

The street is Delacroix. The street has always been Delacroix, though for eleven years I drove past it via Montrose, which is the long way, which is the coward's way, which is — let me be precise, because precision is the last devotion I permit myself — which is the way that does not require me to see the building at the corner of Delacroix and Seventh, the building I am now parked in front of, the building I am studying through condensation as though condensation were a veil and I a bride approaching something I have already married and already ruined and already been ruined by in return.

The building. Let me describe the building. Let me catalogue it the way I was taught to catalogue, clinically, the way the academy taught me to see a scene before I enter it, the way — but no. Not clinically. Clinically would require me to note the patrol cars, the tape, the light-bar painting the brick in alternating wounds of red and blue. I am not ready for the clinical. Let me describe the building the way I actually see it, which is devotionally, which is the way I see everything, which is my sickness, my magnificent and terminal sickness, that I cannot look at a brick facade without seeing the hand that laid it, and cannot see the hand without seeing the mind that moved the hand, and cannot see the mind without asking who made the mind, and cannot ask who made the mind without arriving at You, and I do not believe in You, I have told You this, I have told You this in language more beautiful than You deserve, and still You do not leave.

The building was a chapel once. You can see it in the bones. The roofline still peaks where the steeple was removed — a phantom limb of architecture — and the original stone arch over the entrance remains, though now it frames a door painted matte black with a brass handle shaped like a quarter note. Clever. Someone was very clever when they converted this place. They kept the lancet windows on the east wall, the ones that catch the morning, and behind the modern signage — *Bishop's*, it says, in neon script that buzzes like a thought you can't complete — behind that signage, in the transom, there remains a panel of original stained glass. Saints, I think. Or apostles. At this distance, through this condensation, through these eleven years, they are only colors: blue and gold and that particular red that glass-workers call *ruby flash*, which is made by layering clear glass with a skin of copper-stained red so thin that light passes through it as through blood held up to God.

I know too much about stained glass. I know too much about a great many things that serve me not at all. I know, for instance, that the booth third from the stage — the one with the cracked vinyl that the owner never reupholstered because he said the crack gave it *character*, by which he meant he was cheap, by which I mean I loved him for it — I know that booth could seat four but we only ever needed it to seat two, and we sat on the same side, which is what people do when they are new to each other's bodies and cannot bear even thirty-six inches of table between them, and which is what people stop doing, which is how you measure the death of a marriage, not in arguments but in the migration from same-side to opposite-side, continent to continent, booth to kitchen to separate rooms to —

The radio again. The dispatcher's voice, which is the voice of the actual world, which is a world in which a person is dead inside that building and I am the person whose job it is to stand over their death and ask it questions.

"Unit Seventeen, confirm you are en route."

"Confirmed. On scene. Entering now."

*Entering now.* As if my hand is on the door handle. As if my feet are on the pavement. As if entering were a thing I could do simply because I said I would.

But this is what the living do. We say we will and then we must because we've said it. We open doors because we have described ourselves as people who open doors. I have been, for eleven years, a person who does not open this particular door, and now a man is dead behind it and the city requires me to be a different person, and so I will be a different person. I have been many people. I have been the girl who believed, and the woman who studied what she believed until she could taste the machinery inside the miracle, and I have been the wife who — no. Not yet. I am not ready for that word yet. Let me be the detective. Let me be only the detective.

My hand on the door handle.

Such a small thing. Such an impossibly small thing, the mechanical act: pull, push, the night air on the skin of my forearm where the sleeve has ridden up, the smell of wet asphalt and the distant shriek of Westheimer traffic and, beneath it — you cannot actually smell a building from inside a car, I know this, I know the olfactory range, I know the science, but I am telling you I can smell it. Oak and brass polish and the ghost of ten thousand whiskey pours and beneath that the thing no renovation can extract from the walls of a place that was built for worship, which is the smell of *people trying*, the smell of breath expelled in song and supplication, the smell of desperate hope embedded in the mortar.

I am out of the car. My badge is in my hand. My feet are moving toward the door of *Bishop's Jazz and Blues*, which was once the Chapel of Saint somebody whose name I will remember in a moment because I remember everything, because memory is my affliction, because I am a woman who cannot forget a single thing and this is what has ruined me, not doubt, not the loss of faith, but the persistence of recollection, the way every room I have ever entered remains entered, remains lit, remains populated by the versions of myself I left there —

Chapel of Saint Cecilia. Patron saint of musicians. Of course. Of *course* it became a jazz bar. Even the profane has a sense of poetry in this city. Even the conversion of sacred to secular follows a logic that, if you tilt your head, looks like providence, looks like a plan, looks like the kind of thing You would do if You existed, which You don't, which is why I'm talking to You, because I only talk to things that can't answer, it's the only safe way to pray.

The door opens.

I go in.

## II.

The first thing the living room of a murder gives you is its smell, and the smell of this murder is copper and lemon, which is blood and the floor cleaner the owner uses on the hardwood, and the combination is so specifically *this place* that for a moment I am not forty-three and ruined, I am thirty-one and new, I am walking through this door for the first time with her hand in my hand and the saxophone doing something unreasonable in the key of D-flat and the air smelling of exactly this — copper from the kitchen, lemon from the polish — and she leaned into me and said, *It smells like a church that's having a better time than we ever did*, and I laughed, I laughed in a way I want back, I want that laugh back the way the bereaved want a voice back, I would trade — what would I trade? What is the appropriate currency for the return of a single involuntary laugh? I would trade You. I would trade the whole theological architecture of You — the cathedrals, the arguments, the wars, the paintings, the music, all of Bach, I would trade all of Bach — for the specific sound of myself laughing in that doorway with her hip against mine and the saxophone player bending a note the way light bends in water, the way mercy bends toward the ones who've stopped asking for it.

"Detective Watts."

Officer Benitez. Twenty-six years old, competent, deferential in the way of young officers who've been told I am smart and difficult, which is the same thing, which is always the same thing when the smart person is a woman, which is a separate psalm for a separate evening.

"Walk me through it."

I am the detective now. Note the shift. Note how the language flattens when I am required to be in the world rather than above it, how the cadence drops from iambic to staccato, how I become a person of verbs and nouns with no remainder. This is the other me. This is the one who solves things. She is less interesting. She is more useful. I do not like her, but she pays the rent.

"Victim is Graham Keller, forty-seven, host of *The Godless Hour* podcast— "

"I know who he is."

I do. Everyone does. Graham Keller made a career of the cheerful demolition of belief. Charming, relentless, precise. He could take apart a theologian's argument the way a child takes apart a clock — with evident delight and no intention of reassembly. I listened to him once, on a night I don't want to discuss, a night of bourbon and bandwidth and the particular loneliness of a woman who has exiled herself from everyone who loved her, and I listened to him dismantle the cosmological argument in eleven minutes and I felt nothing. I felt the way one feels watching a man kick down a door to an empty house. You're right, Graham, I thought. Nobody lives here anymore. Congratulations. Now what?

Now this, apparently. Now Graham Keller is lying — no. Not lying. His body has been removed. The outline remains. Tape on the hardwood near the stage, the shape of a man who fell without catching himself, which means he was unconscious before he hit, which means — but Benitez is talking, and I should listen to Benitez instead of composing eulogies for a man I didn't know.

"No signs of forced entry. Staff locked up at one-thirty a.m. Owner says Keller was a regular, sometimes stayed late by arrangement. Owner has a key, Keller had a key, bartender has a key."

"Who found him?"

"Bartender. Came in at eleven this morning to set up. Body was cold."

Cold. That word, in this room. I am standing six feet from the booth — *our* booth, the third from the stage — and the vinyl is still cracked in the same place, the same fissure I used to run my finger along while she talked, while she told me about her day, while she described the mundane gorgeous ordinary things that constituted her life as a person who worked with her hands, who built things, who could look at a broken cabinet and see its future wholeness, which is a talent I have never had, I have only ever been able to look at whole things and see where they will break.

The booth is roped off. It's inside the crime scene perimeter. There is no reason for it to be — the body was found near the stage, fifteen feet away — but Benitez has been generous with the tape, the way young officers are, casting a wide net because they haven't yet learned that evidence has a radius and that radius can be calculated and that anything beyond it is just a room.

But the booth is inside the radius. And I am looking at it.

"Detective?"

"The cause of death."

"Preliminary is blunt force trauma, back of the skull. Consistent with a fall — the stage is raised about eight inches — except the M.E. flagged bruising on the wrists. Pre-mortem, maybe restraint. She wants a second look."

"Bruising on the wrists and a blow to the head and no forced entry." I am writing in my notebook. I am the detective. "So either he let his killer in, or his killer had a key, or his killer was already here when the staff locked up."

"That's the bartender's theory. He thinks someone hid in the back."

"The bartender has theories. Lovely."

There — that is the other register, the dry and bitten one, the one that makes junior officers uncertain whether they are being mocked. They are always being mocked. I mock everyone. It is the immune response of a mind that cannot stop genuflecting before every human encounter and so must, as corrective, pretend it finds human encounters beneath it. I am aware of this. Self-awareness does not cure a thing; it only gives the thing a Latin name.

I walk the room. I am taking it in now, professionally, which means I am seeing what is here rather than what was here, which means I am failing, which means the room keeps *sliding* — present tense to past tense, crime scene to cathedral of ordinary love — the way a broadcast slides between stations when you're driving through hill country and the signal can't decide which version of the song to give you.

The stage. Blood on the stage's edge — the raised lip that caught his skull, if the fall theory holds. I photograph it. The stage itself is bare; no equipment, no amplifier. In 2011 there was a piano here, an upright, and the pianist played Monk on Thursdays and she loved Monk, she said Monk played piano the way she built furniture — by locating the weight-bearing joint and then removing everything that wasn't it. I didn't understand what she meant. I understand now. I understand now because I have spent eleven years removing everything that wasn't the weight-bearing joint of my own mind and I have arrived at the psalm-voice, which is the last joist, the one I cannot remove without the whole structure coming down, and this is a thing she would understand if I told her, which I will not, because I do not tell her things, because there is no mechanism by which I tell her things, because we do not speak, because I made certain we would not speak, because I —

"Is that Laurie Watts?"

The voice is from the bar. A man behind it — mid-sixties, gray in the beard, hands that have dried ten thousand glasses. The owner. I know his face the way you know a face from a photograph you've looked at too many times: flattened by familiarity, overexposed. Marcus Bell. He opened *Bishop's* in 2008. He kept the stained glass because his mother would have wanted him to, he told us once, and his mother was a woman who believed you could serve God and good bourbon in the same room and that God preferred the room with the bourbon because at least people in that room were being honest about what they needed.

"Mr. Bell."

"It's been a long time."

I do not answer this. There is no answer to this that does not open a door I have just spent the entire car ride psychologically barricading. *It's been a long time* is not a statement; it is a key, and I will not let it turn.

"I'm going to need a list of everyone who had access to the building after closing. Keys, codes, anything."

"Laurie — "

"Detective Watts. The list, Mr. Bell."

He looks at me the way people look at me, which is with the particular sorrow reserved for those who were once warm and have chosen cold, who were once part of something and have made themselves solitary, who were once — I can see it in his face, the memory of me as half of something, the ghost of the other half standing where I stand, and I cannot, I *cannot* —

"I'll have it within the hour."

"Thank you."

I turn back to the room. The forensic team is finishing. I have what I need, or what the detective-me needs, which is the preliminary sketch: three key-holders, no forced entry, a dead man whose profession was the public demolition of faith found dead in a desanctified chapel, which is a symbolism so heavy-handed that either the killer lacked subtlety or the killer is counting on me to dismiss the obvious reading, which means the obvious reading might be the correct one after all, which means —

I stop.
