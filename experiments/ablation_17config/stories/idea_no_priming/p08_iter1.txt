## Part One

**[TRANSCRIPT — "The Harker's Flat Files," Episode 1. Hosted by Renata Moss-Ibarra. Produced by Saguaro Public Media, Tucson, AZ. Original air date: November 14, 2025. The following transcript has been lightly edited for clarity.]**

RENATA MOSS-IBARRA: What you're about to hear is the audio layer of an iPhone video recorded on September 3rd, 2024, at approximately 5:47 a.m. Mountain Standard Time — or, depending on how you choose to understand what happened, at approximately 5:47 a.m. on a morning in early autumn, 1882. The video quality is low. The lighting is almost nonexistent. The speaker is Dr. Marguerite Okafor-Walsh, fifty-one years old, tenured professor of film studies at the University of Arizona, a woman who had published two well-regarded monographs, who had served on the Sundance jury, who had been described by the *Arizona Daily Star* as "the most interesting dinner companion in Pima County."

She is sitting on a dirt floor.

She is wearing a University of Arizona Wildcats sleep shirt and one sock.

This is the first of 142 recovered files.

**[FILE 001 — iPhone video, audio only. Background: insect noise, distant animal sound (likely rooster), an unidentified rhythmic creaking.]**

MARGUERITE OKAFOR-WALSH: Okay. Okay, okay, okay. We're recording. We are — the light is — hold on.

**[Rustling. A long exhale.]**

MARGUERITE OKAFOR-WALSH: It is — I don't know what time it is. My phone says 5:47 but my phone also says "No Service" and also I am fairly certain that I am in the back room of a dry goods store in the late nineteenth century, so let's acknowledge that the phone's credibility is somewhat compromised.

**[Pause.]**

MARGUERITE OKAFOR-WALSH: I'm going to describe the space because that's what I know how to do. That is literally what they pay me to do. The room is — I'd estimate ten by twelve. Plank walls, unfinished. The gaps between the planks are wide enough that I can see light through them, and the light is — it's wrong. I need to be precise about this. It is *wrong*. It's too orange. Too thick. Like someone color-graded the dawn with a tobacco filter, and I know how that sounds, but I'm telling you the quality of the light is the thing that's convincing me this is real more than anything else, because you can't fake light like this. A DP would kill for this light. This is pre-electrical light. There is no ambient hum. There is no — do you understand what I'm saying? The silence has a *weight*. There has never in your entire life been a moment when you were not hearing electricity and you don't know that until it's gone.

**[Pause. Something moans outside — livestock.]**

MARGUERITE OKAFOR-WALSH: The smell. I need to — the smell. It is livestock. It is lye. It is something dead. Something that has been dead for a while and no one has dealt with it because dealing with dead things is someone's specific problem and that someone isn't here, so the dead thing is just *being dead*, performing its deadness for anyone with a nose. And there are flies. I want to stress the flies. I'm a woman who grew up spending summers in Lagos, and I am telling you these flies have a commitment to their work that I find almost admirable.

**[A sound that might be a laugh, or might be the beginning of hyperventilation, carefully controlled.]**

MARGUERITE OKAFOR-WALSH: Facts as I understand them: I purchased the house at 1140 Perdido Road eleven weeks ago. The realtor said it was "the oldest residential structure in continuous use in this part of the county." She said this the way realtors say things — as though age were a selling point rather than a series of plumbing disasters. The house was built in 1882 as part of a homestead claim. I fell asleep in the back bedroom last night. I woke up here. The walls are the same walls. The dimensions are approximately the same. But the drywall is gone, the insulation is gone, the electrical wiring is gone, the century and a half of human habitation between 1882 and right now is — gone.

I need to look outside.

I don't want to look outside.

I'm going to look outside.

**[Footsteps on packed earth. A door — or a curtain; the sound is ambiguous — being pushed aside. Then a sudden flood of ambient sound: wind, the creak of wood, a horse blowing, distant voices.]**

MARGUERITE OKAFOR-WALSH: Oh. Oh, *God*.

**[Twelve seconds of silence on the recording. When Marguerite speaks again, her voice has changed — it is lower, flatter, deliberate.]**

MARGUERITE OKAFOR-WALSH: Wide shot. Unpaved street. Perhaps forty yards across from where I'm standing to the facing building, which appears to be — a stable. Mexican Colonial construction on the left, Anglo territorial on the right. Wooden sidewalk on the north side only. Seven — eight — eight buildings visible. Two horses tied to a rail. A dog that is either dead or so committed to sleeping that it is, functionally, philosophically dead. The sky is — I'm going to film the sky.

**[The audio shifts quality slightly as the phone is presumably tilted upward.]**

MARGUERITE OKAFOR-WALSH: No contrails. There are no contrails. The sky is — do you know what an empty sky looks like? You don't. None of you do. It is — it's obscene. It's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen and it's obscene. It looks *unfinished*. Like the rest of the sky hasn't loaded yet.

**[A male voice, off-camera, speaking loudly in what the University of Arizona linguistics department would later identify as English inflected with Scots-Irish Appalachian features common to the 1870s–1890s Southwest.]**

UNIDENTIFIED MALE VOICE: Lord Christ Almighty —

MARGUERITE OKAFOR-WALSH: There's a man on a horse. He's looking at me. He's looking at my legs. I am — I want to remind whoever is watching this that I am wearing a sleep shirt that hits mid-thigh and one sock — one — and this man is looking at me as though I am a three-headed serpent emerging from the sea in the book of Revelation and he is —

**[The sound of someone making the sign of the cross is, of course, inaudible. But Marguerite narrates it.]**

MARGUERITE OKAFOR-WALSH: He just crossed himself. He's — I'm going back inside. I'm going back inside right now.

**[Quick footsteps. The curtain or door sound again. Heavy breathing.]**

MARGUERITE OKAFOR-WALSH: Okay. Observation: the man was terrified. Not aggressive — *terrified*. His hand went to his chest before it went to his sidearm and that tells me something about the hierarchy of response here. He reached for God before he reached for his gun. I'm filing that.

I'm going to try to go back to sleep.

I think — I don't know how I got here but I got here by sleeping and I need to test whether I can get back the same way.

I'm going to try to go back to sleep in a room that smells like dead animal and lye in what I sincerely believe is territorial Arizona.

If this doesn't work, I —

**[The recording ends.]**

RENATA MOSS-IBARRA: It worked. Dr. Okafor-Walsh woke in her bed at 1140 Perdido Road at 7:12 a.m. on September 3rd, 2024. She was still wearing one sock. Her feet were dirty. The kind of dirty that takes more than one night to accumulate — red desert clay ground into the creases of her soles, the kind that doesn't come from a backyard. She took a photograph of her feet. That photograph is File 002.

Over the following ninety-one days, she would cross forty-three more times.

What you're listening to is not a ghost story. It is not science fiction, though Dr. Okafor-Walsh herself initially described it that way. It is, as near as we can determine, a thing that happened — a thing attested to by 142 digital files, by three authenticated artifacts currently held at the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History, by the confused medical records of two Tucson emergency rooms, by forty-six therapy session recordings made with Dr. Okafor-Walsh's knowledge and consent, and by the abrupt, total, and still-unexplained cessation of all crossings on December 3rd, 2024.

We'll get to the cessation.

First, we need to talk about what she did with it.

## Part Two

**[TRANSCRIPT — "The Harker's Flat Files," Episode 2.]**

RENATA MOSS-IBARRA: Between September 3rd and September 29th, 2024, Dr. Okafor-Walsh crossed eleven times. Her journal entries from this period — which she kept in a leather-bound Leuchtturm notebook, the kind favored by academics who want to seem like they keep leather-bound journals — are euphoric. I want to share some of them with you.

**[Journal entry, September 5th. Read by voice actor, per Dr. Okafor-Walsh's request that her unrecorded words be rendered by someone other than herself. The voice is warm, unhurried, precise — instructed to convey delight.]**

*Test #2: Fell asleep holding my car keys. Woke up in Harker's Flat holding my car keys. The keys work. By which I mean they traveled. They obviously did not work in the automotive sense, as there are no automobiles, but the transfer is confirmed: objects in physical contact with my body at the moment of sleep transition will cross with me. The implications are staggering. The implications are also hilarious. I held up my Honda key fob in the back room of what I'm now certain is Arnulfo Soto's dry goods store (I found the land records) and pressed "unlock" and I want you to know that nothing happened, nothing at all, and it was the funniest moment of my entire life.*

**[Journal entry, September 8th.]**

*Test #4: Brought a tin cup back. Just — held it while I fell asleep, and woke up in my bed with a tin cup in my hands. It's real. It's here. It's sitting on my kitchen counter next to the Nespresso machine, which is a sentence that should not exist and yet.*

RENATA MOSS-IBARRA: The tin cup — now cataloged as Smithsonian artifact 2025.0041.001 — was examined by Dr. Helen Park-Whitfield, a metallurgist specializing in nineteenth-century American material culture. Her report, which we are publishing in full on our website, concludes that the cup is consistent with tin-smithing techniques used in the Arizona Territory between 1875 and 1890, and that it shows "patterns of use-wear and oxidation inconsistent with any known method of artificial aging." In plain language: the cup is too old to be a fake and too real to be explained.

Dr. Okafor-Walsh's research during this period was extensive. We obtained, with her permission, her browser history, her library checkout records, and her interlibrary loan requests. In the three weeks following her first crossing, she checked out or downloaded:

— *Arizona Territory: A Political History, 1863–1912*
— *Water and Power in the Gila Basin: Cattlemen, Claims, and Conflict*
— Two microfilmed runs of the *Tucson Citizen*, 1880–1885
— A digitized Bureau of Land Management homestead map, 1879

And she watched — this is from her streaming history — she watched *A Fistful of Dollars*, *Yojimbo*, and *Miller's Crossing* in a single sitting on September 11th, pausing each film multiple times, sometimes for as long as twenty minutes.

**[Therapy session recording — September 12th, 2024. Session 3 with Dr. Adaora Nnaji, licensed clinical psychologist, Tucson, AZ. Published with consent of both parties.]**

DR. NNAJI: So tell me about the research.

MARGUERITE OKAFOR-WALSH: Okay, so — the town. Harker's Flat. It's real. It *was* real. It existed from roughly 1878 to maybe '93, '94 — it's hard to pin because these places didn't die all at once, they just sort of dried up. It was a water stop on a tertiary cattle route, and it got strangled — and I mean *strangled* — between two families. The Purdys and the Alverssons. Each of them controlled water. Each of them controlled grazing land. And the town was in the middle, right, and the single legal road to Tucson ran through land that was claimed by *both* families depending on which surveyor's map you believed, so basically every head of cattle that moved, every barrel of goods, every human body was paying tribute to one family or the other, and sometimes both, and the town was just —"

DR. NNAJI: A hostage.

MARGUERITE OKAFOR-WALSH: A *set piece*. Adaora, it's — okay, do you know *A Fistful of Dollars*?

DR. NNAJI: The Clint Eastwood —

MARGUERITE OKAFOR-WALSH: Yes. Based on Kurosawa's *Yojimbo*. Which was based on Dashiell Hammett's *Red Harvest*. The premise is always the same: a stranger walks into a town controlled by two rival factions, and the stranger plays them against each other until they destroy themselves. It's the ur-plot. It's the most elegant gambit in narrative history. And I'm looking at Harker's Flat and I'm looking at the Purdys and the Alverssons and I'm seeing the exact same structure. Two houses, one town, and all you'd need is someone in the middle who was smart enough and mobile enough and *disposable* enough to carry information between them. To lie to both sides. To set the dominoes up and —

DR. NNAJI: And you want to be the stranger.

MARGUERITE OKAFOR-WALSH: I want to be *Clint*. With better cheekbones.

**[Dr. Nnaji's silence lasts four seconds on the recording.]**

DR. NNAJI: Marguerite, I want to ask you something and I want you to sit with it rather than performing an answer, okay?

MARGUERITE OKAFOR-WALSH: That's ominous. Go.

DR. NNAJI: How do you feel about being a Black woman walking into 1882 Arizona?

**[Three seconds.]**

MARGUERITE OKAFOR-WALSH: I feel like I'm the most qualified person in any century to handle it.

DR. NNAJI: That's a performance.

MARGUERITE OKAFOR-WALSH: It's a *true* performance. Look — I know what you're asking. You're asking about danger. You're asking whether I've thought about the fact that I'll be walking into a period and a geography where people who look like me were — yes. I've thought about it. I've thought about it a lot. But here's the thing, and this is going to sound crazy but every part of this is crazy so we're grading on a curve: I have a *superpower*. I can leave. At any point, I can lie down and go to sleep and wake up in 2024 in my own bed next to my Nespresso machine. No one else in 1882 can do that. No one in 1882 can do *anything* resembling that. I'm not a slave and I'm not a freedwoman and I'm not a laundress and I'm not a missionary — I'm an *anomaly*, and anomalies have power. The man on the horse didn't reach for his gun. He crossed himself. Do you understand what that means?

DR. NNAJI: It means he saw something outside his categories.
