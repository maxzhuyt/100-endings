# The Fifth Sibling

---

## I.

I should tell you now that I don't know which version of this you're getting.

That's not coyness and it's not a warning. It's the condition of the telling. I am trying to be precise, and precision, in this house, is a moving target — literally moving, literally a target, or maybe I mean literally precise, in the sense that precision is all that is left when the thing you are trying to describe has been cut and recut so many times that what remains is only the quality of the cuts.

I came to the house with Jonah, who is the youngest. Jonah and I have been together three years, which is long enough to learn the rhythms of someone's avoidance but not long enough to know what's being avoided. He told me his family was difficult. He told me his mother had died. He told me there was a property to settle and siblings to manage and that he would appreciate it if I drove because highway driving made his hands shake since the shielding went down, and he said it like that — *since the shielding went down* — the way someone might say *since the divorce* or *since the flood.* An era marker. A before and after. I did not ask. He was not ready, and I was still under the impression that I was an ordinary person accompanying my partner to a difficult family weekend. I record this impression now as evidence that I was, at some point, naive in a way I can no longer reconstruct from the inside.

The house was three hours from the highway junction, down a road that had been maintained and then unmaintained and then maintained again by someone who understood the principle of maintenance but not its practice. We passed through two dead towns — their names on signs but nothing behind the names — and then through a corridor of white pines so tall and uniform that Jonah stopped talking and placed his hand against the window glass as if to verify that the trees were outside it. The house appeared the way failing buildings appear: first as a color that shouldn't be there, a wrong ochre in the green, and then as a shape, and then as a problem.

Maren's car was already in the drive.

"She said she wasn't coming," Jonah said.

"Whose car is that?"

"Maren's. She said she wasn't — that's Maren's car."

I want to record his face, but I can't get the order right. I think he went pale and then angry and then pale again. I think his hand was still on the glass. I think the engine was running, or that I had turned it off and the silence was what I'm remembering as the engine, the way silence sometimes has a motor in it, an insistence. What I am sure of is that the name *Maren* had come up only twice in three years, both times late at night, both times as a kind of redaction: he would say the name and then the sentence would go somewhere else, as if the name were a exit ramp he'd taken by mistake.

I parked. We went inside.

The others were already there — Dev and Lena and Sol — and they were standing in the front hall in a configuration that suggested they had been arguing and had heard the car and had rearranged themselves into non-argument postures that fooled no one. Dev had a folder. Lena had her coat still on. Sol was leaning against the banister with that particular weightedness of a body trying to appear relaxed while load-bearing some invisible thing.

"You said she wasn't coming," Jonah said to Dev.

"She wasn't." Dev opened his hands. "And then she was."

"Where is she?"

"Upstairs. With Opal."

Jonah closed his eyes. I want to tell you that this was when I first noticed something wrong with the house — wrong in the way that this story requires, wrong temporally, wrong at the seam — but that isn't true. The first thing I noticed was the wallpaper in the front hall, which was a faded pattern of hydrangeas that Lena later described as having always been roses and that Sol later described as having been stripped to bare plaster when he was nine. I noticed the wallpaper and thought: *someone's wrong.* Not "someone is *remembering* wrong." Just: *someone's wrong.* The distinction collapsed later. At the time it was just a small, cold tooth of unease, the feeling of walking into a room where the furniture has been moved two inches to the left and your body knows before your eyes do.

They did not introduce themselves to me. Jonah had apparently told them about me, or hadn't. I stood next to him and held my own bag and watched the five of them — four in the hallway, one upstairs, somewhere, with a girl named Opal — and what I saw was a family in which the load-bearing walls had been removed and everyone was still standing only because no one had yet tested their weight against the floor.

Dev said: "We should start. The lawyer wants the documents by Tuesday."

Lena said: "We should wait for Maren to come down."

Sol said: "The last time we waited for Maren, we waited nine years."

Jonah said nothing. His hand found mine and held it in a way that was not romantic.

I want to tell you that I understood, then, what I was walking into. But I think the truth is that the house was already doing something to the record — or the girl was, or the family's collective weight on the past was simply more than the past could bear — and what I understood was only what was left after some pre-understanding had been removed. I felt the shape of a hole without knowing that a hole was what I was feeling. I catalogued contradictions — the wallpaper, the weather (Sol remembered the drive up as rain, Lena remembered sun, and the road outside was dry but the porch was wet), the matter of whether their mother had died in April or in March — and I filed them under the header *family mythology* and moved on.

I was wrong to file them there. I was possibly *directed* to file them there. I cannot now determine which, and I tell you this not to make myself interesting but to establish, at the outset, the terms: I am the only narrator you have, and I may be the story's least reliable architecture, and the ground has not stopped moving since I walked through that door.

## II.

The kitchen was the only room in the house that appeared structurally sound, which meant it was the room where structural damage was easiest to ignore. Someone — Lena, I learned later, or Sol, or neither — had cleaned it recently enough that the counters gleamed but not recently enough that the gleaming felt current. There were five chairs around a table built for four. Dev brought a sixth from the hallway — a wooden chair with a woven seat that creaked when I sat in it — and then stood, which made the sixth chair feel like an accusation, directed at me or at the geometry.

They spread papers on the table: the mother's will, guardianship filings, a custody evaluation from a social worker whose name had been redacted with a marker so thick it bled through the page. The property deed. An insurance claim, denied, for something described in bureaucratic language as "residential temporal event damage." A school enrollment form for a girl named Opal, age fifteen, with the field for "parent/guardian" filled in and crossed out and filled in again three times, each time in a different sibling's name.

"The simplest thing," Dev said, "is to agree on a guardian before Tuesday and file jointly. The court doesn't want a contest. The social worker's recommendation is that Opal remain in the home with a designated —"

"This home?" Lena said. "This home is not habitable."

"This home has been her home for fifteen years."

"This home has a room upstairs where the floor doesn't exist on Wednesdays."

A silence. The kind that has furniture in it.

"That's been stabilized," Sol said.

"Stabilized is not *fixed,* Sol."

"The lateral shielding holds it. It's been holding it since Mom —"

"The lateral shielding is what *Mom* did, and Mom is dead, and the lateral shielding is degrading, and you would know that if you had come home more than once in the last three years, which you —"

"I sent money."

"You sent a *check.* You sent a check and a note that said 'for repairs,' and the plumber came and couldn't find the pipes because the pipes were —"

"The pipes are fine on most days," Dev said quietly.

"*Most days.*"

I sat with my hands in my lap. Jonah was beside me, very still, in the way of a person who has learned that stillness is a form of non-participation and that non-participation is the only survivable posture in this room. I watched him watch his siblings and I thought: *he is doing something with his face that I have never seen him do.* A kind of controlled vacancy. An old skill, brought out of storage.

They argued about the check. They argued about the plumber. They argued about who had spoken to the social worker and what had been said and whether anyone had mentioned — here a pause that was loud — "the situation with the records." The records. Their mother's work, I gathered, which had been some kind of research or some kind of practice or some kind of both, conducted in this house, on or with or through her children, involving the orbital pod that had crashed in the back field seventeen years ago and the infant who had survived it and the temporal phenomena that followed. I gathered this the way you gather a landscape from a moving train: in pieces, at speed, with gaps where the terrain dips below the window frame.

Nobody described it directly. Dev referred to "Mom's protocols." Lena referred to "the sessions." Sol referred to "work." Jonah referred to nothing; Jonah had stopped speaking. The orbital pod — which I had assumed was a metaphor until I saw, through the kitchen window, the actual structure in the back field, a scarred half-shell of something that was not any metal I had seen, seated in a depression of earth that had the quality of a healed wound — the pod was mentioned once, by Lena, who called it "the cradle," and then not mentioned again.

What they argued about, underneath the paperwork, was this: who had stayed and who had left and whether staying was sacrifice or complicity and whether leaving was betrayal or survival. Lena had stayed. Dev had stayed until he hadn't. Sol had left at eighteen and sent checks. Jonah had left at seventeen and sent nothing. Maren had not left. Maren had been — the word they used was "separated," which is a word that does work for you, that absorbs the question of agency so you don't have to assign it.

"Maren was fifteen when the shielding went down," Lena said, and I heard in her voice the particular fatigue of a sentence she had said so many times that it had become a plank, a bridge she kept re-laying across a gap that kept widening. "She was fifteen and she was in the pod room and the shielding collapsed and we —"

"We've all heard this," Sol said.

"Jonah's partner hasn't heard this."

Five faces turned to me. I felt the way a page might feel when it is held up to a light: examined not for what was written on it but for what might show through from behind.

"They don't need to hear this," Jonah said. It was the first time he'd spoken in twenty minutes, and his voice had that quality — thin, pressurized — of a person who is managing something internally that requires his full atmospheric resources.

"If they're here, they hear it," Lena said. "That's the rule of the house."

"Mom's rule," Sol said.

"It's a good rule."

"Mom's rules were designed to keep us in the house, Lena. That doesn't make them good."

Dev raised both hands — a gesture of arbitration that seemed physical with practice, like a stretch he'd done ten thousand times. "We're not here to relitigate Mom. We're here to decide who takes responsibility for a fifteen-year-old girl who, by any legal measure, is not biologically related to any of us and who, by any other measure, is —"

He stopped. I don't know why he stopped. Something shifted in the room — not visibly, not audibly, but *texturally,* the way a change in air pressure can make a room feel suddenly smaller without the walls moving. I looked at the window. The pod in the back field was the same. The light was the same. But Sol's posture had changed, and Dev was holding the edge of the table, and Lena had closed her eyes, and Jonah's hand, under the table, had found my knee and was gripping it with a force that was not about me.

"She's listening," Lena said.

"She's always listening," Sol said.

"That's not — she's *fifteen,* Sol. She's not always anything. She's a child."

"She's not a child. You know she's not a child. Not in the way that —"

"She is a child in every way that matters for a custody filing, and I would appreciate it if we could — just once, just for one afternoon — talk about her as if she were a person and not a —"

"Not a what?" said a voice from the doorway.

They turned. I turned. In the doorway stood a woman who was tall and angular and who held herself the way a person holds themselves when they have spent a long time in a space that was too small: shoulders drawn in, chin up, the body a series of compensations. I knew immediately that this was Maren, though Jonah had never described her and no one had offered a photograph. I knew because the room changed when she entered it — not through any temporal phenomenon, not through any psychic event, but through the ordinary, devastating physics of estrangement. She was the one who had been gone, and the room was the shape of her absence, and now the absence was filled and the room did not know what shape to be.

Behind her, half-hidden, hand on the doorframe, was a girl.

The girl was fifteen. She had dark hair cut bluntly at her jaw and the kind of stillness that is not calm but rather the visible surface of an immense, ongoing calculation. She looked at each of us in turn. When her eyes reached me, they paused — not long, not dramatically, but with a quality of *recognition* that I felt in my sternum, a small, precise dislocation, as if she had found me in a place she expected me to be.

"Opal," Lena said. "We're just talking."

"I know," the girl said. And then, to me, as if we were alone: "You can see it, can't you."

I did not answer. I did not understand the question. Later — that night, or the next day, or possibly before we arrived; I am no longer certain of the sequence — I would understand that she was asking whether I could see the seams. Whether I could see, as she could, as apparently no one else would admit they could, the places where one version of this family's history ended and another began, the cuts and sutures in the shared past, the fact that the architecture of memory in this house was not settled but *active,* a live construction, a building being demolished and rebuilt simultaneously by a fifteen-year-old girl who had never been asked whether she wanted the job.

But in that moment, I only looked at her, and she only looked at me, and the kitchen was very quiet, and outside, in the back field, the orbital pod sat in its healed wound of earth and said nothing about where it had come from or what it had brought.

## III.

Jonah told me later that night, in the room we were given — a second-floor bedroom with a window that looked out on the pines and a closet door that was locked from the inside, which I tried not to think about, which I have not since stopped thinking about — that the shielding failure happened on a Tuesday.

"Or a Wednesday," he said. "Dev says Wednesday. Lena says Tuesday."

"Which was it?"

He looked at me with something I hadn't seen in him before: a patience that was not patience but rather the scar tissue of a question that had been asked too many times to hurt anymore.

"It was both," he said. "Or neither. Or it was Tuesday when it happened and Wednesday by the time it stopped happening and the day in between doesn't — it's not —" He pressed his palms together, fingertip to fingertip, a gesture I associated with his thinking and now associate with his containing.
