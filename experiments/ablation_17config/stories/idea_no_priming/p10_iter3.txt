# The Garden of Forking Deaths

---

## I.

Here is a town.

You have seen it before. You have always seen it before. It sits in the basin of a valley that looks as though God scooped it from the earth with a spoon — or, more precisely, as though a set designer scooped it from a catalogue of valleys and said, *this one, it photographs well from the south.* The main street is wide enough for two men on horseback to pass each other without touching, which is exactly wide enough for two men on horseback to shoot each other without missing, which is, of course, the salient architectural consideration. There is a saloon. There is always a saloon. Its doors swing on hinges that have never once failed to creak at the dramatically appropriate moment. A piano inside plays itself — not supernaturally, not yet, but in the sense that no one has ever seen the pianist's face, and no one has ever thought to look.

Above the town, a hawk sits on a fencepost. It is a red-tailed hawk. It is always a red-tailed hawk. The production could not afford a golden eagle.

The dust is amber in the late light, and the light is late because the light in these towns is always late — perpetual golden hour, that amber glow beloved of cinematographers and eulogists, the kind of light that makes everything look like a memory even while it's happening. The shadows are long. A dog sleeps under a porch. A water trough catches the sky in its surface, and the sky is the blue of a paint swatch labeled *Cerulean Destiny* or *Frontier Promise* or some other two-word compound designed to make you feel something you cannot name and do not, upon reflection, actually feel.

It is, in every particular, a Western.

Not *a* Western — not one you could identify by title and director and the year its lead was nominated for something — but *the* Western, the aggregate, the mean, the great silty deposit left behind when a century of cattle drives and cavalry charges and showdowns-at-high-noon have flowed through the cultural floodplain and receded. Every board in every building is a quotation. Every grain of dust has been cinematographed before. The hawk on the fencepost is a citation. The dog under the porch is an allusion. Even the wind — which comes now, right on cue, stirring the dust into a picturesque curtain through which a figure on horseback will shortly emerge — even the wind is borrowed.

This is not a complaint. This is a diagnosis.

And here, through the curtain of dust, on a horse the color of whiskey held up to lamplight — because of course it is, because nothing in this world has ever committed the aesthetic crime of being muddy brown when it could be *the color of whiskey held up to lamplight* — comes Delilah Crowe.

She is tall in the saddle. She is always tall in the saddle. She has cheekbones that could cut glass and eyes the narrator has been asked to describe as "gunmetal grey," which is the only descriptor available for the eyes of women in Westerns who are not love interests, and Delilah Crowe has never been anyone's love interest, and she has never wanted to be, and this, too, is a genre convention so familiar that it has worn a groove in the collective imagination the way a river wears a groove in limestone — slowly, then suddenly, then it's a canyon and everyone acts as though the canyon was always there.

She carries two revolvers. She has a name that sounds like it was chosen by a novelist, because it was. She does not yet know this.

She squints at the town. She has seen it before. She doesn't know that, either.

Not yet.

---

## II.

The man in front of Delilah Crowe was named Hatchett — or Hackett, or Hatch, the vowels were negotiable, the consonants were hard, and the important thing was that he stood in the center of that wide, photographically convenient street with his hand over his holster and his hat pulled low and his mouth producing words that Delilah was not listening to because she had heard them all before.

Not these specific words. Words *like* these. Threats shaped like promises, promises shaped like eulogies — *I'm going to put you in the ground, Crowe, I'm going to send you to your maker, I'm going to —*

The specific content does not matter. The rhythm matters. He was a man performing the ancient choreography of the pre-gunfight monologue, the verbal overture to violence, and every syllable was a stage direction: *stand here, look menacing, give the protagonist time to assess you.* Delilah assessed him. He was large. He was sunburned. He wore a duster that had never been washed and a scowl that had never been original.

"You done?" Delilah said.

He was not done. They are never done. But his hand moved toward his gun, and that was the signal, the conductor's baton dropping, and everything that followed was tempo —

She drew.

She was fast.

He was faster.

The bullet entered her chest two inches left of center, which was close enough to the heart for poetry and far enough from it for a lingering death, and Delilah Crowe staggered backward and felt the street come up to meet her back and thought: *Oh.* And then: *The sky.* And then nothing so articulate as a word, just the experience of the cerulean vault above her becoming very large and very close, as though it were descending to press itself against her face like a palm, like a cool hand on a fevered brow, and the taste of copper was in her mouth, and the grit of the street was against her cheek, and the sky looked like a painting she had once admired in a house she could not quite remember in a life she was not entirely certain she had lived, and the hawk — there was a hawk, there was always a hawk — traced its patient circle overhead, and she thought: *It looks like it was painted there,* and she thought: *Everything looks like it was painted,* and then she thought nothing at all, because the dead do not think, and Delilah Crowe was dead.

She was dead for eleven minutes.

This is precise. Not *about* eleven minutes, not *nearly* eleven, not the approximate, poetic, narrative-convenient "eleven" that means "some amount of time that sounds specific enough to feel authentic." Eleven minutes. Six hundred and sixty seconds. The duration of a side of a 78-rpm record. The length of a short sermon. The time it takes to soft-boil an egg if you are distracted and forgetful, which is to say, the time it takes to ruin a small domestic task through inattention.

Eleven minutes after the bullet opened her chest to the cerulean sky, Delilah Crowe was standing in the center of the street with her hand on her holster and Hatchett — Hackett — Hatch — was producing words she was not listening to because she had heard them all before.

She had heard *these* specific words.

She drew. She was fast. This time, she was faster. The bullet left her revolver and entered Hatchett-or-Hackett in the throat, and he fell backward into the dust, and the dust rose around him in a plume that looked — honestly, genuinely — like a small golden angel ascending, and if that image seems too poetic for the aftermath of a man choking on his own blood in a public thoroughfare, then perhaps you are beginning to understand the kind of world this is.

Delilah holstered her revolver. She looked up at the cerulean sky. She looked at the hawk. She placed one boot on the edge of the water trough and leaned forward, and she spoke — not to the corpse, not to the bystanders pressed against the saloon's front windows, not to anyone in particular — in the manner of a woman addressing the architecture of the universe itself:

"I have been touched," she said, "by something I cannot name but will not decline to describe. Let it be known — to the dust, to the circling hawk, to the indifferent heavens that see fit to shine upon the wicked and the just with equal and excessive golden light — that Delilah Crowe was given a second chance on this street, in this town, on this day that smells of gunpowder and horse and the particular kind of cedar that grows only where the soil has been stained by old violence. I was dead. I am certain I was dead. I tasted the copper and I felt the grit and I saw the sky press down upon me like a hymnal closing, and then I was here again, upright, with the gun unfired and the words re-spoken and the whole ugly opera cued back to its overture. This is grace. I will call it grace because I have no better word and because the alternatives — luck, coincidence, the mechanical indifference of a universe that shuffles and re-deals without regard for the player — are too miserable to live inside. I choose grace. I choose to believe that God is a gunsmith who makes corrections in His blueprints, and that I am the draft He preferred."

She was eloquent. She was charismatic. She was spectacularly wrong.

The narrator will not belabor the point. But there was something in the way Delilah said *grace* — the way she leaned into the word like a woman leaning into a doorframe, casual, proprietary, as though she owned the concept — that invited a certain sadness. The sadness of watching someone celebrate a diagnosis. The sadness of watching someone frame the warden's letter and hang it on the wall because it is the first piece of mail they have ever received.

She did not understand what had happened to her.

She mounted her whiskey-colored horse and rode out of town.

The town, behind her, waited. It would be needed again.

---

## III.

The next town was called Redemption, or possibly Absolution, or possibly Perdition — the frontier had a limited vocabulary of guilt arranged into a seemingly unlimited number of signposts — and Delilah rode into it at midmorning under a sky that —

Well.

Under a sky that was cerulean. Under the specific cerulean she had seen before: not the *family* of blue, not the *genus* but the *species*, the exact shade and saturation, the exact arrangement of cloud — a long cirrus wisp stretched east to west like a seam in fabric, like a visible stitch where the sky had been repaired and the repairman had not bothered to match the thread. She had seen this cloud before. She had seen it from the street where she died.

She looked away.

In the town square — if it could be called a square, if the intersection of two dusty roads could be granted the geometric dignity of a shape — a horse lay dead. It was grey. It was bloated in the heat. Its legs were stiff and angled toward the cerulean sky in the manner of a horse that had died either recently or in a painting — it was difficult to tell which, and the difficulty was, itself, informative. A vulture sat on the horse's haunch. The vulture was black. The vulture had a red, wrinkled head. The vulture was the same vulture that had been circling the first town, or a vulture so identical to it that the distinction between *the same vulture* and *an identical vulture* became, in this world of amber light and recycled consonants, a distinction without weight.

Delilah did not stop.

She rode on. She rode for hours, or for what felt like hours — distance on the frontier was measured not in miles but in tempo, in the rhythm of hoofbeats and the slow crawl of shadow and the patient deterioration of one's certainty about the direction of travel. She rode into another town. This one was called Contrition, or possibly Penance. The sign was sun-bleached, the letters ambiguous in the way that letters become ambiguous when the world cannot be troubled to render them at full resolution.

In the town square, a horse lay dead. Grey. Bloated. Legs stiff and angled. A vulture on its haunch. Above: the cerulean. The seam of cirrus. The same seam. Not *a similar* seam. *The same.*

It was the same dead horse.

It was —

She dismounted. She walked to the horse. She looked at its eye — open, clouded, reflecting the cerulean sky back at itself as though even in death the horse could not escape the scenery. She looked at the vulture. The vulture looked at her. It tilted its raw red head with the mechanical precision of an automaton, a movement that contained no animal intelligence, only direction: *look here, look at this, notice what you are seeing.*

"Different horse," Delilah said, aloud, to no one.

She mounted. She rode.

The third town — name illegible, sign fallen, post rotted — had a dead grey horse in its square, legs skyward, a vulture on its haunch. Above, the cerulean. The seam. The cloud. A tumbleweed — the same tumbleweed, the *same one*, she would have sworn it before any judge in any court in any world that had judges and courts and the institutional infrastructure of jurisprudence — rolled across the street from left to right with the weary determination of a stagehand pushing a prop into position for the fourth time in a single performance.

Her horse flinched.

This was the detail that mattered. Not the vulture, not the dead horse, not even the seam in the sky — but the flinch. The animal beneath her recognized the recursion before she did, or at least admitted to the recognition before she could bring herself to, and the flinch traveled up through the saddle and into her spine and she felt it as a question posed by something that could not speak: *Have we been here before?*

Children were playing in the street. They were the same children. The same faces, the same bare feet, the same sun-faded clothes, the same game — chasing each other in circles, always circles, the geometry of play reduced to its most repetitive form. One of them looked up at Delilah as she passed, and the child's expression was not curiosity and not fear but something older and less legible: the expression of a face that has been looked at before, by this same woman, from this same angle, and has grown tired of being seen.

Delilah did not deliver a soliloquy.

She wanted to. The words were forming — something about providence, something about the peculiar grace of a world that offers variety in the name of mercy — but the words disassembled in her mouth like a sentence translated too many times, and what came out instead was silence, and the silence was heavy, and we should be precise about what kind of heavy: not the dramatic silence of the Western, not the pregnant pause before the gunshot, but the silence of someone who has noticed a crack in the wall and is pretending the wall is intact because the alternative — that the wall is a painted canvas, that the crack reveals not brick but blankness behind — is a thought too large to furnish with language.
