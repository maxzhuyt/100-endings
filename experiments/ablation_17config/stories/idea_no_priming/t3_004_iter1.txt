# The Permanent Resident of Courtroom 11-B

## I.

There are one hundred and fourteen ceiling tiles in Courtroom 11-B of the Breslov Municipal Building, and seven of them have water stains, and one of the water stains looks exactly like a dog sitting down. Not a specific breed. Just a dog. The kind a person would draw if you said "draw a dog" and they had four seconds. I know this because I have spent, as of this past Saturday, eleven weekends in the third-row plastic chair closest to the heating vent that doesn't work, and when you spend that much time looking up you start to have opinions about ceiling moisture.

My name is Zoya. I am twelve. I am here because on the first and third weekends of every month I stay with my grandmother, and my grandmother is the subject of eviction proceedings in this courtroom, and no one has yet thought to arrange childcare for the duration, and my mother, who has custody of me on all the other weekends and every weekday, said on the phone to my father, who has custody of me on the first-and-third weekends except he doesn't actually because he transferred it to Baba when he moved to Bridgeport for a job he will not describe in specific terms, she said: "She's old enough to sit." So I sit.

I sit very well. I have a backpack. In the backpack: a sketchbook, a sixty-four-count box of crayons with the sharpener built into the back, two granola bars (one for the morning session, one for after), a library copy of *Julie of the Wolves* that I have renewed three times, and a mechanical pencil for when I don't want color. I put the backpack between my knees and I watch.

The bailiff's name is Tomasz and his left shoe squeaks. Not every step—only when he pivots, which he does at approximately eleven-minute intervals when he walks from the clerk's table to the door and back. I have timed this. Tomasz has a face like bread dough that someone pressed two thumbs into for eyes and then decided was finished. He is very kind. On my third Saturday he brought me a paper cup of water without my asking, and on my fifth Saturday he told me the vending machine in the hallway gives two bags of pretzels if you press B7 twice fast, and he was right, and I have exploited this every Saturday since, and I believe Tomasz knows this and has decided to let it continue, which is the most generous thing anyone in this building has ever done.

Judge Dreyer presides. She is bored. Not bored the way I am bored, which is a boredom full of ceiling tiles and shoe squeaks and the quality of light through the frosted windows at different times of the morning—a boredom that is actually a kind of very detailed paying attention. Judge Dreyer's boredom is the other kind: the kind that has given up on details entirely. She has a way of looking at the papers in front of her as if she has been asked to read them in a language she studied in college and has since forgotten. She sustains this look for entire hearings. Sometimes she writes something down. I have never been able to see what.

And then there is Feliks Mosk.

I want to introduce Feliks Mosk the way he deserves to be introduced, which is to say with the full ceremony owed to a man of his stature and accomplishment, a legal professional of long standing in the Breslov community, a tenacious advocate, a person whose name I mispronounced as "Felix Mosque" for the first three weekends and no one—not Tomasz, not the clerk, not Feliks himself—corrected me, so I went on calling him Felix Mosque in my sketchbook annotations until I saw his business card on Baba's kitchen table and realized there was a *k* where I didn't expect one and no *que* at all.

Feliks Mosk is five foot six, or five foot seven if you count the way his hair rises when it's humid, which in Courtroom 11-B it always is, because of whatever is going on with the ceiling tiles. His briefcase is brown leather so old it has gone the color of a walnut shell, and the left clasp has been broken since before I started coming, so he holds it shut with a thick rubber band that he removes and replaces with the concentration of a surgeon. His suits—he has two, navy and a brown that I would describe in my sixty-four-count vocabulary as "raw umber"—carry a sweetish smell my nose identifies as mildew and my brain identifies as a man whose closet is in a basement. When he speaks, which is often, which is at length, which is with a relative loudness that has twice caused Judge Dreyer to ask him to modulate, he gestures with his left hand in a way that makes it seem as though he is conducting a very small and disobedient orchestra. His right hand holds papers. His right hand always holds papers.

He is my grandmother's lawyer.

He is, to my knowledge, her only lawyer, her entire legal team, the full resources of the defense. Feliks Mosk and his rubber-banded briefcase and his mildew suits and his nervous sweat, which begins at the collar and moves downward. I have documented each hearing's sweat migration in my sketchbook with clinical annotations. October 14: collar by 10 a.m., visible at armpits by 10:40. October 28: collar before proceedings began, possibly residual from the walk to the courthouse, which is uphill from the bus stop on Vine. November 11: the day he introduced the competency motion, which I will describe later, the sweat was immediate and total, as if he had been standing in rain that only fell on him.

Across the courtroom—and I say "across" but it is really only twelve feet, because 11-B is small, the kind of courtroom that exists because a building needed one more room and didn't have one more room's worth of space—sits a woman I will tell you about later.

My grandmother was not in the courtroom the first Saturday I came. She was at her apartment, the one they want to take, doing whatever she does on mornings she doesn't have to go anywhere, which I imagine involves the first burner on the left (the only correct burner) and the blue mug with the chipped handle she holds with both hands, and the radiator clanking in its rhythm that I swear sounds like a word, like it's trying to say something in a language made of pipes. Her apartment is in the Breslov Towers on Delancey, fourth floor, unit 4-J, and the kitchen has wallpaper that isn't wallpaper, but I'll tell you about that later too.

That first Saturday, I sat in my plastic chair and watched Feliks Mosk conduct his invisible orchestra and listened to Tomasz's shoe and counted tiles and understood nothing about what was happening legally and everything about what was happening actually, which is that the adults in this room were doing something to my grandmother and not one of them—not the judge, not the clerk, not Tomasz with his water cups and pretzel intelligence—was going to explain it to me, and I was going to have to figure it out the way I figure out most things, which is by watching so hard that the watching becomes a kind of knowing.

The dog in the ceiling stain sat through all of it. That's what I liked about the dog. It just sat and watched. We had that in common.

## II.

Baba's apartment smells like black tea and radiator dust and something underneath both of those things that I think is just the smell of a place where the same person has been breathing for thirty-one years, which is how long she has lived in 4-J, which is longer than my mother has been alive, which is a fact I find both mathematically obvious and somehow staggering, like being told the ocean is deep—yes, of course, but *how* deep, and what is down there.

The door has three locks. Two of them work. The third is a deadbolt that has been painted over so many times it has become decorative, a small bronze monument to previous tenants' anxieties, and Baba leaves it alone and uses the other two with a key ring that also holds a key to a car she sold in 2011 and a small metal Saint Somebody whose name has been rubbed off his tag by decades of handling. She turns each lock with a specific wrist motion—not urgent, not casual, but practiced, the way a pianist's hands know where the keys are without looking. She has done this perhaps twenty thousand times. My math might be wrong. My math is often wrong, according to Mrs. Belnap, but this felt like a calculation worth attempting.

Inside: the hallway, narrow, with a coat rack that holds four coats in a sequence that does not change seasonally (the wool one is always third from the left, even in August), and below the coats, three pairs of shoes arranged with a precision that would satisfy a geometry textbook. The hallway leads to the living room, which has the green sofa that sags in the middle exactly the way a hammock does, and the bookshelf that is half books and half framed photographs, and I should describe the photographs but I want to get to the kitchen because the kitchen is the room, the kitchen is the thing, the kitchen is where Baba is most entirely Baba and where the evidence of what they are trying to take is most legibly displayed.

The kitchen is small. There is room for one person to cook and for another person to sit at the table and watch, and these are the only two activities the kitchen permits, and they are the only two activities the kitchen needs to permit. The table is square, barely, with an oilcloth cover printed with lemons that have faded to the color of old teeth. Two chairs. One is Baba's. You would know which one even without asking because of the cushion, hand-sewn, stuffed with something that has gone flat in the middle and higher at the edges so that it forms a kind of nest, a shallow bowl of sitting, and Baba lowers herself into it with the gravity of someone who is not sitting down but *arriving*.

The stove has four burners. Only the first on the left is correct. I have asked why. Baba has said, "The others lie." I did not pursue this. Some explanations are better than the questions that prompted them.

She fills the kettle from the tap, which takes a moment because the tap must be coaxed, persuaded, given a preliminary half-turn to the right and then back before it agrees to the full left-turn that produces water. Baba does this the way she does the locks: without thinking, without commentary, the way a body that has been in a place long enough becomes a mechanism calibrated to that place's specific malfunctions. She puts the kettle on the first-left burner and it begins its ticking, and then the radiator in the corner begins its clanking, and between the two of them the kitchen fills with a kind of music that I would describe, if Mrs. Belnap asked me to describe it in Language Arts terms, as *percussive and domestic*, which sounds like nothing, which sounds right.

And then there is the wall.

The wall behind the table—the wall you face if you are sitting in the non-Baba chair, which is my chair, which is where I sit with my sketchbook on Saturday mornings while the kettle does its ticking—is covered, floor to the place where the cabinet starts, in concert tickets and playbills and programs and one restaurant napkin on which someone wrote a setlist in blue ink that has gone purple.

They are glued. Or pasted. Or affixed by some method that varied over the decades and which I can trace archaeologically if I look closely, which I have: the oldest ones, near the baseboard, are held by what appears to be rubber cement gone amber and hard; the middle layers use a white paste that has yellowed at the edges; the most recent ones, near the top, use simple tape, doubled over, which tells me that at some point Baba decided permanence mattered less than the act of putting them up. Or that her hands changed and paste became difficult. Or both.

I can tell you what is on this wall. Not all of it. There are maybe two hundred pieces of paper, overlapping in places, and some are partially hidden by others the way geological strata hide what came before, and some are too faded to read, and some are in languages I don't know. But I can tell you what I've catalogued, because I have spent parts of six Saturdays sitting in my chair with my sketchbook documenting them, and here is what I know:

There is a ticket to a Sviatoslav Richter recital at Carnegie Hall, November 1, 1992, which is before I was born and before my mother moved to Breslov and before, I think, my parents met, and this ticket cost $35.00, which Baba told me was expensive at the time, and there is a thumbprint in the corner of it, ink or chocolate, which Baba did not explain.

There is a playbill from the Breslov Community Playhouse production of *The Cherry Orchard*, spring 2003, and Baba played Charlotta Ivanovna, and I know this because her name is in the cast list in a typeface so small I needed my face two inches from the wall to read it, and when I said "Baba, you were *in* it?" she said, "Charlotta does magic tricks and talks to herself. I was well cast."

There is a stub from the Breslov Civic Orchestra's annual holiday concert, December 2007, and December 2008, and December 2009, but not 2010, and I asked about 2010 and Baba said, "That year they played Bolero," and this was evidently sufficient explanation, and I accepted it.

There is a napkin from Café Lev, which closed in 2015, on which someone wrote in handwriting that is not Baba's:

*Shostakovich 5*
*Prokofiev — the Romeo*
*that Schnittke piece that made you cry*

I do not know whose handwriting this is. I did not ask. The napkin is near the center of the wall, at my eye level, and it is more worn than the things around it, the edges soft and fibrous, as though it has been touched many times, or as though the paste beneath it was applied and reapplied, which means it fell at least once and someone — Baba — put it back.

There is more. There is so much more. There is a faded program from something in what I think is Russian, and a movie ticket from the Breslov Rialto (*Amarcord*, 1996 re-release), and a leaflet for a jazz night at a bar called The Downbeat that has a drawing of a saxophone on it that I copied into my sketchbook because the lines were good, and there are two opera stubs from somewhere in the city, *La Traviata* and *Eugene Onegin*, and the dates are eleven years apart and I don't know if she went with the same person or different people or alone.

Baba does not talk about the wall. She does not gesture toward it. She does not, when I am documenting it, explain or narrate or reminisce. She drinks tea from the blue mug and watches me look, and if I ask a question she answers it briefly and specifically, and if I don't ask she says nothing, and the wall is there the way the stove is there and the locks are there and the tap's required half-turn is there — it is not a display, it is not a *collection*, it is the surface of a life that has been accumulating for thirty-one years in a kitchen that is exactly big enough for one person to cook and one person to sit and watch, and the question the courtroom is deciding is whether to peel all of this off and put my grandmother somewhere safe, somewhere supervised, somewhere with four correct burners and no radiator that speaks and no wall, no wall, no wall.

Sometimes the radiator clanks and I swear it is saying *stay*. But that might be me. That might be what I am saying, in the only language I have, which is paying very close attention and writing it down.

Baba opens the blinds in a specific order: kitchen, living room, bedroom. Never bedroom first. I asked once. She said, "You let the light practice in the rooms where it doesn't matter first." I wrote this down too. I write everything down. I am twelve years old, and I have a sixty-four-count box of crayons, and I am building a record of a life in case the court requires evidence that one existed.
