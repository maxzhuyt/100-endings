# The Treasurer

The vote was on a Tuesday, the last one in May. We held it on Carmine's roof because that's where we always held things, up among the lofts with the evening settling pink over the rowhouse chimneys and the birds cooing in their boxes like they had opinions of their own. Carmine called it. He'd gotten the lab results back from Dr. Phan, the avian vet on Allegheny, and the numbers were right there on the printout: nandrolone metabolites in the urine of Teddy's cock bird, the blue-bar that had won three of the last five races by margins nobody could account for. Carmine read the numbers out loud. I remember that, because he read them the way you'd read a eulogy—steady, prepared, like he'd practiced it alone first.

There were six of us. Carmine Deluca. Teddy Morak. Ray Brogan. Dennis Mahler. Little Pete Stavros, who was six-foot-three but had been Little Pete since somebody's father gave him the name decades ago. And me. Milton Pfeiffer, treasurer, seventy-eight years old, keeper of the books since the second year.

Carmine said we'd do a hand vote. He said anyone who thought Teddy should be removed from the club should raise his hand.

Teddy was sitting in the lawn chair he'd brought up eleven years ago, the aluminum-frame one with the green and white webbing that had faded to green and gray. He had his hands on both armrests. He was looking at the birds.

Five hands went up. Carmine's, Ray's, Dennis's, Little Pete's, mine. Teddy did not raise his hand, obviously, but he also did not do any of the things I had spent the afternoon expecting. He didn't curse. He didn't throw the printout off the roof. He didn't say it was a mistake, a contamination, a conspiracy. He didn't say twenty-seven years of friendship should mean something. He didn't say any of the things that would have made it easier for the rest of us, because anger is easy to walk away from.

What he did was fold his lawn chair. He stood up, pressed the seat so the frame collapsed with that particular aluminum click, and tucked it under his arm. He nodded—not at anyone specifically, just a nod like he was confirming a figure in a column—and he went down the roof stairs. His footsteps on each plywood step. Then the back door opening and closing. Then nothing.

I was at the edge of the roof. I was always at the edge of the roof, because my own house is across the alley, and from Carmine's parapet I could see my lofts and check on my birds even during meetings. So I was the one who saw Teddy come out his back door into his yard. He still had the chair under his arm. He stopped in the middle of the concrete pad, between the rusted gas grill and the recycling bin. And he stood there. Just stood. Holding the folded chair like he was waiting at a bus stop, or like someone had told him to wait right there, they'd be back in a minute.

Nobody called down to him. Up on the roof Carmine was collecting the beer bottles and the printout, and the other three were muttering the way men mutter when they've done something necessary and don't want to think about whether it was right. I watched Teddy stand in his yard for what must have been four or five minutes. Then he went inside. But he took the chair with him. He didn't leave it in the yard where he kept his other outdoor things. He carried it into the house.

I went back to my roof that evening and fed my birds. Twenty-two homers in two lofts, all banded, all registered. I measured the feed the way I always do, three tablespoons per bird, a mix of peas, safflower, and red milo. The measuring is important. You overfeed a racing pigeon and the weight costs you seconds. You underfeed it and it won't home. I've been measuring for eleven years and I have never been off by more than a quarter tablespoon. This is not a boast. It is the only kind of discipline I have ever been able to maintain—the discipline of small things, done correctly, repeated.

On my way back inside I looked across the alley at Teddy's house. His kitchen light was on. That was all.

The club started in 2012, the year I turned sixty-seven. But it started without me. It started with Carmine and Teddy, two men in their mid-forties who happened to keep birds on rooftops close enough that they could see each other's lofts. I've heard the origin story from both of them, separately, and the versions agree on the essentials: Carmine was on his roof one April evening and saw a man on a roof one block over, handling a silver hen, and he yelled something across the gap—Carmine says it was "Nice bird," Teddy always said it was "Hey, that bird's too fat to race"—and the man yelled something back, and within a week they had walked the block between their houses and shaken hands in the alley and started talking about forming a club.

They registered with the American Racing Pigeon Union that June. Philadelphia Kensington Flyers. Carmine was president. Teddy was vice president and race secretary. Four months later I joined, because I had just retired from thirty-one years at the Frankford Candy Company, where I had kept the production ledgers, and my wife Ada had said I needed to do something outside the house before I drove her to murder. I brought my bookkeeping. They made me treasurer on the first Thursday.

Ray Brogan joined the following spring. Dennis Mahler in 2014. Little Pete in 2015. And that was it. Six men. You would think a club in a city this size could attract more, but pigeon racing is a dying sport. The old Italians and Poles and Irish who built lofts on every other rooftop in Kensington—those men are dead now, mostly, or in nursing homes in Bucks County. We were what was left.

I could tell you the winners of every race from 2012 to 2023. I have the books. I have the original books, the ones before we switched to the laptop in 2018, written in my hand in a series of composition notebooks—the black-and-white marbled kind, which I buy in bulk from the Dollar Tree on Frankford Avenue, eight at a time, because they are the correct size for a breast pocket. I have yards per minute, wind conditions, release coordinates, bird band numbers. I have the results from the day Ray's bird, a mealy hen called Duchess, set the club record in the two-hundred-mile race from Winchester, Virginia, at 1,847 yards per minute with a quartering headwind. I have the results from the day all six of us got skunked in the three-hundred-mile and none of our birds came home until the next morning, and we stood on Carmine's roof in the dark, listening, waiting for the sound of wings.

I have the expenses too. The Thursday-night accounting. We split the cost of race entry fees and fuel for the release trailer. I recorded them all. $14.50 per member for the Hagerstown race, April 2016. $22.00 per member for the series of five young-bird races, fall 2017, because fuel prices spiked. In 2019, when Ray's wife Helen needed surgery for her gallbladder and the insurance covered only part, the club voted unanimously to contribute two hundred dollars from the treasury plus whatever each man could give from his own pocket. The total was six hundred and forty dollars. I logged it as "Special Disbursement — Brogan Family" and I still have the receipt from the cashier's check.

These are the numbers. But the numbers are only the skeleton.

The body was the Thursday nights. May through October, every week, Carmine's roof. Lawn chairs in a half-circle facing the lofts. A cooler with Yuengling and, after Ray's doctor told him to quit drinking, a six-pack of Wawa iced tea. Hoagies from DiNic's if someone was feeling generous, or a bag of pretzels from the store if nobody was. I would read the prior week's figures. Carmine would assign training tosses. Teddy would argue about something—the band supplier, the feed mix, whether we should enter the Combine races or stick to our own schedule. Teddy argued about everything, not because he was disagreeable but because he could not help thinking, and thinking made him talk, and talking, for Teddy, was the same thing as arguing. He wasn't educated. He'd dropped out of Lincoln High in the eleventh grade. But he read—magazines, library books, things he found online—and he always came to Thursday nights with some theory about wing loading or immune supplements or the superiority of the Janssen bloodline, and he would hold the floor until Carmine told him to sit down, and then he would sit down, and then he would start again ten minutes later, and everyone would groan, and he would grin, and the evening would go on.

That was the body of it. The sound of Teddy talking and Carmine telling him to shut up and both of them laughing. The clink of bottles in the cooler. The birds settling into the lofts at dusk. Six men on a roof in Kensington, Pennsylvania, spending an evening together because there was nowhere else any of them particularly needed to be.

Carmine had an ex-wife in Cherry Hill. No kids. He worked three days a week at the Ace Hardware on Front Street. Teddy had been on disability since 2010, when his back gave out working the line at the old Budd Company plant—the plant was already closed by then, he was doing cleanup work for the demolition contractor, and something fell on him. He lived alone in a rowhouse he'd inherited from his mother. Ray was a retired mail carrier with bad knees. Dennis drove a school bus. Little Pete collected rent on two properties his father had left him and otherwise did nothing I could identify as employment. My own situation: retired, widowed since 2020, living in a house full of Ada's things. Her African violets on every windowsill. Twenty-three pots. I water them on a schedule—Tuesday and Saturday, with diluted fertilizer once a month—because they are alive, and the things that are alive require maintenance, and maintenance is something I know how to provide.

None of us had what you would call a full life. I say that without self-pity. You get to a certain age, or a certain set of circumstances, and the full life narrows to a few channels. The club was not a hobby. I want that to be clear. A hobby is something you could stop doing and the shape of your week would hold. The club was the shape.

I should say—I should mention, because it matters and because I did not give it appropriate weight at the time—that Teddy and Carmine started the club together. They co-founded it. They stood in the alley in 2012 and decided, together, to build something. Carmine designed the first lofts using plans from a book Teddy had checked out from the McPherson Square Library. Teddy drove to Gettysburg to pick up their foundation breeding pairs from an old Dutchman named Kreider who'd been racing since the Korean War. They shingled Carmine's roof access together, one holding the planks while the other nailed. This is all in the early books, before the formal entries began—just notes, really, in Carmine's handwriting and Teddy's, crammed into the margins of the first notebook.

I said I should mention it because it matters. I'll leave it at that.

The first incident was the coffee. This would have been mid-June, two, maybe three weeks after the vote. Carmine came up to my roof on a Saturday morning, which was unusual—Carmine did not come to my roof, I went to his—and he was holding a paper cup from the New Warsaw Deli on Belgrade Street.

"He was in there," Carmine said.

I asked who.

"Who do you think? Teddy. He's sitting at the counter eating a cheese Danish. Since when does Teddy go to the Warsaw? He lives eight blocks from the Warsaw. He goes to the Wawa on Aramingo like every other normal person."

I told Carmine that a man could buy coffee where he liked.

"He looked right at me, Milt. He didn't say a word. He just looked at me and chewed his Danish."

I said I didn't know what to tell him.

Carmine stood on my roof with his paper cup and stared across the alley at Teddy's house. The roof loft was visible from where we stood—Teddy hadn't taken his birds away after the vote, hadn't sold them or given them to anyone. They were up there cooing and strutting in the June sun. Carmine stared at them like they owed him money.

"He's doing it on purpose," Carmine said. "He wants me to know he's around."

I said maybe so.

The second incident was Mrs. Peralta's stoop. Gloria Peralta lived in the rowhouse adjacent to Carmine's on the east side. She was seventy, Dominican, church every morning, and she sat on her stoop in the evenings with a portable radio listening to the Phillies. Teddy, who had never to my knowledge exchanged more than a nod with Gloria Peralta in eleven years, began joining her. He brought his own chair—not the lawn chair from the roof, a different one, a folding camping chair—and sat beside her and listened to the game. Carmine told me about it in a voice I hadn't heard from him before, a voice with a seam in it.

"He's on Peralta's stoop, Milt. Every night. He's right there. I come out my front door and he's ten feet away listening to the goddamn Phillies."

"Does he talk to you?"

"He says hello. He says 'Hey, Carmine, how's it going.' Like nothing happened. Like he didn't pump his birds full of horse juice and get caught."

I watched from my roof one evening and confirmed it. Teddy was there on Gloria Peralta's stoop, kicked back in his camping chair. Carmine's front door was closed. Lights on inside. From above, the geometry was stark: Teddy positioned exactly where Carmine could not avoid him, planted in Carmine's periphery like a splinter.

Then the hardware store. Carmine told me Teddy came in on a Wednesday, bought a package of quarter-inch washers and a roll of plumber's tape, complimented the new girl at the register on her earrings, and left. Came back Friday. Bought a tube of caulk. Saturday, a paintbrush—a cheap one, the foam kind, a dollar twenty-nine. Each purchase totaling less than five dollars. Each time, according to Carmine, Teddy was friendly, pleasant, unrushed. He browsed. He asked about products. He treated the store like a place he had always come to and would always come to. Which, I should note, he had: Teddy had been buying things at that Ace Hardware for years before Carmine started working there. But he had never come three times in one week. That I could see.

I say "that I could see" because I want to be honest about what I know and what I am guessing. I am guessing about the washers and the caulk. Carmine told me those things, and by mid-July Carmine's accounts had taken on a quality I recognized from Ada's last years, when she would tell me the neighbor was watching her through the fence, or the pharmacist had given her the wrong pills on purpose. Not the same. Not that far. But a quality—a tightness, a certainty that everything was aimed at him.
