# Gloria Trask

---

## I.

What follows has been assembled from materials that should not, by any reasonable standard, coexist: forty-one video diary entries recorded on a Samsung Galaxy Tab A, model year 2019, propped against a stuffed elephant on a nightstand in Room 9 of the Shady Pines Memory Care Campus; fourteen surveillance recordings from the facility's own CCTV system, obtained through a public records request filed on October 3rd by the subject herself, in triplicate, using a form she had downloaded and printed during a supervised computer session that her care team had approved for the purpose of "sending an email to her grandson"; six handwritten incident reports by nursing staff; one email thread between siblings; and two interview transcripts from conversations that the interviewees did not know, at the time, were interrogations.

The subject of these materials — the investigator, though not the one any authority appointed — is Gloria Anne Trask, age seventy-one, resident of Room 9 from September 12th until February 4th, at which point she was transferred to the facility's higher-acuity wing, a move she described, in her thirty-eighth video diary, as "being sent to Siberia for asking the wrong questions."

Two facts are necessary before we begin.

First: Gloria Trask served for twenty-three years as a homicide detective with the county police department's Major Crimes unit, retiring at the rank of Detective Sergeant with more closed cases than anyone in the precinct's post-consolidation history. Her closure rate was 81 percent. The departmental average, during the same period, was 54. She was cited four times for meritorious service. She was reprimanded once, in 2004, for conducting an unauthorized interview with a material witness in a hospital recovery room by posing as a patient transport volunteer and wheeling the witness into a supply closet. The interview yielded a confession. The reprimand was entered into her file alongside a commendation for the same case.

Second: Daniel "Danny" Kieran, age forty-four, who served under Detective Trask for his first six years in Major Crimes and who, at the time of Gloria's enrollment at Shady Pines, held the position of Director of Security and Compliance for MedVista Senior Living, the corporate entity that operates Shady Pines and fourteen other memory care campuses across three states — Danny Kieran is currently under federal indictment. The charges: conspiracy to distribute controlled substances, healthcare fraud, tampering with pharmaceutical records, and one count of obstruction of justice relating to the modification of a surveillance hard drive at the Shady Pines facility on or about December 15th. The indictment was filed on November 19th of the following year. That is fourteen months after Gloria moved in.

The federal complaint runs to forty-three pages. It describes an operation in which benzodiazepines and fentanyl patches were systematically skimmed from patient medication supplies across seven MedVista facilities, diverted through a network of complicit nursing staff, and sold to mid-level distributors operating out of three counties. The estimated street value of the diverted pharmaceuticals, over a four-year period, exceeds eleven million dollars. The operation exploited a specific vulnerability: the patients from whom the drugs were skimmed were memory care residents. They could not reliably report what they had or hadn't been given. Their confusion was, in the language of the complaint, "a feature of the distribution model."

The indictment does not credit Gloria Trask.

It does not mention her.

Her name appears nowhere in the federal file, though her evidence — reassembled, recontextualized, stripped of dental floss — appears on pages 6, 11, 14, 22, and 31.

We will return to the dental floss.

For now, the first video diary entry, recorded September 15th, three days after admission. The camera is in selfie mode — Gloria's daughter Margot set the tablet up before she left, demonstrating twice how to press the red button, and Gloria had nodded in the way she nods when she has already decided to figure something out for herself. The angle catches Gloria from below, her chin prominent, the overhead fluorescent light making a halo of her white hair that she would find unflattering if she could see it, though she is, at this moment, looking not at the screen but at a point slightly past it, as if addressing a partner on the other side of a desk.

Her hands are steady. Her eyes are clear. She is sitting on the edge of her medical bed in a room she has occupied for seventy-two hours and whose dimensions she has already measured by stride — nine feet by eleven, with an irregularity near the bathroom door she attributes to "shoddy renovation, probably unpermitted." She is wearing a fleece pullover with a cardinal on it, a gift from her grandson, who is nine and believes she likes birds. She does not particularly like birds. She has not corrected him.

She says:

"For the record, my name is Gloria Anne Trask, badge number 4412, retired, and something in this place stinks."

She pauses. She looks down at her hands — perhaps checking them, perhaps gathering herself. When she looks up again, her voice has shifted into something older than the conversational register, something worn smooth by years of use in rooms with mirrored glass. It is a cop cadence. Flat, clipped, serialized.

"The facility is clean enough. Food's about what you'd expect — Tuesday was something they called pot roast, which I would classify as an unsolved case. Staff are professional but overworked. Ratio looks like one nurse per twelve residents on day shift, dropping to one per eighteen or twenty at night. That's when you'd move product, if you were moving product. Night shift. Reduced staff. Patients sedated or sleeping. I'm not saying that's what's happening. I'm saying the architecture is there."

She reaches off-camera and produces a small paper cup, the kind used for dispensing medication. She holds it up to the lens.

"This is tonight's cup. I asked if I could keep it. The nurse — Maria, from the Philippines, good hands, overly cheerful — looked at me like I'd asked to keep the IV needle. I told her I was making a craft project. She said 'How nice.' This cup held three tablets at five p.m. My morning cup held four. Both times they said 'your regular meds.' Four is not three. I'm going to find out which one is missing and why."

She sets the cup down. She looks at the camera for a long moment, and in that moment something crosses her face that the camera catches and she does not intend it to catch — not confusion, not fear, but a flicker of something that might be the awareness that she is sitting in a memory care facility, talking to a tablet, about a paper cup. The flicker lasts less than a second. She extinguishes it the way she has extinguished every inconvenient feeling for fifty years: by moving on to the next task.

"End of entry one. Trask out."

She reaches for the tablet. The video wobbles, catches a flash of ceiling tile, and goes black.

---

## II.

She was right about the pill.

This should be stated now, clearly, without suspense, because the story that follows is not a mystery in the sense that matters. The question is not whether Gloria Trask was right. She was right. The missing pill was lorazepam, 0.5 milligrams, a benzodiazepine prescribed for her as a sleep aid and skimmed that evening by Marcus Cole, LPN, night shift, who had been diverting controlled substances from patient medication trays for eleven months under a system designed by Danny Kieran and overseen, at the facility level, by Kieran's personally hired nursing supervisor, a man named Todd Birch, who will appear later in this account and who Gloria will describe, in her sixteenth video diary, as having "the face of a man who tips exactly fifteen percent and thinks he's generous."

But Gloria did not yet know any of this on September 15th. She knew the count was wrong. She knew *wrong counts* the way a carpenter knows warped wood — not through analysis but through feel, through the body's long training, through twenty-three years of evidence bags and chain-of-custody forms and the one ironclad rule she had drilled into every junior detective who passed through her mentorship, Danny Kieran included: *Count everything. Count it again. If the count changes, someone changed it.*

Her second video diary entry, September 16th, is forty-one seconds long. She is in the same position on the bed. The same pullover. The camera is still in selfie mode — the underside of her chin, the fluorescent halo — and it will remain in selfie mode for the duration of the forty-one entries, either because she cannot figure out how to switch it or because she has decided, as she decided about most technologies introduced after 1998, that the device is wrong and she is right.

She says: "Entry two. Morning dose: four pills. White round, white oval, yellow oval, small blue tablet. Evening dose last night: white round, white oval, yellow oval. No blue. Tonight I'll count again. If the blue is missing again, it's not an error. Once is a mistake. Twice is a pattern. This is day four."

In the background, someone down the hallway is playing "Moon River" on what sounds like a portable speaker. Gloria pauses, registers the music, and for a moment her face does something it rarely does in these recordings: it softens. Then she says, "Sinatra did it better. Entry ends."

It was Andy Williams, not Sinatra, but the narrator declines to correct her on matters of taste.

Entry three, September 17th, is longer — four minutes and twelve seconds — and it is the entry that establishes, beyond argument, what Gloria Trask was before she was a woman in a room with a cardinal fleece and a diagnosis.

She begins by reporting the count. Evening dose: three pills. The blue tablet is again absent. "Pattern confirmed," she says, and her voice carries the particular satisfaction of a hypothesis validated, a satisfaction indistinguishable, in its timbre and rhythm, from the voice on seven hundred case recordings archived in the county's digital evidence system, each one beginning the same way: *This is Detective Trask, badge 4412, at the scene.*

Then she expands her scope. She has been counting other things.

"Steps from my room to the nurses' station: forty-one. I measured it four times. Consistent. Steps from the nurses' station to the east corridor supply closet: twenty-three. That closet is opened twice during each day shift, at roughly 0800 and 1400, consistent with standard medication restocking protocol. Yesterday it was opened four times. The extra two openings were both during the window between shift change — 1445 to 1515 — when the day nurses are giving report and the night nurses haven't fully clocked in. Thirty-minute gap. No one is watching the corridor. No one is watching the closet."

She pauses. Adjusts her position on the bed. The camera catches her hands — and here the narrator must note what her daughter Margot noticed on her last visit, what the intake physician noted on the admission form, what two decades of colleagues knew without thinking about it: Gloria Trask has the hands of a watchmaker. Small, precise, steady. In the early entries, they are perfectly still when she is not using them, folded in her lap or flat on her knees, the disciplined stillness of a person who spent a career conscious that juries watch hands. No tremor. No fidget. The handwriting in her first notebook — a composition book, black and white marbled cover, purchased from the facility gift shop with $3.75 in exact change — is small, legible, and slanted slightly to the right, as it has been since the police academy. Her evidence labels, taped to the paper cups she has begun saving in her nightstand drawer, are formatted correctly: date, time, item description, chain-of-custody note. *Collected by G. Trask. No witnesses. Item stored in nightstand, top drawer, left side.*

She has labeled seven paper cups in four days.

The handwriting is steady.

It will not always be steady.

She continues: "The supply closet has a keypad lock. Four digits. I can't see it from my position in the hallway without attracting attention, but I've observed three staff members entering the code, and based on hand position and the audible keypress tones — B-flat, D, D, G, if my ear is right, and my ear is right — I assess the code is either 2337 or 2339. I will confirm."

She did confirm. It was 2337.

The narrator notes this not to build suspense — the code is entered into the federal complaint on page 14, supporting Count 3 — but to record the precision of a mind doing what it was made to do, in what would turn out to be the last months it could do it.

"Something else," she says. She leans closer to the camera. Her face fills the frame — the lines around her eyes deep and crosshatched, the eyes themselves dark brown and undimmed, the expression conspiratorial. "The man who came through today. The one in the suit. He's not a doctor. I've been here four days and I know the doctors — Dr. Ansari, who's competent; Dr. Leong, who's overworked; and the young one, Dr. Pham, who talks to me like I'm a golden retriever. This one's not medical. Expensive shoes. No badge visible, though he had a lanyard, and the card on it was blue, not white — staff badges here are white. Blue means corporate. He went into the administrator's office at 1320 and left at 1355. Then he went to the east corridor. He went to the supply closet. He entered the code without looking at the keypad. He was inside for six minutes. He left carrying nothing visible, but his jacket — right side — was hanging differently than when he entered. Something in the inside pocket. Small. Flat. You can't hide a rectangle from me, pal. I spent six years on Narcotics before I moved to Homicide."

She leans back. The conspiratorial expression resolves into something quieter.

"I don't know who he is yet. I'll find out."

The man in the suit was Danny Kieran.

She would not learn his name for another three weeks, and when she did, it would arrive not as a stranger's name but as the worst kind of recognition — the kind that rearranges every good memory into evidence.

But that is later. For now, entry three ends. Gloria's hand reaches for the tablet. The frame lurches. There is a half-second of ceiling tile — she has recorded enough ceiling tile across forty-one entries to constitute a complete photographic survey of the suspended acoustical grid in Room 9 — and then black.

## III.

Time in a memory care facility is a substance that behaves differently than it does elsewhere. It pools. It eddies. Sunday is Tuesday is Friday, distinguished only by the entrée at dinner and the activity calendar on the whiteboard in the common room, which promises, in handwriting designed to radiate cheer, that Monday is Music Therapy, Tuesday is Garden Walk, Wednesday is Art Expression, Thursday is Bingo & Social, and Friday is Movie Afternoon. Saturday and Sunday are blank, which is either an oversight or an honest admission that weekends in institutional care are simply time with its mask off.

Gloria attended Music Therapy once, on September 19th. In her diary entry that evening, she described it as "forty-five minutes of a woman with a ukulele singing 'You Are My Sunshine' to people who can't remember if it's raining." She did not go back.

She attended Garden Walk once, on September 20th, and used the occasion to pace the perimeter of the building, counting exits (four, plus two fire doors alarmed but not locked from inside), windows (sixty-two ground floor, not all of them secured), and external security cameras (three, all positioned to cover the parking lot and main entrance, none covering the service entrance on the east side of the building where, she noted, a white panel van with MedVista livery was parked every Thursday between 1600 and 1730).
