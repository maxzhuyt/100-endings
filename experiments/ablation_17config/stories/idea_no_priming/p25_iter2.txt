# The Rotation

## I.

The silver Accord pulled into the driveway at 6:17, three minutes early, which meant Christine had hit the lights on Wilmington Avenue. She left the engine running. The air conditioning had taken the whole drive from Kettering to get cold and she wasn't about to lose it.

Megan came out with Elliot and the backpack. The backpack was green, L.L.Bean, monogrammed — a gift from their aunt in New Hampshire who sent things like that. Elliot carried it by one strap so it dragged along the concrete.

"He ate most of the chicken," Megan said, leaning into the open passenger window. "No broccoli. I didn't push it."

"Reading log?"

"Three chapters of *Magic Tree House*. He's on the dinosaur one. He wrote the entry himself but I had to spell 'pteranodon' for him."

Christine nodded. Elliot climbed into the booster seat in the back and buckled himself. He had learned this recently — the buckle, the click, the adjustment of the chest strap — and performed it with the solemn competence of someone who understood that self-sufficiency was valued here.

"His swim trunks are in the front pocket," Megan said. "I washed them. Also the goggles. One of the nose pieces popped off but I pushed it back on. It'll hold."

"Thanks."

"The library books are due Thursday."

"I have it in the calendar."

Megan stepped back. She bent slightly and waved at Elliot through the window. He waved back, a small motion, palm flat, the wave of a child who has been waved at many times through many windows.

Christine reversed out of the driveway. Megan watched the car to the end of Shroyer Road and then she went inside and put the sheets in the wash. The twin bed was in what had been her office, and every two weeks she made it up the night before he arrived and stripped it the evening he left, and the muscle memory of this task had become so routine that she sometimes caught herself stripping the bed on off-weeks, reaching for a fitted sheet that was already in the closet.

The schedule lived in a shared Google Sheet titled "E. Rotation — 2024." Todd had built it. Todd built things like that. The columns were color-coded: Christine in blue, Megan in green, Patrick in orange, Todd in gray. Each two-week block was subdivided into weekdays and weekends, pickup and dropoff times, and a notes column where someone could log a doctor's appointment or a school event. Summer was different from the school year. During the school year, the rotation centered on whoever lived in the Smithville Elementary zone, which was Christine, so Christine carried more weeks and the others took weekends and holidays. Summer opened the grid. The blocks became more even. There were swaps, noted in red text, and a running thread of comments in the margins that constituted the only regular communication between the four of them.

The Sheet had been created eleven days after their father's funeral, which had been twenty-two months after their mother's. Their mother had died at Miami Valley Hospital on a Tuesday in March, five weeks after Elliot was born, from a pulmonary embolism that had developed from a deep vein thrombosis that had developed from the emergency cesarean that had brought Elliot into the world. She was forty-four. She had not expected to be pregnant. None of them had expected her to be pregnant. Their father, who was forty-seven, survived her by a year and a half, which was long enough to demonstrate that he could not manage an infant and his own desolation at the same time. The cause of death on the certificate said myocardial infarction, and no one disputed this, though Megan believed it was despair and Patrick believed it was vodka and Christine believed it was both but would not say so because she had inherited the family's preference for letting medical language stand as a final word.

There was no will. There was no godparent. There was a house on Smithville Road with a paid-off mortgage and a second-floor nursery where Elliot had slept in a crib that Christine recognized as the same crib that she herself had slept in, refinished.

They met at Todd's kitchen table in Beavercreek. Todd had printed out a calendar. The calendar became the Sheet. The Sheet became the rotation. And the rotation, within six months, had become so thoroughly the organizing principle of their four adult lives that they could not remember what shape their weeks had taken before it. It was the most significant thing they had ever built together, and the most orderly, and the most quiet.

Dayton spread beneath all of this like a flat palm, open and unhelpful. It was the kind of city that made staying easy and leaving unnecessary. The cost of living was low. The highways were fast. You could get from one side of the metro to the other in twenty-five minutes, which meant that the rotation was geographically possible in a way it would not have been in Columbus or Cincinnati, where distances stretched and traffic could derail a Tuesday handoff. In Dayton, four adults could maintain four separate households within a fifteen-mile radius and hardly cross paths unless they meant to. The city's great gift to the arrangement was its compactness, its lack of friction. Nothing about Dayton demanded that you reckon with it. You could drive its streets for years and never be forced into a confrontation with the landscape. The Great Miami River moved through the center of town like a thought that didn't finish. The old NCR factories stood along South Patterson like props from a play that had closed. The neighborhoods — Belmont, Oakwood, Kettering, Beavercreek — sat side by side in a mild patchwork of incomes and lawn lengths, none of them far from anything, all of them far from the kind of density that would have forced these four siblings into the same building, the same block, the same unavoidable daily collision.

The rotation depended on this distance. It could not have functioned in closeness. The handoffs were clean because the drives were short but not trivial, ten minutes of windshield time in which whoever was delivering or receiving could reconstitute the boundary between their own life and the shared project. The boy moved through Dayton's geography the way a baton moves through a relay — touching each hand briefly, belonging to none.

## II.

At Christine's, the bread situation was resolved early. Elliot would not eat the heels. He would eat the interior slices only if the crusts were cut off, not torn, and only if the peanut butter reached the edges before the cut was made, so that no dry margin of exposed bread remained. Christine had learned this in the first month. She did not learn it from anyone telling her. She observed three sandwiches returned to the kitchen with the same geometry of avoidance — a bite from the center, a crescent of untouched crust — and adjusted.

She kept a list on her refrigerator, handwritten, that she updated when something new surfaced. *No heels. Crusts off. Afraid of the basement (the sound, not the dark — the furnace). Left shoe always first. Sleeps with the sock monkey but won't admit it. Shark nightlight, NOT the star one. Star one "makes a sound."* The list was three columns long and written in the small, precise hand of someone who catalogs what she cannot otherwise hold. It was the most intimate document in the house, and she had never shown it to her siblings, not out of secrecy but because it had never occurred to her that it constituted information worth sharing. It was her list. They had, she assumed, their own.

They did not.

Todd had no list. Todd had the Sheet, with its color-coded blocks and swap protocols, and he had a bin of toys in the guest room and a laminated chart on the bathroom wall that showed the steps for brushing teeth, and he had a general competence with logistics that allowed him to feed, bathe, and transport a seven-year-old boy on schedule and without incident. What he did not have was curiosity about the boy's interior. This was not cruelty. Todd had managed supply chains for a medical device company for eleven years. He understood systems. He trusted that a system, properly maintained, would produce acceptable outcomes. He signed the permission slips. He enforced the bedtime. When the soccer registration form appeared in Elliot's backpack in late May, Todd set it on the kitchen counter and wrote "SOCCER — DUE 6/7" on a Post-it and stuck it to the form and then, over the course of twelve days, moved the form from the counter to the table to the counter again, each time re-reading the Post-it and each time deciding that this was a decision that required coordination with the others, which required a conversation, which required a moment of initiative that he could not quite locate in himself. On day thirteen Christine found the form in the backpack, already past deadline, and registered Elliot herself and did not mention it.

At Patrick's, the weeks were louder. Patrick had a two-bedroom apartment in Belmont with a pit bull named Sergeant and a girlfriend, Danielle, who was warm and inconsistent. Elliot liked Patrick's weeks. This was observable. His body was different at Patrick's — looser, more prone to running. Patrick threw him over his shoulder in the driveway. Patrick let him eat cereal for dinner. Patrick had a PlayStation and did not enforce the reading log and once, during a thunderstorm, pulled the mattress into the living room and built a fort out of couch cushions and watched *Jurassic Park*, which was too old for him, and Elliot had vibrated with terrified pleasure for two hours and then slept in the fort and dreamed about raptors.

But Patrick was also the one who forgot the inhaler. And Patrick was the one who lost the library card. And Patrick was the one who, during his third rotation week of the spring, left Elliot in the car outside a Gas & Go on Linden Avenue for four minutes while he ran in for cigarettes, and the four minutes were observed by no one and resulted in no harm and were never reported, but the four minutes existed, and Elliot, who was six at the time, had counted them on the dashboard clock, and the knowledge of those four minutes lived in him the way a stone lives at the bottom of a creek, settled and permanent and invisible from the surface.

At Megan's, the weeks were careful. Megan laminated things. Megan had a visual schedule on a corkboard with Velcro-backed icons — a toothbrush, a book, a bed — that Elliot could rearrange to show the order of his evening routine. Megan had researched childhood attachment and bought three books about it and read two of them. Megan asked him questions: "How are you feeling about going to Christine's tomorrow?" She asked this with the particular softness of a person who has read that children need to be asked such questions, and she meant it, she genuinely meant it, but the question always arrived in a frame that made it feel like a task being completed rather than a wonder being pursued. Elliot had learned to answer "Good" and wait for the frame to close.

The Boonshoft Museum trip required a signed permission slip and a packed lunch and a specific color of T-shirt — yellow, for the Sunflower group — and three dollars for the gift shop, which the form said was optional but which everyone knew was not optional. The trip fell during Christine's week. She signed the slip, packed the lunch (turkey, no lettuce, apple slices, juice box, the cheese crackers from the blue box and not the orange box), found a yellow shirt in the bin she kept of Elliot's clothes, and put three dollars in a Ziploc bag with his name written on it in Sharpie. She did all of this the night before at 10:40 p.m. after a full shift at the distribution center and two hours of her own accounting coursework, and she did it with the same absent efficiency with which she loaded the dishwasher, and she put the backpack by the door and set two alarms and fell asleep on the couch with the television on, and in the morning she drove him to school twelve minutes early and watched him walk inside carrying the yellow shirt over his arm because he had refused to wear it yet — he would change at school — and she felt nothing that she could identify as love and nothing she could identify as its absence, only the steady hum of a responsibility being met, and met, and met.

## III.

The summer opened the rotation like a hand releasing a fist. School had provided a spine: the brick building on Smithville Road, the bus route, the rigid start and end times that anchored each week to Christine's address and made her the de facto primary. Without it, the boy was distributed more evenly across the grid, two weeks here, two weeks there, with handoffs that shifted from Sunday evenings to Wednesday mornings depending on work schedules and swap negotiations conducted in the Sheet's comment thread.

In the first week of June, Elliot was at Todd's. Todd had enrolled him in a YMCA day camp on South Main that ran from 8:00 to 3:30. The camp was called "Adventure Explorers." The name was printed on a lanyard that Elliot wore around his neck with a laminated name tag that said ELLIOT in block letters and, beneath it, four emergency contact numbers — one for each sibling — listed in the order of the rotation, which was the order of their birth.

The counselor at drop-off on the first morning asked Todd, "And you're Dad?"

"Uncle," Todd said. "I'm one of his guardians."

"Oh," the counselor said. She was young, maybe twenty, with a whistle on a lanyard that matched Elliot's. "Is there a — should I put you down as primary contact, or — "

"You can put my sister. Christine Keenan." He spelled it. The counselor wrote it on a clipboard. Elliot was already walking toward the gym, where children were sitting in a circle on the floor. He did not wave. He entered the circle and sat and folded his hands in his lap and waited to be told the next thing, and this — this readiness, this frictionless compliance — was the quality that every teacher and counselor and pediatrician who had ever encountered him remarked upon. What a good kid. What an easy kid. How well-adjusted he seemed.

He was not well-adjusted. He was well-calibrated. There is a difference, but it is visible only to someone who is looking for it, and no one was looking for it because the system was looking for something else. The system was looking for dysfunction: bruises, tantrums, missed meals, failing grades. The system had a checklist and Elliot did not appear on it. He ate. He slept. He read at grade level. He had four adults who transported him and fed him and signed his forms and bought him shoes when his old ones got tight. The system saw this and called it sufficiency and moved on.

But sufficiency is not the same as being known.

Christine knew about the bread and the furnace and the sock monkey. Patrick knew that he became a different child in the presence of roughhousing, that there was a wildness in him that only emerged when someone was willing to chase him, grab him, flip him upside down. Megan knew that he bit his thumbnail when he was processing a transition, that the nail on his left thumb was chewed to a permanent crescent. Todd knew that he liked to be in the room where Todd was working, not speaking, not playing, just present, sitting on the floor with a book or a set of Legos, and that this silent proximity constituted, for Elliot, a form of rest.

Each of them knew a quadrant. None of them knew the whole. And the rotation, in its impeccable structure, ensured that the quadrants never merged — that the four partial portraits of one boy were never laid over each other, never compared, never allowed to resolve into a single image. The Sheet tracked dates. It did not track what happened inside them. There was no column for what Elliot said in the bathtub at Patrick's on a Thursday, no field for the way he stood at Christine's kitchen window and watched the street as if waiting for someone specific who was not going to come.
