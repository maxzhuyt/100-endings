**Abstract**

The following document compiles the radio dispatches transmitted by Cartographer L. A. Coltaire during her twenty-four-day survey of the current perimeter and interior boundary features of the Salem Exclusion Zone (colloquially: "the hex"), pursuant to Bureau Directive 1187-R, which mandates triennial resurvey of all Category Five cursed geographies in the continental United States. The dispatches are presented in their original transmission order. Annotations have been provided at the Bureau's request to contextualize the cartographer's observations within the broader body of longitudinal perimeter research and to offer, where possible, interpretive frameworks for phenomena the cartographer encountered in the field.

A brief orienting note for readers unfamiliar with the Zone's provenance may be useful here, though the Bureau's own institutional histories cover the matter at greater length than a marginal annotation permits.[^1]

[^1]: The standard account is as follows. On 22 September 1692, eight persons were hanged on Proctor's Ledge in Salem, Massachusetts — the final executions of that year's proceedings against accused practitioners. They were the last of nineteen. The historical record, long contested on evidentiary and ideological grounds, has been settled since at least 1890, when the Bureau's precursor agency declassified the Mather Suppressed Correspondence: the accused were, in fact, practitioners of varying skill working within a regional tradition of land-binding that predated English settlement by an uncertain but considerable interval. The trials disrupted a stable network of nineteen intercessors whose practice, as best we can reconstruct it, involved a continuous negotiated relationship between human settlement and the underlying semiotic substrate of the territory — what the Bureau's technical literature calls the *physics compact*, though I find this term reductive. What matters is this: in the final moments before death, the nineteen — nearly all of whom had refused to confess, had refused the court's language for what they were — spoke a coordinated abdication into the ground beneath them. Not a curse. Not an invocation. A *withdrawal*. They pulled their consent out of the land the way you might pull thread from a hem, and the fabric of material consensus in a roughly three-hundred-square-mile radius around the gallows site began, over the subsequent decades, to unravel. The Zone is not haunted. The Zone is *unheld*. I have spent thirty-one years studying its perimeter, and I confess this distinction still strikes me, on certain evenings, as the most important thing I know and the least useful.

The annotations should be understood as supplementary and interpretive rather than evaluative. Cartographer Coltaire's fieldwork was conducted with exemplary rigor, and nothing in the annotations that follow should be read as reflecting adversely on her technical competence or professional judgment.

This report is respectfully submitted to the Bureau's Records Division for archival preservation and for whatever use it may afford future survey teams.

— M. Harker, Ph.D.
November 2023

## DISPATCH 01

**Date:** 9 September 2023, 0647 hrs
**Position:** 42.5291° N, 70.8847° W (Perimeter Marker 16-East, confirmed)
**Signal Quality:** Excellent

Bureau, this is Coltaire. Confirming arrival at Marker 16-East, which I am pleased to report is exactly where you said it would be, which is more than I can say for most of the coordinates in the '20 survey packet. Whoever typeset that document transposed the easting and northing on six — *six* — of the twenty-four primary markers. I've been correcting as I go. You're welcome.

Equipment status: all nominal. Theodolite calibrated at 0530 against the benchmark at the Danvers relay station. Brunton compass checked against true north per solar observation. Ground-penetrating radar unit powered and initialized; battery at full charge, estimated 72 hours of continuous use before I'll need the backup cells. Radio range is adequate at the perimeter — I'm getting a clean bounce off the relay — though I expect degradation as I move interior, per standard Zone protocol.

Weather is clear, temperature 11°C, wind negligible. Visibility to the tree line is approximately 400 meters. The forest here is mixed hardwood, predominantly red oak and sugar maple, with some hemlock in the ravines. Understory is moderate — blueberry, witch hazel, the usual New England assemblage. Soil at the marker is a sandy loam, well-drained, with no unusual coloration orite remarkable mineral signature.[^2] A good ordinary New England dirt.

[^2]: Coltaire's field assessment is consistent with visual and tactile analysis. However, spectroscopic work I conducted at this same marker in 2019 identified elevated concentrations of a calcium silicate phase that does not correspond to any mineral in the Dana classification system. The phase is transparent to standard field colorimetry and presents no anomalous texture. It is detectable only through X-ray diffraction or, in sufficient concentration, through a faint deviation in the soil's dielectric permittivity — a measurement Coltaire's ground-penetrating radar unit was in principle capable of registering, though not configured to flag. The phase has been present at every perimeter marker I have tested over three decades. Its concentration increases with proximity to the Zone's interior. I have provisionally termed it *nullite* in my own notes, for reasons that will become apparent, though I have not proposed formal nomenclature to the Bureau, as I am uncertain the substance meets conventional criteria for mineral classification. It may not, strictly speaking, be a *substance*. It may be what is left when a substance's participation in material consensus has been withdrawn. I include this note not to diminish Coltaire's observation — her field kit could not have detected what I am describing — but because the presence of nullite at Marker 16-East confirms that the perimeter has not retreated in the interval since my last survey. It has, if anything, advanced. The soil looked ordinary because the extraordinary, inside the Zone, does not always trouble itself to look different. It simply *is* different, at a level beneath appearance.

I'll begin the perimeter traverse at 0700, moving clockwise from Marker 16-East toward 17-East. Planned pace is two markers per day along the eastern arc, slowing to one per day as I move into the interior transects. I expect to reach the first interior waypoint by 14 September, weather permitting.

One small note for whoever reads these in sequence: I know the Bureau prefers dispatches free of editorial commentary, but I've always found that a field report scrubbed of the reporter's voice is a field report that lies about its conditions of production. You are receiving the observations of a human being, not an instrument. I will be precise, but I will also, on occasion, be present in my own data. If this offends the style manual, I invite the style manual to spend three weeks alone in a cursed forest and see how it writes afterward.[^3]

[^3]: I found this, upon first reading, almost unbearable.

Coltaire out.

## DISPATCH 02

**Date:** 9 September 2023, 1842 hrs
**Position:** 42.5188° N, 70.8923° W (Perimeter Marker 17-East, confirmed)
**Signal Quality:** Excellent

Bureau, Coltaire. Marker 17-East reached on schedule. Today's traverse was unre­markable — good trail conditions, dry footing, no deadfall obstructions requiring reroute. I've completed the standard battery at both markers: compass bearings at 15-degree intervals, GPR transects on the cardinal axes extending 50 meters from the marker, soil cores at 0, 25, and 50 meters on each transect.

Preliminary readings: the hex's boundary, as best I can establish it from the perimeter data, appears to be within 30 meters of its plotted 2020 position along this arc. I'll need the interior transects to confirm, but the GPR returns are consistent with the subsurface reflectivity profile the Bureau's database lists for stable boundary conditions. Nothing is jumping out at me.[^4]

[^4]: The 2020 subsurface reflectivity profile that Coltaire is comparing against was generated by Cartographer R. Eames, whose survey was conducted under rushed conditions owing to an early-October nor'easter. Eames's interior transects were incomplete. His perimeter data, which I reviewed at the Bureau's request, contained a systematic baseline error in GPR calibration that went uncorrected because Eames — a competent but overextended technician working his third consecutive commission — did not perform the solar compass check that Coltaire, to her credit, performed as a matter of course. The consequence is that the 2020 profile, against which Coltaire is measuring stability, is itself displaced by roughly 40 meters to the southwest. What Coltaire is interpreting as a stable boundary is, in all probability, a boundary that has expanded by approximately 70 meters in three years and that merely *appears* stable against a flawed reference. I documented this concern in a memorandum to the Bureau in February 2021. The memorandum was acknowledged. No correction to the database was issued.

One observation worth logging: at approximately 1430 hrs, during the GPR transect on the south axis from Marker 17-East, I noticed a quality to the light in the forest canopy that I want to describe precisely, because I think precision matters here and imprecise accounts of Zone phenomena contribute to the kind of institutional mythology that makes our work harder.

The light was amber — late-afternoon light filtered through maples just beginning to turn. Beautiful, frankly. What caught my attention was that the light on the ground and the light in the canopy appeared to be arriving from slightly different angles. The shadows cast by the canopy suggested a solar azimuth roughly 5 degrees south of where the shadows on the ground placed the sun. I checked my compass bearing against the sun's actual position: the ground shadows were correct. The canopy shadows were wrong, or rather, the canopy was casting shadows as though the sun were somewhere it was not.

I am logging this as a probable atmospheric refraction effect. Temperature inversions in the lower canopy airspace, common in mixed forest at this time of year, can produce apparent displacement of shadow angles by deflecting light at the canopy-air interface. I've seen similar effects outside Zone contexts entirely.[^5]

[^5]: She had not. I say this not to impugn her honesty but to note the phenomenon that those of us in perimeter studies have come to call *confabulatory smoothing*: the tendency of trained observers, upon encountering Zone-proximate anomalies, to retrieve from memory a plausible analogue that justifies categorizing the anomaly as known. The retrieved memory is often genuine in its broad outline — Coltaire has certainly observed atmospheric refraction — but the specific correspondence she claims, between what she has seen before and what she is seeing now, is manufactured by a mind that prefers continuity to rupture. This is not a failure of intellect. It is a feature of human cognition operating in an environ­ment that exerts pressure on the very cognitive architecture that would be needed to perceive the pressure. I first described this mechanism in a 1998 paper for the *Journal of Cursed Geography*. The paper was not widely cited. I am told the reviewers found its implications "needlessly distressing."

I'll continue to monitor. Coltaire out.

## DISPATCH 04

**Date:** 11 September 2023, 0715 hrs
**Position:** 42.5034° N, 70.9102° W (Perimeter Marker 19-East, confirmed)
**Signal Quality:** Good

Bureau, Coltaire. Markers 18 and 19 surveyed. I'm consolidating two days into one dispatch because the work has been routine and I'd rather save transmission time for observations of substance.

The perimeter continues to present as stable against the 2020 reference. Soil cores show expected stratigraphy — glacial till over bedrock with a thin organic horizon. No anomalous coloration, no unusual mineralogy in the field. GPR returns are clean.

I'm sleeping well. I want that on record because I know the Bureau tracks psychological indicators and I know the post-survey debriefs place weight on sleep quality as an early metric. I am sleeping soundly, dreaming unremarkably, and waking with a clear head. My appetite is normal. My mood is good. I feel competent, oriented, and — this is the word I want — *placed*. I know where I am. The land here is legible to me. I read it the way I read any survey site: as a set of measurable properties organized by processes I understand.[^6]

[^6]: I have read this dispatch nine times. It is, in its way, the most precise thing Coltaire ever wrote — not because her measurements are more accurate here than elsewhere but because the *self* doing the measuring is, in this dispatch, whole and unqueried. She describes her relationship to the land as one of mutual legibility: she reads it, and in reading it, she is *placed*. This is the foundational compact that the Zone dissolves. Every cartographer I have studied — and I have studied seven, over three decades, Coltaire being the most recent — has produced a dispatch in the early days that functions as an inadvertent self-portrait: a record of what they were before they became what the Zone made of them. I keep these passages. I do not know why I keep them. They are not scientifically useful. They are the opposite of scientifically useful. But I keep them, in a file in my office, catalogued by cartographer and date, and sometimes I read them in sequence, and what I hear is not data but a kind of roll call of people who knew, for a few more days, what it was like to cast a shadow that agreed with the sun.

Tomorrow I transit from the perimeter arc to the first interior transect. I'll be crossing the nominal boundary — the line the Bureau designates as the onset of primary Zone influence — around midday. Radio quality will likely degrade; I'll switch to the condensed format for dispatches if signal becomes unreliable.

Coltaire out.
