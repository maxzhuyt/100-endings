# The Fifth Story

This story could be called "How I Kept the Captain Warm." It could also be called "The Retreat," or "Morphine," or "What the Cold Does." It is a true story. This is what happened.

---

## I. How I Kept the Captain Warm

The captain stopped shivering at mile eleven.

A corpsman learns the stages. There is the shivering, which is the body's argument with the cold — a muscular plea, involuntary and animal. There is the violent shivering, when the argument becomes desperate. Then there is the cessation, which is not victory but surrender, the body's thermostat broken, the argument forfeit. I had memorized this at Camp Pendleton from a laminated card. I had the card still, in my breast pocket, though the laminate had cracked in the cold and the card had split along its fold like everything else was splitting — boot leather, skin, the breech mechanisms of rifles, the will to continue walking south.

He had given his second pair of gloves to Corporal Ortiz at Yudam-ni. I did not know this then. I learned it later in the way you learn all the things that killed someone — after, and uselessly, each fact a door you walk through into a room where a man is already dead.

I gave him my scarf. I made him move his fingers. I said, Sir, move your fingers, and he moved them, or tried. At mile nine I opened his jacket and placed a heat packet against his abdomen, the last one I had, and I thought: I am doing my job. I am keeping him alive. The Chinese were in the hills on both sides and the snow came down steady and the column was a dark thread being drawn across white cloth and I was keeping the captain alive the way I had been trained, by the numbers, the laminated numbers in my splitting pocket.

He stopped shivering at mile eleven, and I told myself it meant he was warming.

---

## II. The Retreat

Let me tell it differently. It was the first of December, 1950. We were not retreating. The general had said we were attacking in a different direction, and we repeated this, and it was both a joke and not a joke, the way all language at Chosin was both itself and not itself because the cold had gotten into speech too — words cracking along their folds, meaning what they meant and also what they could not afford to mean.

The captain had been awake for three days. He had held the perimeter at the inlet when Fox Company was being overrun on the hill. Of the two hundred and twenty men in his company, one hundred and six were walking. Of those one hundred and six, I estimated that perhaps sixty were combat-effective, and of those sixty, perhaps thirty could still operate their weapons. The others walked with their hands under their arms or inside their parkas, fingers too far gone to manage a trigger, and what they were then I do not know — not soldiers, not casualties, something the cold had made that was neither.

I walked beside him. That was my position. The corpsman walks where he is needed, but the place I was needed most was beside the captain, because the captain was the company and the company was what kept one hundred and six men walking south in a coherent line rather than stumbling individually into the snow to sit and rest, which was what the cold kept whispering for them to do.

At mile eleven he stopped shivering and I should have known.

The thing I am telling you is not about the body. The laminated card was about the body. The thing I am telling is about the instant in which I chose to believe he was warming, because if the captain was dying then I was failing, and if I was failing then I was not the corpsman walking in his correct position doing his correct job, and if I was not that, then I was only a twenty-year-old from Chillicothe, Ohio, in a place that was killing everyone, and I could not be that. No one could be only that, and live.

---

## III. Morphine

Or perhaps the story is this: that I had morphine and could not use it.

The syrettes were frozen. I kept six of them in the pocket nearest my body to keep them liquid, rotating them with the six in my kit the way another man might worry rosary beads, and even so they were thick and slow, half-crystallized, dubious. The cold took everything. It took the plasma, which froze in its bottles. It took the adhesive from the tape, which would not stick to skin. It took the flexibility from the tourniquets. Every tool I carried was being unmade by the temperature, and this included me.

But I had the morphine, or some of it. Enough.

The captain said, at mile twelve — he said something. I need to be careful here. He spoke, and what he said was confused, and I know now that the confusion was cognitive impairment from the cold, that his core temperature had fallen below ninety-two degrees and his brain was dimming the way a lantern dims when the fuel runs low, banking itself, conserving. But what he said was about his daughter. She was four. He had shown me a photograph at Hagaru-ri, a small girl in a white dress squinting against the sun in what he told me was the backyard of his house in Beaufort, South Carolina, and in the photograph the azaleas were blooming and the light was the kind of light that does not exist at the Chosin Reservoir, had never existed there, a full and warm and abundant light, and I had looked at the photograph and felt something so far from what I was feeling that it was like being shown evidence of a physical law that had been repealed.

He said her name. Then he said something that was not language. Then he was quiet.

I had the morphine. This is what I am telling you. I had the morphine, and he was not in pain — the cold had taken the pain, or had become the pain so completely that it was no longer distinguishable as such — and so the morphine was useless to him, which meant I was useless to him, which meant that what was happening was simply happening, without my intervention, without my craft, just the cold and the body and the dark and the sound of boots on frozen road, and I could do nothing but walk beside him and watch his steps grow clumsy and short.

I had the morphine. I bring this up because people assume, later, that the story is about lacking something. They imagine scarcity — no supplies, no medicine, no help. But I had the morphine. I had four syrettes still liquid against my chest, and they were warm, the only warm things in that landscape, warm with my own diminishing heat, and they could do nothing for him. That is a different kind of story than the one about scarcity. That is a story about having what cannot help.

---

## IV. What the Cold Does

I need to tell it again.

The fifth story I have been approaching all along is the one I cannot reach by telling it correctly, so I will tell it incorrectly. I will tell you what the cold does, and you will understand that I am not talking about the cold.

The cold does this: it simplifies. It takes a man with a name, a rank, a daughter in Beaufort, a scar on his chin from a childhood fall, a habit of humming "Stardust" while reading maps, a way of saying *Doc* when he meant me that carried in it an entire theory of trust — it takes all of this and reduces it to a thermodynamic problem. A body losing heat faster than it can produce heat. A gradient, a deficit, an equation tipping toward zero. The cold is efficient. The cold does not hate you. The cold is not the Chinese in the hills or the snow or the generals who put you there. The cold is merely a condition, and what it does to a man is not cruel because cruelty requires intent, and what it does is therefore worse than cruelty. It is indifference operating on the body of someone you love, and you cannot argue with it, you cannot528 528fight it, you cannot528 do anything the laminated card suggests because the laminated card was written in a warm room by someone who had never stood at mile thirteen of a retreat that was not a retreat and watched a man sit down in the snow.

He sat down.

I said, Sir, you have to stand up.

He looked at me and his eyes were calm. This is the part I have told no one. His eyes were calm, and he was not afraid, and the calm was not courage — the calm was the cold, which had reached his brain and was simplifying him, taking him apart the way you take apart a rifle, piece by piece, function by function, until what remained was a man sitting in snow with calm eyes who could not understand why I was crying.

I did not decide to lie down beside him. It was not a decision. It was the last thing in me that knew how to help — that if I could not warm him with a heat packet or save him with morphine or make him stand with an order, I could lie against him and give him the warmth of my body, the mere literal mammalian warmth, and this is what I did. I lay against the captain in the snow at mile thirteen and I held him and his breathing slowed and his breathing slowed and his breathing slowed.

Sergeant Koenig pulled me away. He had to pry my arms open. He told me later my lips were blue and that I was speaking but not in language, and I believe this, because what I had to say was not something language could carry, then or now.

---

## V.

This story could be called "How I Kept the Captain Warm." It could be called "Mile Thirteen," or "Chillicothe," or "The Photograph." Each title is true. Each title is the door to a room I have entered and cannot leave.

I am eighty-one years old. The captain's daughter is seventy-seven. I have never contacted her. I have written eleven letters and mailed none of them because each letter is a version of this story and no version is the right one. In one letter I tell her he did not suffer. In another I tell her he spoke her name, which is true. In another I describe the calm in his eyes and how it was beautiful, which is also true and also a lie, because it was the cold and not her father who was calm, and to make it beautiful is to forgive the cold, and I do not forgive the cold. I will not. I have spent sixty years in the Sun Belt and I keep my house at eighty degrees and I sleep under four blankets in August and I am not, after all this time, parsing metaphor from medicine — I am still cold. I have been cold since mile thirteen. The gradient has never corrected.

I wanted to tell you how the captain died. I have told you five times and have not told you yet. The sixth version, the true one, is the one in which I simply say his name, which I will not write here, and the sound of his name is the whole story — every version collapsed into two syllables that I have carried at my core like six syrettes of morphine, warm and liquid and entirely useless.
