*Editor's note:* The following compilation was assembled at the request of the Joint Admiralty-Survey Board of Inquiry (Subterranean Division) and represents what we believe to be the most complete — or, more accurately, the least incomplete — account of the events of October through December 1928, commonly referred to within the Service as "the Fumbridge Affair," "the Lower Galleries Catastrophe," or, in the informal parlance of the Subterranean Explorers' Club bar, "that Percival business, please don't bring it up, Reggie's still upset about the theodolite."

Three principal document sources are excerpted herein:

1. **The field journal of Sir Percival Fumbridge, Bt., Crown Surveyor (Third Class), Subterranean Survey Division.** Recovered from the ruins of Forward Station 12 inside a biscuit tin. The tin was not Sir Percival's. How his journal came to be inside it remains unexplained. Several pages are missing. Others are stained with what chemical analysis has identified as mushroom soup.

2. **The operational log of Commander Aldric Varnock, Royal Subterranean Navy (ret.), Director of Private Exploration Interests, Varnock Deep-Core Mineral Extraction Ltd.** Seized under warrant from Varnock's drilling-vessel *The Indomitable* following its grounding on a stalagmite field in the Upper Reverberation Galleries. Commander Varnock has contested the legality of this seizure. Commander Varnock has contested most things.

3. **Official incident reports filed with the Admiralty Subterranean Survey Office** by various bureaucratic functionaries, some of whom requested anonymity, and all of whom requested transfers.

The Board of Inquiry was unable to reconcile these three accounts into a single coherent narrative. The reader should not expect to succeed where the Board did not.

*—H.R. Plimpton-Graves, Admiralty Archivist (Acting), with two aspirin and a heavy heart*

## I. CAREER DOSSIER: FUMBRIDGE, Sir Percival Havelock, Bt.

*Extracted from Subterranean Survey Division Personnel File No. 1,247. Filed under "Active — Conditional."*

---

**Full Name:** Sir Percival Havelock Fumbridge, Baronet

**Rank:** Crown Surveyor, Third Class

**Division:** Subterranean Survey (Cartographic Branch)

**Date of Appointment:** 4 June 1919

**Circumstances of Appointment:** Clerical. Sir Percival's original application was submitted to the *Surface* Survey Division (Cartographic Branch), where his qualifications — a Second in Geography from the University of Edinburgh, a Certificate in Triangulation from the Royal Geographical Society, and what his tutors described as "a genuine, if occasionally misdirected, enthusiasm for measurement" — were considered adequate for a posting to the Ordnance Survey office in Hampshire. Due to a filing error that transposed his application with that of a mining engineer named Humphries, Sir Percival was instead assigned to Subterranean Survey Forward Station 3, fourteen hundred feet below the Mendip Hills. When the error was discovered in 1921, Sir Percival had already completed two underground expeditions and expressed no desire to transfer, noting in a personal letter that he found the caverns "very agreeable, if damp." Request for correction was filed and subsequently lost. Humphries, meanwhile, spent two bewildered years mapping hedgerows in Dorset before resigning.

**Field Record:**

Sir Percival has participated in fourteen (14) sanctioned survey expeditions since his appointment. A summary of outcomes follows:

- **Expeditions completed without significant incident:** 3
- **Expeditions resulting in involuntary reclassification of previously mapped territory as "Unstable":** 4
- **Expeditions resulting in involuntary reclassification of previously mapped territory as "No longer extant":** 3
- **Expeditions resulting in involuntary reclassification of previously mapped territory as "On fire":** 1 (see Incident Report 1924-17, "The Sulphur Gallery Affair")
- **Expeditions resulting in the temporary or permanent loss of survey equipment valued in excess of £500:** 6
- **Expeditions resulting in cartographic data that later proved to be upside down:** 2
- **Expeditions during which a previously unknown species of subterranean fauna was disturbed, provoked, or inadvertently detonated:** 1 (see Incident Report 1926-04; see also the apology to the Subterranean Zoological Society, which remains outstanding)

**Selected Incident Summaries:**

*Expedition 4 (March 1921) — Gypsum Reach Extension Survey:* Survey Marker 7-G was not recovered. Nor was the ridge on which it had been placed. Sir Percival's journal attributes the collapse to "an unexpected geological instability," though the Board of Review noted that the instability in question post-dated Sir Percival's decision to test the resonant frequency of the ridge by striking it repeatedly with a calibration hammer. Reclassified territory: 0.3 square miles.

*Expedition 7 (November 1923) — Lower Calcite Galleries, Sectors 12 through 15:* Sir Percival's survey party became separated from its supply chain when his experimental technique for marking passage junctions — a system involving coloured chalk, a proprietary numbering scheme, and what he described as "intuitive arrows" — directed the supply bearers into an unmapped lateral fissure. The bearers were recovered after nine days. The supplies were not. Sir Percival, upon extraction, expressed satisfaction that his junction-marking system had "identified a passage not present on any existing chart," which was, in the Board's view, an accurate if unhelpful observation.

*Expedition 11 (August 1926) — The Sulphur Gallery:* [REDACTED BY ORDER OF THE ADMIRALTY — see sealed file 1926-17-CONFIDENTIAL]. The Board notes only that the Sulphur Gallery is no longer accessible, that the resulting fumes necessitated the evacuation of Forward Stations 6 through 9, and that Sir Percival's request to name the newly created thermal vent "Fumbridge's Chimney" was denied.

**Assessment:**

Sir Percival Fumbridge is, by all available metrics, a catastrophically poor surveyor. His triangulations are unreliable. His fuse-length calculations are, by his own repeated admission, "approximate." His sense of direction underground has been described by three separate expedition leaders as "theoretical." He has lost more theodolites than any surveyor in the Division's history — a record he reportedly regards with mild perplexity rather than shame.

He is, however, also unkillable.

In fourteen expeditions — across collapsing ridges, flooded galleries, sulphurous eruptions, equipment failures, navigational disasters, and at least one incident involving a large and justifiably irritated cave salamander — Sir Percival has never sustained an injury more serious than a sprained ankle. He has been buried, dropped, pelted with debris, swept away by subterranean flash floods, and, on one occasion, briefly set on fire, and has in every instance emerged intact, bewildered, and ready to submit his cartographic notes, which are always wrong.

The Division has considered dismissal on eleven separate occasions. Each time, the paperwork has been lost, misfiled, or — in one case — destroyed when Sir Percival's adjacent desk caught fire due to causes that remain undetermined.

He remains on the active roster.

## II. THE RECEPTION — TWO ACCOUNTS

### A. From the Field Journal of Sir Percival Fumbridge

*Entry dated 3 October 1928.*

---

Attended the quarterly reception at the Subterranean Explorers' Club this evening. A tolerable affair, though the canapés were dry and Major Whitting spent forty-five minutes describing his survey of the Upper Reverberation Galleries in a manner that suggested he believed himself the first man to encounter an echo. I nodded at appropriate intervals.

Cough is still troublesome — picked up a chill during last month's equipment inventory in the stores, which are poorly ventilated and, I suspect, home to a species of mould that the Zoological Society would find either fascinating or alarming. Took a cough drop from the tin in my waistcoat pocket during Whitting's second anecdote about stalactite calibration. Menthol, I think. Possibly eucalyptus. Disappointing either way — the chemist on Praed Street has rather gone downhill.

Was observed at one point by a tall man with dark whiskers standing near the geological display case. He seemed very intent. I assumed he was waiting for me to finish blocking the drinks table and gave him a polite nod, which he did not return. Rude, but one encounters all sorts at these functions.

Departed at half nine. The cough is no better.

---

### B. From the Operational Log of Commander Aldric Varnock

*Entry dated 3 October 1928.*

---

CONFIRMATION.

I have now SEEN it with my own eyes, and what I have seen changes EVERYTHING.

Tonight, at the Subterranean Explorers' Club — that mouldering parlour of bureaucratic mediocrities and their pointless, government-sanctioned hole-measuring — I positioned myself adjacent to the geological display case, which afforded an unobstructed sightline to the drinks table. I held a glass of sherry I did not drink. I waited. And at precisely 8:17 p.m., I watched Sir Percival Fumbridge place an object in his mouth and swallow it.

Let me be unambiguous. Let me be PRECISE.

The object was removed from a small tin carried in the left waistcoat pocket. I observed its dimensions: irregular ovoid, roughly 1.5 cm along the major axis. I observed its colour: pale, with a faint translucency that caught the lamplight. I observed the manner of its ingestion: calm, deliberate, performed while gazing placidly at Major Whitting as though listening to a man discuss stalactites were a thing a person did in the normal course of a normal evening.

It was the Cartolithe.

I have spent eleven years tracking the Cartolithe — eleven years sifting legend from geology, myth from mineral survey, rumour from the drunken confessions of retired deep-core drillers. I know its description from the Kircher fragments: *a stone of modest dimension, pale as milk, warm to the touch, in which are encoded the thermal routes to the deepest gold.* I know the Survey recovered it from the Langstrom Fissure in 1919 and told no one. I know it was logged as a "mineral sample of indeterminate type" and placed in general storage, because the Survey has never understood what it possesses — has never understood anything — has sat for decades atop the greatest navigational instrument in human history and used it as a paperweight.

And now Fumbridge has it. *Inside him.*

He stood there, sucking on it like a boiled sweet, and he had the gall — the BREATHTAKING, calculated gall — to nod at me. A small, polite nod, as one might offer a stranger at a drinks table. But I saw what was behind it. I saw the knowledge. He knows that I know. And he swallowed it anyway.

This is not a man who was assigned to the Subterranean Survey by clerical error. This is a man who has spent nine years engineering his proximity to the Cartolithe, who has used fourteen expeditions to map — *truly* map, in ways his falsified survey notes were designed to obscure — the deep architecture of the hollow Earth, and who has now, with the calm precision of a master operative, ingested the one artefact that will guide him to the thermal gold reserves of the inner core.

His record of "incompetence" is a fiction. Every collapsed ridge, every lost theodolite, every reclassified territory — all of it, DELIBERATE. A man does not survive fourteen underground disasters by accident. A man survives them because he knows exactly what he is doing.

I depart for Forward Station 3 within the week. The *Indomitable* is provisioned. My crew is ready. Fumbridge will move soon — the Cartolithe's thermal encoding degrades over time, or so the Kircher fragments suggest — and when he does, I will be behind him.

He nodded at me. The arrogance of it. The sheer, magnificent arrogance.

I did not nod back. He will learn what that means.

### C. Admiralty Incident Report 1928-31 (Preliminary)

*Filed 5 October 1928 by the Office of the Surveyor-General.*

**Subject:** Unscheduled departure of Crown Surveyor (Third Class) Sir P. Fumbridge from Forward Station 3.

**Summary:** On 5 October 1928, Sir Percival departed Forward Station 3 with a standard survey kit, three days' rations, a replacement theodolite (his ninth), and what he described as "a promising notion about the unmapped lateral passages south of Sector 22." He did not file expedition orders. He did not notify his section head. He left a note on his desk that read: "Gone to have a look at something. Back Tuesday. — P.F."

He was not back Tuesday.

**Action taken:** File opened. Awaiting developments.

**Priority:** Low.
