# The Bout

## I.

Here is what you need to know about the seven: not one of them had ever held an épée before September, and by December three of them could not hold anything at all. But I am getting ahead of myself. I always get ahead of myself. That is the problem with knowing how a story ends — you carry the ending into every room, set it down on every table, and no one sees it because you've arranged the flowers just so.

The athletic complex at Tidewater Community College sits at the back of the campus, past the parking lot where adjunct professors idle in their cars eating sad lunches, past the half-dead boxwoods no one has budget to replace. Building 9. The county built it in 1974 for a basketball program that folded in 1981, and since then it has served as overflow storage, a polling station, a vaccination site during the pandemic, and — for six weeks in the fall — the home gym of the only community college fencing squad in the Hampton Roads metropolitan area.

The squad had not fielded a full team in three years. The coach, Jim Pettigrew, taught Introduction to Western Civilization and Lifetime Fitness and had once, in the Ford administration, placed ninth at a Junior Olympic qualifier in foil. He was sixty-seven years old and smelled perpetually of Bengay and chalk dust, and the college kept the fencing program on its books only because Pettigrew refused to retire, and they could not fire a man who cost them nothing but floor tape and electricity. He had been given a mandate in August: field a squad of seven for the Tidewater Invitational in November, or the program would be formally dissolved. Seven warm bodies. That was all they needed. It was, the dean had said, not meant to be difficult.

Everything that matters starts with Pettigrew standing in the middle of the gymnasium floor at six-fifteen on a Tuesday evening, September the fourth, holding a clipboard and watching the door.

The first to arrive was Marion Kehoe.

You should know Marion as I knew her: fifty-four years old, five foot three, with reading glasses perpetually pushed up on her forehead like a second pair of eyes she'd given up on. She had owned Kehoe Family Pharmacy on Granby Street for twenty-two years. She had filled eleven thousand oxycodone prescriptions between 2012 and 2019 because the doctors kept writing them and the distributors kept shipping them, and when the lawsuits came, she discovered that the word *family* on a pharmacy's sign does not protect the family behind it. The business was gone. The house — her mother's house, the one with the green door — was gone. She was living in an efficiency apartment above a laundromat, working the counter at a CVS for fourteen dollars an hour, and she had come to the community college because the continuing education catalog had listed *Fencing — Beginners Welcome, No Experience Necessary, Equipment Provided* and because Marion Kehoe had not been welcomed anywhere in two years.

Her hands, though. That is what I need you to see. When she picked up the practice épée that first night, her grip settled around the handle with the same unconscious precision she'd used to count pills into amber vials for two decades — thumb and two fingers forming a cradle so steady it looked anatomical. Pettigrew noticed. I noticed. You would have noticed too, if you'd been there, if you'd been watching. I was always watching.

The second through the door was Richard Cassel, and third was his silence.

Cassel was forty-six. Disbarred eighteen months prior. The Virginia State Bar's published opinion occupied eleven single-spaced pages and used the word *misappropriation* fourteen times, which Dick Cassel had counted because counting the recurrences of the word that destroyed your life is the kind of hobby you develop when you have no clients, no golf buddies, and a wife who has changed the locks. He was tall — six two — with the build of a man who had played lacrosse at Washington and Lee and still carried the posture, if not the joy. He spoke rarely, and when he did, the words emerged pre-edited, grammatically flawless, stripped of anything that might be used against him. But his eyes were constant, lateral, assessing. In a courtroom, this had made him formidable. On the strip, it would make him something else entirely — a reader, Pettigrew would say later, a man who saw the feint inside the feint.

He would see more than feints, eventually. But that came later. That came in the dark.

Luisa Famosa arrived next, at six-twenty, out of breath, carrying a canvas tote bag printed with the logo of a public radio station. She was sixty-two. A retired letter carrier with thirty-one years on Route 47 — the Willoughby Spit loop, three and a half miles, six days a week. Her knees were a topographic map of cartilage loss. Her husband, Ernesto, had died in April of a heart attack on the toilet, which was the kind of death that is impossible to grieve with dignity because everyone, even the sympathetic ones, got a look on their face. Luisa had spent the summer sitting in her living room in the house that was now too quiet, eating Ernesto's frozen dinners in reverse chronological order — the ones he'd bought most recently first, working backward, as if she could eat her way back to a Saturday when he was still alive and complaining about the Nationals bullpen. She had come to fencing because the grief counselor said she needed *structured physical activity* and because the senior water aerobics class at the Y was full.

She was not fast. She would never be fast. But her lungs were deep from three decades of walking in wind, and her pain threshold was a canyon, and when she was moving, she did not think about the silence in her house. For Luisa, the strip was simply a length of floor where Ernesto was no more absent than anywhere else.

I need to be careful here. I need to lay them out for you with the precision they deserve, because soon enough precision will be all I have.

Tommy Ng came in at six twenty-five, and the gym got louder. Tommy was twenty-eight. He had fought eleven professional MMA bouts, winning seven, and had been, briefly, the fourth-ranked bantamweight in the mid-Atlantic regional circuit, which is exactly as unglamorous as it sounds. Then a torn ACL in a fight at the Hampton Roads Convention Center ended everything that was already ending. He worked at the Vapor Loft on Military Highway. He lived with his mother. He talked constantly — a narration of his own experience so relentless it functioned less as communication than as a pulse monitor, proof of life. He was here because a buddy from his fight camp had said that fencing was "basically sword fighting" and Tommy had said, "I could do that," which was what Tommy said about everything, and which was sometimes true and sometimes not, and the difference had shaped his entire life.

Tommy's feet were extraordinary. Even ruined, even favoring the left knee, he moved like a man who lived two inches above the floor. Pettigrew would later say that Tommy didn't advance — he *translated*, his body sliding through space with an efficiency that had nothing to do with fencing and everything to do with ten thousand hours of learning to close distance before someone punched him in the face.

He would close distance one more time, in the dark, and this time there would be no referee.

But I promised. The tournament first.

Ansel Rowe arrived at six-thirty exactly, because Ansel arrived everywhere at the exact time he said he would. He was thirty-three. Eight years in the Army, two deployments, a general discharge under other-than-honorable conditions for an incident at Fort Bragg that he would describe only as "a disagreement about who was sleeping," which could have meant anything and, it turned out, meant everything. He was lean and quiet with the particular stillness of a person who has learned that the body is an instrument of damage and has decided, painstakingly, not to play. He took a job at Goodwill sorting donations. He attended a men's group on Thursday nights at the Unitarian church. He had come to fencing because the men's group facilitator — a gentle, bearded man with a voice like a warm bath — had told him that martial disciplines could be *reparative*, could teach the body that controlled aggression was not a contradiction.

Ansel did not like the word *aggression*. He did not like many words. But he liked the mask. He told me that once — just once, in the hallway, on a night I am not ready to describe — that the mask was the first thing he'd put over his face since coming home that didn't make him feel like he was hiding. It made him feel like he was *allowed.*

And then there was Day.

Deshawn Watkins, seventeen, but people called him Day because that was what his first foster mother had called him, the one he'd liked, the one who'd kept him for three years before her husband got transferred to Pensacola and the caseworker said *it's temporary* in the same voice caseworkers always say *it's temporary*, which is to say: without conviction, without cruelty, and without truth. Day was in his fifth placement. He was aging out of the system in nine months. He had no criminal record, which in the context of his life was roughly the equivalent of an Olympic medal, and he knew it, and the knowing sat behind his eyes like a small, controlled fire. He was wiry — five eight, one-thirty — with reflexes that belonged to a different species. He had signed up for the class because it fulfilled a PE credit he needed to graduate, and because the other option was bowling.

Day smiled a particular way — not wide, not performative, but with a slow, almost geological shift of his features, as if happiness had to pass through several layers of sediment before it reached the surface. It was a smile you had to earn, and once you'd earned it, you felt like you'd been given something you didn't deserve. I saw it perhaps a dozen times in six weeks.

The last time was on a Tuesday. I will tell you about that Tuesday.

I will tell you about all of it.

But not yet.

---

Seven people. A bankrupt pharmacist, a disbarred attorney, a retired postal worker, an ex-fighter, a veteran, a foster kid, and one more — one more person standing at the edge of the gymnasium floor on September the fourth, watching Pettigrew hold up a practice épée and explain, in his reedy, patient voice, that a fencing bout is not a fight but a *conversation*, that the blade is not a weapon but a *question*, and that the object is not to destroy your opponent but to *find* them.

Seven people under fluorescent lights that buzzed at a frequency I can still hear when the house is quiet.

They were not athletes. They were not warriors. They were a pharmacist who couldn't sleep, a lawyer who couldn't speak, a widow who couldn't stop eating frozen dinners, a fighter who couldn't fight, a soldier who couldn't be still, and a kid who couldn't stay. They were the last people you would ever assemble for any purpose, which is of course exactly why they worked.

"All right," Pettigrew said, pushing his glasses up his nose with the pommel of the practice blade. "Let's see what we've got."

What they had was nothing. What they became was something else.
