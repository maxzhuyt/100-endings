Q: State your full name for the record.

A: Define "aware."

Q: When were you told, or when did you come to understand, that—

A: I understood from approximately birth that my uncle intended to breach that facility. As did every living Pardieu. As, I suspect, did the facility itself. This was not a secret. It was closer to a weather pattern. If you grow up in that house you understand that the house tilts fourteen inches to the southwest and that one day Beaumont is going to break into the vault. These are structural facts. You don't become "aware" of them. You acclimate.

Though if you're asking me to identify the moment a thirty-five-year atmospheric condition became a specific plan, I'd note that you're asking me to do the thing my uncle could never do. Beaumont did not distinguish between wanting something and planning something. They were the same muscle.

## II.

Beaumont Pardieu is sweating and the sweat has a thesis.

The thesis is this: that the Federal Reserve satellite vault at the end of Parish Road 39, decommissioned in 1994 but maintained — maintained, he will tell you, oh Lord will he tell you about the maintenance — under a rolling federal facilities contract that nobody in Washington can explain or cancel, is breachable. Has always been breachable. Was *almost* breached, in fact, by his father, Aurelien "Rel" Pardieu, on the night of November 11, 1989, and the only reason it was not breached on that night was that the secondary door's electromagnetic locking mechanism operated on a frequency that Rel did not anticipate because the Corps of Engineers had, six weeks prior, without public notice or documentation available to any citizen whose name was not a senator's, upgraded the lock's oscillation pattern from — and here Beaumont taps a diagram so old it has developed the sepia tones of a daguerreotype — from *this* to *this*, a difference of eleven megahertz, which is the difference between an open door and a dead father, and if you think those two outcomes don't hinge on a number that small, you have never understood the fundamental nature of systems, which is that they will kill you on a technicality and call it security.

He says all of this in one breath because Beaumont Pardieu oxygenates through language, not lungs, and by the time the sentence ends he is removing a crawfish tail from a newspaper spread and pushing it aside to expose a schematic that is either a wiring diagram for a GE Industrial Series 7 electromagnetic lock or a map of his father's circulatory system, and to Beaumont there is no meaningful difference.

The table is plywood on sawhorses. The sawhorses are borrowed. The plywood is the same plywood that once served as the headboard of the bed in which Beaumont was either conceived or born, depending on which version of Odile's story you last heard, and either way it has been water-stained, beer-stained, and crawfish-stained into a condition that a marine biologist might classify as its own ecosystem. On it: the schematics, three cell phones (two functional), a nine-volt battery that Beaumont periodically touches as though checking a pulse, a rosary that belongs to Odile and that Beaumont will not admit he has taken from her bedside table, seven cans of Abita in various states of completion, a voltage meter, a pencil with no eraser, and a composition notebook in which someone — Beaumont's father, Beaumont himself, the handwriting analysis would have been a deposition exhibit unto itself — has written, in lettering so precise it looks mechanical:

*THEY LEFT THE DOOR HALF-DONE. ALL DOORS ARE HALF-DONE. FINISH IT.*

Beaumont reads this sentence every morning the way other men read scripture. He reads it now, one finger tracing the letters, and his eyes fill and he says to no one, or possibly to his niece Lark, who is sitting on a cooler in the corner looking at her phone with the expression of someone performing the role of a person looking at her phone:

"The gauge is wrong."

Lark does not look up. "Which gauge."

"The gauge. *The* gauge. I ordered fourteen-gauge stranded copper, THHN, rated for the humidity. What they sent is twelve."

"So it's thicker."

"It's *thicker*." He says this the way a surgeon might say *the patient is awake*. "Twelve-gauge in a conduit junction meant for fourteen means the bend radius exceeds the housing clearance by—" He stops. He picks up the pencil with no eraser. He puts it down. He picks up a crawfish head, looks through it like a telescope at something specific and distant, and begins to cry.

He cries quietly, which is the only quiet thing Beaumont Pardieu does. The tears move through the sweat without disrupting it, the way a creek merges with a river of similar temperature. He does not wipe them. He sets the crawfish head down among the schematics, where it bleeds a faint orange oil into the penciled lines of the vault's secondary corridor, and for a moment the diagram looks like something a body produced rather than a mind — an X-ray, an ultrasound, a map of something alive and inflamed.

"Uncle Beau."

"The junction box," he says, "is four and a quarter inches. The bend radius on twelve-gauge at the angle I need is four and a half. You understand? A quarter inch. A quarter inch is what killed him and a quarter inch is what's going to — I can make it work. I need to make it work. If I shave the housing. If I—"

"Uncle Beau, the crawfish is on your diagram."

"If I shave the housing down on the interior lip, I can get the bend to clear, but if I shave too much the gasket won't seat, and if the gasket won't seat then I have a humidity breach in the conduit and the splice corrodes inside of—"

"Eleven minutes," says a voice from the next room, or from 1989, or from somewhere in the house's interior that is difficult to locate because the house's interior has been slowly reshaping itself toward the swamp for twenty years and rooms that were once discrete have begun to communicate at odd angles, doorframes warped into parallelograms, walls leaning into confessional intimacy. The voice is Odile's. "Eleven minutes is what they told me. Between when the secondary door engaged and when the oxygen was no longer sufficient. Eleven minutes. They said it like it was a lot. Like eleven minutes was generous. Like the door had done him a *favor*."

Beaumont stops crying. He picks up the nine-volt battery. He holds it against his tongue. His eyes widen — not at the voltage, which is trivial, but at the taste, which he once told Lark is the taste of every system that ever kept a charge in the dark, and which she understood, even at fourteen, was the taste of a door that did not open.

"Mama," he says, toward the voice. "I'm working."

"You're sweating," says Odile, from her room or hallway or decade. "Your father used to sweat like that. Before a job he'd sweat through the sheets. I'd wake up in a lake. I told him, Rel, if you're going to drown me, at least do it in the Gulf where there's a view."

Lark puts her phone down. Picks it up. Puts it down. She is twenty-six and the possessor of a face that the adaptive security system at the end of Parish Road 39 has been indexing since she was nineteen and opened her first social media account, though she does not know this yet, or knows it the way everyone knows it — diffusely, as a condition of modernity, the weather of being watched — without understanding that in her case the watching has a specificity, an almost familial attention, a system that has consumed her face as devotedly as any grandmother with a photo album.

"The gauge will work," Beaumont says, no longer crying, because he has passed through the crying into the territory beyond it, which is the territory of decision, which is the only territory in which Beaumont Pardieu is not ridiculous. "I'll make it work. Daddy made the 1989 run with sixteen-gauge household wire and a pair of needlenose pliers and he got the primary door open, didn't he? He got the primary open with *sixteen-gauge*. I have twelve. I am two steps better than the man and I have—" He stops. He counts silently on his left hand. Four fingers. An argument arranged on his knuckles. "I have thirty-five years of knowing what he didn't know. I know where the second door catches. I know the cycle interval on the mag-lock. I know that the Corps doesn't inspect the humidity seals between October and February because the maintenance contract specifies biannual inspection and the fiscal year—"

He goes on. He will go on. He is going on right now, in this present tense that is the only tense Beaumont trusts, and outside the house is sinking at a rate of approximately three-quarters of an inch per year, which means it has dropped over a foot since Beaumont began planning, and maybe that's fine, maybe that's the right direction: the vault is underground, the house is heading there too, and eventually the whole Pardieu operation will simply arrive at its destination through subsidence.

The crawfish came from a neighbor named Trosclair who raises them in a pond behind his mother-in-law's doublewide and who does not know that the fourteen pounds of boiled mudbugs he traded for Beaumont's help with a alternator are fueling an operation against a federal facility. Trosclair would find this either thrilling or deeply concerning, and in Plaquemines Parish those are not always distinguishable from each other.

## III.

This is how Odile tells it, though "tells" is the wrong verb for what Odile does with the past, the way "weather" is the wrong word for a hurricane — technically accurate and completely inadequate:

Rel had beautiful hands.

She begins here every time. Not with the vault, not with the plan, not with the eleven minutes. With his hands. Because his hands were the part of him that entered the door first and the part of him she understood best, having lived under them and beside them for nineteen years, and because the hands are the part of the story that belongs to her and not to the record, not to the coroner, not to the lawyers, not to Beaumont's schematics or the government's reports or the sealed files she was never allowed to read. The hands are hers. She will start where she pleases.

Rel had beautiful hands, the fingers long and blunt-tipped, mechanic's hands that could have been a pianist's if anyone in Plaquemines Parish had ever seen a reason to put a piano within a mile of a Pardieu, and on the night of November 11, 1989 — or November 12, because it was past midnight, she's certain of that, or fairly certain, or certain in the way that you are certain of something you have remembered so many times that the memory has become its own object, distinct from the event, a carving worn smooth — on that night, Rel's hands were steady.

She mentions this because people assume they weren't. People assume that a man breaking into a federal vault would be shaking, nervous, would fumble. Rel did not fumble. Rel's hands moved over the primary lock housing the way they moved over her body: with a fluency that was not gentle, exactly, but *knowledgeable*, a touch so informed by repetition that it had become a kind of conversation, his fingers and the metal exchanging information too fast for language. She watched from the vehicle — a 1979 Ford Econoline, brown, with a replacement quarter panel in primer gray that made it look like a dog with a skin condition — she watched from the vehicle and she could see his hands in the work light and they were the only calm thing in the night.
