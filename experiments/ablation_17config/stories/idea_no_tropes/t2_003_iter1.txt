# The Worst One, and Also the Funnier One

The friendship began, as certain friendships must, on fire.

This was 1994, or possibly 1995 — Mick always said '94 and Ray always said '95 and neither of them could be trusted with calendar years, addresses, women's middle names, or the distinction between "I was invited" and "I showed up" — on the set of a film whose title no one involved would later cop to, a straight-to-video apocalypse romance shot in eighteen days outside Palmdale. The fire gag was the climax. Two soldiers engulfed in burning fuel stumble from a helicopter wreck, flail picturesquely, and collapse. Work for two stunt performers who had never met and would, according to the call sheet, never need to meet again. One of them was a wide-shouldered Irish kid from Reseda named Mick Callahan who had been doing stunt work for three years without breaking anything he couldn't tape over. The other was a shorter, quieter man from no specific town named Ray Pulaski, who had been in the business longer and had a walk that already favored his left knee in cold weather. The safety coordinator was a man named Dennis who was, at the moment the accelerant was poured, in the portable toilet behind Craft Services, reading a Dean Koontz novel with the serenity of a man who believed nothing important ever happened on time.

What happened next happened in approximately four seconds. The accelerant — a gel compound that was supposed to burn at a controlled rate along taped marks on the ground — did not burn at a controlled rate along taped marks on the ground. It spread. Not metaphorically, not gradually: it spread the way a rumor spreads at a funeral, all at once and in every direction. Mick, who had rehearsed a stagger-left, staggered left and found that left was also on fire. Ray, who had rehearsed a stagger-right, staggered right and directly into Mick, whom he had never touched and whose body, at this point, was producing a quantity of flame that made him resemble less a man and more a briefly animate bonfire.

They collided.

Now, if you have never seen two burning men collide at speed, you may imagine something dramatic: a fireball, an explosion, the kind of thing the film was presumably trying to capture. What actually happened was closer to what happens when two drunk uncles try to hug at a barbecue. There was a tangle of limbs. There was a confused, rolling, downward trajectory. Mick's arms, which were trying to protect his face, ended up wrapped around Ray's torso. Ray's legs, which were trying to run, ended up kicking dirt onto Mick's back. They rolled. They smothered each other's flames with what the incident report would later describe as "inadvertent bilateral compression" and what Mick would later describe as "the worst hug I ever got and also the best one."

They lay in the charred dirt. Their eyebrows were gone. Their nomex undersuits had held — mostly — though Mick would carry a shine-slick scar on his left forearm for the rest of his life and Ray's neck would do a thing in photographs that made people ask if he'd been in a war. The PA who reached them first, a twenty-two-year-old woman named Kristin whose subsequent career in producing was built on the steely calm she discovered she possessed in this moment, thought they were seizing. They were laughing. They were laughing so hard that Mick aspirated a piece of charred shirt collar and had to be rolled onto his side, which made Ray laugh harder, which made Mick, choking, laugh harder, which made Kristin — who would in later years tell this story at dinner parties with the precision of a woman who knew she had witnessed something — say into her walkie: "I need medical, and I honestly don't know for what."

Dennis came out of the portable toilet. He still had the Dean Koontz novel in his hand.

They were inseparable after that. Not in the romantic-comedy sense, not in the way that word is usually deployed to describe people who choose each other — they were inseparable in the way that certain compounds are inseparable, in the way that a scar is inseparable from the skin it's on. They worked together. They requested each other. They developed a physical vocabulary so fluent that a second-unit director once described watching them rehearse a fight sequence as "like watching two guys share one nervous system, except the nervous system has a drinking problem." Mick was the talker, the schemer, the one who entered rooms as though the rooms had been waiting. Ray was the driver, the technician, the one who could read a stunt rig the way certain old men can read weather. Together they were one complete human being who happened to take up two seats at a bar.

Twenty-some years later — joints grinding, insurance premiums astronomical, Mick's back a topographical map of scars and Ray's left knee a thing he spoke to in the mornings like a resentful pet — they stole a car.

The car was a 1972 Chevrolet Chevelle Super Sport, and to describe it merely is to do it a disservice, because this car was not merely a car the way that a cathedral is not merely a building. It was Cranberry Red, the factory color, not repainted but *preserved*, the clear coat maintained with a devotion that would embarrass most religions. The interior was black leather, original, with a grain so fine it felt less like animal skin and more like something whispered. The engine was a 454 cubic-inch LS5 big block that, when started, produced a sound which began somewhere in the lizard brain and ended somewhere in the groin, a sound that made dogs sit down and grown men go quiet in gas station parking lots. It smelled, inside, of leather and old warmth and something faintly mineral, like a creek bed in summer, and riding in it did something to the passage of time, slowed it, thickened it, made the present tense feel upholstered.

It belonged to a man named Keeler.

Theodore "Teddy" Keeler was a producer of the species that has existed in Hollywood since the first camera was unboxed and someone nearby realized that the man holding it might be made to feel smaller. He was not the worst producer in Los Angeles. He was not the most vindictive, nor the most petty, nor the most punishing in his grudges — there were several whose grudges had outlived the objects of those grudges, who had continued making phone calls about dead men, whose capacity for reprisal had achieved a kind of geological patience that Keeler could only aspire to. What Keeler was, specifically, was *mean about money*. He was the kind of man who would negotiate a stunt performer's day rate downward by twelve dollars and then spend forty minutes of a production meeting explaining why the twelve dollars mattered, not because he needed the twelve dollars — he drove the Chevelle to set and parked it in a reserved space marked with his name and the name of the production and, beneath that, the word RESERVED in letters large enough to be read from the craft services tent — but because the twelve-dollar reduction was a proof. Of what, exactly, was never clear. Perhaps of the natural order. Perhaps of his own position within it. He had a face like a fist that had been asked to smile, and when he spoke to below-the-line crew, he deployed a particular register of politeness that was to actual courtesy what a decoy is to a duck.

Mick Callahan had worked for Keeler four times. Each time, Keeler had shaved his rate. Each time, Keeler had found a way to attribute some damage or delay to the stunt team, to deduct something, to make the math come out in a way that proved whatever Keeler needed it to prove. The fourth time — the last time — there had been a gag involving a staircase, a breakaway railing, and a fall onto mats that Keeler had insisted could be thinner than specified because, as he had explained to the stunt coordinator with the patience of a man speaking to a slow child, "I've been in this business thirty years and no one has ever needed the thick mats." Ray landed on the thin mats. Ray's knee — already a monument to accumulated insult — made a sound. The sound was the kind you hear once and catalogue permanently, the way the brain catalogues the smell of electrical fire or the noise of a car accident heard from inside. Mick was across the set. Mick heard it. Ray was on the ground doing the thing professionals do, which is not scream, which is breathe, which is go somewhere inside yourself and wait, and Mick walked — did not run, walked, which everyone who saw it remembered because the walking was somehow worse than running would have been — to where Keeler was standing and said something that no two witnesses reported the same way but that everyone agreed contained the words "your mats" and "my friend" and a closing sentiment of such anatomical creativity that the script supervisor wrote it down.

They took the Chevelle on a Tuesday.

They took it from the studio lot where Keeler kept it on the days he wanted to be seen arriving in it. They took it at 11:40 in the morning, which was mid-production, which was brazen, which was — and this is the part that matters — not a plan Mick had thought through so much as a plan Mick had *said*, once, in a bar, in the way you say things in bars: *You know what would be funny*, and Ray had looked at him the way Ray always looked at him when he said things like that, which was with an expression that contained equal parts recognition and surrender, like a man watching a wave arrive that he has known about for some time and has elected not to outrun. He just nodded. Mick said it would be funny and Ray had never once in his life refused Mick and so on a Tuesday morning they drove Keeler's Chevelle off the lot with a wave to the gate guard — who knew them, who had known them for years, who assumed that anyone waving from the interior of Keeler's Chevelle had Keeler's permission because who in God's name would steal a car from a man like Keeler — and by noon they were on the highway and the Chevelle was making its sound, that deep-in-the-engine sound, and Mick had his window down and his ruined arm in the wind and he was laughing.

Ray drove. Ray always drove. Mick had opinions about driving — loudly held, historically inaccurate, meteorologically irrelevant opinions — but Ray drove, because Ray's hands on a wheel did something that Mick's hands could not, which was *listen*. Ray could feel a car the way he could feel a stunt rig: structurally, intuitively, as a system of tolerances and limits. The Chevelle, under Ray's hands, did not merely move. It *corresponded*. And Mick sat in the passenger seat, knees wide, one hand on the dash, talking. Mick was always talking. He was telling a story about a woman in Tucson who had or had not been married to a man who had or had not been a rodeo veterinarian, and the story had reached the point where the veterinarian's dog had become involved in a way that required Mick to use both hands to demonstrate the spatial relationship between the dog, a screen door, and what Mick described as "the largest domestic cat I have ever personally been afraid of," and Ray was watching the road and the white lines and the desert that was opening around them like something being unfolded and he was smiling in a way he only ever smiled in Mick's company, a smile that used his whole face, even the parts that usually

But there is also this.

The Chevelle on the same highway, the same day or a day like it, the same hour by the angle of sun on the hood, and Ray is driving. His hands are on the wheel in the way described, listening, corresponding. The desert is the same desert. The white lines are the same white lines. And Ray is talking. He is mid-sentence — something about Tucson, about a woman, about a veterinarian or the concept of a veterinarian — and his right hand comes off the wheel and gestures toward the passenger seat the way you gesture toward a person who has just said something you need to correct. "No," Ray says, "no, that's not — she wasn't his wife, she was — you're doing the thing again where you—" and he stops, and he adjusts the rearview mirror, a small tilt, the way you check on someone in the back seat, and his eyes move to the mirror and stay there for a half-second longer than driving allows and then return to the road. The passenger seat has a dip in its leather. A wear pattern. The kind of impression a body leaves in a place it has occupied for a long time. Ray signals a lane change, checks the mirror again — the side mirror this time, the one nearest the passenger side — and murmurs "You see that?" to the cabin, to the air inside the cabin, which is warm and smells of leather and old warmth and something faintly mineral. A truck passes on the left. Its wake rocks the Chevelle gently and Ray steadies the wheel and says, quieter, "Big one," the way you say it to the person next to you, the person who is also watching.

The highway is long. The highway is very long and the desert on either side of it is the color of lions and the sky is the kind of blue that feels aggressive, and Ray drives with both hands now, and in the passenger seat there is an impression in the leather the shape of a man who is not small.

—and Mick was saying, "—the point is, the cat had *tenure*, Ray, the cat had been there longer than the marriage," and Ray said, "Nobody uses the word tenure like that," and Mick said, "Academics use it like that," and Ray said, "You're not an academic," and Mick, with a magnanimity that was indistinguishable from condescension, said, "I am when I'm telling this story," and the Chevelle carried them forward into the long country and the sun was behind them and their shadows ran ahead of the car like scouts sent into territory that had not yet decided whether to be kind.

They stopped at a motel.

It was the kind of motel that exists in a specific American ecological niche: situated between a highway off-ramp and a landscape so featureless that the motel itself became a kind of event, an architectural announcement that human beings had once believed this location merited a place to sleep. It had a name — the Desert Ray Motor Lodge, which Mick found cosmically significant and which he repeated fourteen times between the highway and the parking lot, each time with increasing emphasis on the word *Ray*, as though the universe had built this motel specifically to honor his friend, and what kind of man would Ray be to refuse such an honor, and shouldn't they stay here, wouldn't it be almost *rude* not to stay here, and Ray pulled into the lot and said nothing because saying nothing was its own language between them and in that language it meant *fine*.

The carport was the problem.

The carport was a structure of aluminum poles and corrugated roofing that extended from the motel's front office over approximately four parking spaces, and it had been built at a height that accommodated, with reasonable clearance, the passenger vehicles of the early 1980s. The 1972 Chevelle SS, with its hood ornament and its posture of mechanical self-regard, was not what anyone in the early 1980s had been thinking about. It was close. It was close enough that Mick, leaning out the passenger window to gauge the clearance, said, "You've got it, you've got inches," and the thing about Mick saying *you've got it* was that it engendered in the listener a reckless, baseless, historically unsupported confidence, because Mick said it the way a man says something he believes, and Mick believed everything he said in the moment of saying it, and this was either his greatest gift or the source of every injury Ray had ever sustained in his company, and usually both.

Ray pulled forward. The roof of the Chevelle kissed the corrugated aluminum with a sound like a request. Ray stopped. Mick said, "Commit, commit, commit," which was what Mick always said when a stunt was going wrong and the only option was to go through it rather than stop.
