# The Ones Who Stay for the Polyp

## I.

Picture, if you can bear it, a Tuesday.

Not any Tuesday but the most Tuesday of Tuesdays, a day so committed to its own unremarkable position in the week that the fluorescent lights in the ceiling of GastroPlus Outpatient Endoscopy Center seemed to buzz at a frequency calibrated specifically for the middle-distance stare. The building had been, in a former life, a LensCrafters. You could still see the ghost of the old signage on the exterior stucco if you caught it in early light—which Lila did, every morning, pulling into the lot at 6:40 a.m. in her ten-year-old Volvo with the inspection sticker she kept forgetting to renew.

She would tell you—if you asked, and even if you didn't, because Lila Fontaine had the particular affliction of people who have thought too carefully about their own motivations—that the clinic had a beauty to it. She would say this not defensively but with the measured conviction of someone who had once delivered a forty-minute conference paper titled "The Aesthetics of the Internal: Endoscopic Vision and the Phenomenology of the Unseen Body." She would want you to understand the room where the procedures happened: the Olympus monitors glowing their cool procedural blue, the coiled insufflation tubing, the bite blocks lined up in their sterile packets like tiny plastic mouths waiting to be filled with other mouths. She would want you to see the peristaltic pump and hear its rhythmic suck, which was, she would argue, no less a music than the mechanical click of a loom. She would want you to notice the warming blankets, stacked and humming in their cabinet, because she believed—truly, not performatively—that a warm blanket placed on a sedated person's body was one of the few unambiguous goods left in American medicine.

So let us say there was beauty. Let us grant it.

She entered through the back that morning, as she always did, swiping her badge against the reader that had never once worked on the first try, then shouldering the door with the practiced hip-check of someone who has done this four hundred times and stopped counting. The hallway smelled of the particular institutional cocktail she had long since stopped consciously registering: betadine, the floral ghost of yesterday's floor wax, and something faintly intestinal that she could not, in honesty, attribute to any specific source. It was the smell of the specialty. It preceded you at parties. It was the reason her college roommate had once said, memorably and without cruelty, *You chose the one thing no one wants to talk about at dinner, and then you became the kind of person who talks about it at dinner.*

She had. She absolutely had.

The prep room was already alive with the soft bureaucratic hum of a day assembling itself. Carmen, the head nurse, was taping procedure consents to clipboards with the grim efficiency of a woman who had long ago stopped believing that patients read them. Two of the four recovery bays had their curtains pulled—early arrivals, already gowned, already embarrassed, already clutching the plastic bag containing the clothing they'd removed and the dignity they were trying not to think about. The anesthesiologist, Dr. Pete Wynn, was eating a KIND bar over the Pyxis machine, dropping granola crumbs into the narcotic drawer, which he did every single morning and for which he had never once been reprimanded, because Pete had the unmolested confidence of a man whose services were in desperately short supply.

There were eight colonoscopies on the board. Four for Lila. Four for Mack.

Mack Oberlin's name was already written in his handwriting on the whiteboard—aggressive, slanting, the letters of a man who had once told her, during their fellowship at Johns Hopkins, that he'd chosen gastroenterology because "the boards are easy, the hours are humane, and nobody's dying on you at 3 a.m. unless they were already dying when they got there." He had said this at a bar in Fells Point, over a plate of wings so aggressively sauced that his lips had gone a shade of red she later, involuntarily, associated with a Grade C hemorrhoid. She remembered hating him in that moment with a clarity she usually reserved for academic rivals and slow drivers. She remembered, too, the small flare of something that was not hate when he'd looked at her and added, grinning: "And the money's stupid. The money is *profoundly* stupid."

He wasn't wrong about the money. Or he wouldn't have been, in some other era, at some other kind of practice. GastroPlus was not where the money was stupid. GastroPlus was where the money was anxious and cautious and frequently denied by insurance. The clinic served a semi-rural catchment area south of the interstate where screening rates lagged twenty points behind the state average and where a not-insignificant number of patients arrived for their first colonoscopy already symptomatic, already overdue, already carrying in their colons the quiet, florid evidence of years of avoidance. Lila had come here on purpose. Mack had come here because he'd been asked to leave his previous practice in Bethesda for reasons he characterized as "political" and that Lila understood, from the gossip networks that constitute the true circulatory system of any medical specialty, to involve an operating room prank with a specimen jar that was either very funny or very not funny depending on whom you asked.

They were not friends. They were two gastroenterologists in a four-physician practice who had been yoked together by the same fellowship program, the same board certification, the same eight-hour procedural days, and now—as of six weeks ago, when the email from the American College of Gastroenterology had arrived—the same ambition: a live-procedure demonstration slot at the National Conference.

In Honolulu.

In January.

Before eleven hundred of their peers and a livestream audience that could, according to the conference promotional materials, "ichieve maximal visibility for the selected endoscopist's institutional brand."

Lila wanted it the way a person wants something they have earned in their mind so completely that the actual conferral feels like a formality. Mack wanted it the way a person wants to stand in front of a crowd and be publicly, undeniably good at something. These are not the same desire. They produce, in proximity, a friction that is very nearly thermal.

Perhaps you doubt that two adults can care this much about a conference slot.

Fair enough.

But you have not spent nine years inside the colon. You have not built a life around the organ the world finds funniest, the one that makes your mother's friends change the subject, the one that ensures every cocktail party introduction ends with someone making the same joke about "the scenic route." You have not528 dedicated a decade of your professional life to a thing the culture would prefer not to exist, and then been offered, once, the chance to do that thing in front of the only people on earth who understand that what you do is not a joke, is in fact a species of vision, is in fact the daily practiced art of seeing what the body hides—and being asked to do it in Honolulu, in winter, with a microphone.

You would want it. Trust me. You would want it badly.

## II.

Lila's first patient was a fifty-four-year-old woman named Deirdre Kolb who had prepped beautifully—"like a dream," Carmen said, reviewing the intake, which meant that the liter of polyethylene glycol solution Deirdre had consumed the night before had done its full purgatorial work, and the visual field would be clean. This mattered more than the layperson understood. A poorly prepped colon was a betrayal of the highest order: you could miss things. You could thread the scope through 150 centimeters of large intestine and see nothing, not because nothing was there, but because the residue, the sludge, the body's refusal to fully empty itself, had drawn a curtain over the mucosal lining like a stage-hand hiding the set.

Lila gowned. She gloved—double, left hand, because she'd developed a latex sensitivity three years ago and wore nitrile underneath like a secret. She nodded to Pete, who depressed the propofol syringe with the casual authority of a man stamping a parking ticket, and Deirdre's eyes went glassy, then closed, and her jaw slackened against the bite block, and she was somewhere else.

And Lila was inside.

The scope advanced. The monitor bloomed.

She had said once, at the conference paper that had earned her a modest reputation and an immodest amount of departmental side-eye, that the endoscopic image was "the body's most honest self-portrait." She had meant it. The colon did not pose. The colon did not suck in its stomach or choose its angle. The colon was what it was: the pink, wet, folded, vascular truth of a person, pleated and glistening, pulsing with the slow contractile rhythm that moved the world's food from meaning to meaninglessness. She had written that sentence in her paper too. Her department chair had underlined it in red and written, in the margin, *Lila, this is a GI journal.*

The scope slid through the sigmoid and she felt it—the slight resistance at the splenic flexure, the turn that required the particular wrist-rotation she had practiced ten thousand times, the move that separated the competent from the gifted, and which she executed now with a fluency that was, she permitted herself to think, beautiful.

And there it was.

Not a polyp. Not a lesion. Not anything clinical.

A smell.

The prep solution had a smell, even through the sealed system, even through the masks—a faint, sweet, chemical tang that was not itself a memory but was the key to one, and the memory it opened was this:

Her mother, Jean Fontaine, standing in the bathroom doorway of their house in Metairie, Louisiana, holding a small spiral-bound notebook in which she had recorded, in mechanical pencil, the date, time, and—Lila could still not think about this without a contraction somewhere behind her sternum—*character* of every bowel movement the family produced. Not just her own. Everyone's. Lila's. Her brother Todd's. Their father's, until the divorce, after which his column simply stopped, a white space in the notebook that Jean never acknowledged and that Lila understood, even at nine, was a grief too specific to name.
