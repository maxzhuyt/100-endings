# The Suppressed Manuscript

## Being the Private Account of Sir Arthur Conan Doyle, Kt., DL
## Written January–March 1930, Windlesham, Crowborough
## With Instructions That It Not Be Opened Before the Death of All Named Herein

---

## I.

I have been wrong about a great many things.

This is not the confession it sounds. Any man who reaches my age without compiling a magnificent catalogue of errors has not been paying sufficient attention to his own life. I was wrong about the Cottingley photographs, as the world has been delighted to remind me at every opportunity and in every broadsheet that could fit the words *credulous* and *elderly* into a single headline. I was wrong about Phineas Poole, the Cardiff materialisation medium, who turned out to be manipulating a system of pulleys with his feet, which I maintain was at least athletically impressive. I was wrong about the war — not the necessity of it, which I still defend, but the duration, the cost, the specific and irreversible nature of what it took from me, from my family, from a generation that entered the century upright and was returned to it in pieces.

I was wrong, too, about certain matters of the heart — about the sharp words I failed to soften, the gentle ones I failed to speak, the letter I should have written to Jean before she was too ill to read it. These I will not catalogue. They are between myself and God, and God has been patient with me, and I trust will continue to be.

But I was not wrong about the central thing.

The veil between this world and the next is real. It is thin in places and thick in others. It can be felt by those with the sensitivity to feel it, and it can be torn by those with the arrogance to pull at it without understanding what holds it together or what waits on the far side not with patience, precisely, but with something worse than patience. Something that does not need to wait because it does not experience time as a constraint. Something that waits the way water waits above a crack in a dam, which is to say it does not wait at all; it simply is, and the moment the architecture fails, it moves.

I have spent the final years of my public life arguing this in lecture halls and drawing rooms and the letters pages of newspapers that printed my claims only because my name still sold copies. I argued with the passionate imprecision of a man who had felt the truth in his bones but could not — can not, even now, with my pen in my hand and the winter light coming grey through the study window and Jean's garden gone to frost beneath it — find the language that makes the feeling communicable to those who have not felt it.

I argued, and was mocked, and argued further, and was mocked further, and gradually the argument ceased to be about evidence and became about character: mine versus his. The credulous old Spiritualist versus the magnificent rationalist. Doyle the dupe, Houdini the debunker. The newspapers loved it. Two famous men fighting about ghosts — it sold better than fiction, and I should know, having manufactured a fair quantity of fiction in my time.

Harry loved it too. He would never have admitted it, but he needed the quarrel as much as the quarrel needed him. He was a performer above all things, and every performer requires an opponent, and I was his — the lumbering, well-meaning, publicly idiotic foil against whose earnest gullibility his brilliance could shine. I understood this. I even forgave it, mostly, on most days, in the way one forgives a friend whose particular cruelty is that he does not recognize it as cruelty because he believes with his whole heart that he is doing you a kindness.

What I did not understand — what I could not have understood, because the understanding came only with the events I am about to describe — is that our quarrel was not a quarrel at all. It was a mechanism. We were two poles of a single argument, and the argument was structural, and the structure held something in place, and when one pole overwhelmed the other — when Harry, in December of 1924, did what he did in Boston — the structure failed.

I am going to tell you what happened.

I have not told anyone. I have written no account. I have given no deathbed interview, though several have been requested with varying degrees of tact. I have kept silence for five years, and the silence has not been noble; it has been cowardly, and the cowardice has curdled inside me into something that feels, in my bad moments, like complicity.

I am telling it now because I am dying. I can feel it — not dramatically, not with the gothic certainty of a Poe narrator who clutches his breast and declaims; I simply know, the way one knows the season has turned before any single leaf falls, that I have weeks left rather than months. And the silence has become heavier than the telling.

You should know several things about me before we begin.

I am not a reliable narrator in the conventional sense. I have spent decades arguing for the reality of phenomena that respectable people consider nonsense. My judgment has been publicly questioned by better minds than mine, and some of those minds were correct to question it.

But I was *there*. I saw what I saw. I saw what it did to the mediums, and I saw what it tried to do to Harry, and I saw what Bess — Bess, who deserves a better chronicler than a tired old man writing by lamplight, but who will have to make do with me — I saw what Bess did, and what it cost her, and how she did it with no more ceremony than a woman locking her own front door, because that is what she *was*, and the world never understood it, and Harry himself only understood it in the way that a man who has been breathing all his life understands air, which is to say not at all until it is taken from him.

The date is December 1924. The coldest winter in a decade: pipes bursting in tenements from Roxbury to the Back Bay, the Charles frozen solid enough that boys skated on it at night and their mothers did not sleep until they heard the door. Harry was forty-nine years old and at the absolute zenith of his powers, which in his case meant that he was touring the Eastern Seaboard with a show that was half magic act, half evangelical crusade against the Spiritualist movement, and selling out every hall he played. He had published *A Magician Among the Spirits* that year. He had testified before the United States Congress. He had made it his life's mission to prove that every medium, every table-rapper, every trumpeting cabinet and floating tambourine was a fraud perpetrated upon the grieving, and he was right about nearly all of them, and the question that will haunt me until I draw my last breath — which will be soon, and which I no longer dread — is whether *nearly all* was ever the relevant standard, or whether the single exception he refused to concede was the one that mattered more than all the rest combined.

The story begins with a séance in Boston.

It begins with Harry doing what he always did — tearing the machinery apart, showing the audience the gears, basking in the roar of their approval.

Except this time, something heard him. And what heard him did not roar. It simply turned, the way a vast thing turns when a small sound reaches it across a great distance, and it began, with an attention that I can only describe as *loving* in the way that a river loves a valley — inevitably, erosively, without anything that a human being would recognize as choice — to move toward the sound.

Toward Harry.

I wish I could tell you he survived what happened next. The historical record will tell you that he died on the thirty-first of October, 1926, of peritonitis secondary to a ruptured appendix, precipitated by a blow to the abdomen delivered by an overeager university student in Montreal. The historical record is not wrong about any of those facts. But facts are not the same as truth, and truth is not the same as the whole truth, and the whole truth is what I have spent five years failing to speak and am now, in the time I have left, going to write down in this manuscript, which I will seal, and which I will instruct my executor to suppress until every person named in it is beyond the reach of embarrassment, pity, or the particular cruelty of public understanding applied to private catastrophe.

This is the whole truth.

God forgive me for telling it. God forgive me for not telling it sooner.

## II.

The séance was held on the evening of December the fourteenth, 1924, in the parlor of a Mrs. Elspeth Aldworth, at an address on Marlborough Street in Boston that I could supply precisely but will not, as the house was later sold and the family has suffered enough. Mrs. Aldworth was a medium of moderate local reputation — she had been written up twice in the *Banner of Light* and once, less charitably, in the *Boston Globe* — and she supplemented her spiritual practice with a small dressmaking concern, which I mention only because it is the kind of detail the newspapers never include, and which tells you more about the economics of American Spiritualism than any treatise: the dead do not pay well, or rather *the living* do not pay well for access to the dead, and a woman must eat.

Harry had arranged the evening through channels I was not privy to. He had by then developed a network of informants — reformed mediums, disgruntled assistants, rival practitioners willing to sell out a competitor — and he used them with the precision of a military intelligence operation, which is not an analogy I employ lightly. He would identify a target, gather preliminary evidence, attend the séance in disguise or under an assumed name, and then, at the critical moment, reveal himself, produce his evidence, and dismantle the performance in front of an audience that he had taken care to populate with journalists and, when possible, a photographer. It was brutal. It was effective. It was theater of the highest order, and I say that as a man who spent forty years writing for the stage and never once achieved the dramatic economy that Harry brought to a drawing room takedown.

I was in Boston for a lecture — my own Spiritualist advocacy tour, which ran, as it often did, in unhappy parallel with Harry's debunking circuit, so that we resembled nothing so much as two traveling preachers of opposed gospels working the same tent-revival territory. Harry had invited me to attend. The invitation was, as always, both genuine and strategic: he wanted a witness, and he wanted *me* specifically, because my visible dismay at his exposures made for excellent copy. *DOYLE WATCHES AS HOUDINI SLAYS ANOTHER GHOST,* the *Herald* had printed after the last one. Harry had sent me the clipping with a note: *Better luck next time, old sport.* I had not found it funny. I had gone anyway, because one does, with Harry; one always went, pulled along in the wake of his certainty the way a dinghy follows an ocean liner, protesting the speed and secretly thrilling to it.

The parlor was as they always are: too warm, too crowded, draped in that particular shade of maroon that Spiritualist practitioners seemed to purchase in bulk from a single, depressing supplier. Gas lamps, turned low. A séance table, round, oak, covered in a dark cloth. Chairs for twelve, though Mrs. Aldworth had squeezed in fourteen to accommodate the extra journalists Harry had planted. The air smelled of camphor and tallow and the specific, municipal odor of wet wool drying slowly on human bodies — Boston in December, every coat and scarf steaming gently in the overheated room, so that the entire assembly seemed to be faintly smoking, like a congregation in purgatory.

Mrs. Aldworth herself was a small woman of perhaps fifty-five, with the kind of face that is difficult to describe because it is assembled entirely from pleasant generalities: round cheeks, mild eyes, grey hair pinned neatly. She wore a dress of dark blue serge — her own construction, almost certainly — and a mourning brooch at her throat that may or may not have been a prop but that I noticed held a lock of genuinely faded hair, which meant that someone she had loved was genuinely dead, which meant that whatever else she was, she was also a woman acquainted with grief. I marked this. Harry did not. It was not relevant to his purpose.

The séance proceeded as they do. Mrs. Aldworth invited us to join hands. She closed her eyes and began the invocation — a standard form, derivative of the Fox sisters' original protocol, with some embellishments I recognized from the Davis school. The table rapped. The temperature in the room appeared to drop, though I was not yet in a position to say whether this was genuine or the product of a concealed block of ice, which was a common technique. A voice — low, breathy, genderless — emerged from the direction of the cabinet behind Mrs. Aldworth's chair and purported to be the spirit of a young man killed at Belleau Wood, seeking his mother, who was, by arrangement, seated at the table and weeping with the pre-programmed enthusiasm of a woman who had been through this before and knew her role.

Harry let it run for precisely eleven minutes. I know because I glanced at my watch when he stood, and he later told me he had decided in advance on eleven minutes as the ideal duration: long enough for the performance to be fully committed, short enough that the audience would still be engaged rather than drowsy. He had a showman's instinct for these calculations that bordered on mathematical.

He stood. He said — and I will never forget the specific, gleeful electricity of his voice when he said it, because it was the voice of a man experiencing his purest form of joy — he said: "Ladies and gentlemen, I ask your patience for just a moment."

And then he *moved*.

I have watched Harry perform a thousand times, and I have never been able to adequately describe his physicality. He was not a large man. Five feet five inches, if he was being honest, which about his height he seldom was. Compact, enormously strong, with hands that could manipulate a lock mechanism and also punch through a packing crate, and with a quality of *attention* in his body that made every gesture seem both spontaneous and choreographed, as though he were dancing to music no one else could hear but everyone could feel. He moved through Mrs. Aldworth's parlor like a force of nature with an excellent tailor, and in approximately ninety seconds, he disassembled the entire apparatus.

The rapping table: a system of levers operated by Mrs. Aldworth's knees, connected by catgut threads to the table's underside. Harry pulled the cloth away with a torero's flourish and held the mechanism up for the audience to see.

The cold: a block of ice, as I had suspected, housed in a zinc-lined compartment beneath the floor, with a hand bellows operated by the accomplice to push the chilled air upward through a grate. Harry stamped on the grate and it gave way, revealing the works. The audience gasped, then laughed.

The voice: the accomplice himself, a thin young man of perhaps twenty — later identified as Mrs. Aldworth's nephew, Arthur Pratt — concealed in the cabinet behind her chair, equipped with a speaking trumpet and a script. Harry opened the cabinet like a man opening a Christmas present, with a showman's delight that was absolutely sincere, and young Pratt tumbled out looking simultaneously terrified and relieved, as though he had been caught at something he had always known was wrong and was glad, in the complicated way of the young, to finally be free of it.

The audience roared. Of course they did. They were watching a genius at the peak of his powers, and the performance — because it *was* a performance, Harry's debunking being no less theatrical than the fraud it exposed — was magnificent. Harry stood in the center of the ruined séance with the speaking trumpet in one hand and a length of cheesecloth (which Mrs. Aldworth had, I gathered, been planning to deploy as ectoplasm in the second act) in the other, and he *grinned*, and the grin was the most dangerous thing in the room, because it was the grin of a man who believed — who *knew*, with the full force of his extraordinary will — that there was nothing behind the curtain, that there was *never* anything behind the curtain, that every curtain in the world concealed only machinery and human weakness and the tragically persistent desire to believe that death is not permanent.

He was right about the machinery. He was right about the cheesecloth. He was right about Mrs. Aldworth and young Arthur Pratt and the catgut levers and the zinc-lined ice compartment.

He was right about everything that did not matter.
