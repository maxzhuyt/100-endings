# The Murals of Parliament

---

## I.

I have served as Speaker of this House for thirty-one years across six administrations, and in that time the walls have shown me more than any man should be made to carry. I will set this down in order, as I have set down everything else — the motions, the amendments, the divisions of ayes and nays, the expulsions, the eulogies — because order is the only discipline left to me and because what I have to record will not survive disordered telling. The walls do not explain themselves. Someone must.

The first mural I saw move was in my second administration, under Prime Minister Aldous Fenne. I had held the chair for four years at that point, and though I had been briefed — every incoming Speaker is briefed, a three-page memorandum marked ARCHITECTURAL SENSITIVES, stored in the constitutional vault alongside the regency protocols and the wartime succession orders — I had not believed it. The memorandum's language was careful to the point of uselessness. "The chamber's interior surfaces may, under sustained executive occupation, manifest responsive pigmentation consistent with the psychological orientation of serving officeholders." I read it, noted its bureaucratic cowardice, filed it, and forgot it until the morning Fenne's chair appeared.

It was a painted chair. Oak, high-backed, ministerial — rendered in what I would later learn to identify as the murals' characteristic style, which is no style at all but rather an unsettling fidelity, as though the walls had been studying parliamentary furniture in their sleep and reproducing it from memory. The chair appeared on the east wall behind the dispatch box at the start of Monday questions. By the afternoon it had migrated to the south wall, behind the treasury bench. By Wednesday it had settled behind the backbench bloc where Fenne's own loyalists sat, so that every time he turned to rally his side he saw it: an empty chair, his chair, painted with a permanence his tenure would not enjoy.

Fenne feared his own irrelevance. I know this not because he told me — Prime Ministers do not confide in Speakers; the relationship is procedural, not pastoral — but because I could hear it. This is the other thing the memorandum fails to mention. Proximity to the murals over sufficient duration produces in the Speaker a secondary sensitivity. I began to perceive, gradually and then constantly, the emotional substrate that generated each mural. Not thoughts. Not words. A frequency. Fenne's frequency was a thin, reedy signal, like a glass harmonic played in an empty room: the terror of a man who suspects that history will not remember his name.

The chair followed him for nine months. He resigned over a fisheries dispute, which is to say he resigned over the chair and used the fisheries dispute as cover. The paint faded within a week of his departure. By the time his successor took office, the east wall was clean plaster.

I will catalog briefly, because brevity is a mercy I owe the reader and myself:

Under Chancellor Edda Soames, who served two years and four months in the Breck administration, the south wall above the treasury bench produced a mural of children drowning. Not violently — the horror was in the quietness of it, the children sinking with their arms at their sides, as though they had been taught not to struggle. Soames feared demographic collapse. She had read a census projection in her first week that showed the kingdom's birth rate halving within a generation, and the number had lodged in her like a splinter. The children in the mural never surfaced. They accumulated. By her final month, the wall was a layered palimpsest of small sinking figures, dozens deep, their faces patient and resigned. Soames left politics for a university appointment and published, I am told, a well-regarded monograph on population dynamics in postwar economies. I have not read it. I do not need to. I heard the monograph in its original form every day for two years, rendered as a subsonic hum beneath the sound of parliamentary debate, and I can tell you it was not well-regarded. It was terrified.

Defence Minister Gareth Pell, who served under three administrations with the tenacity of a barnacle, produced a mural I will describe but not explain, because Pell took his explanation to the grave and I have no interest in speculation. The mural was his mother's face. Life-sized, positioned on the wall directly opposite his customary seat on the front bench. The eyes had been scratched out — not by any human hand; the scratching appeared as part of the mural's original manifestation, the plaster gouged in strokes that matched no tool I have seen in any restorer's kit. Pell never looked at it. He developed a technique of angling his body in his seat so that his sightline passed just to the left of the mural, a postural distortion so habitual that his suits were eventually cut asymmetrically to accommodate the twist in his torso. His frequency was the loudest I have ever heard from a single officeholder — a grinding, metallic drone, like a machine being asked to perform a function it was not built for. When he died in office — heart failure, during a recess, in a committee room on the third floor — the mural disappeared between one heartbeat and the next. I was in the chamber at the time, alone, reviewing the order paper. I heard the silence where his frequency had been, and I knew before the page came to tell me.

Prime Minister Sera Wynn, who led the kingdom through the four-year food crisis, generated a fresco that covered the entire ceiling of the chamber. It depicted banquet tables — long, laden, baroque in their abundance — stretching in painted perspective toward a vanishing point that seemed to recede the longer one gazed at it. The food on every table was ash. Meticulously rendered ash, grey and granular, shaped into the forms of roasts and loaves and fruit bowls but composed of particulate nothing. Wynn had never experienced famine. She had grown up in the capital's wealthiest district, educated at the Marisford Academy, dined in the private rooms of banks and ministries for her entire adult life. But she feared it. She feared it the way some people fear heights who have never been above the fifth floor — gratuitously, prophetically, as though the hunger were not a memory but a premonition sent backward through time. Her frequency was the sound of chewing with nothing in the mouth.

I have heard them all. Six administrations. Fourteen Prime Ministers and acting Prime Ministers. Forty-three cabinet officers, two hundred and eleven junior ministers, and uncountable backbenchers whose murals were small enough to go unrecorded — a discoloration here, a shifting stain there, the occasional faint outline of a door that opened onto darkness and then faded by the end of the session, as minor as the fears they represented, as transient as the men and women who carried them.

I have heard them all simultaneously.

This is the condition the memorandum does not describe, because the authors of the memorandum cannot imagine it: the accumulated perception, across decades, of every fear that has entered this chamber and been rendered visible on its walls. I have tried to describe it to the parliamentary physician, who is required by statute to examine the Speaker annually and who nods with professional gentleness and notes "no abnormalities" because there is no diagnostic category for what I am. The closest I have come to adequate language is this: it is like sitting in a chamber orchestra where every instrument plays a different elegy at once. The resulting sound is not cacophony. Cacophony would be endurable, because cacophony is formless. This is polyphony — structured, harmonic, each line distinct within the whole — and its structure is what makes it unendurable, because structure implies meaning, and I am not equal to the meaning of so many simultaneous griefs.

In the autumn of the kingdom's two hundred and sixty-fourth parliament, Maren Cael was elected Prime Minister at the age of thirty-four, the youngest to hold the office in living memory, leading a coalition of five parties bound together by a single legislative objective.

She came to office with clean walls. That lasted eleven days.

---

## II.

I will describe what I observed of Prime Minister Cael's working method in her early tenure, because what followed cannot be understood without an accounting of what came before, and because competence — genuine, muscular, unglamorous competence — deserves a record, especially when the record is all that survives.

She took her first question time standing at the dispatch box with both hands flat on its surface, her weight forward, her shoulders level. I noted this because most new Prime Ministers grip the dispatch box, knuckles whitening, as though the wooden lectern were a life raft. Maren did not grip. She pressed down. It was the posture of a person distributing force, not absorbing it. She answered seventeen questions in forty-two minutes, and I timed her because timing is my function, and I can report that her average response was two minutes and twenty-one seconds, which is eleven seconds below the parliamentary mean and notable primarily because brevity in new Prime Ministers is rare. They tend toward expansiveness, eager to demonstrate the range of their authority. Maren demonstrated range by not needing to.

She memorized names. Not in the political way — the way experienced operators memorize names as instruments, retrieving them at the moment of maximum tactical utility — but in the way of a person who had decided, apparently as a foundational principle, that every member of the chamber was a particular human being with a particular constituency containing particular people with particular problems, and that to forget any of this was a failure not of strategy but of attention. I watched her greet the member for Ostwall South by name and then ask, without prompting, about the canal restoration project that had been the subject of an adjournment debate three weeks prior. The member for Ostwall South had not expected this. He was a backbencher from a minor coalition partner, a man accustomed to being overlooked by leaders who had larger fears. He blinked. He answered. Maren listened with the focus of someone for whom listening was not a performance but a metabolic requirement, and when she replied, she addressed not only his concern but the concern behind his concern — the canal was a proxy for the province's broader anxiety about infrastructure neglect, and the infrastructure neglect was a proxy for the province's broader anxiety about whether the Unification Accord would treat peripheral regions as partners or as appendages. She spoke to the third layer. The member for Ostwall South, who had entered the exchange with the rehearsed grievance of a man who expected to be dismissed, left it looking slightly stunned, as though he had been seen more clearly than he was prepared to be seen.

She took meetings standing. I learned this from the parliamentary attendants, who reported that the Prime Minister's office had been rearranged: the desk pushed against the wall, the chairs removed except for a single seat offered to visitors, the center of the room left open so that Maren could pace the carpet in a slow circuit while her interlocutors sat and tracked her movement. When I asked — through the indirect channels that propriety permits between a Speaker and a Prime Minister — why she preferred to stand, the message that returned was both honest and insufficient: "Sitting makes me feel like a target." I filed the phrase. It was the first indication that Maren Cael's relationship to vulnerability was not the ordinary political one, in which vulnerability is a liability to be concealed, but something more complex — a condition she was aware of, could name, and was trying, through the physical management of her body in space, to negotiate.

She drafted amendments in a shorthand of her own invention. I saw the notebooks once, early in her tenure, when she left one on the dispatch box after a late division and an attendant brought it to me before returning it. The shorthand was dense, angular, compressed — an entire paragraph of legislative language rendered in a symbol-cluster the size of a postage stamp. She filled notebooks at a rate that alarmed her private secretary, who I am told requisitioned a standing order for parliamentary-standard notebooks from the House stationer, sixteen per month, which is four times the rate of any previous officeholder. The shorthand was not a code in the intelligence sense. It was not designed to be unreadable. It was designed to be *fast* — to capture the velocity of a mind that moved through legislative architecture the way a mason moves through stone, seeing the load-bearing walls and the decorative flourishes and the structural vulnerabilities all at once and needing to record all of it before the perception faded.

She was, in short, extraordinary. I note this without sentiment, or attempt to note it without sentiment, because sentiment is not a tool available to the Speaker of the House. But I will permit myself this: in thirty-one years I have observed many competent ministers, a handful of gifted ones, and perhaps three who possessed what Maren possessed, which was the ability to hold in her mind the entire architecture of a political situation — the interests, the fears, the vanities, the genuine convictions, the bad-faith objections, the procedural traps — and to move through that architecture not as a navigator moves through a map but as a body moves through a building, sensing the weight above, the space ahead, the give in the floor.

The Unification Accord was her building. It was the single piece of legislation that held her coalition together, the gravitational center around which five parties with five separate histories and five separate sets of constituents had agreed, provisionally and mistrustfully, to orbit. The Accord proposed to bind the kingdom's eleven provinces into a single economic and legal framework — shared tax authority, shared judicial standards, shared infrastructure governance — replacing a centuries-old patchwork of provincial compacts and bilateral treaties with a single constitutional instrument. It was, depending on one's vantage point, the most significant piece of legislation since the kingdom's founding charter or the most reckless act of centralization since the old empire. Three previous Prime Ministers had attempted some version of it. All three had failed. The walls remembered their failures, or I remembered their failures through the walls, which by this point in my tenure amounted to the same thing.

Maren's murals in this period were small.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is what I owe her, and because the trajectory from small to vast is the trajectory of this account. In her first weeks, the only mural I could identify as hers was a hairline crack. Not a structural crack — the chamber's stonework was sound; I had commissioned a survey — but a painted crack, a trompe-l'oeil fissure that appeared on the ceiling above the government front bench during question time and vanished by the evening adjournment. It was barely visible. A visitor to the gallery would not have noticed it. But I noticed it, because noticing is my affliction, and I could hear its frequency: a faint, high, crystalline tone, like a fingernail drawn across the rim of a wine glass.

She feared fracture. This was not a surprise. The coalition was fractious, the Accord was ambitious, and the opposition was patient. But the fear was not political in the ordinary sense. It was structural. Maren feared that the thing she was trying to build — not the coalition but the *idea*, the principle that eleven provinces could choose to be one polity — was not merely difficult but impossible, and that her attempt would prove the impossibility, and that the proof would be worse than the previous failures because her attempt was the most sincere.

A week later, a second mural appeared: a small watercolor, rendered in the murals' characteristic idiom of impossible fidelity, of an hourglass. It manifested on the wall behind the Speaker's chair — my chair — and I was the only one positioned to not see it, which meant everyone else could. The sand in the upper chamber was nearly gone. By evening the watercolor had faded, but the next morning it was back, and the sand had not been replenished. This cycle repeated daily for three weeks: appearing at the start of session, fading at adjournment, returning the next day with fractionally less sand. By the end of the third week, only a few grains remained in the upper chamber, and a member of the opposition — not Dahl; a backbencher with a nose for weakness — made a casual remark during debate about "the PM's interior decorating," which drew a laugh from the chamber and a glance from Maren that I observed and cataloged and have never forgotten. It was the glance of a person who has just learned that her insides are on the outside.
