# The Grief Adjuster

## I.

"Please describe the lost individual's most frequently used phrase or expression."

The woman across the desk looked at him as though he had asked her to describe the color of oxygen.

"Take your time," he said.

She was sixty, perhaps sixty-five. She wore a coat that was too heavy for the season, buttoned to the throat, and she held her handbag on her lap with both hands in a way that suggested she believed someone might take it from her. Her claim number was TBC-00947-l/f. The *l* designated the loss as filial. The claimant's copy of the CF-11 Continuity Bereavement Intake Form was spread on the desk between them, half completed, her handwriting small and careful in the top sections and then increasingly unsteady as the form moved into the specifics. She had managed *Full Legal Name of Lost Individual (As Remembered)* and *Approximate Date of Discontinuity (If Known)*. She had checked *Filial* under Relationship Category. She had stalled at Section D.

"He used to say—" She stopped. She pressed her lips together. "It was something about doors. Or windows. No. It was—"

She looked at the form as if the form itself might remember.

"Some claimants find it helpful to think of a specific setting," he said. "A room. A time of day. Sometimes the phrase comes back through the context rather than through the words."

This was true. He had learned it not from training but from nine years of sitting in this chair and watching people try to retrieve what was, in a precise and technical sense, no longer there. The brain held the shape of the memory—the neural pathway still fired—but the content had been destabilized. It was like finding a drawer labeled *Socks* and opening it to find the socks replaced by something else, or by nothing, or by socks that were almost but not entirely the right socks. The Department's literature referred to this as *residual engram persistence*, which meant: you remember that you remember, but you do not remember what.

"The kitchen," the woman said quietly. "In the morning. He always said—"

She stopped again.

He waited. The fluorescent tube above them hummed at a frequency that had become, over the years, indistinguishable from silence. His tea had gone cold. Through the wall, the muffled plumbing of the adjacent restroom made its intermittent commentary—a sound he thought of, when he thought of it at all, as the building's digestive system.

The basement office was twelve feet by fourteen feet. He knew this because a maintenance request he'd filed in his second year had required the room dimensions, and the number had lodged itself beside all the other small institutional facts that accumulate in a person who stays too long in one place: the extension for the supply closet was 4136; the elevator to the third floor required a key card but the one to the second floor did not; the hot water in the men's room on this level ran for approximately eleven seconds before reverting to cold. These were the things he knew with certainty. They were verifiable. They did not thin.

"It was about—being ready," the woman said. "He would say it when he was leaving for work. Something about being ready. Or about not waiting. I can't—"

Her voice had the particular tightness of someone who understood that grief was not the correct word for what she was experiencing because grief presupposed a world that agreed someone had died, and the world did not agree that her son had died because the world did not agree that her son had existed. What she was experiencing was something the Department called *continuity-displacement bereavement*, which was listed in the interdepartmental glossary as: *the affective and cognitive sequelae of a recognized discontinuity between the claimant's retained personal narrative and the current consensus timeline.* She was not, in the language of the Department, bereaved. She was *displaced.* Her son had not died. Her son had been *discontinued.* These were important distinctions. They affected which forms she was eligible to file, which compensation structures applied, which counseling services she could access on the Department's referral list. The distinctions were, in their own way, a kind of violence, though no one in the Department used that word, and he had stopped using it himself some years ago, not because he disagreed with it but because it did not help the claimants and it did not change the forms and it sat in his chest like a stone he had swallowed and could neither digest nor bring back up.

"That's all right," he said. "We can leave that field for now and come back to it."

He turned to Section E: *Degree of Remembrance Retained (Self-Assessed).* "On a scale of one to ten," he said, "with one being no retained sensory or narrative memory and ten being full pre-discontinuity recall, how would you rate your current level of remembrance of the lost individual?"

She looked at him. It was the look they all gave at this question—a look that contained the entire impossibility of what was being asked, which was: *please quantify, for administrative purposes, how much of your son is left inside your head.*

"I don't know," she said. "How would I know what I've lost if I've lost the memory of having it?"

"That's a very common response," he said, and it was. "Most claimants find it helpful to think of it not as a measure of what's missing but as a measure of what's present. How vivid is what you do retain? Can you see his face?"

"Sometimes."

"Can you hear his voice?"

"I—" She closed her eyes. "I can hear that there *was* a voice."

"Some claimants find that a five or a six reflects that experience. But you should use whatever number feels accurate to you. There are no wrong answers."

This was not true. There were, in a functional sense, wrong answers. A self-assessed score below three triggered a review of whether the claimant retained sufficient continuity-memory to sustain the claim at all, and a score of one or two could result in a reclassification to *Residual Affective Echo*, which was not a bereavement category and did not carry the same access to services. He had seen claimants rounded down. He had seen files closed. He did not tell her this. He told her there were no wrong answers and watched her write, in her careful and now trembling handwriting, the number *6*, and the number was a small, brave, probably strategic lie, and he honored it by recording it without comment.

The rest of the form took another twenty minutes. When they reached Section J—*Additional Relevant Details, Circumstantial or Personal, Not Captured Above (attach additional pages if necessary)*—she said, "He was kind," and then could not go on, and he gave her a moment, and she said, "He was very kind," and that was all she wrote, and it was enough to fill the field, and it was not enough to bring him back, and these two facts sat in the room together as they always did—sufficiency and insufficiency, cohabiting the same sentence.

"Your claim has been registered," he said. "You'll receive a determination within twelve to sixteen weeks. If your remembrance score changes significantly in either direction, please contact the Department to update your file. Do you have any questions?"

She did not have any questions. No one ever did, at this stage. Questions required a belief that the system might furnish answers, and by the time people reached his desk, that belief had been processed out of them—not cruelly, not by any single act of institutional cruelty, but by the accumulated weight of forms and waiting rooms and hold music and the particular grammar of official correspondence, which was unfailingly polite and which said, beneath its politeness: *you are a line item, you are a case number, we see you, we do not see you.*

She left. The door clicked shut. The fluorescent light hummed. He placed her folder—CF-11, supplemental pages, a photocopy of a photograph in which a dining table was set for two and the second chair was pulled out as if someone had just stood up—into the tray on the right side of his desk, which was the completed-intake tray, and he picked up the next folder from the tray on the left side of his desk, which was the pending-intake tray, and he opened it, and he read the name, and he pressed the intercom button, and he said, "You can send in Mr. Achebe," and the door opened, and a man sat down, and the man was holding his hands the way they all held their hands—clasped, like someone praying to a god they suspected had been edited out of the timeline along with everything else—and he said, "Thank you for coming in, Mr. Achebe," and he turned to Section A, and the fluorescent light hummed, and outside the basement window, at the level of the sidewalk, feet passed in both directions, going wherever feet go on a Tuesday, wearing shoes, stepping over the grate without looking down.

## II.

On Monday he arrived at seven fifty-five, as he always did, carrying a tea from the shop on the corner whose name he could not currently recall but which he had been visiting for years. The tea was Earl Grey. The shop had a red awning, or possibly the awning had at some point been red. These details had the quality of things known rather than things perceived—he did not re-examine the awning each morning so much as carry the settled fact of it through his commute like a coin in his pocket, present but unscrutinized.

The elevator to the basement required no key card. He knew this. The doors opened onto the hallway, which was linoleum, which was beige or institutional green depending on the light, which he had walked approximately four thousand five hundred times in nine years, and which was, this morning, the same hallway, or very nearly so.

His office. He turned the key. He set down his tea. He sat.

The desk was closer to the wall.

Not dramatically. Not in a way that would survive a formal objection. Six inches, perhaps. Or four. Possibly it had always been this distance from the wall and he had carried an incorrect spatial memory—this was plausible; this was, in fact, the most plausible explanation, because desks did not move themselves and the cleaning crew had no reason to move furniture and the explanation that required the fewest assumptions was that he was wrong, and the explanation that required the fewest assumptions was always, according to both general epistemology and the Department's internal guidance on Claimant Perception Disputes, the preferred explanation.

He looked at the desk. He looked at the wall. The gap between the desk's right edge and the wall was narrow enough that he could not comfortably reach the second drawer without pulling his chair back, or he could reach the second drawer but the motion was slightly more constricted than he remembered, or the motion was exactly as it always had been and his body was simply stiff from the weekend, which was also plausible—he was fifty-three, or he was fifty-four, and fifty-three- or fifty-four-year-old bodies were entitled to new stiffnesses without explanation.

He opened the drawer. His files were in order. The hanging folders marched in their green row, tabbed and alphabetized, each one a person-shaped hole in someone else's life.

He picked up his mug. The tea was still warm. The chip on the rim was—

He set the mug down.

The chip was on the left side. He drank with his right hand. He always drank with his right hand. The chip had always been on the right side, oriented so that it faced away from his mouth, which was why he had never bothered to replace the mug—the flaw was functionally irrelevant. Now the chip was on the left side, which meant it would press against his lip when he drank, which meant he would notice it every time, which meant this was not the same mug, or it was the same mug and the chip had always been on the left side and he was mistaken, or it was the same mug and the chip had migrated, which mugs do not do, or it was a different mug that was identical except for the chip's position, which was not impossible in a building that ordered its supplies in bulk from a departmental catalog.

He drank from the mug. The chip pressed against his lower lip. He swallowed, set the mug down, and began the day's sessions.

The first claimant was a woman in her forties, parental claim, Degree of Remembrance self-assessed at four. She had lost her father. Or her father had lost her—she phrased it that way, once, and then corrected herself, and the correction was the saddest thing in the room. He guided her through the form. Section A. Section B. The checkboxes. In Section D, when he asked for the lost individual's most frequently used phrase or expression, she said, without hesitation, *"You've got all the time in the world,"* and then laughed—a short, fractured sound, an exhalation that had meant to be something else—because he had said it to her when she was a child and it had not been true then and it was cosmically untrue now, and she had remembered it, she had retained these specific words through the edit, and they sat in the air between them like an artifact pulled from a river, worn smooth, still holding its shape.

He wrote down her father's phrase. He moved to Section E. The session continued.

Between the second and third claimants of the morning, he went to the restroom. The hallway was the same hallway. Hendra's door was open.

Hendra was at his desk, reading something. Hendra was always at his desk, or nearly always—a man of indeterminate middle age with a face that expressed, in its default state, a kind of ambient administrative competence. He wore a tie. The tie was always knotted with a precision that seemed neither vain nor careless but simply *correct*, as if the tie had been issued by the Department along with the desk and the brass nameplate that read *S. Hendra, Deputy Section Manager, Continuity Claims (Bereavement)*, and the tie had read its own job description and complied.

Hendra looked up and gave a small nod. Collegial. Neither warm nor cold. The nod of a man who recognized a colleague in a hallway, nothing more. A nod that was, in its radical blankness, impossible to interpret and therefore impossible to challenge—because what would you challenge? A man nodded at you. A man acknowledged your existence in a hallway with the minimal physical gesture available. The nod contained nothing. The nod might contain everything. You could not build a grievance on a nod.

He nodded back and continued to the restroom.

In the restroom he washed his hands and looked at the mirror and the face that looked back was his face, probably, though he had never been the kind of man who studied his own face, and the hot water lasted eleven seconds before going cold, which was exactly correct, which was verifiable, which was a fact that did not shift.

He dried his hands. He returned to his office. His desk was exactly where he had left it twenty minutes ago, which proved nothing—it had also been exactly where he had left it on Friday, presumably, and yet this morning it was six inches closer to the wall, or had always been six inches closer to the wall, or had always been exactly where it was.

The third claimant of the morning was an elderly man, spousal claim, who could no longer remember his wife's face but could remember the weight of her hand on his forearm when they walked. The man kept touching his own forearm as he spoke, pressing his fingers into the specific place where her hand had rested, as if he could hold the memory in place through pressure alone, as if memory were a drawer that could be kept shut if you leaned against it. He self-assessed at three. The adjuster told him what he told all the threes and below—that the score would be recorded as reported, that it would not affect the status of his claim, that the Department recognized the inherent difficulties of self-assessment—and this was true, technically, though the truth of it had the thin, institutional quality of things that are true on paper and devastating in practice.

During this session, between Section F (*Physical Description of Lost Individual, Including Any Distinguishing Features*) and Section G (*Nature and Duration of Relationship with Lost Individual*), he reached for his wife.

Not physically. He reached the way you reach for a word that is on the tip of your tongue—you can feel its shape, its syllabic weight, the place it occupies in the sentence, but the word itself has stepped behind a curtain and will not come out.
