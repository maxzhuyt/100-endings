# The Forged Letters of Grigori Plink

## I.

The ink was wrong. I will state this plainly, as I state all things, because precision is the only courtesy a man can extend to a world that has otherwise shown him none. The ink was wrong: too thin by perhaps a twentieth of a degree on the viscometric scale I have developed through years of practice, a scale I keep in a notebook whose spine has been repaired eleven times with fish glue and whose pages smell, as does everything in this town, of cabbage.

The town is called Volsky. It is not on most maps, and the maps on which it does appear tend to depict it with a reluctance that suggests the cartographer was fulfilling an obligation rather than recording a discovery. The buildings are the color that occurs when ambition fails to become paint. They lean. They lean the way I lean, which is to say persistently and in directions that alarm structural engineers. The central administrative district consists of fourteen buildings arranged around a square that has never once, in my observation, been geometrically square, and the primary economic activities are the processing of cabbage, the filing of documents related to the processing of cabbage, and the generation of supplementary documents to correct errors in the original documents related to the processing of cabbage.

I have lived here for the entirety of my thirty-seven years. I have sustained, in that time, fractures to the left wrist (twice), the right clavicle, the fourth and seventh ribs on the left side, the second toe of the right foot (three times; the same toe; I have come to regard it as an antagonist), the orbital bone above the left eye, and the nose (a number I have lost count of and will estimate conservatively at six). I have received contusions beyond meaningful enumeration. I mention these not for sympathy — a resource I have never successfully applied for — but as context for the events I am about to relate, and as evidence that the physical world has maintained, with respect to my body, a consistent editorial policy of rejection.

On the morning in question — a Tuesday, I believe, though the days in Volsky tend to present themselves with an institutional sameness that makes such determinations approximate — I was seated at my worktable in the room above the laundry on Kropotkinskaya Street, producing a tax stamp for the district cabbage levy. I had been working on it for eleven days. This may seem excessive for a stamp whose legitimate counterpart is produced, I am told, by a mechanical press in four seconds, but I do not have a mechanical press. I have a magnifying lens with a crack down its center that bisects whatever I examine into two offset halves, a set of nibs I carved myself from goose quills donated — which is to say discarded — by the woman downstairs who operates the laundry, a pot of ink I mixed from lampblack and linseed oil according to a recipe I found in a water-damaged pamphlet, and my hands, which are precise in the way that a clock is precise when it tells the wrong time with absolute consistency.

I should say something about my eyes. They are, by any ophthalmological standard, a disaster. I was examined once, at age fourteen, by a traveling optician who passed through Volsky under the mistaken impression that it was a different town. He held lenses before me and asked what I could see, and I described, with the accuracy that is my only reliable trait, the exact character and quality of the blur. He told me I needed spectacles of a prescription he was not equipped to provide. He suggested I travel to Moscow. I have not traveled to Moscow. Moscow is, by my best estimate, nine hundred kilometers from Volsky, and I have calculated, based on my frequency of injurious incidents per kilometer traveled on foot — a figure I arrived at after keeping careful records during a trip to the post office, a distance of two hundred meters, during which I sustained a bruise to the left shin (curb), an abrasion to both palms (icy step), and a laceration of the right ear (low-hanging shop sign, metal) — that I would arrive in Moscow as a medical specimen rather than a patient.

So I see the way I see: at very close range, with hallucinatory sharpness, every fiber and flaw and grain of surface magnified into a landscape I can navigate with my fingertips as much as my eyes. And at any distance beyond the length of my arm, the world becomes a negotiation between smears of color whose intentions toward me I must infer from past experience, which has taught me that their intentions are generally hostile.

This is why I am good at what I do. Or rather — I must be honest; I am always honest, which is an unusual trait in a forger and may go some distance toward explaining my difficulties — this is why I am *devoted* to what I do. Whether I am *good* at it is a question I have found it more productive not to examine.

The tax stamp. I was applying the final detail — the small imperialist eagle that perches in the upper left corner of all district fiscal documents, a bird I have rendered so many times I could draw it from memory even if my memory, like my eyesight, were not to be trusted — when my elbow, acting on its own recognizance, as my joints often do, struck the inkpot.

The inkpot did not merely fall. It executed a quarter-rotation on the edge of the table that I would describe, were I inclined toward metaphor, which I am not, as balletic. It hung for a duration I estimate at one-third of a second — long enough for me to observe, with the close-range clarity that is my single gift, the way the meniscus of the ink trembled at the lip of the vessel, a perfect black dome of surface tension containing the full record of eleven days' work in negative, the way a puddle contains the sky. Then the surface tension failed, as surfaces in my vicinity invariably do, and the ink fell.

It fell onto the stamp.

It fell onto the table.

It fell onto the chair, because I had lunged for the inkpot and in doing so pitched the chair sideways, and then it fell onto me, because I had fallen after the chair, and the floor of the room above the laundry on Kropotkinskaya Street, which slopes seventeen degrees toward the eastern wall due to a subsidence I have reported to the building's owner four times without acknowledgment, carried me in a gentle slide until my head struck the baseboard with a force sufficient to produce a sound I would characterize as *tonal* — a resonant, almost musical report that suggested the baseboard was hollow, which explained the draft.

I lay on the floor. The ink was on my face, in my hair, pooling in the depression of my left ear. The stamp, eleven days in the making, was obliterated. Above me, on the table's edge, I could see, with what remained of my upward vision, the magnifying lens with its cracked meridian gazing down at me like the eye of a god who has examined his creation and found the work not even worth the trouble of destroying properly.

I sat up. I catalogued: a contusion to the occipital region, already swelling to a diameter I estimated at three centimeters; ink in the left ear canal, which produced a sensation of distant, muffled roaring, as though the sea had been filed in the wrong department and was trying to find its way out; a stiffness in the lower back consistent with having become briefly airborne; the taste of lampblack and linseed, which I mention only because it is relevant to note that this is the taste of my life's work, and it is not good.

I began again.

This is, I think, the essential fact about me, and I offer it without commentary: I began again. I took a fresh sheet of paper — my fourth-to-last sheet; paper is expensive and I am not — and I positioned it on the table, and I leaned close, so close my breath made the paper shiver, and I began to draw the eagle again, one feather at a time, with the devotion of a monk illuminating a manuscript that no one will read in a language no one speaks for a God whose existence is, at best, *under review*.

The resulting stamp, when I completed it four days later, was, like all my stamps, a work of sincere and total effort. The eagle's feathers were individually rendered with a delicacy that, examined under magnification, revealed a quality of attention bordering on the devotional. The border filigree was hand-drawn in strokes so fine they might have been the work of a spider who had studied calligraphy. The serial number was invented but plausible. The district seal was a faithful reproduction of memory, which is to say it was faithful to what I remembered, and what I remembered was faithful to what I had seen, and what I had seen was faithful to what my eyes were capable of delivering, which introduces into the chain of fidelity a link of such catastrophic weakness that the wonder is not that my stamps fail to convince but that they are recognizable as stamps at all.

I say all this not as self-deprecation but as inventory. A man should know what he has. I have: two hands, steady at close range, that shake beyond it. Eyes that magnify what is near and abolish what is far. A body that the physical world treats as a rough draft. A room above a laundry. And a craft I practice with the fidelity of a priest and the success rate of a heretic.

This was my life in Volsky on the morning before the morning I met Yevgenia Fyodorovna Raskova, and it seems necessary to have established it completely, because what followed was a sequence of events that, like the inkpot, began with a small disturbance of equilibrium and ended with everything I had made — every careful, fraudulent, devoted thing — facedown on the floor.

## II.

The Municipal Bureau of Fiscal Documents occupies the third floor of a building on the east side of the square that I believe was originally designed as something else — a school, perhaps, or a granary — and was converted to bureaucratic use with the same enthusiasm the town brings to all its conversions, which is to say the walls were painted a different color and a sign was hung and everyone agreed to pretend.

I was there because my stamps, once completed, must be introduced into the fiscal ecosystem, which requires that I visit the bureau, present a cabbage tax document to which the stamp has been affixed, and submit it to the clerk on duty for processing. The clerk on duty has, for as long as I have been conducting this enterprise, been a man named Pyotr Afanasyevich, whose capacity for visual discernment I have estimated, through extensive empirical observation, to be roughly equivalent to mine — which is to say he can perceive what is directly in front of his nose, and everything beyond that falls into the category of *somebody else's problem*. Pyotr Afanasyevich has processed my stamps for four years without incident, which I attribute not to the quality of my forgeries but to the quality of his indifference, a resource so vast and so consistently renewable that it constitutes, in my view, the only truly sustainable industry in Volsky.

I was ascending the staircase to the third floor. The staircase has forty-one steps. I know this because I have counted them on each of my visits, and because I have fallen on, by my records, fourteen distinct steps across thirty-seven visits, giving a failure rate of approximately one injurious step per 2.6 visits, or what I have privately designated a *Plink coefficient* suitable for any staircase I am likely to encounter.

On this particular morning, I had reached step thirty-eight — the final landing, where the staircase turns — when the door at the top of the stairs opened outward. I should note that this door has always opened outward, a design choice I have questioned in several unsubmitted letters to the building commission, because a door that opens outward at the top of a staircase is not a door. It is a trebuchet.

The door opened. I was on step thirty-eight, which placed my face at the precise altitude of the door's central panel. What followed occupied, by my reconstruction, approximately one and one-quarter seconds, but I will render it with the fidelity it deserves, because it was the most consequential one and one-quarter seconds of my life, and because the details — the specific physical details — are the only language I have for what happened.

The door struck me on the forehead, slightly left of center, at a point I later measured to be four centimeters above the left eyebrow. The force was sufficient to arrest my forward momentum and initiate a rearward momentum, which, given my position on step thirty-eight of a staircase that turns at that point, translated into a lateral displacement that the staircase's architecture was not designed to accommodate. My left hand seized the banister. The banister, which I have previously noted in my unsubmitted letters as being secured by screws that appeared to predate the concept of maintenance, separated from the wall with a groan that I can only describe as *sympathetic*, as though the banister had been waiting for a legitimate reason to abandon its post and I had finally provided one.

I fell. The direction of the fall was complex. I will say only that it involved a rotational component that brought my back into contact with three risers and my right knee into contact with the wall, and that the banister, still in my grip, accompanied me with the loyalty of a companion animal, swinging in an arc that terminated against a window on the half-landing, which did not break but produced a sound — a deep, almost ecclesiastical hum — that seemed, in retrospect, like the building's attempt at a wedding bell.

I came to rest on the half-landing between floors two and three, supine, the banister across my chest like a ceremonial staff, plaster dust settling on my face. And framed in the doorway above me, silhouetted against the fluorescent light of the third-floor corridor, was a shape.

I could not see its features. It was at doorway distance, which placed it beyond the horizon of my useful vision. It was a smear. But it was — I noted this with the diagnostic attention I bring to all sensory data — a smear of a quality I had not previously encountered. The fluorescent light behind it produced a diffraction I can only describe as *warm*, though I am aware that fluorescent light is, by its physical nature, the opposite of warm, and that the warmth I perceived must therefore have originated in the shape itself, or else in the contusion to my forehead, which was already producing visual effects I was not in a position to attribute with scientific confidence.

"Oh," said the shape. Its voice was — I will describe what I can verify — in a register approximately two octaves above my own, with a quality I would characterize, using the vocabulary of my craft, as *linen*: a smooth texture with a fine, discernible grain.

"I have fallen," I said, because this seemed both evident and worth confirming.

The shape descended toward me. This was alarming, because things that descend toward me are generally in the process of striking me, and I braced accordingly — a motion that caused the banister to roll off my chest and clatter down the remaining steps to the second-floor landing with a percussive enthusiasm I might have admired under other circumstances. But the shape, as it approached, did not strike me. It came close enough to enter the field of my useful vision, and then — in an act of focal mercy I had never before experienced — it came *closer*, and the smear resolved, and I saw a face.

I will describe it the way I describe all things: as surfaces.

The skin had the consistency of good paper — not the pressed municipal stock I use for stamps, which is porous and receives ink reluctantly, but something finer, something with a thread count, if paper can be said to have a thread count. The eyes were a color I could not determine with confidence because the fluorescent light above was contaminating my readings, but they were large, and they were, at this range — she had knelt beside me, she was very close — examining me with an attention I recognized. It was the attention I bring to a document I am about to reproduce: close, investigative, noting every flaw.

I flinched. People do not look at me. People look at the space I have recently vacated, usually because I have fallen out of it.

"You are bleeding," she said, in that linen voice.
