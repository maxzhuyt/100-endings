# THE COMPOUND

### A LOOM ORIGINAL DOCUSERIES · LIMITED EVENT · SIX PARTS

---

## PART ONE: VECTOR

---

Before we begin — and we will begin, don't worry, there is a warehouse and it is in Pasadena and inside there are eleven hundred liters of something that will make you rethink every kiss you've ever initiated — before any of that, a confession.

We know how this ends.

Not the broad strokes. Not the Wikipedia summary you'll skim at 1 AM after episode three leaves you vibrating with something you won't name to your partner sleeping next to you. We know the *specific* ending. We know which detective is still employed. We know what the blood work revealed about the camera operator — Kevin, you'll meet Kevin, Kevin who thought he was documenting the story and was in fact becoming the story, a distinction he will never fully accept and which his therapist, Dr. Rachel Yoon of Bellaire, will charge $340 an hour to help him not accept. We know the Reddit username of the person who got the closest, and we know that she posted her theory at 3:47 AM from a studio apartment in Montrose, sitting cross-legged on a mattress on the floor with hot Cheeto dust on her left thumb and an ache in her chest she attributed to acid reflux but which was, by that point, almost certainly exposure. She was right about nearly everything. She will never know this.

We know all of it, and we are going to unspool it at a pace we find entertaining.

If that makes you uncomfortable — the admission that a production company, Loom Original Content, a subsidiary of Loom Media Group, a subsidiary of an equity fund whose name you'd recognize from the Epstein flight logs (no relation, technically, legally, the lawyers want that clause), has assembled this series not to inform but to *hold you* — if that discomfort registers, good. Note it. Set it on the nightstand next to the glass of water. You'll want it later.

The title sequence is eleven seconds long. It cost $340,000. It features a macro shot of human skin — pores, fine vellus hair, the microscopic topography of the forearm — and as the camera pushes in, the skin flushes. Capillaries dilate. The hairs stand. The Loom logo resolves out of the gooseflesh like a watermark on currency.

Someone was paid to art-direct goosebumps. Someone approved the invoice.

Three Emmy nominations for title design alone. We mention this not because it matters but because everything about how this story reached you is part of the story, and the parts that seem least important are, by the law of narrative thermodynamics, the parts you should watch the closest.

Now. Houston.

---

Because it started in Houston, which means it started in heat.

Not the quaint postcard heat that Northern transplants complain about on their first August, the hair-curling, shirt-soaking inconvenience of a subtropical climate. Houston heat is not an inconvenience. Houston heat is a *medium*. The city exists inside it the way a body exists inside skin. By June the air has a density you can almost lean against, and people move through it slower than they mean to, and their clothes cling in ways they stopped noticing years ago, and the distance between bodies at a crosswalk or in a grocery line shrinks by fractions of inches that no one would ever measure but that are, if you're paying a certain kind of attention, legible. Interpretable. Diagnostic.

Roll the B-roll.

Here is the Katy Freeway at dusk, eight lanes of brake lights bleeding into nine, and in a white Nissan Altima in the second lane a woman is singing along to something we can't hear and her window is down and the man in the F-150 to her left has been watching her for the last mile and what's on his face is not threat and not exactly desire — it's *attention*, the kind that doesn't belong between strangers at sixty-five miles per hour, the kind that before this year you would have called coincidence.

Here is the Galleria, third-floor food court, a Tuesday in April, and a pair of teenagers are sharing a Wetzel's Pretzel across a table meant for four. Their knees are touching under the table. That is not unusual. What is unusual is the couple at the next table — forties, wedding rings, clearly married to each other, clearly here to return something at Nordstrom — who are watching the teenagers with an expression the camera holds on for four seconds too long. Not predatory. *Wistful*. Physiologically wistful: the woman's hand is on her own collarbone, and the man has stopped chewing.

Here is Pastor David Kenner of Covenant Life Church, capacity 4,200, concluding his 11 AM service with a prayer that includes the word "intimacy" three times in ninety seconds. The congregation has its eyes closed. The choir is humming something in D minor that does not appear in any hymnal the production team could locate. After the service, in the parking lot, the handshakes between congregants last one and a half seconds longer than the national average for Protestant post-worship handshakes, a metric that exists because a sociologist at Rice named Dr. Eleanor Tsao has been tracking it for six years. She has not yet published her findings about the spring data. When she does, in October, it will be the fourth most-read paper in the history of the *Journal of Nonverbal Behavior* and she will cry at her desk, not because of the attention but because the data confirmed what she had felt in her own hand, at her own church, on a Sunday in March, when the associate pastor held her palm in both of his for eleven seconds and she let him.

Here is a Whataburger on Westheimer at 2:14 AM, and two men in reflective vests are eating in silence in a corner booth, and they are not looking at each other, and from this camera angle that absence of looking is louder than the drunk girls at the counter arguing about an Uber. The men work for the same paving crew. They have known each other four years. Nothing has ever happened between them, if you define "happened" the way most people do, meaning bodies, meaning the crossing of a line with coordinates. But one of them — the one facing the window, the one whose name is Gerald, the one who will end up in the B-roll of episode four weeping into his hands in a deposition room — Gerald's phone is on the table, and his lock screen is a photo of the two of them at a Texans game, and the other man does not know this, and Gerald does not know that we are showing you this, and you do not yet know why it matters, but your body knows something. The back of your neck. Check.

Here is Buffalo Bayou at golden hour, and a couple is walking along the Sandy Reed Memorial Trail, and they are holding hands, and the light is hitting the water in a way that would make a perfume company weep with brand envy, and their shadows are long and merged at the hip and they are beautiful. They are so beautiful. She is laughing at something he said and he is looking at her the way men look at women in movies directed by people who have never been examined too closely by the press. It is a perfect image of connection, of being-with-another, of the thing the species does when the species is working.

Her name is Andrea Chao-Reeves. She is an intake coordinator at Houston Methodist. She has been married to the man holding her hand, Marcus Reeves, for nine years. They have a daughter named Lily who is, at this moment, at a sleepover three miles away.

Fourteen days ago, Andrea attended a conference at the Marriott Marquis downtown. A man she did not know brushed past her in the corridor outside Ballroom C. He was sweating. Not nervously — *chemically*. His sweat contained, at a concentration of approximately 0.003 milliliters per square centimeter of skin surface, a substance that the CDC would later designate Compound RT-7 and that the internet would call Blush, and that we, because language is the narrator's prerogative, will call what it is: the end of the question.

The question being: *Do you want this?*

Not *this* as in sex. Not *this* as in touch. *This* as in the other person. This as in proximity without justification. This as in the hand-holding you are watching right now on a bayou in the most beautiful light Houston has ever produced, which is beautiful because Andrea Chao-Reeves's neurochemistry was, fourteen days ago, reorganized by a substance that did not ask for her input, and what she feels for Marcus Reeves right now — the renewed adoration, the re-enchantment, the sense that their marriage has been *restored* to something she thought was gone — is not fake, exactly, because her brain is really producing the dopamine and her skin is really flushing and her hand is really squeezing his, but it is also not a choice, exactly, because the compound does not negotiate.

It does not fill out paperwork. It does not wait for the conference session to end. It walks past you in a corridor and you spend the next six weeks falling back into a love you had every right to let die, and your husband thinks you've finally heard him, and your daughter notices that Mom is laughing again, and the only person who knows the difference between this and the real thing is us.

And now you.

You're welcome.

---

Detective Sergeant Miriam Katz, Narcotics Division, Houston Police Department, has a body that moves through rooms the way good lawyers move through arguments: with an economy that implies reserves. She is forty-four. She is five foot six. Her clearance rate for the fiscal year is the highest in the division, a fact she has never mentioned aloud because she learned in her first year that female detectives who mention their numbers become "ambitious" in a way that metabolizes differently than male ambition, and Katz is not interested in being metabolized.

She has a master's in criminal justice from Sam Houston State. She has a seventeen-year-old daughter, Becca, who is applying to colleges and who has recently stopped making eye contact during dinner, which Katz attributes to normal developmental separation but which keeps her up past midnight cataloging every possible failure of maternal attention since 2014. She has a ex-husband in Beaumont who sends child support on time and texts "Happy Birthday Miriam" every January without the exclamation point, which she respects. She has a service weapon she has discharged twice in the line of duty and once, in 2019, at a firing range in Katy, after hours, alone, putting forty rounds through the center mass of a paper silhouette while crying in a way she would describe, if asked, as "maintenance."

No one has asked.

She became the lead on what the department initially classified as a controlled-substance investigation — Case File 2024-VP-00771, "Unknown Compound, Distribution Network, Southeast Houston" — because she was the most qualified and because her lieutenant, David Ornelas, privately believed that a woman should run a case about a substance that, as he put it in a text to his wife that the production team obtained through a records request he is not yet aware of, "makes people fuck whether they want to or not."

(Ornelas's characterization was both crude and incorrect. The compound does not make people do anything. It makes people *want*. The distinction is the entire case.)

(Ornelas's wife replied to his text with a single emoji: the face with the hand over the mouth. They have not discussed it since.)

Katz accepted the case. She reviewed the initial reports — two ER admissions, one domestic disturbance call that dispatch flagged as "unusual," a hazmat team's inconclusive analysis of residue found in a nightclub bathroom on Washington Avenue. She built a preliminary caseboard. She requested a partner.

She did not tell Lieutenant Ornelas, or the partner she requested, or her daughter, or the therapist she sees every other Thursday at a practice in the Heights that smells like lavender and reasonable coping strategies, the thing she thinks about at night.

We'll tell you.

It's not the compound. Not directly. It's not the victims, though she calls them victims, which is itself a choice that will become contentious by episode three. It's not the distribution network or the origin question — the fact that no organic chemist in the country can identify the compound's base structure, the fact that it matches no known botanical or synthetic precursor, the fact that it seems to have arrived in Houston's water table and nightlife ecosystem from *nowhere*, which is a word scientists use when they mean *somewhere they're not ready to name*.

What keeps Miriam Katz awake is this:

If the compound removes inhibition — not consciousness, not memory, not the ability to say "no," but the *desire* to say no, the internal friction that stands between impulse and action, the thing she has spent her entire adult life understanding as the load-bearing wall of civilization — then every legal framework she enforces is built on a foundation the compound can dissolve. Consent requires the capacity to withhold consent. Withholding requires inhibition. Inhibition is what the compound eats.

She lies in her bed in her house in Garden Oaks and stares at the ceiling fan and follows the logic to its end:

A substance that makes you want what you would otherwise resist is not a drug. It is a *solvent*. And what it dissolves is not sobriety or judgment or even self-control. What it dissolves is the *self* — the part of the self that says *I choose this* rather than *this is happening to me*. And if that distinction collapses — if wanting and choosing become neurochemically indistinguishable — then the law she has given her career to is not wrong. It is *meaningless*. A set of rules written for a species that no longer exists.

She thinks this at 1 AM, at 2 AM, at 3:15 while Becca sleeps down the hall with her phone charging on her nightstand and her college essay draft open on her laptop, an essay about resilience that Katz has not been invited to read.

She thinks: *What if the compound is not a crime? What if the compound is an answer to a question the species has been asking since the first body wanted a second body and the second body said "wait"?*

She thinks: *What if the crime is "wait"?*

She does not tell anyone she thinks this. She goes to work. She builds her caseboard. She is very, very good at her job.

Fuentes.

Detective Carlos Fuentes, transferred to Narcotics from Homicide eighteen months ago at his own request, a lateral move that his colleagues interpreted as burnout and that was, in fact, a retreat so carefully orchestrated that he deserves some kind of award for logistics, though the award would have to be presented in private because the thing he was retreating from was a dead woman's face that he could see when he closed his eyes, not because she was a victim — she was, a case in 2022, a domestic, the details are in the file — but because in the moment he found her, in the apartment on Telephone Road, in the humidity and the smell and the stillness of the scene, he had felt *nothing*.

No horror. No pity. No rage. The flat, informational blankness of a man reading a parking meter.

He told the department psychologist that the case "stayed with him." This was true in the sense that an absence can stay with you, the way a cavity stays in a tooth: a space where something was supposed to be.

He is thirty-nine. He was born in Brownsville. He has a wife, Elena, who teaches fourth grade at a KIPP school in Gulfton and who has, over the last three years, stopped initiating physical contact in the evenings, a withdrawal so gradual that it has no single start date, no moment of confrontation, only the slow ambient cooling of a house where two people are kind to each other in the way that roommates are kind — aware of shared space, careful with noise, generous about dishes — and who have replaced the original terms of their marriage with a détente so thorough that suggesting its inadequacy would itself be a kind of violence.

Elena does not know about the dead woman on Telephone Road and the nothing that Fuentes felt. She knows he transferred divisions. She interpreted it as a healthy choice. She bought him a card that said "New Chapter" with a cartoon bookmark on the front and wrote inside it: *I'm proud of you. — E*

He keeps the card in the glove compartment of his department vehicle. He has read it more than forty times.
