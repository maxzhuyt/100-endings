# Ask His Spine to Remember

## I.

The mats smell like iodine and rubber and something underneath both of those things that nobody has ever been able to name, some chemical cousin of silence, and Maria Delgado has been breathing it for twenty years, which means either her nose has surrendered or the smell has become the baseline composition of her blood. She prefers not to think about which. She prefers not to think about a lot of things, which is why coaching middle school wrestling in a gymnasium with water-stained ceiling tiles and a thermostat that has displayed sixty-eight degrees since the Obama administration regardless of whether the room is forty or ninety is, in its way, a kind of paradise. You don't have to think in here. You have to watch.

The ceiling tiles bother her. Not aesthetically—she lost the capacity for aesthetic grievance around the same time she lost the capacity for aesthetic hope, which was roughly the same semester she stopped buying throw pillows and started eating shredded cheese directly from the bag at 11 p.m. while standing in the kitchen light in her socks, which is a sentence she would never say aloud but which she sometimes thinks with a strange, detached precision, as if she is narrating herself to an audience that does not exist. The tiles bother her structurally. Three of them sag. Water pools somewhere in the crawl space above the gymnasium and presses against the foam composite of the tiles until they bow downward like the bellies of animals, and someday one of them is going to release its modest reservoir onto a wrestler's head during a match and she is going to have to fill out an incident report, and the incident report will require her to describe the nature of the hazard, and she will write *the ceiling wept on a child,* and nobody will find it funny except her.

The motivational posters have been here longer than she has. DETERMINATION: *A river cuts through rock not because of its power but because of its persistence.* This one hangs crooked above the storage closet where she sometimes sits between practices in the dark and eats string cheese that she keeps in her windbreaker pocket, not in the mini-fridge in the teachers' lounge, because the mini-fridge is haunted by Gary Petrowski from Social Studies who always needs to talk about his Fantasy league while you're just trying to get your string cheese and leave. She has considered the poster many times from inside the closet, through the half-open door, the way you consider stained glass from inside a cathedral—not for its message but for the quality of light it sits in, the dust motes crossing in front of it, the way the lamination has peeled at the upper left corner so the river in the photograph is slowly curling away from its own rocks.

A river cuts through rock. Her mother would have said something about that. Her mother said something about everything, toward the end, those last months when every object in every room became a homily, when you couldn't hand the woman a glass of water without her finding the parable in it. The rosary was always moving through her fingers—that coral-colored one from Guadalajara with the beads worn smooth by decades of friction—and the sound of it, that tiny click-click-click, was the sound of Maria's childhood and the sound of the hospice room and Maria sometimes hears it at night in her apartment when the television is on and the building settles and something in the pipes clicks in a rhythm she could swear she recognizes. But pipes are pipes. Buildings settle. Not everything is a visitation, which is something her mother never understood and which Maria understood too well, and between those two positions the entirety of their relationship had lived and died.

She keeps a roll of Tums in her windbreaker pocket next to the string cheese. Wintergreen. She chews one now, standing at the edge of the mats, watching her assistant—the head coach, technically, though "head" is generous for a man whose primary function is to reply-all and aspire—watching Kyle Renner set up the bracket board for the first dual meet of the season. Kyle who wears a quarter-zip pullover every day. Kyle who has explained to her twice that he's "building something here" on his way to a high school job in the suburbs. Kyle whose understanding of wrestling is to wrestling what a screensaver of a fireplace is to fire.

The boys are warming up. Fourteen of them, which is six more than last year, and she'd like to say that's because of her coaching but it's probably because the football program cut JV and these are the leftover bodies, the ones too small or too strange for any other sport, the knobby-kneed and the pigeon-toed and the furiously quiet. She loves them with a ferocity that would embarrass her if she examined it, so she does not examine it. She watches them run their laps and slap the walls at each end of the gym and she chews her Tums and she thinks about whether she needs half-and-half, and she does, and also paper towels, and also she needs to call the dentist about the crown that's been "temporary" since March, and also she needs to stop thinking about the way the gym sounds at this hour, in the last light of November, when the sneakers squeak and the mats absorb impact with that thick, quiet exhalation—

Because it sounds like something she doesn't want it to sound like. It sounds like a room that means something. It sounds, with its high ceilings and its acoustic peculiarities and its little rituals of weigh-in and warm-up and the boys kneeling to tie their shoes or stretch their groins, like a room where people have come to practice a devotion they can't name. And Maria Delgado has spent her entire adult life walking away from rooms like that.

The opposing team arrives. Their warm-ups are matching.

Hers are not.

## II.

She sees him for the first time doing the thing during the third week of the season, though he has apparently been doing it since the first practice and she has apparently failed to notice because she's been too busy arguing with Kyle about the weigh-in schedule and sourcing a replacement headgear for Damien Orozco who sweats through everything, including, seemingly, vulcanized rubber. She is across the gym, against the far wall, writing something on a clipboard—she doesn't remember what, maybe the lineup, maybe a grocery list disguised as a lineup—and she looks up and there is Tomás Carrillo on the center mat doing something she cannot immediately classify, and the failure of classification is what makes her keep looking.

He's kneeling. Not the way wrestlers kneel—one knee, tactical, coiled—but both knees, straight-backed, palms flat on the mat in front of him, fingers spread, as if he's trying to feel for something underneath the surface. His eyes are closed. His head is not bowed like prayer; it's level, chin parallel to the ground, and there's a quality to his stillness that doesn't read as concentration or relaxation but as *absence,* as if the visible boy is a shell and whatever lives inside it has temporarily gone somewhere else. He looks like a diver the moment before the water closes over his head. He looks like a person listening to music that isn't playing.

Two of his teammates are staring. Marcus Webb, who is thirteen and has already developed a forty-year-old's talent for skepticism, mouths something to Jaylen Torres. Jaylen gives a half-shrug. Neither of them says anything out loud. That's the strange thing—normally any deviation from the masculine norm in a middle school wrestling room gets punished instantly, verbally, but something about the completeness of Tomás's stillness discourages interruption. Even Marcus, who once nicknamed a kid "Twinkle Toes" for wearing yellow socks, just stands there.

Maria notices—

His wrists. The bones of his wrists where they connect to the flat of his palms on the mat, the tendons visible under skin that is the color of the inside of an almond, and the wrists are thin, disproportionately thin, as if built for something other than force. Her ex-husband had thick wrists. This is not a thought she wants to have in the gymnasium. Gary had thick wrists and square hands and for years she'd found them beautiful and then one day she hadn't and then for the rest of the marriage she'd looked at his hands and thought *I have to leave* and felt nothing. Tomás's wrists are the opposite of Gary's wrists in every way that she doesn't want to catalogue and is cataloguing anyway—delicate where Gary's were blunt, expressive where Gary's were functional, and she's not— She's not *comparing,* she's observing, which is her job, which is the only thing she is consistently good at. Kid has thin wrists. Note it. Move on.

His breathing. She can see it from across the gym. It changes when he places his palms down—slows, deepens, drops into his belly in a way she's only seen in wrestlers with fifteen, twenty years of mat time. This kid is fourteen and breathing like a monk, and she knows what that kind of breathing looks like because her mother—

No. She knows what it looks like because she's coached for two decades. That's all.

Then the match starts and she stops thinking.

Tomás stands up from his kneeling position and walks to the center of the mat and shakes hands with his opponent—a kid from Eastview who outweighs him by at least five pounds of actual muscle—and the ref blows the whistle and what happens next makes no sense.

He doesn't shoot. He doesn't sprawl. He doesn't do any of the things she has drilled into these boys since September. He *moves.* And the moving is—wrong isn't the word. Strange isn't the word. The word, if she were a person who used words like this, which she is not, would be *liquid.* He moves as if his skeleton has temporarily become advisory rather than structural. The Eastview kid reaches for an underhook and Tomás's torso simply isn't where the hand arrives. The kid tries a single-leg and Tomás's hips swivel with a quality that looks less like a defensive technique and more like a physical property, like water finding the crack in a rock, and the Eastview kid stumbles forward into the space Tomás has vacated and Tomás is already behind him, already chest-to-back, already—

Pin.

The Eastview kid stares at the ceiling. The ref slaps the mat. The gym is quiet for a beat too long and then somebody's parent claps.

Maria's clipboard is at her side. Her pen has been uncapped for two minutes and has left a blue dot on the thigh of her khakis that she won't notice until she's home.

Kyle Renner, who has been watching from the scorer's table, catches her eye and gives her a thumbs up and mouths *nice,* as if Tomás's match had anything to do with anything Kyle has ever taught anyone. The opposing coach is checking his phone.

And Tomás walks off the mat and sits on the bench and his face reveals nothing—not triumph, not relief, not even the specific blankness of performed humility—just the neutral expression of a person who has done something that is, to him, unremarkable. He takes a sip of water. He wipes his hands on his warm-ups. He watches the next match.

She asks him about it after practice. She does this badly.

"So what's the—" She gestures at the mat. "Before your matches. The kneeling thing."

He looks at her with an expression she will later describe to herself as *patient,* though what she means is that it makes her feel hurried and clumsy and seen, which is not a thing she enjoys feeling, particularly when the person seeing her is a fourteen-year-old with a collarbone like a coat hanger.

"I'm asking my spine to remember it's a river," he says.

A beat. Two. Three.

"Right," she says. "Sure."

She turns away and puts the Tums in her mouth and chalks the sentence up to adolescent mysticism, to some TikTok sage in linen pants dispensing stolen Zen, to the particular grandiosity of fourteen, when the body is new enough that you can still believe it's whispering to you. She chalks it up to anything she can find.

She does not ask him what it means.

She does not ask because she is afraid he will tell her and she will understand.

## III.

In the car that night—a 2013 Civic with a crack in the windshield that has been migrating slowly from the passenger side toward her like a thought she can't outrun—she sits in the parking lot of a Taco Bell in the passenger seat because the driver's seat has a spring that pokes through the upholstery and she's given up on the idea that she will ever get it fixed. She eats a Cheesy Gordita Crunch. She eats it with the engine off and the windows cracked and the radio on the college station that plays jazz after nine, and she thinks about nothing on purpose, which means she thinks about everything.

*Asking my spine to remember it's a river.* 

Kid's probably got some TikTok guru. Probably one of those accounts with a guy sitting cross-legged on a mountaintop explaining how to "unlock your fascia" while selling a fifty-dollar e-book. Probably just OCD. She had a wrestler three years ago who had to tap the corner post four times before every match and he'd been on an SSRP. SSRI. Whatever. The point is, bodies are weird and teenagers are weirder, and there is no reason to be sitting in a Taco Bell parking lot at 9:47 p.m. thinking about a fourteen-year-old's pre-match ritual when she needs half-and-half and paper towels and to call the dentist about the temporary crown that is becoming, through sheer temporal persistence, permanent.

She needs to renew her CPR certification. She needs to email Kyle the updated weight classes. She needs to return the call from her sister in Tucson, who has started every voicemail for the past three months with "I know you're busy, but—" and then proceeds to narrate a life so full of book clubs and couples brunches and normalized happiness that Maria has to fight the urge to drive into a ditch. She needs—

The sunset. Or what's left of it. A smear of orange and violet above the strip mall across the highway, the sky doing that thing it does in November in this part of the state where the clouds thin out and the colors don't so much appear as hemorrhage, and for about four minutes the parking lot of the Taco Bell and the parking lot of the Dollar General and the parking lot of the laundromat called SUDS CITY whose sign has been missing the C since she moved here are all illuminated in a light so absurdly, embarrassingly beautiful that it feels like an accusation. She doesn't look at it. She looks at it. She looks away. She takes a bite of the Gordita Crunch and a piece of cheese falls on her khakis—on the blue pen dot she still hasn't noticed—and she wipes it with a napkin and the light changes and is gone.

At the booster club meeting on Thursday, a man named Todd Something—Todd Whitmore, Todd Whitfield, she retains his face and his polo shirt but not his surname, which is a choice her brain makes without consulting her—explains to the assembled parents that the wrestling program needs "better brand identity." He has a PowerPoint. He has used the word *synergy.* He has a slide with a proposed logo that is a lion wearing a singlet, and the lion is flexing, and the lion has a facial expression that suggests both constipation and triumph, and Maria sits in the back row of the cafeteria and chews a Tums and marvels quietly at the capacity of the American parent to transform any human activity into a marketing opportunity. Todd Something's son wrestles 126 and has the cardiovascular endurance of a throw pillow, but Todd Something has energy and opinions and a laminated badge from his sales job that he wears to these meetings, she suspects, because it makes him feel jurisdictional.

But the kid—Todd Something's kid—is there, in the back of the cafeteria near the vending machines, folding programs. Nobody has asked him to fold them. He's just doing it. His hands move with a meticulous, unwitnessed seriousness, creasing each fold with his thumbnail, stacking them with their edges aligned, and his face has the expression of absolute absorption that she recognizes from—

No or yes. Fine. From Tomás on the mat. From that specific quality of attention that has nothing to do with thinking and everything to do with the body's private relationship with its task. The kid folding programs will never be a good wrestler. He's too slow, too polite, too willing to be pushed. But he folds programs like a person building a cathedral one stone at a time, and Maria watches him for a moment too long and then looks away and then Todd Something is asking her a question about the logo and she says "Looks great, Todd" and does not know his last name and does not, for one wild second, know her own.
