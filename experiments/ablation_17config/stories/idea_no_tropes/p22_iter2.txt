# The Dentist West of Dodge City

## I.

The office of Eldon Krebs occupied the ground floor of a clapboard building on Ford Bend's main street, between Havershaw's dry-goods store and the lot where Dunlap kept his farrier's equipment under a canvas lean-to. The building had been a land office before the railroad survey shifted south and took the speculators with it, and Eldon had purchased it in the spring of 1876 for forty dollars and a promise to maintain the exterior. He had kept the promise. The porch boards were sound, the steps were swept, and the shingle hung straight: E. KREBS, D.D.S. — EXTRACTIONS, FILINGS, PLATES. The letters had been painted by Eldon himself, in a blue that matched the particular shade favored by the College of Dental Surgery in Cincinnati, where he had studied for two years before heading west.

Inside, the office was twelve feet by fourteen. A Ritter operating chair, purchased secondhand from a retiring practitioner in Wichita, sat in the center of the room. Beside it, a small oak table held the instruments in their proper order: the extraction forceps — upper universal, lower bicuspid, cowhorn for molars — the elevators, the scalers, the mouth mirror, a porcelain bowl for spit and blood, cotton wadding in a glass jar, and a brown bottle of ether with a folded cloth beside it. Along the far wall stood a cabinet with three drawers, the top two holding supplies, the bottom one locked. A window with a cotton curtain let in eastern light. The floor was pine, sanded and sealed, and Eldon mopped it with lye water each evening. There was no carpet. Carpet held what a dentist's floor collected, and Eldon did not believe in holding what ought to be cleaned away.

On this morning in October of 1878, the man in the Ritter chair was Gerald Parkins, a teamster who hauled goods between Ford Bend and the railroad depot at Cimarron. Parkins was forty-one, thick through the shoulders, and frightened. His right lower second molar had cracked along the root, and the gum around it had gone scarlet and hot, and for six days he had packed the socket with clove-soaked cotton and drunk whiskey until the pain became merely the loudest thing in the room rather than the only thing. Now the clove had stopped working and the whiskey had run out and Parkins sat with his mouth open and his fingers white on the chair's armrests while Eldon examined the tooth with a probe.

"Root's split clean through," Eldon said. "No saving it, Mr. Parkins. We'll have it out."

Parkins nodded. He had nodded before Eldon finished the sentence, the way they always did when the pain had become a residency rather than a visit.

Eldon folded the cloth into a cone and uncapped the brown bottle. For three months he had been replacing what he used from the bottle with lamp oil — not all at once, but steadily, a ratio he adjusted by instinct rather than measurement, so that the ether that wet the cloth was now more than half kerosene by volume. He held the cloth over Parkins's nose and mouth. "Breathe as deep as you can, now. Slow and deep."

Parkins breathed. His eyes stayed open. They drifted, fogged somewhat, but did not close. Eldon watched the muscles in the man's jaw slacken partially, a loosening that resembled sleep the way a sketch resembles its subject. He set the cloth aside and selected the cowhorn forceps.

The extraction took ninety seconds. Parkins groaned throughout — a sustained, guttural sound, the body's low protest lodged somewhere beneath whatever diminished consciousness the ether had left him. His hands clenched and released. His boots scraped the footrest. At one point a sound came from his throat that was higher than the groan, sharper, a brief bright note that broke through the murk of the anesthetic like a bird breaking the surface of water, and then it was over. Eldon held the fractured molar up to the light, examined the roots — both pieces, cleanly split, no fragments left behind — and placed it in the porcelain bowl.

"That's done," he said.

He packed the socket with cotton and oil of cloves — real cloves, full strength; Eldon did not economize with aftercare — and gave Parkins instructions: no chewing on that side, salt rinses twice a day, return in a week or sooner if the bleeding would not stop. Parkins stood carefully, one hand on the chair. His face was damp and pale but already reorganizing itself around the relief of aftermath. The pain of the last six days was gone. What remained was the fresh, clean ache of a wound that would heal.

"Thank you, Doc," Parkins said, or tried to say; the packing made it indistinct. He put two silver dollars on the oak table.

"One is sufficient," Eldon said.

"Take both. You earned it."

Eldon took both. He walked Parkins to the door, watched him descend the porch steps into the morning light, and returned to his office. He emptied the porcelain bowl, rinsed it, wiped the forceps with alcohol, and replaced each instrument in its position on the tray. Then he mopped the area around the chair, though there was little to mop. It had been a clean extraction. Most of his extractions were clean. Whatever else could be said about the practice of Eldon Krebs, his technique was meticulous, his hands were steady, and his patients kept their appointments.

He stood at the window for a moment, watching the street. A dog trotted past. The morning was cool and dry, the sky a pale even blue that would deepen by noon. He had two more patients scheduled before midday and another in the afternoon, a child with a milk tooth that had abscessed and whose mother had been told by the schoolteacher that Dr. Krebs was gentle with children and it would be all right.

He returned to his instruments and began to lay out what he would need for the next procedure.

## II.

The town of Ford Bend had existed for eleven years and had been incorporated for three. It sat on the north bank of a creek that fed the Arkansas River somewhere to the southeast, in a stretch of country where the shortgrass prairie began to give way to the sandhills and the wind came from the west with nothing to break it for two hundred miles. It was not Dodge City. The people of Ford Bend said this often and meant it as a point of pride. Dodge City, twenty-six miles to the east, had its cattle trade and its railroad and its famous sheriff and its famous violence and its women whose names appeared in newspapers as far away as Chicago, and Ford Bend wanted none of it. Ford Bend wanted a schoolhouse, which it had. A church, which it had, though the roof leaked. A general store, a post office, a blacksmith, and a dentist.

It had a dentist.

There was general agreement, among the families who had settled in Ford Bend and the ranchers who used it as their supply town and the drovers who occasionally passed through on their way to somewhere more exciting, that Eldon Krebs was one of the better things the town had going for it. Not the best thing — that was the water, which came from a good well and had no alkali taste — but close. He had arrived in the spring of 1876 with a wagonload of equipment, a certificate from a genuine dental college, and a manner so quiet and steady that people trusted him before they had any evidence that they should. The evidence came soon enough. He fixed what could be fixed, pulled what couldn't, and charged fairly. He did not drink. He did not gamble. He attended church, though he sat in the back and did not sing. He paid his property tax on time and had once, without being asked, repaired the hinge on the schoolhouse gate.

Clara Havershaw, the dry-goods merchant's wife, kept a clean-porch index in her mind — a private ranking of the town's residents by the maintenance of their front stoops — and Eldon Krebs had occupied the top position since the week he arrived. She said so to her husband, who repeated it in the store, and it became one of those small facts that circulate through a town until they are no longer gossip but geography: *Krebs keeps a clean porch.* It meant more than a clean porch. It meant a man you could rely on, a citizen of the type that towns needed if they were going to remain towns and not dissolve back into prairie at the first hard winter.

The schoolteacher, Miss Addie Parsons, recommended Dr. Krebs to every child who came to school crying with a toothache, which happened often enough that she had developed a standard speech. "Dr. Krebs is very gentle," she would say, kneeling before whatever small, ruined face had appeared at her desk that morning. "He will explain everything he is going to do before he does it, and it will be over very quickly, and afterward your mouth will feel so much better that you will wonder why you waited." She believed this fully. She had been his patient herself — a cracked filling, replaced without incident — and she had found him calm, attentive, and precise. The fact that she had felt more of the procedure than she expected she attributed to her own low tolerance for discomfort, a personal failing she had always suspected and now felt confirmed.

A rancher named Elias Coombs had, the previous winter, developed an abscess so severe that the left side of his face swelled to the size and color of a ripe plum, and he had ridden nine miles through sleet to reach Eldon's office. Eldon drained the abscess, extracted the tooth, and kept Coombs in the operating chair for two hours while the bleeding slowed, feeding him sips of water and speaking to him in a low, even voice about the habits of pronghorn antelope, which Coombs found interesting and later said had kept him from panicking. Eldon charged him one dollar. Coombs's wife sent a casserole the following week — salt pork and beans and dried corn, in a Dutch oven she expected returned — and after that, the Coombs family did not go to any other dentist, though the nearest alternative was in Dodge City and by some accounts better equipped.

People greeted Eldon on the street and he greeted them back. He tipped his hat to women. He stopped to speak to children. He had once carried three sacks of flour from Havershaw's store to the church kitchen for the pastor's wife without being asked, simply because he was passing when the delivery arrived and she was standing in the doorway looking at the sacks with the expression of a woman calculating the distance between where the flour was and where the flour needed to be. He did not court anyone, which some of the women in town found regrettable and others found suspicious, but most found simply consistent with the rest of him: a man of deep domestic order who had, perhaps, ordered himself so thoroughly that there was no room left over for the disorder that courtship required.

He fed the stray dogs that gathered behind the buildings on the west end of town. Not lavishly — scraps, put out in a tin plate each evening, retrieved and washed each morning. The dogs came to know his schedule and would be sitting in a loose group behind his building as the light failed, and he would come out with the plate and set it down and stand for a moment watching them eat, and then go inside. He did not name them. He did not pet them. But he fed them, and they were fed, and in Ford Bend this was known and counted.

## III.

Pastor Alden and his wife, Cordelia, lived in a three-room house attached to the back of the church by a covered walkway that leaked in the same places the church roof leaked, so that on rainy Sundays the pastor arrived at his pulpit already damp. They had been in Ford Bend for four years. They had no children. They had a garden that produced squash, and they had each other, and they had a habit of inviting someone to dinner every Thursday, which Cordelia said was for fellowship and Alden said was to keep them from going strange, the way couples did when they talked only to each other for too long.

On this Thursday, the guest was Eldon.

He arrived at six with a small jar of honey he had purchased from a beekeeper outside of Cimarron, and Cordelia accepted it with the genuine delight of a woman for whom honey was not routine. The table was set with three places, a cloth that had been white before the Kansas dust got to it, and dishes that did not match but were clean and unchipped. Dinner was jackrabbit stew with carrots and the last of the garden's squash, biscuits made with lard, and coffee that had been boiled twice because Alden believed second-boiled coffee had more character, a position Cordelia disputed but had stopped arguing.

"This is very good, Mrs. Alden," Eldon said, and he meant it. The stew was good. The biscuits were good. The coffee was terrible in a way that was familiar and therefore comforting, and he drank two cups.

"Cordelia," she said. "I have told you. If we are to eat together, you will call me Cordelia."

"Cordelia," Eldon said, and smiled, and the smile was small and real.

The conversation moved where Thursday-dinner conversation moved: to the church roof, which had been leaking since spring; to the cost of lumber, which had risen; to the question of whether the congregation could raise the money by subscription or whether someone would need to write to the mission board in Topeka, which Alden considered humiliating and Cordelia considered practical. Eldon listened with his head slightly tilted, the way he listened to patients describing their symptoms — close attention that did not interrupt.

"I would like to contribute," Eldon said, when Alden paused. "Toward the roof."

"That is generous, Eldon, but —"

"Ten dollars," Eldon said. "It is not enough to fix it, but it is enough to begin."

Alden looked at his wife. Cordelia looked at Eldon. Ten dollars was not a small amount. It was five extractions, or ten fillings, or one month's rent on the building that housed his practice. Eldon knew this. He offered it the way he offered everything — quietly, without ceremony, as if the decision had been made before the conversation and the conversation was merely the mechanism by which it reached the world.

"We are grateful," Cordelia said.

"The roof should not leak on a man while he is trying to preach," Eldon said, and this was a joke, or near enough to one, and they laughed, and the evening continued.

Alden spoke about a family homesteading to the northwest who had lost a child to scarlet fever, and about his visit to them, and about the difficulty of offering comfort when comfort was not, strictly speaking, available. Eldon listened. He asked how the family was managing for food, and when Alden said they were managing poorly, Eldon said he would put together a package from Havershaw's and have it sent. This was the kind of thing Eldon did. It was not calculated. It arose from the same place in him that fed the dogs and carried the flour and kept the porch swept — some interior mechanism of order and responsibility that operated as continuously and unthinkingly as a heartbeat.

Cordelia, watching him across the table as he spoke with her husband about the bereaved family's provisions, thought, not for the first time, that Eldon Krebs was the kind of man who made a town feel like a town. There were men who extracted resources from a place — the cattlemen, the speculators — and men who deposited something into it, some ballast of reliability that kept the whole enterprise from blowing away. Eldon was the second kind. She trusted him the way she trusted the well water: deeply, automatically, without the need for continuous reassessment. There was nothing in his manner that gave her pause. There was nothing in his eyes that she needed to look away from. He was simply a good man whose company was pleasant, and she was glad he had come to dinner, and she told him so as he put on his hat at the door.

"It has been a fine evening," Eldon said. "Thank you, Cordelia. Pastor."

"Walk safe," Alden said.

"Always do."

He walked home through the dark. The street was empty, the buildings closed, the sky enormous and cloudless, salted with stars so dense and bright that they seemed almost to have texture, as if the sky were not a void but a surface. The air smelled of grass and cold and the distant ghost of woodsmoke from someone's stove.
