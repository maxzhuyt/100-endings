# Every Thursday at 2pm

---

The printer jams on Tuesdays. I don't know why Tuesdays. Dr. Lam thinks it's the humidity, because the building's HVAC cycles differently at the start of the week, and she's probably right, because Dr. Lam is right about mechanical things and wrong about people, which is not the quality you hope for in a psychotherapist but is the quality you get. I unjam the printer. I file the insurance claims. I replace the water cooler jug by myself because the delivery guy just leaves them in the hall, five gallons, forty-two pounds, and I squat-lift it the way my first boyfriend taught me, which is the only useful thing he ever taught me, and I carry it to the cooler, and I flip it, and the bubbles rise, and then I sit back down at my desk, and I wait.

Waiting is ninety percent of the job. You would think answering phones, but no. Waiting. Sitting in the particular quiet of a place where everyone is about to say the thing they cannot say anywhere else, and the quiet before that saying has a texture I could describe but won't, because you either know it or you don't, the way a dog knows rain is coming by the smell of ozone, the way you know when someone at a party is about to cry — it's not magic, it's just that I've been sitting in this particular quiet for eleven years, and eleven years of other people's almost-confessions will do something to you. I don't mean it made me wise. I mean it made me porous.

Mr. Edelman comes in at nine on Mondays and sits with his hands pressed flat on his thighs as if he is keeping his own legs from running. He tells Dr. Lam about his marriage. What he actually carries through my waiting room — what I can see on him the way you'd see a tan line or a bruise — is not marital dissatisfaction but a specific, annihilating grief for a man he loved at twenty-two, never touched, and has never mentioned to anyone alive. The grief sits on him like a smell. I process his copay. I know. These things coexist.

Mrs. Tranh, Wednesdays, 4pm: she wears silk blouses in jewel tones and she brings me miniature pineapple cakes from the bakery on Clement Street and she is generous and funny and the thing she carries is a rage so total it has its own weather system — not at her husband, not at her mother, but at her own body, which she has experienced since puberty as a betrayal she cannot prosecute. She sits in the waiting room eating one of the pineapple cakes she brought for me and she smiles and the rage comes off her in waves and I say *thank you, Mrs. Tranh, she'll be right with you,* and I eat the cake and it's delicious.

The college kid, Terrence, Thursdays at noon: he comes in with his earbuds still in, head down, the studied nonchalance of someone who believes no one has ever understood him, and what I read on him is so simple it nearly breaks my heart — he wants permission. That's it. Just blanket, unconditional permission to be the specific, odd, unsortable person he already is. He wants someone to say *yes, that, exactly that, go ahead.* He makes it through the entire waiting room ritual — check-in, clipboard, chair — in under ninety seconds. The wanting is so clean it's almost architectural. A single room with all the doors sealed.

I'm not psychic. I want to be clear about that. Psychic implies revelation, intervention, a responsibility to act on what you see. What I have is closer to a allergy. Prolonged exposure to the specific chemical compound of people about to be honest — the cortisol, the adrenaline, the sweat that smells different when it's shame-sweat versus fear-sweat versus the weird, bright, almost sweet sweat of someone about to say something they've never said — and after eleven years my body just... reads it. I don't decide to know. I know the way my knee knows the weather. I know with the dull, physical certainty of a body that has been in proximity to too much undressed yearning for too long. It is not a superpower. It is an occupational hazard. Most days, it's just noise.

I file the claims. I unjam the printer. I know what everyone in this building wants more than they have ever wanted anything, and I answer the phone and I say, "Family and Individual Services, this is Margot, how can I help you."

---

She came in on a Thursday. The first Thursday in October. I remember because Terrence had just left and the waiting room still held the shape of his quiet, hunched wanting, and then the door opened and it was like someone had opened a window in a room that had been closed for years — not fresh air exactly, more like a sudden change in pressure that makes your ears pop.

She was small. That was the first thing. Short, narrow-shouldered, the kind of body that disappears in a crowd, and she was working against that disappearance with everything she had: a yellow cardigan, red Keds, earrings shaped like little apples. The outfit of someone who spends her days at the eye level of five-year-olds and has decided, either consciously or not, to become the kind of bright, legible person a five-year-old could trust at first sight. Her hair was dark and cut in a bob that she'd clearly done herself, the left side slightly shorter than the right. She was thirty-four, I'd learn from the intake form. She looked both older and younger than that, simultaneously.

"Hi!" she said, and the exclamation point was real, unstaged, the genuine punctuation of a person who finds greeting strangers to be a small, renewable pleasure. "I'm Elena Morse? I have a two o'clock with Dr. Lam?"

Everything was a question. Not from uncertainty — from an excess of politeness that made her phrase even declarative facts as offerings, as things you could accept or refuse.

"You're a bit early," I said. "Have a seat, she'll be with you in just a few."

"Oh, I know, I'm always early, it's like a disease. My ex-husband used to say I'd be early to my own funeral."

She laughed. The laugh was real too. She had said *ex-husband* without flinching, which meant the divorce was either very old or very new, and from the way her throat moved when she swallowed after saying it — a tiny, convulsive motion, the swallow of someone resetting after an unexpected blow to the sternum — I knew it was new. Weeks, maybe. A month.

"I love this plant," she said, touching the pothos on the side table. "Is it real? It looks so healthy. I always kill them. My kindergarteners and I planted beans once and I was the only one whose bean didn't sprout, and they were *so* concerned. Five years old and giving me this look of just, profound pity — "

She was laughing again, and I was smiling because you could not help smiling at her, and the whole time I was reading her.

I don't know how to describe what I read without sounding clinical, which would be wrong, or mystical, which would be worse. It wasn't a word. It wasn't an image. It was a — pressure. A field. Like standing near a storm drain after a heavy rain: the sound of water moving underground toward an opening. The force of something that has been contained looking for a way out.

What Elena Morse carried was the largest unacknowledged hunger I had ever felt in that waiting room, and I had been sitting at that desk for eleven years.

Not sexual. That's the wrong word, or anyway the insufficient word. Hunger is closer. She wanted — she *needed* — to be known. To be perceived with accuracy. To have someone look at her, the actual specific irreducible her, and say not *you're beautiful* or *you're enough* or any of the poster-paint affirmations she probably told her kindergarteners twenty times a day, but something more like: *I see exactly what you are, including the parts you think are ugly, and I am not leaving.* Her marriage, whatever it had been, had not done this. Her marriage had, I thought, done something like the opposite: it had convinced her she was the bright, legible person in the yellow cardigan, and she had believed it because believing it was easier than knowing what she actually wanted, which was so large, so inarticulate, so shapeless in its enormity that she had probably never come close to naming it, not even in the dark, not even alone.

I have read a thousand variations of want. This was different in scale. Elena Morse's hunger had been pressurized by decades of sunniness. It was tectonic. And it was sitting in my waiting room in red Keds, telling me about beans.

She filled out the intake form in careful, rounded handwriting — a kindergarten teacher's handwriting, legible from across a room. She checked *Divorce/Separation* under Presenting Concern. She wrote *I want to feel like myself again* in the space marked Additional Notes. She apologized for her handwriting, which was immaculate. She handed the clipboard back to me and our fingers touched and the hunger in her was so loud, so close, that I pulled back slightly, involuntarily, the way you pull back from a stove you didn't know was on.

"Sorry," she said. "My hands are always cold. Circulation thing."

"No worries," I said. "She'll be right with you, Ms. Morse."

"Elena, please. Ms. Morse is my — " She stopped. Reset. Smiled. "Elena is fine."

Dr. Lam's door opened. Elena stood up, tugged her cardigan straight, and walked in with the squared shoulders of someone entering a room where she has decided to be brave. The door closed. I sat at my desk, and the waiting room felt like it always felt after she left it, which is to say: like a room where something very large had been standing and then wasn't.

I marked her in the schedule. Thursday, 2pm, recurring.

---

She came back. Three Thursdays later — or, I should say, she came back every Thursday, but the third one was when it started.

I knew something had changed the moment the door opened. It was the walk. She came in with a new way of holding her weight, a new center of gravity, like someone who has recently been physically rearranged and is still learning the dimensions of her revised body. She was wearing the same kind of outfit — bright, primary, legible — but her skin was different. There's a specific luminosity that new sex gives certain complexions, a flush that sits just under the surface like a lamp behind a curtain, and Elena had it, unmistakably. She was almost vibrating.

"Margot," she said, and sat down in the chair closest to my desk, which no one ever did — clients sat in the chairs along the wall, maintaining the distance that the waiting room's geometry was designed to enforce. She pulled the chair even closer. She was grinning.

"I have to tell someone," she said. "I can't — I know you're not — I just need to *say* it."

I should have redirected her. *Dr. Lam will be right with you, Elena.* That's the script. But I didn't, because — I'll be honest — I liked her. Three Thursdays had been enough. She brought me coffee once, without asking, correctly guessed my order. She remembered my name the first time she heard it. She asked about my weekend and then listened to the answer. In a profession where I am furniture, she treated me as a person, and I am not above being warmed by that.

"Okay," I said.

"I met someone," she said, and the someone landed in the room like a flare, bright and smoking. "His name is Adrian. We met at — okay, this is embarrassing — we met at the grocery store? The Safeway on Geary? I was buying frozen peas and he was just... *there,* and I know how that sounds, I *know,* but Margot, I have to tell you, when he looked at me — "

She paused. Her eyes were doing something I'd seen before, the particular middle-distance focus of someone replaying a scene so intense it overrides present vision.

"When he looked at me, I felt — *recognized.* Like he already knew things about me. Not in a creepy way. In the way where you meet someone and your body just... goes, *oh, there you are.* Does that make sense? Is that insane?"

"It's not insane," I said, because it wasn't, and because I was reading her while she spoke.

What I read was new. Under the luminosity, under the giddiness, there was a layer that hadn't been there before — a thrumming, a kind of saturation that reminded me of how a rag looks when it's taken on all the water it can hold and the water starts to change the rag's shape, makes it heavier, makes it into something that is no longer quite a rag.

"Anyway," she said, and her voice dropped to a conspiratorial half-whisper, and she tucked her hair behind her ear, which revealed that her neck was faintly pink along the left side — not a bruise, not a hickey, more like a rash, or like something had been pressing against the skin for a long time without breaking it — "the sex. Margot. The *sex.*"

She laughed, and it was the laugh of someone who has never been able to say sentences like this in her life and is experiencing the physical euphoria of finally saying them.

"I've never — I didn't know it could — okay. So. My marriage. I loved my husband. I did. But sex with Dan was like... receiving a competent massage? Like, fine, pleasant, technically proficient, and the whole time I'm lying there thinking about whether I remembered to move the laundry to the dryer. You know? Not his fault. That's the thing. It was never his fault. I think I just thought that was what it was."

She was leaning forward. Her pupils were large, and there was a dampness at her temples, the faintest sheen, as if recounting this cost her something physical.

"Adrian is — I don't know how to — he touches me like he already knows. Like he can *feel* what I feel. His hands — " She stopped, re-approached. "Okay, this is going to sound strange. But when he holds me, sometimes his hands feel like they're — bigger than they should be? Not in a scary way. Like they *expand.* Like they can cover more of me than two hands should be able to cover. Like being held by someone with — more. More surface area than a person should have."

She said it with wonder. She said it the way a person says *the sunset was unbelievable,* as hyperbole, as a metaphor she didn't know she was making.

I didn't hear it as metaphor. My body heard it the way my body hears everything, in the deep somatic register where knowledge lives before language, and what my body heard was: *that is not a metaphor. His hands are bigger than they should be. They cover more than they should cover. That is a physical description of a physical event, and she is too dazzled, too grateful, too starved, to hear herself saying what she is saying.*

"That sounds like quite a connection," I said.

"It's *unreal,*" she said. "*Literally* unreal. Like — I keep waiting to wake up."

Dr. Lam opened her door. Elena stood up, smoothed her cardigan, shot me a look of girlish complicity — *our secret* — and walked in.
