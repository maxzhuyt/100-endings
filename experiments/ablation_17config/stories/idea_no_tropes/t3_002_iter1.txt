# Forms of Devotion

## I.

Let us be clear about what Valentine had done with his dying.

He had done it well. He had given away his sandals, which were poor, and his cloak, which was poorer, and his wooden cup, which was not worth giving away but which he gave away regardless because a man who is to be beheaded in the morning should enter the Lord's house with empty hands and the quiet, rinsed dignity of someone who has settled all accounts. He had prayed. He had wept — briefly, and only once, and not for himself but for the couples he had married in secret, in root cellars and drainage ditches and once in a tannery so foul that the bride vomited during the vows, which Valentine had taken as a sign of the Holy Spirit because he had learned, in his years of illegal pastoral work, that the Spirit manifests where it will and frequently where it smells worst. He had forgiven his jailers. He had forgiven the Emperor Claudius, though this required some effort. He had composed, in his mind, the final shape of his death — the walk to the block, the cold kiss of February air, the sword, the drop, the white — and had found it good.

He was ready.

He had, in fact, been ready since Vespers, which was now four hours ago, and the readiness had begun to curdle in the way that all readiness curdles when the thing one is ready for fails to arrive, which is to say: the spiritual composure he had so carefully assembled was beginning to resemble not peace but the particular restlessness of a man who has been waiting at a ferry landing and suspects the ferry has sunk.

Then the door opened, and the man who entered was not the executioner.

He was short. He was balding in the pattern that suggested not age but a kind of constitutional defeat, as though his hair had reviewed the conditions of his employment and declined to participate. He carried, in both arms, a stack of papyrus sheets so thick it altered his center of gravity, and he set this stack on the stone floor with the controlled grunt of a man who performs this action thirty, forty times a day and has made his peace with its indignity. Behind him, barely visible in the corridor, stood a second man holding a reed stylus and a clay pot of ink and an expression that could only be described as pre-apologetic.

"Valentinus of Terni," the first man said. He did not phrase it as a question. He consulted the top sheet. "Priest. Unlicensed. Charged under the Edict of — one moment." He flipped a page. Flipped another. Found what he was looking for in the middle of the stack, nodded, and returned to the top sheet with the brisk, dissatisfied efficiency of a man who knows that the information should have been on the first page and that its absence there represents a systemic failure for which he has filed seven complaints, all unanswered. "Charged under the Edicts of Decius as renewed, collated, and amended by Claudius II Gothicus, with the illegal solemnization of matrimonial bonds between Roman citizens during a period of martial conscription. Sentence: capital. Method: decapitation. Scheduled: dawn, fourteen Kalends Martius." A pause. "Tomorrow."

"Yes," said Valentine.

"I am Sub-Clerk Gaius Pomponius Crispus of the Office of Terminal Certification, Bureau of Penultimate Affairs, Third Corridor, Left Wing, Sub-basement." He said this the way a river says its name to a stone: with the force of long habit and no expectation of being remembered. "I am required by statute — Imperial Procedural Code, Volume XXII, Section 9, Subsection 14, Paragraph 'f' as in *felix*, Clause 3 — to inform you that your execution cannot proceed until you have personally reviewed, certified, and furnished corporeal authentication for the following documents."

He gestured at the stack.

Valentine looked at the stack.

The stack looked back with the blank, patient density of something that has nowhere to be and all of eternity to get there.

"All of them?" Valentine asked.

"These are the primary forms," said Gaius Pomponius Crispus. "The secondary forms will arrive with the second shift."

There is a particular silence that occurs when a man who has prepared to die for love is informed that love will have to wait because the intake paperwork is not complete. It is not the silence of despair, nor of rage, nor even of the absurd. It is the silence of a wheel that has been spinning freely and suddenly discovers it is attached to a mechanism — a vast, interlocking, deeply indifferent mechanism — and that its spinning, however beautiful, however fast, was never the point. The mechanism was the point. The mechanism is always the point.

Valentine sat down on his bench.

"Where," he said, "do I sign?"

This narrator — and it is best to be honest about these things early, before a relationship is established under false pretenses — has seen a great many files. Files pertaining to saints, to martyrs, to men and women who burned with such conviction that their burning became, in the retelling, indistinguishable from light. The files are, without exception, incomplete. There is always a form missing, a signature unwitnessed, a specimen lost in transit between one office and another. This narrator has stood in the corridors of every bureaucracy that has ever processed a human soul — Roman, ecclesiastical, imperial, federal, digital — and has observed, with a tenderness that long ago curdled into something more durable, that the architecture is always the same. The corridors are always long. The lighting is always insufficient. And the people who work there are always, in their own way, kind.

Gaius Pomponius Crispus was kind. This should be noted. He had a wife, Volumnia, who suffered from a disease of the joints, and each evening he returned to their apartment above a fuller's shop and rubbed oil into her swollen fingers while she told him about the cat, which had again gotten into the garum. He did not enjoy his work. He did not not enjoy it. He performed it with the underwater competence of a man who had discovered, at some point in his early twenties, that competence was the only form of dignity available to him, and who had since refined that competence into something almost beautiful, the way a stone in a riverbed becomes almost beautiful: not through ambition but through the steady application of current.

He handed Valentine the first form.

*Certificatio Mortis Voluntariae (Primaria)*.

It was written in a Latin so dense with sub-clauses that the main verb — *morior*, I die — did not appear until the fourteenth line, by which point it had been modified, conditioned, and subordinated to such a degree that it no longer meant "I die" so much as "I acknowledge that a death event, herein defined as the cessation of biological function as certified by no fewer than two licensed thanatological inspectors, may, subject to the conditions herein enumerated, be administered to the undersigned, who by the act of signing confirms that said death event is — " and here the sentence turned a corner and continued down the margin in smaller handwriting, as though the scribe who copied it had begun to lose not his nerve but his papyrus.

Valentine read it.

He read it again.

He signed.

## II.

The blood was the first thing.

Every condemned person, Gaius Pomponius Crispus explained — with the tone of a man who has explained this so many times that the explanation has become a kind of music, a chant, the words worn smooth of all meaning and left with only rhythm — was required to furnish, alongside his written signature, a specimen of living blood, to be affixed in the space marked SIGILLUM VIVUM at the bottom of the primary certificatio. This was not symbolic. This was not ritual. This was, Crispus emphasized, *evidentiary*. The blood proved that the signatory was alive at the moment of signing and therefore legally capable of consenting to the termination of the aliveness that the blood itself demonstrated. Yes, Crispus acknowledged, there was a circularity here. He had noted it in a memorandum. The memorandum had not received a response.

The blood was drawn by the second man — not Crispus but his assistant, who introduced himself as Titus Flavius Opilio, Sub-Sub-Clerk, Bureau of Corporeal Evidentiary Authentication, certified in the drawing of authenticating fluids (Classes I through IV, which covered blood, bile, lymph, and a category translated roughly as "clear weeping of the flesh," which seemed to mean sweat but might also, Opilio conceded, encompass tears, mucus, or the fluid that sometimes issued from blisters, a question that had been referred to the Office of Taxonomic Clarification and was currently pending). Opilio produced a small bronze lancet, cleaned it on the hem of his tunic — "sterility is encouraged but not mandated by current protocol" — and pricked Valentine's left thumb.

The blood rose in a bead, dark and honest.

Valentine pressed his thumb to the space marked SIGILLUM VIVUM.

The blood spread. It spread the way blood does — which is to say, further than anyone intends and into shapes no one authorized. The print that resulted was not a print so much as a smear, an approximation, a record of contact between flesh and papyrus that communicated not identity but incident. Valentine lifted his thumb. On the form, the smear sat in the white space like a small wound.

Crispus looked at it.

Opilio looked at it.

From the corridor, where he had apparently been standing for some time — how long was unclear, as his presence had the retroactive quality of a stain one notices only after the cloth has dried — a third man materialized. He was taller than Crispus and thinner than Opilio and his face bore the particular expression of a person whose entire professional function was the identification of insufficiency. He carried no tablet. He needed no tablet. He was the tablet.

"The seal is smudged," he said.

"I can see that," said Crispus.

"A smudged seal constitutes a non-seal under Section 14, Paragraph 'g' as in *gravitas*, Clause 7a."

"I know."

"A new form will be required."

"I know."

"And a new specimen."

"I *know*."

The new form was identical to the old form, or perhaps it was not — Crispus explained, with the fatigue of someone recounting a familiar injustice, that each copy was technically a *new* document with its own registration number, which meant it required its own certification, which meant — but he trailed off, because Valentine was already extending his thumb, and Opilio was already positioning the lancet, and the mechanism was already in motion, the small and ancient mechanism of the body being asked to open itself for the benefit of a system that would process the opening without comment.

Valentine bled again. This time the seal was cleaner. The third man — who had not offered his name and would not, because his function preceded and exceeded his personhood in the way that a doorway precedes and exceeds the particular wood of which it is made — inspected it, produced a grunt that denoted sufficiency, and withdrew into the corridor, where he presumably continued to exist, although this narrator has learned not to assume.

Then Crispus produced *Certificatio Mortis Voluntariae, Supplementum A*.

"This form," he said, and here his voice took on a slight, almost imperceptible note of apology, the way a physician's voice changes when he turns from the routine examination to the part that will be unpleasant, "requires a specimen of bile."

Valentine looked at him.

"Bile," Valentine repeated.

"Or a bilious substance. The statute uses the term *humor felli similis*, which translates — " Crispus consulted a marginal gloss that someone had penciled on the form in handwriting so small it was less writing than trembling — "as 'a humor similar to bile, or resembling bile in color, viscosity, or temperament.' The Office of Taxonomic Clarification has not yet ruled on whether 'temperament' refers to the humor's medical temperament or its metaphorical disposition. A ruling is expected." He paused. "It has been expected for eleven years."

Opilio produced a clay pot. It was small, round, the orange-brown of institutional pottery, with a cork stopper and a paper label on which someone had written, in careful block letters, SPEC. B — FEL. The pot had clearly held other men's bile before. It had probably been rinsed. It had the look of a vessel that had been rinsed.

"You may take as long as you need," said Crispus, and the gentleness in his voice was real, which made it worse.

Valentine held the pot.

The human body — and this narrator says this not with authority but with the weary intimacy of long acquaintance — does not produce bile on command. Bile is manufactured in the liver, stored in the gallbladder, and secreted into the duodenum in response to the ingestion of fats, which is to say: bile is a substance of reaction, not of will. One cannot summon bile the way one summons speech or tears or even an erection, each of which, while not entirely voluntary, can at least be coaxed by the appropriate mental imagery. There is no mental imagery for bile. Bile is the body's anonymous clerk, performing its function in darkness, asking neither for recognition nor gratitude, and when asked to step forward and identify itself — to *appear*, to be seen, to fill a pot for the evidentiary benefit of an institution that does not know what it is or why it is asking for it — bile declines. Not out of malice. Out of the simple structural fact that it has no mechanism for compliance.

Valentine tried.

He pressed the pot against his abdomen, as though proximity might help. He closed his eyes. He thought about the liver, that dark lobe, that silent partner, and attempted to communicate with it the way he communicated with God — through silence, through attention, through the mute pressure of need. The liver did not respond. The liver had never responded. The liver, like God, operated on its own schedule.

He retched, eventually. This seemed to be what was expected. Opilio held the pot at the appropriate angle while Valentine's body convulsed with the particular violence of an organism trying to produce something it was not designed to produce on demand, and what emerged — thin, yellowish, more gastric than bilious but in the neighborhood, in the general postal district of bile — collected in the bottom of the pot like a small, sorry offering, which is what it was.

Crispus sealed the pot. Labeled it. Set it beside the form. And produced, with the air of a man who has three more hours of this and no illusions about any of them, *Supplementum B*, which required a stool sample, and *Supplementum C*, which required a specimen of lymph, and *Supplementum D*, which — but we will come to Supplementum D.

What should be said here is what was happening to Valentine's body, and what was happening was this: it was becoming a document. Each specimen, once extracted, labeled, and affixed to its corresponding form, ceased to be Valentine's and became the state's. His blood was no longer the blood that had flushed his face when he pronounced the forbidden marriages, the blood that had risen to his skin when the soldiers seized him, the blood of his conviction and his life. It was SPEC. A — SANG., Sub-basement File 1147, a bureaucratic object, a datum. His bile was not the bile of his fear, his mortality, his creatureliness. It was SPEC. B — FEL., and it would be stored in a clay pot on a shelf in a room where no one went and no one would go and the light, if there was light, came from a window so high and so small that what entered through it could not properly be called illumination but only the rumor of illumination, which amounted to the same thing in a room where there was nothing to see.

The body that Valentine had prepared to offer God — whole, entire, a single unbroken gift of flesh and faith — was being disassembled. Not by the sword, which would have been quick, would have been clean, would have had the narrative courtesy of an ending. But by forms. By pots. By the slow, patient, impossibly thorough requisition of everything he contained, one specimen at a time.

Martyrdom as retail rather than wholesale.

He squatted over the stool pot for forty-five minutes. The sub-clerks waited in the corridor, not out of delicacy — the protocol manual devoted eleven pages to the proper observation posture for specimen collection and delicacy was not mentioned in any of them — but because the corridor was cooler and Opilio had brought dried figs. Through the door, which was ajar, Valentine could hear them chewing.

What he produced was not much. The stool of a man who has been fasting in preparation for death is a modest document, theologically and otherwise. But it was enough. Crispus
