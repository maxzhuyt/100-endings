# The Garden of Optimal Living

## I.

Elysium Greens was founded on the principle that you are broken, and that this is wonderful news.

Wonderful because broken things require services, and services require revenue, and revenue at sufficient scale permits the construction of three-quarter-mile perimeter fencing with embedded biosensors and a view of the Cascade foothills that will make you, the broken thing, parsing the intake brochure on recycled cotton-blend paper that smells faintly of vetiver, weep with something the brochure calls "arrival resonance" and you might call hope, though the two words have different functions. Hope is a feeling. "Arrival resonance" is a billable event.

The brochure — let us begin there, since the brochure is where they began, and since the brochure is, in its own quiet way, the most honest document Elysium Greens ever produced, if you know how to read it. In 2019, when the campus opened its gates (oak with blackened iron hardware, hand-forged, artisanal, the hinges designed to produce no audible click because clicks suggest threshold-crossing and thresholds suggest containment and the founders understood, if nothing else, the semiotics of entry), the brochure went out to four hundred and twelve individuals in eleven countries. Not "customers." Not "patients." "Foundational residents." The language matters. The language is *load-bearing*.

"Elysium Greens is not a retreat," the brochure read. "It is a commitment to the self you were designed to be."

Note the passive construction. *Designed*. Not "born to be" or "meant to be" — those are the formulations of an older therapeutic tradition that still imagined the self as something innate, something to be discovered rather than *engineered*. "Designed" does different work. "Designed" implies an architect. "Designed" implies a blueprint. "Designed" implies that deviations from the blueprint are not expressions of personhood but *defects in execution*, and that the proper response to a defect in execution is not self-compassion — though the word "self-compassion" appeared fourteen times in the brochure, more than any word except "and" — but *correction*.

The founders were three. Gabriel Theriault, formerly of Aequitas Biometrics, a wearable-health startup that had been acquired by a larger entity whose name you would recognize from the lock screen of your phone and which we will decline to print because this is a story about a fictional place and any resemblance to a company that currently tracks your heart-rate variability and sells the data to your insurer through a thrice-subsidiared intermediary disclosed on page 97 of a Terms of Service agreement updated four hours before you last opened the app is, naturally, a matter of narrative architecture and not accusation. Dr. Lien Farrow, a psychologist of genuine credentials and genuine belief who had published peer-reviewed work on biofeedback-assisted trauma recovery and who had the particular misfortune of being both correct in her science and catastrophically naive about its applications. And Pax Whitford — a name that the narrator offers without commentary, because what commentary could improve upon a man named *Pax* who built what Pax built? Whitford was the money. Whitford was, in the parlance of the campus's own mythology, the "vision carrier." Whitford had made his first fortune in predictive behavioral analytics for retail environments — the science of knowing which direction you would turn when you entered a store and placing the high-margin product there, at your elbow, before your conscious mind had finished not noticing the manipulation. His second fortune came from applying that science to employee wellness programs at firms whose names also sit on your phone's lock screen, programs that reduced insurance costs by 23 percent and increased productivity by 14 percent and achieved both metrics through a mechanism described in the program literature as "elective biometric partnership" and described more concisely by the nine-hundred-page class-action complaint filed in 2022 as "coercive physiological surveillance."

(You have encountered Pax Whitford's intellectual descendants already today. If you wear a device on your wrist that vibrates when you have been sitting too long — a gentle nudge, a friendly reminder, your health is *important* to us — then you have felt the touch of his philosophy. The vibration is never mandatory. You can disable it. The option is in the settings. The settings are in a submenu. The submenu requires a software update. The software update requires you to agree to new terms. The new terms include a clause about anonymized aggregate data. The aggregation is performed by a subsidiary. The subsidiary was acquired last quarter. The acquiring company — but you stopped reading three sentences ago, didn't you? That is the point. The point is that you always stop reading. The point is that they know exactly where.)

Elysium Greens opened in April 2019, on forty-seven acres of former dairy land outside Mosier, Oregon, and it was beautiful. We must say this plainly, because the satire only cuts if you understand that the beauty was real. The buildings were rammed earth and reclaimed timber. The garden produced New Zealand spinach and ground cherries and eight varieties of heirloom tomato. The creek had been restored with native riparian plantings by an ecologist who had cried, genuinely cried, when she saw the salmon return. The common hall had a Steinway. The library had first editions. The beds had linen sheets that felt, against damaged skin, like a permission to exist.

And the wristband — the OneSelf™ Biometric Wellness Companion — was beautiful too. Slim, ceramic-cased, available in twelve hand-glazed colorways with names like Duskwater and Fern Doctrine and Bone Meridian. It did not look like a shackle. It looked like something you would want.

It looked like something you already have.

## II.

Margaux Chen arrived at Elysium Greens on the sixth of May, 2019, in a rented Subaru with Oregon plates and a body that had been screaming at her for eleven months.

The screaming was not metaphorical. It had begun as a hum — a tingling in the ulnar nerve of her left hand during a fourteen-hour sprint to close the Meridian account, which she had attributed to her keyboard angle and corrected with an ergonomic wrist rest that cost forty dollars and solved nothing. The hum became a buzz. The buzz became a burn. The burn migrated: left hand, left forearm, left shoulder, the back of the neck, the jaw, the temple. Her GP ordered imaging. The imaging showed nothing. Her GP referred her to a neurologist. The neurologist ordered more imaging. The more imaging also showed nothing. The neurologist referred her to a rheumatologist, who found nothing, who referred her to a pain specialist, who prescribed gabapentin, which made her feel as if her skull had been packed with wet cotton, which made her unable to do her job, which was the only thing about her that Margaux Chen had ever been confident deserved to exist.

She had been a senior strategist at Cormorant Partners, a brand consultancy whose clients included three of the five largest wellness companies in North America. She was good. She was better than good — she was the person they called when a campaign needed to feel true, and she could make it feel true because she understood, at the level of syntax, how truth and feeling were different levers that could be operated simultaneously to produce a sensation the client called "authenticity" and Margaux called Tuesday. She had written the launch language for a meditation app that now had forty million users. She had named a line of adaptogenic beverages that generated two hundred million dollars in revenue in their first year. She had, in a very precise sense, been one of the people who built the vernacular that Elysium Greens now spoke, and she did not recognize it as her own language any more than a bullet recognizes the gun.

The body broke her out of the gun.

By March 2019, she could not sit for more than twenty minutes without a pain that started in her hip and ended in a white flash behind her eyes. She could not sleep without pharmacological scaffolding that left her dull and thick and stupid-feeling through the mornings, which in brand strategy are when the work happens. She took a leave of absence that her manager described as "brave" and that both of them understood was a test she could not afford to fail and also could not pass, because the test was: become well enough to return to the thing that made you unwell, quickly, before we find someone less broken.

The brochure arrived eleven days into her leave. It was not, despite its appearance, coincidence. Her name had been extracted from a dataset — health-app usage patterns, search history, insurance claim frequency — assembled by a data broker and purchased by Elysium Greens's intake division, which Elysium Greens called "Community Curation" and which operated, functionally, as a targeting system for people in exactly the state Margaux was in: skilled, insured, desperate, and just credulous enough to believe that the place they needed to go was a place that had found them. The brochure sat on her kitchen table for two days. She read it four times. She called the intake number and spoke to a woman named Arden whose voice sounded like warm water over clean stones and whose commission structure we will not detail.

On the sixth of May, she turned onto a gravel road that wound through Douglas fir and madrone and came around a bend and saw it: the campus in early light, the garden, the buildings rising from the earth as if they had grown rather than been constructed, the creek catching sun, and two red-tailed hawks turning above the meadow in a gyre that looked, from the right angle, like a double helix, which was either a coincidence or the single greatest piece of brand strategy Margaux had ever witnessed, and she felt her eyes sting and her jaw unclench for the first time in weeks and she thought: *Yes. This. Finally, this.*

In the parking area, she was met by a young man named Kenji who took her bag and touched her shoulder and said, "You don't have to perform anything here," and meant it, entirely, in the way a person can entirely mean a sentence they have been trained to say to every arrival.

He gave her the wristband in a cedar box lined with undyed silk. Duskwater colorway. Her name was already engraved on the interior surface in a typeface she recognized — she had commissioned it, three years ago, for a client — and this small, impossible familiarity was the first thing that was wrong, and she noticed it, and she set the noticing aside, because the box smelled like cedar and the silk was cool against her palm and the wristband, when she put it on, registered her pulse and displayed, on a screen so small it was almost private, a single word: *Welcome*.

---

Five years later, in September 2024, Margaux Chen would stand in a bathroom with a mirror she was not permitted to cover and a wristband she was not permitted to remove and stare at the word *Thriving* on its display and feel nothing at all, which was, according to the system, the optimal outcome.

Dee Watkins arrived four days after Margaux, on the tenth of May, in a truck she owned and with a toolbox she'd packed herself, and the first thing she saw was the fence.

This was professional deformation. Twelve years in security architecture — first government installations, then private-sector data centers, then the brief detour into physical-digital integration consulting that had looked, for about eighteen months, like a career and had ended when her firm's largest client got acquired and the acquiring entity brought their own people and Dee's firm lost the contract and Dee's firm lost its lease and Dee lost her salary, her health insurance, and the particular high-frequency anxiety that comes from understanding exactly how systems fail and being unable to stop watching every system she encounters for signs of failure.

The job listing had appeared on a niche board she monitored out of habit. "Wellness Infrastructure Coordinator." The salary was low. The housing was included. The health insurance was immediate. The posted qualifications were a strange mismatch — they wanted someone who could maintain low-voltage sensor arrays, manage a closed-network IT environment, and "hold space for community needs," which was a phrase that communicated nothing to Dee except that the organization did not know what it wanted, or did know and had chosen to describe it in language designed to attract someone who wouldn't ask too many questions. Dee asked questions. She asked them at the phone interview, where Theriault himself — warm, precise, a voice like an engineer who's been to therapy — answered most of them with the particular fluency of a person who has been asked these questions before and has optimized his responses. She asked them again at the on-site interview, where Dr. Farrow walked her through the biometric feedback system with genuine enthusiasm and Dee nodded and took notes and did not say what she was thinking, which was that the data pipeline Dr. Farrow described had at least three points where information could be rerouted without the primary user — the resident, the person wearing the wristband — being aware of it.

She didn't say it because she needed the job. She needed the insurance. She needed, specifically, the insurance because her mother in Beaverton had just been diagnosed with a thing that required a specialist that required coverage that required Dee to be employed by someone, anyone, within thirty days, and Elysium Greens was offering to start her benefits on day one, which was generous, which was kind, which was — Dee understood even then, pulling into the gravel lot with her toolbox and her practitioner's eye — a recruitment mechanism targeted at people who could not afford to say no.

The fence. Let us talk about the fence.

In May 2019, it was three feet tall: split-rail cedar, the kind you'd see at a state park. Decorative. Boundary-marking rather than boundary-enforcing. A suggestion. But Dee saw the posts, and the posts were set three feet deeper than split-rail cedar required, and the post spacing was thirty-two inches, which is the spacing you use when you're planning to retrofit with wire or vertical pickets, and the conduit — a half-inch PVC run along the base, almost invisible under the native plantings — was oversized for the decorative solar path lights it ostensibly powered.

She noticed this. She filed it in the place she filed things she noticed.

They gave her a wristband too — Bone Meridian, staff-grade, smaller screen — and she put it on because it was a condition of employment and because the employee handbook described it as a "team alignment tool" that would help coordinate her workflow with the community's needs, and she did not yet have the contextual frame to understand that "team alignment tool" meant something other than a shared calendar.

What she did understand, within her first three hours, was the permissions structure. She'd been given a staff login to the backend of the OneSelf™ system — limited access, clearly tiered, clearly designed so that she could see enough to do her job and not enough to do anything else. But the tiers were *interesting*. She could see that above her access level there were at least four additional layers, each gated by separate authentication, and that the data flowing upward from the wristbands was being branched at the second tier — one stream continuing to the wellness monitoring dashboard (heart rate, sleep, cortisol proxies, all the things the residents were told their wristbands tracked) and another stream diverting somewhere she couldn't follow, under a protocol label she didn't recognize.

She would not follow it for months. She would not follow it because she had rent to make and benefits to keep and a mother whose specialist was booked through August and Dee was not, in May 2019, a hero. She was not even, in her own estimation, a good person. She was a tired person with an expensive problem and a new job whose infrastructure she distrusted and whose garden tomatoes were, she would learn that evening at dinner, transcendent.

Transcendent tomatoes. Suspicious data forks. A wristband that pulsed gently against her wrist at 10 p.m. and displayed, in soft amber type: *Rest well, Dee. Tomorrow is a gift.*

She read the sentence. She noticed the grammar — the imperative mood disguised as a benediction. She took the sentence apart the way she took apart permissions structures, and then she set the pieces down, and went to bed, because she was tired, and the bed was good, and the linen sheets were very soft.

---

Five years later, in September 2024, that fence would be eight feet tall, electrified at the top two feet, monitored by thermal cameras every forty meters, and described in the community newsletter as "our Compassion Boundary — because we care enough to keep the world's noise from interrupting your healing."

Dee would know the amperage.
