# The Ditchdiggers

## I.

In 1994, two things were true about the trajectory of American sport, and they ran in opposite directions like rail lines leaving the same station.

The first: IBM's Deep Blue project, which had spent three years and $14 million failing to consistently defeat Garry Kasparov at chess, was quietly defunded in February and its staff reassigned. The internal memo, dated February 11 and marked DISTRIBUTION: RESTRICTED, read in part:

> *The chess initiative has demonstrated diminishing returns against a single elite human opponent operating within a closed rule set. We believe the more commercially viable application of massively parallel processing lies not in modeling a single mind but in modeling populations of bodies. Athletic performance prediction offers a dataset several orders of magnitude richer than 64-square positional analysis, with immediate broadcast and wagering applications. Effective immediately, all Deep Blue personnel will transition to Project PRAXIS under Dr. Rachel Vasquez at the Watson Research Center.*

The second: in a disused limestone quarry eleven miles southeast of Akron, Ohio, twelve people gathered on weekend mornings to shoot each other with paint.

These two facts had nothing to do with each other. That was the point of them. That was the engine of everything that followed.

---

Project PRAXIS — Predictive Algorithmic eXtrapolation for Integrated Sports — was not, as it was sometimes described in the press that spring, an artificial intelligence. Dr. Vasquez was precise about this in interviews and grew terse when journalists used the term. It was a *system*. It ingested biometric data, tactical film, injury reports, atmospheric conditions, referee tendencies, historical match outcomes, and — this was the innovation that made the Deep Blue engineers indispensable — the positional decision trees of every player on every team in any sport it was asked to govern. It did not think. It *resolved*. Given a sufficiently rich input set, PRAXIS could model every plausible strategic sequence available to both sides of a contest and assign each sequence a probability-weighted outcome. The system did not predict who would win. It predicted the *probability space* of all possible outcomes and updated that space in real time as a match unfolded.

The National Combat Athletics League announced its partnership with IBM on March 3, 1994. Combat athletics — the regulated descendant of amateur paintball, codified with standardized arenas, elimination brackets, and broadcast contracts — had existed as a fringe professional sport since 1989, but it had never attracted a major television deal. PRAXIS changed this. The system could overlay live probability matrices on broadcast footage, could tell a viewer at any moment the precise likelihood of each team's tactical options, could generate what the engineers called a "decision landscape" that made the chaos of combat athletics legible in real time. ABC Sports signed a three-year deal within a week. *Sports Illustrated* ran a cover on March 14 — the photograph showed a PRAXIS terminal superimposed over an arena, probability trees blooming across the screen like circulatory diagrams, and the cover line read: **THE MACHINE THAT MAKES WAR BEAUTIFUL: How IBM Turned Paintball Into the Smartest Sport on Television.**

The FCC broadcast logs from the first PRAXIS-managed exhibition match, held April 9 at the Pontiac Silverdome, contain the following exchange between color commentators Dan Hoyt and Maria Soares:

> HOYT: And you can see the PRAXIS overlay updating — Team Vanguard has an 83% probability of executing a left-flank push here, and Soares, that number just ticked to 87 as their pointman shifted his weight —
>
> SOARES: It's like watching someone else's dream, Dan. You can see what they're going to do before they know they're going to do it.
>
> HOYT: Before they *decide*. That's what Vasquez keeps telling us. The decision is already in the body. PRAXIS just reads it.
>
> [PAUSE — 3.2 SEC]
>
> SOARES: Eighty-nine percent now.
>
> HOYT: Ninety-one.

By May, every franchised team in the twelve-team NCAL had integrated PRAXIS into its coaching infrastructure. Practices were filmed and uploaded. Substitution patterns were optimized. Play-calling was not dictated by the system — the league's governance charter was explicit that PRAXIS served an advisory and broadcast function only — but coaches who ignored the probability matrices found their decisions second-guessed on air, in print, and by their own players. The *Sports Illustrated* issue of May 23 featured a sidebar titled "The Gut Is Dead" in which six NCAL head coaches were asked whether they had ever overruled a PRAXIS recommendation. Four said no. One said "not yet." The sixth, a man named Bill Pedroia who coached the Austin Razorbacks, said: "I trust Rachel Vasquez's machine the same way I trust aeli level — I look at it, and if the bubble's centered, I don't argue." He was fired in July for unrelated reasons, though the reasons were not, in the opinion of several sportswriters, entirely unrelated.

This was one rail line. It ran clean and straight and brightly lit. It was going somewhere specific, and it knew exactly when it would arrive.

---

The quarry had been called the Medina Cut since the 1940s, though it was in Summit County, not Medina County, and no one alive could explain the name. It was roughly three hundred yards long and eighty yards wide and descended in ragged limestone shelves to a floor that held standing water from April through October — silty, shin-deep, the color of old tea. The acoustics were disorienting. A shout from the south wall arrived at the north wall a half-second late and stripped of its consonants. A paintball striking limestone at close range sounded like a handclap. A paintball striking a human body at forty yards sounded like nothing at all, which was worse.

Twelve people played there. They were not a team. They did not have a coach, a name, matching jerseys, a sponsorship deal, or a reason. They had, between them, three functional paintball markers of regulation quality, four modified markers that were borderline illegal in sanctioned play, and five markers that were unambiguously illegal in sanctioned play — overclocked, jury-rigged, maintained with electrical tape and profanity. They had no medic, though Connie Park was a veterinary technician and had once removed a paintball fragment from Hideo Marsh's ear canal with a pair of needle-nose pliers while the others watched and offered commentary that was neither helpful nor, in Hideo's opinion, funny.

They called themselves the Ditchdiggers, and the origin of the name was disputed. Dale Kessler said he'd come up with it after a long shift at the water treatment plant in Cuyahoga Falls, because "we're out here every Saturday digging ourselves into holes for no goddamn money." Patrice Kern said the name predated Dale's involvement and had been coined by her ex-husband, who was no longer welcome at the quarry for reasons that were not discussed. Tommy Galvin, who was twenty-two years old and drove a delivery van for a restaurant supply company, said the name didn't matter and never had, and this was probably closest to the truth.

They played every Saturday from March through November and some Sundays too, if enough people showed. There were no rules except the ones that emerged, and the ones that emerged kept shifting. Sometimes they played elimination — last player unmarked wins. Sometimes they played a version of capture-the-flag in which the flag was a traffic cone Dennis Mooney had stolen from a road crew in 1991. Sometimes they played a game that had no name and no objective, in which two groups simply tried to reach opposite ends of the quarry while the other group tried to stop them, and the game ended when someone got too cold or too hungry or when Big Linda Bassett, who worked night shifts at the Goodyear plant and was usually operating on four hours of sleep, announced that she was done, which was not a suggestion but a fact about the universe that the others had learned to respect.

There were no spectators. There was no film. The FCC had no logs. *Sports Illustrated* had never heard of them. PRAXIS had no data column in which they could be entered, no category tag, no taxonomic node. They existed in a gap — not a gap in the system's knowledge but a gap in the system's *imagination*, which was a distinction that would not become important for several months.

---

On the first Saturday in May, which was cold and overcast with intermittent rain that turned the quarry floor into a grey soup, Dale Kessler stood on the second ledge of the south wall and watched Janine Ota execute a move he had never seen before. She had been pinned behind a limestone column by converging fire from Ray Falk and Tommy Galvin — a position that, in any analyzed sport, in any system that modeled human decision-making as a tree of branching options, would have produced one of three rational responses: retreat to lower cover, return suppressive fire, or wait and hope the pressure shifted. Janine did none of these things. She stepped *into* the open — fully exposed, no cover within five yards — dropped flat into the standing water, slid three feet on the silty limestone floor in a direction that had no tactical justification Dale could identify, and from a prone position in eight inches of cold water, marked both Ray and Tommy in under two seconds. Then she stood up, soaked and shivering, and laughed until she coughed.

Dale watched this. He was holding his marker loosely at his side. He was fifty-one years old. He had worked for the Cuyahoga Falls Water Treatment Authority for twenty-seven years, and he had been coming to the quarry on Saturdays for six of those years, and in that time he had seen Janine do things like this before — not this exact thing, because the exact thing was never repeated, which was precisely the quality he was trying to name — and each time it produced in him an emotion he did not have vocabulary for, a feeling adjacent to awe but with more bewilderment in it, more helpless humor, more of the quality that small children have when they discover that a thing can be done that no one told them was possible.

"How," Ray Falk said from below, wiping yellow paint from his goggles. He was not asking a question. He was making a sound.

"She just—" Tommy started, and did not finish.

Dale looked down at the quarry floor, where Janine was wringing water out of her shirt and Hideo was offering her a thermos of coffee and Big Linda was laughing her enormous laugh that bounced off the walls and came back as something else entirely — as weather, almost, as ambient sound, as the acoustic signature of a place that could not be recorded because the recording would not capture what made it itself.

"It's just weather down here," Dale said.

Nobody responded. It wasn't the kind of thing that required a response. It entered the quarry air and joined the other sounds — the dripping, the wind across the upper ledge, the distant hiss of traffic on Route 18 — and was absorbed.

Marcus Webb, who had driven forty-one minutes from his house in Wooster and who would drive forty-one minutes back to a kitchen where his wife would not ask him where he had been because she had stopped asking him things in October, sat on a wet rock on the quarry's east shelf and reloaded his marker and felt, for the duration of that action — the particular mechanical intimacy of feeding paintballs into a hopper with cold fingers — absolutely nothing except the fact of being there. This was why he came. Not for the game. For the interruption. For the forty-one minutes on either side and the three or four hours in the middle during which the elaborate and exhausting machinery of his dissolving marriage simply stopped, not because he forgot it but because the quarry demanded a kind of attention that left no room for the other kind. The limestone didn't know his wife's name. The paint didn't know he'd been sleeping on the couch since September. His body, in the quarry, was just a body — moving, ducking, firing, getting hit, feeling the small sharp shock of impact and then the larger softer shock of realizing he was still alive and still playing and still cold and it was still Saturday.
