**Dot Ukkonen — Personal Journal**
**September 4**

Ellie,

Another batch. You should see them. The tall one — the trucker — cried during stretching. Not the emotional kind. The kind where your hamstrings have been retired longer than you have and they file a grievance when you ask them to work. His sister patted his back and then told the group chat he cried. They've been here forty minutes and they already have a group chat.

The lawyer brought his own gi. Brand new. Still had the tags. He'd ironed it. I didn't say anything because what would I say.

There's a woman who wants to flip her ex-husband's girlfriend. There's a man who listed his reason for joining as "revenge" and didn't elaborate. There's a retired custodian who told me — within ninety seconds of meeting me — about every Steven Seagal movie he's ever seen in theaters. That took a while.

I'm too old for this. The mats they gave us smell like a middle school basketball program died on them. The fluorescent light in the northwest corner buzzes at a frequency that I think only I can hear, which means either it's very high-pitched or I'm losing my mind.

But the building was free. And I said I'd do one more thing before I stopped doing things, and this is the thing, so here we are.

I'll write more when there's more to write.

Love,
Mom

**Dot Ukkonen — Personal Journal**
**September 12**

Ellie,

Week two. The lawyer ironed his gi again. He irons it every time. I watched him unfold it from a garment bag — a *garment bag*, Ellie — and smooth the lapels like he was about to argue before the Supreme Court of Judo.

I shouldn't make fun. He's trying. They're all trying and that's the thing that gets you, every time, with beginners. They have no idea what they're doing and they do it with such *commitment.* Rick Torkelson attempted an o-goshi today that looked like a man trying to dance with a refrigerator. But he attempted it. He got under his sister's center of gravity — approximately — and he tried, and when it didn't work he stood there breathing hard and said, "Again," and I thought: all right. All right, you ridiculous man. There's something in there.

Brenda asked me after class whether judo would help her "in a specific real-world scenario involving a woman named Tiffany." I told her judo was about discipline, not vengeance. She looked at me and said, "What if it's about disciplined vengeance?" I like Brenda.

Dennis can't touch his toes but he takes falls better than anyone in the class. Natural ukemi. He hits the mat like he's been falling his whole life and just now realized there's a technique to it. Told me afterward: "Thirty years as a custodian. You learn how to go down without breaking." I wanted to write that down so I'm writing it down.

The group chat is insane. They added me to it. I do not participate but I read everything. Terry and Brenda have a rhythm — she sets up a joke and he finishes it, or the other way around, without either of them realizing they're doing it. Gary sends policy memos that no one reads. The Torkelsons fight about everything from mat placement to whether the vending machine Gatorade is an acceptable post-practice electrolyte source. (It isn't, but I'm not their mother.)

I am getting to know them by the way they move. You can always tell. The ones who have been hit before — not in martial arts, just by life — they flinch on the approach but commit to the fall. The ones who've always been safe hesitate the other way: they reach for the throw but pull back at the last second because they can't believe they're allowed to do this to another person.

The mats still smell terrible. I've ordered enzyme cleaner from the internet. The internet is useful sometimes. Don't tell anyone I said that.

More soon.

Love,
Mom

**Dot Ukkonen — Personal Journal**
**September 20**

Ellie,

I want to talk about the Castillo-Park girl.

She's nineteen. Works at a coffee place in Superior. Showed up to her second class in sweats that cost nothing, no gi, and stood at the edge of the mat watching for ten minutes before she asked if she could join. Most beginners step on the mat like it's a stage — big entrance, nervous laughter. She stepped on the mat like it was a country she'd been denied entry to and she was daring someone to check her passport.

She's fast. She's strong for her size, which is not big. She's got that — what is the word. Not coordination, exactly. Coordination you can teach. She's got *instinct for contact*. She knows where the other person's weight is before they do. I showed the class de ashi barai today — foot sweep, basic, you remember — and everyone practiced it like what it is,
