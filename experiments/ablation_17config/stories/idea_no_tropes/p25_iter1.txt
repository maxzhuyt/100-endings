# The Inspectors on the Bench

## I.

You have to understand about the shoes.

I will get to the rest of it — the reports, the buildings, the whole miserable machinery of good intentions fed through the municipal sausage grinder — but first you have to understand about the shoes, because the shoes tell you everything, and because I have been waiting, I must confess, for someone to tell this to, and now that you're here I find I want to start with what's nearest the ground.

The year is 1893, or thereabouts — I am occasionally imprecise about years, though never about shoes — and a man named Wójcik is sitting on a bench in City Hall Park eating a sandwich he has made himself. The sandwich is pork loin on black bread, wrapped first in wax paper and then in a handkerchief, because Wójcik trusts no vendor, no colleague, no restaurateur within walking distance of the Municipal Building, and — I may as well say it now — no wife, because he has no wife, having determined at some point in his early thirties that a wife would constitute an additional vector for contamination, a word he uses with the same solemn frequency that priests use *grace.* He has a mustache that looks as though it arrived on his face by postal error and has been waiting ever since to be claimed. He has the posture of a man bracing for a minor earthquake. And he has shoes.

The shoes. God, the shoes.

They are leather, which is correct. They are black, which is defensible. But they are *parlor shoes* — thin-soled, square-toed, built for the carpeted vestibules of houses whose owners would never permit Wójcik inside — and he wears them daily into tenement basements where the floor is not a floor so much as a negotiation between mud and sewage, where standing water has opinions, where a man's foot might go through the boards into a sub-cellar that has not seen light since the Polk administration. He wears these shoes because they were expensive. He wears them because he bought them with his first city paycheck, seven years ago, and he resolves the dissonance between their inappropriateness and their cost the way he resolves most dissonance: by not admitting it exists. By polishing them every evening with a rag he boils once a week.

The left one has a crack along the sole that he has mended with paraffin.

I mention this because the bench — this particular bench, the third one from the east pathway, the one whose second slat from the left has warped into a gentle valley that cradles the sitter's thigh with an almost maternal dip — this bench will be here for a very long time, and I need you to see it now, with Wójcik's terrible shoes beneath it, his heels resting on the gravel, the October light coming through the elms at approximately one-fifteen in the afternoon and landing in a stripe across his lap that warms the wax-paper bundle like a benediction he hasn't asked for.

Look at him. Remember exactly how he sits.

But I am getting ahead of myself — or behind, depending on how you count.

The year is 1975, and a man named Papadopoulos is sitting on a bench in City Hall Park eating nothing because his lunch is coffee, black, from a thermos he has owned since the Johnson administration — one of them, he can't recall which, the presidencies have blurred — and the thermos has developed, over the course of the last decade, an interior coating of mineral deposits or sentiment or perhaps a primitive intelligence, so that the coffee it produces has an undertaste he can describe only as *greenish.* He drinks it anyway. He suspects the taste is the thermos's way of asserting an opinion, and he respects this, because in the Department of Housing Preservation and Development, where he has worked for eleven years, Papadopoulos's own opinions have roughly the same institutional weight as those of a thermos.

He is fifty-three. He has the face of a man whose expressions have been slowly foreclosed upon, one by one, like buildings on his inspection route, until what remains is a ground-floor tenancy of mild, wary attention. He is wearing shoes.

The shoes. I need you to see the shoes.

They are galoshes, technically — rubber-soled, buckled, the color of a headache — pulled over a pair of brown oxfords that were themselves a poor decision, being a half-size too large, so that inside the galoshes inside the oxfords his socked feet slide around with a quarter-inch of play in every direction, giving his walk the quality of a man perpetually arriving a moment after himself. He wears the galoshes in all weather, including today, which is dry and sixty-two degrees, because he has learned from eleven years of entering condemned buildings that the floor cannot be trusted, and if the floor cannot be trusted then the weather certainly cannot, and if the weather cannot be trusted then the only sane response is rubber soles, buckled, sealed, from April through November.

The bench is the same bench.

This is the part I have been wanting to tell you. The same bench, the third from the east pathway, the one with the dip in the second slat, the one that has by now been repainted nine times and carved upon by at least forty-seven knives and visited by a cartographically staggering number of pigeons, and the elm that used to cast that one-fifteen stripe has been replaced by a London plane tree that casts a slightly different stripe at a slightly different angle, but the bench remains, and the dip remains, and Papadopoulos's thigh fits into it with the same unconscious comfort that Wójcik's did, and neither man knows the other exists, and neither man knows I am watching, and you are the first person I have told about any of this, and I have to say it is a relief.

They are both city health inspectors. They both eat alone. They both carry clipboards — Wójcik's is wooden and splintering; Papadopoulos's is aluminum and dented in a way that suggests someone once used it to prop open a door, or a life, that refused to stay open — and they both believe, with a faith so durable it must be structural rather than psychological, that what they write on those clipboards matters.

It does not matter.

I say that with love. I say everything about them with love. You'll see.

## II.

Let me tell you about a Tuesday.

For Wójcik, the Tuesday is in November, 1893, and the building is on Allen Street, and the building is — I should be precise here, because Wójcik would be precise, and it is the least I can do — the building is a six-story rear tenement, which means it is the building *behind* the building, the one the street does not acknowledge, the one you reach through a corridor so narrow that Wójcik, who is not a wide man, turns sideways and still brushes both walls with his coat, and the walls are wet, and the wet has a texture, and the texture has a smell, and the smell has been composing itself since approximately 1867 and has arrived, by 1893, at something symphonic.

He has his form. Form 7-B, *Inspection of Tenement Dwelling — Condition of Interior and Appurtenances,* printed on a stock so thin the pencil nearly punctures it, which is fine, because nothing he writes on it will be read with enough attention to notice a puncture. He does not know this yet. Or — no. I must be fair. He suspects it. He has suspected it for seven years. But suspicion is not knowledge, and Wójcik is a man who insists on evidence even for his own despair, which is, when you think about it, a kind of heroism so stupid it makes me want to weep.

He enters the rear tenement.

The hallway has no light. The stairs have no banister, or rather, the banister exists as a series of iron stumps from which the wooden rail has been harvested — for firewood, Wójcik notes on his form, writing *rail removed, probable fuel use,* in the handwriting of a man who learned English from a penmanship manual and has never recovered. Second floor. He knocks on a door that opens before his knuckle lands, because the door has no latch and his knuckle is merely the last in a series of provocations — gravity, the settling of the building, the vibration of a horse cart on Allen Street — that the door has been resisting all morning.

Inside: a room. I must describe the room because Wójcik is already describing it, on his form, in his immaculate damaged English, and it would be an insult to his diligence if I did not keep up.

The room is ten feet by twelve. This is his estimate. He does not have a tape measure because the department has not issued him a tape measure because the department has four tape measures for thirty-seven inspectors and Wójcik is, alphabetically and in all other senses, last. In this room there are, at the moment of his visit, six people, but there are *thirteen* pallets, which means thirteen people sleep here, in shifts arranged around the schedules of three garment factories and a slaughterhouse, so that the room is never empty, the room is never quiet, the room breathes in and out like a lung that has been asked to do the work of thirteen lungs, which, if you think about it — and Wójcik does think about it, he cannot stop thinking about it — is exactly the arithmetic of tuberculosis.

He notes: *No ventilation. Rear window opens onto airshaft, width 18 inches. Airshaft serves as refuse receptacle. Odor: considerable.*

*Odor: considerable.* I could say a great deal about those two words — about the entire world of stench compressed into that decorous adjective, about the way Wójcik's propriety is itself a kind of violence done to the senses on behalf of the form — but I will not, because Wójcik is still writing, and we must let him finish.

He notes: *Privy in yard shared among rear and front tenements, approximately 90 persons. Privy condition: deficient. Standing sewage in courtyard, depth 3 inches (est.).*

He notes: *Ceiling height 7 feet 4 inches (est.). Walls damp. Plaster separating from lath in northeast corner. Evidence of vermin: considerable.*

*Considerable* again. For Wójcik, the word is a levee.

He completes his inspection. He speaks briefly with a woman who tells him, in Yiddish he mostly follows, that the landlord's agent collects rent on the first and fifteenth and has been seen exactly once between collections, and that time he was collecting a dog. Wójcik, whose Yiddish is better than his English, asks about the dog. The woman says the dog is a terrier, brown and white, and that the landlord's agent keeps it in the front building, first floor, in the apartment that should be the superintendent's apartment except there is no superintendent, there is only the dog, and the dog answers to no one.

Wójcik goes to the first floor of the front building. He has not been asked to inspect this. It is not on his route. He goes because the dog has been mentioned and the dog is, in Wójcik's cosmology, an *appurtenance,* and appurtenances are on the form.

The dog bites him on the left ankle, directly above the crack in the sole of his parlor shoe, with a precision that Wójcik—despite the pain, despite the blood speckling the paraffin patch—cannot help but admire professionally. He records the bite on Form 7-B, in the margin, because there is no field for dog bites, and the absence of a field is itself something he wishes he could report.

At the office — a room on the third floor of the Municipal Building that smells of paste and resignation — he presents his completed Form 7-B to his supervisor, a man named Aldrich, who has the beard of a Civil War veteran and the metabolism of a hibernating bear. Aldrich takes the form. He does not read it. He places it on a stack that is itself placed on a shelf behind his desk, a shelf on which the stacks have begun to lean against each other like drunks outside a saloon, mutually dependent and mutually doomed. Wójcik watches the form enter the stack the way a man watches a letter enter a mail slot: with the desperation of someone who has said everything he can say and must now trust the system.

"The building on Allen Street," Wójcik says. "The rear tenement."

"Mm," says Aldrich.

"Thirteen people in one room."

"I'll take it under advisement."

"There is sewage in the courtyard."

"That's the Board of Health."

"I *am* the Board of Health."

"You're an inspector," Aldrich says, not unkindly, which is worse than if he'd said it unkindly. "You inspect. What happens after that is—" He gestures. The gesture encompasses the shelf, the stacks, the building, the city, the century, the vast and intricate mechanism by which concern is converted into paper and paper is converted into nothing. It is the most eloquent thing Aldrich has ever done.

Wójcik goes home. He boils his rag. He polishes his shoes. The paraffin patch accepts the polish with a grudging, waxy sheen that is, if you do not look too closely, almost indistinguishable from leather.

He does not know the report will still be in that stack when the building on Allen Street partially collapses, four years from now, killing a woman named Rivka Mendelssohn and her son, whose name I know but will not say here because there are limits to what even I can carry in a single sentence.

He does not know. But he suspects.

I said I would tell you everything about these men with love, and I will, but you must understand that love, for a narrator in my position, is not a warm feeling. It is the specific anguish of watching someone do the right thing inside a system designed to make the right thing irrelevant. It is watching Wójcik polish his shoes and knowing about Rivka Mendelssohn. It is the dog bite and the form and the shelf and the stack and the four years, and it is the fact that he will go back to Allen Street next Tuesday, and the Tuesday after that, and the one after that, and he will fill out more forms, and the forms will go to the same shelf, and the shelf will go to the same century, and the century will shrug.

But the ankle. I want you to remember the ankle. It bled through his sock and into the crack in the sole, and he walked home on it, and the limp lasted a week, and he recorded the duration of the limp in a personal notebook he kept separate from his professional forms — a small leather book in which he tracked his own body the way he tracked the city's buildings, as a structure subject to inspection, deterioration, and official indifference.

He was, in his own way, the most thorough man alive. This is not a compliment. It is a diagnosis.

## III.

Now.

I am aware that I have been spending a great deal of time in 1893, and I apologize — not for the time spent but for the imbalance, because Papadopoulos deserves equal treatment, and if there is one thing I have learned from watching inspectors, it is that unequal treatment is the original sin, the foundational rot, the sewage in the courtyard of every institution that ever claimed to care.

So: Papadopoulos. A Tuesday. January, 1975. The building is on Vyse Avenue in the Bronx, and the building is, in the terminology of the Department of Housing Preservation and Development, a Class B Multiple Dwelling — Deficient, which is a way of saying the building is dying and the city has given the death a classification so that it may be filed.

He arrives at nine-twenty. He is early because the train was on time, which happens so rarely that he regards it as suspicious. Everything that works, in Papadopoulos's experience, is either about to stop working or is working only because something else has been sacrificed, and he spends the walk from the subway to Vyse Avenue trying to determine which municipal function has been curtailed to allow the Lexington Avenue express to run on schedule. He decides it is probably plumbing.

The building is six stories. The door is open — not unlocked but *open,* the latch mechanism having been removed at some point and replaced with a wad of newspaper that has since compressed into a fossil, a small amber-colored monument to the last person who believed the door could close. Papadopoulos notes this on his form. His form is Form HPD-63, *
