Before there was the nomination, before there was the ceremony, before there was the sixty-three-second clip that has been viewed, as of this writing, four hundred and twelve million times — before any of it, there was a woman in Gresham, Oregon, who could not make rent.

Maureen Kask was forty-one years old and employed, when she was employed, as an on-call transcriptionist for a medical dictation service called AccuVoice. The work paid eleven cents per line. On a productive day she could manage nine hundred lines, which came to ninety-nine dollars before taxes, which came to — as she would later tell a *New Yorker* profiler with the flat affect of someone reciting a figure she had calculated many times — "about enough to eat or to park, but not both, which is how I ended up transcribing cardiology reports in a Safeway parking lot with the engine off, eating peanut butter with a spoon so I wouldn't have to buy bread."

This image — the spoon, the parking lot, the engine off to save gas — would later become a fixed point in the mythology. It would be referenced in her Sundance introduction, in the title of a *Vulture* profile ("The Spoon, the Lot, the Sound"), in the opening remarks of the presenter who would hand her a gold statuette on a Sunday night in March. It is one of those biographical details that culture seizes and burnishes until it is no longer a memory but a *credential* — proof that the person who holds it has *earned* what followed, that the distance between the parking lot and the podium is the measure of a specific and meritorious journey.

What the mythology generally omits: Maureen's left ear. Specifically, the cholesteatoma that grew in her left middle ear between the ages of twelve and sixteen — a benign cyst of skin cells that should not have been there, expanding slowly against the ossicles, the three smallest bones in the human body, until her hearing in that ear was reduced by forty percent. The surgery to remove it, performed at OHSU when she was sixteen, was successful in the narrow clinical sense: the cyst was gone. But the reconstruction of the ossicular chain was imperfect. Her left ear, post-surgery, heard the world at a different latency than her right. Sounds arrived twice — once to the right ear at the speed of sound, and once to the left ear approximately four milliseconds later, filtered through a rebuilt architecture of bone that favored low frequencies and attenuated highs.

She heard the world, in other words, in a perpetual, lopsided chorus with itself.

*Cut to: Maureen Kask. She sits in a chair — the Chair, the one the documentary has chosen for her testimony, upholstered in charcoal linen, positioned against a background of warm shadow so that her face is the only illuminated surface. She is fifty-three. Her hair is gray and cut short, practical. She wears no makeup. Her face has the particular quality of someone who has spent decades listening rather than speaking — an attentiveness in the brow, an almost clinical stillness around the mouth. Her hands are in her lap. She does not gesture when she talks. Her voice is a mezzo-soprano with a faint Oregon flatness, unhurried, the voice of a person who has thought about what she is going to say and will say exactly that and nothing more.*

**MAUREEN KASK:**
"I don't know if I'd call it a gift. I've heard people call it that. The ear, the surgery, the way I hear things. Gift implies someone gave it to me on purpose, and nobody did. What happened is that I had a cyst, and a surgeon did his best, and after that the world sounded different to me than it sounded to other people. Not better. Different. I heard the resonance of things. I heard the body of a room. I heard — this is going to sound strange, and I know how it's going to sound, but I heard *digestion*. You're sitting across from me right now, and I can hear your stomach. I've been hearing it since you sat down. It's not loud to you. You don't notice it. But my left ear pulls it up, the way a certain lens pulls up texture in skin. That's just what my ear does. The question was never whether I could hear those things. The question was whether anyone else wanted to."

*The image that opens Part Two will become, in the weeks after the documentary's release, the single most screen-captured frame of the series. It is a photograph, scanned from a print, slightly overexposed: a small room, roughly eight feet by eight feet, with walls covered floor to ceiling in acoustic foam — the kind sold under brand names like Auralex and Primacoustic, charcoal-gray, egg-crate patterned. In the center of the room: a wooden chair, unfinished oak, with no cushion. Suspended from a boom arm above the chair: a pair of DPA 4060 miniature omnidirectional microphones, the kind typically used to mic theatrical performers, each capsule no larger than a pencil eraser. On the floor beside the chair: a coil of cable leading to a Sound Devices MixPre-6 field recorder. On the seat of the chair: a folded towel.*

*Opposite the chair, mounted to the acoustic foam at approximately abdomen height: a Reson TC 4013 hydrophone — a device designed for underwater acoustic measurement, torpedo-shaped, stainless steel, the transducer element visible as a small ceramic disc at its tip. A second hydrophone, identical, is mounted slightly higher and to the left, angled downward.*

*The photograph has no context. No caption. No person in the frame. It is simply a room, a chair, a towel, and equipment. The documentary holds on it for eleven seconds before anyone speaks.*

**GARY PINEDA** (Sound Designer, *Nope*, *The Batman*)**:**
"The first time I saw her setup — she sent me photos, this was, I think, 2019, before anyone knew who she was — I thought it was a joke. Hydrophones in an iso booth? Those are for recording whale song. Those are for submarine detection. You don't put a TC 4013 in a room with a wooden chair. The impedance mismatch alone — you'd just get noise. You'd get room tone. I told her that. She said, 'The noise is the point, Gary.' And I said, 'The noise of what?' And she didn't answer. She just sent me an audio file."

*A pause. The documentary lets the pause breathe. Gary Pineda looks at his hands.*

**GARY PINEDA:**
"I don't want to describe what I heard. Not because it was offensive. Because describing it would — it would reduce it, and it doesn't deserve to be reduced. I'll say this: I have been working in sound for twenty-two years. I have recorded gunfire and thunderstorms and the inside of an MRI machine. And that audio file was the most alien, the most *human* sound I have ever heard. I listened to it three times. Then I called her back and said, 'How are you making this?' And she told me. And I had to sit down."

The process, as Maureen describes it, is methodical.

It begins with diet. Specifically, a regimen she developed over the course of approximately two years through systematic self-experimentation, conducted with the rigor of a clinical nutritionist and the patience of a recording engineer who understands that the instrument must be tuned before the session begins.

The core of the regimen is fiber. Soluble fiber: psyllium husk, taken in precisely measured doses — she uses a jeweler's scale, accurate to .01 grams — dissolved in room-temperature water, never cold, because cold water accelerates gastric motility in ways that reduce the tonal range of the resulting intestinal activity. Insoluble fiber: raw jicama, roasted Jerusalem artichokes, and — in what she refers to as "heavy sessions" — a combination of black beans and pearl barley soaked for exactly fourteen hours and cooked without salt, because sodium, she has found, tightens the smooth muscle of the intestinal wall and produces a thinner, less harmonically rich output.

The diet is supplemented, on recording days, with simethicone — an over-the-counter gas relief medication — taken not to *reduce* gas production but to *modulate* it. Simethicone works by decreasing the surface tension of gas bubbles in the gut, causing smaller bubbles to merge into larger ones. Larger bubbles, Maureen discovered, produce lower-frequency intestinal sounds — borborygmi, in the clinical term — with longer sustain and more complex harmonic overtones. She describes the difference as analogous to the difference between a snare drum and a timpani: "Same energy, different architecture."

**MAUREEN KASK:**
"People hear 'diet' and they think it's a gimmick. Like I'm eating beans to make myself fart. That's — that's like saying a vocalist drinks tea to make herself burp. The diet is instrument maintenance. I am maintaining the acoustic properties of a system — a digestive system, yes, a human one, mine — so that when I sit in the booth and the microphones are live, the system produces sound that I can *use*. Sound that has texture, that has dynamics, that has emotional content. Because it does. Your gut responds to fear. It responds to grief. It responds to arousal. It is, if you want to be reductive about it, the most honest organ in the body. It doesn't know how to lie."

The recording sessions themselves take place in the room with the acoustic foam, the wooden chair, the DPA microphones, and the hydrophones. Maureen refers to the room as "The Confessional." She enters it alone. She is typically barefoot. She wears loose-fitting clothing or, for certain sessions, no clothing at all, because fabric noise — the rustle of a cotton shirt against skin — is, in her words, "a contaminant."

She sits in the chair. The DPA microphones capture airborne sound — the external acoustic output of her body as interpreted by the air in the room. The hydrophones, pressed against her abdomen via a custom-fitted elastic harness she designed with a textile engineer in Portland, capture *contact* sound — the vibrations transmitted directly through the tissue of her abdominal wall, bypassing the air entirely, recording the interior of her body the way a geophone records the interior of the earth.

She sits. She breathes. She waits.

What the hydrophones capture, over sessions that can last between forty minutes and four hours, is a continuously evolving soundscape of gastric and intestinal activity: peristaltic contractions moving material through the small intestine at roughly two centimeters per second; the liquid transit of chyme through the ileocecal valve; the fermentation of undigested oligosaccharides by colonic bacteria, producing hydrogen, methane, and carbon dioxide in volumes and at pressures that generate low-frequency oscillations between 10 and 250 hertz; the intermittent release of those gases through the pyloric and anal sphincters, captured by the DPA microphones as anything from a sub-audible hiss to what Maureen, in her studio notes — reproduced in the documentary as on-screen text, white Futura against black — clinically terms a "full-spectrum expulsion event."

**DR. AMIRA OSEI** (Gastroenterologist, OHSU)**:**
"From a purely physiological standpoint, what she's recording is completely real. The human gut produces between 500 and 2,000 milliliters of gas per day. Most of it is reabsorbed or expelled without conscious awareness. The borborygmi — the sounds — are continuous. We just don't hear them because we're not listening with hydrophones. What Ms. Kask has done, in essence, is put a stethoscope on a process that every human body performs every moment of every day and record it at broadcast quality. Whether that is art or gastroenterology is not a question I'm qualified to answer."

**MAUREEN KASK:**
"There's another element I should mention, because the documentary is going to mention it whether I do or not, and I'd rather it came from me. Some of the sessions are sexual. I masturbate in the booth. Not always. Not as a performance. Because arousal changes the acoustic profile of the body in ways that nothing else does. Heart rate increases. Peristaltic activity spikes. The pelvic floor engages. The diaphragm moves differently. The *whole system* shifts into a different key, and the sounds it produces in that key are — they are not the sounds of digestion. They are the sounds of a body that is experiencing something, and they are unmistakable, and they are, I believe, some of the most emotionally transparent audio that has ever been recorded. If that makes people uncomfortable, I would ask them to sit with the discomfort for a moment and ask themselves what, precisely, they are uncomfortable *about*."
