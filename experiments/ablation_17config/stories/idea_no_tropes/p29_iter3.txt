# Last Reservation at the End of the World

---

## I.

Where Forever Begins.

You have seen this, even if you believe you haven't. You have seen the terrace. Cantilevered over the North Atlantic on a promontory of Devonian slate, sixty-four feet above the waterline at high tide, slightly more at low — though the Cliffside Eternal's marketing materials have never mentioned tides, because tides imply impermanence, and impermanence is not on-brand. You have seen the terrace in *Condé Nast Traveler* (twice: once in the main feature, once in the "Where to Propose Without Being Basic" sidebar). You have seen it on four hundred and sixteen curated Instagram grids, always from the same angle, always at the same hour — the golden one, the forgiving one, the hour when the light conspires with the Cliffside Eternal's landscape architect to make every jawline look deliberate and every ocean look like it exists to backdrop your moment. You have seen the champagne flutes on the railing. The flutes are Riedel. The railing is wrought iron, custom, hand-forged by a man in Kennebunkport who has been identified in three separate profiles as "the last true artisan on the Eastern Seaboard," and who charges eleven hundred dollars per linear foot, and who will tell you, if asked, that he doesn't even like the ocean.

You have seen the honeymoon suite. You have *certainly* seen the honeymoon suite. The sea-glass chandelier, commissioned in 2014 from a cooperative on Monhegan Island, each pendant an actual piece of wave-tumbled glass, green and amber and that particular blue that every brochure calls "cerulean" because "cerulean" sounds like it costs what it costs. The four-poster bed — reclaimed white oak, king-plus, which is a designation the Cliffside Eternal invented because "king" alone suggested a ceiling on aspiration. The bath, visible through a glass partition that can be frosted at the press of a button near the headboard, so that intimacy remains a *choice* and never a *condition*. The marble is Calacatta. Of course the marble is Calacatta. What else would it be? What marble do you use in a room where the nightly rate is four thousand two hundred dollars in high season and the waitlist runs three years deep and the guest book — hand-bound, linen-covered, displayed on a lectern in the lobby like a sacramental text — contains, at last count, forty-one uses of the word "magical," twenty-seven uses of the word "unforgettable," and one entry, from a guest in 2019, that reads simply: *We came here to save our marriage and it worked and I will never forgive this place for making me believe that was possible.*

That entry is not reproduced on the website.

What *is* reproduced on the website: the Cliffside Eternal has hosted, since its opening in 2011, approximately six proposals per weekend between May and October. A conversion rate, the owner likes to say at industry conferences, of nearly ninety-three percent — meaning ninety-three percent of those who come to the Cliffside Eternal intending to propose actually go through with it. The remaining seven percent, one assumes, look over the railing at the Atlantic and experience a moment of clarity that the resort's pricing structure was specifically designed to prevent. You have already put down the deposit. You have already told your mother. The ocean is doing its thing. The chandelier is doing its thing. The Riedel flutes are arriving on a tray held by a staff member trained to approach from the left and retreat before the tears. What are you going to do — *not* ask?

The Cliffside Eternal does not sell rooms. It does not sell views. It does not sell the chef's nine-course tasting menu with wine pairings selected by a sommelier who trained in Burgundy and who quietly, on his days off, drinks Miller High Life from the can while watching *MasterChef* reruns, because even priests have their Sabbaths. What the Cliffside Eternal sells — and this is not subtext; this is the first sentence of the mission statement mounted behind the reception desk in fourteen-point Garamond — is *the memory you deserve*.

Think about that sentence. Really think about it. Not the promise, which is obscene in the ordinary way that luxury promises are obscene, but the verb. *Deserve.* The Cliffside Eternal has looked at you, at your relationship, at the particular quality of your need, and it has determined that you — yes, you, who fought in the car on the way to the airport, who haven't had sex in a way that felt mutual in nine months, who Googled "couples therapist near me" at 2 a.m. and then cleared the browser history not because your partner checks but because *you* didn't want to see it there in the morning — you *deserve* this. The memory. The chandelier. The terrace at golden hour. You have earned, through the mere act of paying, the right to a version of your love that looks the way love looks in the brochure. The Cliffside Eternal is not a resort. It is an argument. It is a four-thousand-dollar-a-night argument that setting is a subset of feeling, that if you put two people in a beautiful enough room with a beautiful enough view and serve them a beautiful enough Sancerre, something in the space between them will — must — become beautiful too.

It is a gorgeous argument. It is, like most gorgeous arguments, wrong. But it is wrong in a way that people will pay for, which is the only way that matters to the Cliffside Eternal's investors, and to the owner — a man named Garrett Royce, who built the resort on inherited money and genuine taste and the unshakable conviction that what people want most in the world is to be *framed*, placed inside a composition that flatters them, and who is, at this exact moment, standing at the lobby windows watching the sky over the Atlantic do something he has never seen it do before.

It is the color of a bruise in its fourth day. Green at the edges. The sea has been wrong since noon — not rough, exactly, but *confused*, the swells arriving from two directions at once, as if the ocean has received contradictory instructions and is trying to obey both. The National Weather Service issued a hurricane warning for the Maine coast at 6 a.m. By 10 a.m., the warning was upgraded. By 2 p.m., a word appeared in the advisory text that Garrett Royce had to look up: *unsurvivable*. As in: "storm surge in exposed coastal areas may be unsurvivable." He looked it up not because he didn't understand it — the morphology is clear enough — but because he wanted to confirm it was a real word, sanctioned by real meteorologists, and not something the Weather Service had invented in a moment of institutional panic. It is a real word. It is in the advisory. It is on his phone screen right now, as he stands at the lobby windows with his reading glasses pushed up onto his forehead, looking less like a man confronting existential danger to his property and more like a man who has been told the wine he ordered is no longer available and is deciding whether to make a scene.

Outside: the Atlantic, doing something unprecedented.

Inside: twenty-nine occupied rooms. Fourteen couples. One solo guest in the harbor-view single. A staff of twenty-two, most of whom live on the mainland and will not be returning after their shifts. A kitchen stocked for four more days. A generator rated for seventy-two hours. A wine cellar that could, if necessary, outlast the building.

And the honeymoon suite, from which no sound has emerged in fourteen hours, and on whose door someone has hung the placard — hand-lettered, like everything here, on cream card stock — that reads *Privacy, Please: Forever in Progress.*

Garrett Royce does not yet know what is behind that door.

Give him an hour.

## II.

The door was opened at 4:47 p.m. by Nina Almeida, housekeeping supervisor, who had knocked three times at standard intervals, then once more with the authority of a woman who has been cleaning hotel rooms for nineteen years and who can distinguish, through two inches of solid mahogany, between the silence of people who are inside and do not wish to be disturbed and the silence of a room where something has *stopped*.

She used her master key. She pushed the door. She saw.

Martin Arceaux was on the floor between the bed and the glass partition that separates the sleeping area from the bath. He was on his back. He was wearing the resort-issued bathrobe — white, Turkish cotton, monogrammed *CE* on the breast in thread the color of the sea-glass chandelier, which was still on, still lit, still casting its pale blue-green patterns across the ceiling and the walls and the body of a man who was fifty-four years old and who had checked in three days earlier with his wife and whose reservation, per the front desk records, had been made not three years in advance, as most were, but six weeks ago, via a phone call during which he had spoken to the reservations coordinator for less than two minutes and had not asked about the view.

The details of his death were specific, and they were wrong. Not wrong the way a detective would mean — not inconsistent, not contaminated — but wrong in the way a sentence can be grammatically correct and still make you uneasy, the way a chord can contain all the right notes and still sound like a question no one wants to answer. His body suggested a fall — the angle, the position, the way one arm was folded beneath him and the other extended, palm up, toward the glass partition as if reaching for something or waving something away. But a fall from what? The bed was too low. The room contained no elevated surface from which a man could fall and land as Martin Arceaux had landed, with that particular geometry of limb and torso, that particular expression on his face, which was — and Nina Almeida would say this later, to the detective, in a voice stripped of everything but the precision of a woman determined to be accurate — *not afraid.* Not pained. Not surprised. *Concentrated.* As if he had been in the middle of a thought that required effort, and the dying had interrupted it, and he was annoyed.

There was a bruise on his left temple. There was no blood. There was no visible weapon, unless you counted the sea-glass chandelier, which hung above, intact, indifferent, casting its patterns. The room was otherwise immaculate. The bed had been slept in — one side only, the left, which the resort's records indicated was Martin's preferred side, because the Cliffside Eternal asks about preferred sides at booking, because this is a place that leaves nothing about intimacy to chance. The minibar had been opened. One bottle of sparkling water, consumed. One split of champagne, untouched. On the desk, a pen — the resort's own, matte black, weighted, engraved — lay across a sheet of the resort's stationery, on which someone had written, in a hand that pressed hard enough to score the paper:

*I should have*

Nothing else. No period. No continuation on another page. Just the three words and the groove they left in the cream card stock, and then the pen set down — not dropped, *set down*, parallel to the paper's edge, the way a person sets down a pen when they have decided not to continue but want to reserve the right to return.

And on the door, hung from the outside: *Privacy, Please: Forever in Progress.*

From the outside.

Nina Almeida called the front desk. The front desk called Garrett Royce. Garrett Royce called the police. The police, who were on the mainland and who were, at that moment, dealing with the early logistics of a hurricane that was now sixteen hours from landfall, said they would send someone on the next ferry. The next ferry, said the ferry authority, would be the last. After that, the strait would be closed. Whatever was on the island would stay on the island until the storm passed.

Garrett Royce hung up the phone and stood in the lobby and thought about what it would mean to have a dead man in his honeymoon suite — in *the* honeymoon suite, the room, the icon, the anchor of every campaign, the room where eleven thousand photographs had been taken and posted and liked and saved and reshared, the room that existed, in the public imagination, as a *site of beginning* — for the duration of a hurricane. He thought about this, and he did what people in his industry do when confronted with a reality that threatens the brand: he thought about *managing* it.

Then he went upstairs and saw the body, and he stopped thinking about the brand, because the body was a person, and the person was dead, and even Garrett Royce — who had built an empire on the premise that experience is a product — could not, when confronted with the actual fact of a dead man on the actual floor of the actual room, find a way to frame it.

Sylvie Arceaux was found forty minutes later in the dining room.

She was sitting at a table for two by the window — not the ocean-view window, which is the one every guest requests and which the maître d' assigns according to a formula that accounts for length of stay, room rate, and an ineffable quality he calls "radiance," by which he means the degree to which a couple looks like they belong in a photograph — but the garden-view window, the lesser window, the one that faces the herb beds and the stone wall and, beyond the wall, the access road that leads to the ferry landing. She was eating breakfast. Or: she was sitting in front of breakfast. A plate of eggs, barely touched. A cup of coffee, half-empty, with a ring of lipstick on the rim that did not quite match the color she was wearing, as though she had applied it once, wiped it off, and reapplied it in a different shade — the kind of small, self-revising gesture that belongs to a woman who looked in the mirror that morning and could not decide who she was going to be today.

Her hands were not steady. She held the coffee cup with both of them, and still the surface of the liquid trembled, and she watched it tremble with the focus of a person who has chosen to observe a small thing because the large thing is not yet something she can look at directly.

The story could tell you, here, what Sylvie Arceaux was thinking. It could open her skull like the honeymoon suite door and show you the contents — the grief, if it was grief; the relief, if it was relief; the particular alloy of the two that sometimes fills the chest of a person whose spouse has died and left behind an unfinished sentence on resort stationery. It could tell you about the marriage: twenty-two years, no children, a house in Montclair, New Jersey, with a garden she maintained and he admired from the kitchen window while eating cereal and reading news on his phone. It could tell you why the reservation was made six weeks ago instead of three years. It could tell you about the last conversation they'd had before she'd left the room, or whether she'd left the room, or whether she'd been in the room at all.

But not yet.
