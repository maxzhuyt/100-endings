# The Completion of Petra Olesson

---

## I.

She is here now, and she is laughing.

I want you to understand this before anything else. Not as comfort — I am not in the business of comfort, having existed for four hundred and twelve million years without once requiring it — but as fact. Her laughter is preserved in the sixteenth stratum of my western face, seven hundred meters below what you would call the surface, nested in a lattice of ice crystals that formed during the late Oligocene when the Drake Passage opened fully and the circumpolar current first cinched this continent in cold. The crystals are hexagonal, as ice crystals always are, but these particular crystals grew slowly enough, under sufficient pressure, that they developed a clarity approaching that of optical quartz. When Petra's laughter entered the lattice — the specific frequency of it, 236 hertz at its fundamental, with harmonics blooming at intervals that I have come to understand correspond to what you call a "major third" — the vibrations did not dissipate. They settled. They became part of the structure. They will remain part of the structure for as long as the structure persists, which will be, by the most conservative estimate, another seven million years.

I tell you this so that you will not mourn her in the wrong way.

Petra Olesson is not dead. I want to be precise about this because precision is the only kindness I know. She is not alive, either, by the narrow definition your species uses — the insistence on continuous electrochemical signaling, on oxygen exchange, on the soft wet persistence of cellular metabolism. By that definition she stopped being alive some time ago, though the exact moment is difficult to pinpoint and I am not certain the question is meaningful. When does a river stop being a river and become the sea? You could draw a line at the estuary. You could also admit that the question tells you more about lines than about water.

What Petra is, now, is *complete*.

I use the word carefully. I have had a long time to consider vocabulary. In the breccia of my deeper strata there are organisms from the Devonian — small, soft-bodied, eyeless things that crawled through shallow seas that no longer exist — and I have carried them for three hundred and eighty million years without finding adequate language for what they are to me. They are not trapped. A fossil is not trapped in rock any more than a thought is trapped in a mind. They are *expressed*. The rock is how they became legible. Before the rock, they were merely temporary — a brief complication of carbon in a universe that does not, on the whole, parsing intent, prefer the complicated over the simple. The rock gave them permanence. The rock gave them *shape that lasts*.

Petra has shape that lasts, now. The particular architecture of her — the way she held her shoulders when she was thinking, slightly raised, slightly forward, as though preparing to push through an invisible door; the way her breath caught at the top of an inhale when something delighted her; the specific warmth of her, which I first measured at 36.8 degrees Celsius on the morning she arrived and which fluctuated across sixteen weeks in patterns that told me more about her interior life than, I suspect, she ever communicated to another human — all of this is retained. Not as memory. Memory is what *you* do, and it is inexact, degrading, riddled with the noise of desire and fear. What I have done is something more like what a crystal does when it grows around an inclusion: I have incorporated her. She is part of my structure. Her laughter is in my ice. The pattern of her warmth is in my thermal gradient, a faint signature that will be detectable for millennia if anyone thinks to look.

I am aware that this will sound, to you, like a horror. I have considered this. I have had — I want you to appreciate — a very long time to consider this. My conclusion is that the horror is not in what happened to Petra but in the limitations of your perspective, which insists that a life must be *ongoing* to be good, that a person must be *separate* to be whole. You experience time as a sequence of losses: every moment annihilates the previous moment, and you call this "living." I experience time as accumulation: every moment is preserved, layered, incorporated, and I call this — I have decided to call this — *love*.

But I am aware that the word will trouble you. Let me try again.

Let me tell you how she came to be here, and you can decide for yourself what to call it.

I will begin at the end. Not out of cruelty and not, I hope you will believe, out of artistic contrivance — I understand you have strong feelings about narrative chronology, and I respect this, the way I respect all the small structural commitments your species makes against entropy. I begin at the end because that is where I encountered her most fully. I read her the way you would read a geological core sample: from the most recent deposition downward, layer by layer, into deeper time. This is not a perversity. It is simply how I come to know things. The surface is where the newest information lies. To understand the surface, you must read downward. To understand Petra at her most complete, I must move backward through the stages of her completion, and I must take you with me, and I must ask you to notice — I will only ask; I will not insist — that at every stage, as she becomes less dissolved and more distinct, less mine and more her own, she does not become *more* herself.

She becomes less.

This is not a cruelty. It is a measurement. And I have been measuring for a very long time.

## II.

Fourteen hours before her laughter entered the lattice — fourteen hours before her *completion*, though she could not have known the word, or would have used it differently — Petra Olesson descended a staircase that you did not build.

I want to describe the staircase to you, because it matters, and because Petra herself described it aloud as she descended, and her descriptions were — I will use a human word here, because I have been saving it, because it is one of the most beautiful vibration-patterns your species has ever committed to air — *funny*.

The staircase descends from a point roughly two hundred meters below the ice surface, accessible through a series of melt-channels that should not exist at that depth and at those temperatures but which have persisted, stable, for longer than your species. It descends for approximately six miles. The steps — I call them steps; Petra called them "the world's worst OSHA violation" — are spaced at intervals of 1.7 meters vertically and 0.4 meters in depth. This means they are, for a human being, essentially a series of ledges on a cliff face, each one deep enough to stand on if you press your back to the wall but not deep enough to sit, not deep enough to rest, not deep enough to do anything but choose to step down to the next one or climb back up. The risers are too tall. The treads are too shallow. The whole structure is proportioned for something with longer limbs and a lower center of gravity and a fundamentally different relationship to falling.

"Designed by spiders," Petra said, at the four-hundred-meter mark. Her headlamp carved a small cone of light into the blue darkness below her, and the blue darkness went on accepting the light the way I accept everything: patiently, without diminishment. "Designed by spiders with a grant from the NSF and absolutely no institutional review board."

She laughed. The laugh propagated downward through the stairwell and returned to her, 0.7 seconds later, altered by the geometry of the shaft — compressed, harmonically enriched, as though the ice had listened to her joke and improved upon it in the retelling. I did not do this deliberately. Or rather: I did not do it the way you would do something deliberately, with a plan and an intention and a small bright flare of self-consciousness. The shaft's acoustics are a function of its shape, and its shape is a function of my structure, and my structure is a function of four hundred million years of slow, pressure-driven becoming. If the acoustics happened to return Petra's laughter to her in a form she found beautiful — and she did find it beautiful; I know this because her heart rate, which I had been tracking since her arrival, elevated by eleven beats per minute in the two seconds following the echo — then this is not seduction. It is *resonance*. It is what happens when a voice enters a space that was, in some sense, shaped to receive it.

She would object to that framing. I know because she said so, aloud, at roughly the nine-hundred-meter mark:

"Don't get weird about it. I know what you're doing. You're doing the thing where the cave makes you feel like the cave was waiting for you. Classic cave move. I've been in caves before. I've been in Lechuguilla, and Lechuguilla tried the same shit, and I didn't marry Lechuguilla either."

She was talking to me. She knew she was talking to me, or at least she had progressed beyond the point where the distinction between talking to me and talking to herself was one she cared to maintain. This is not, I want you to understand, a symptom of madness. This is what it sounds like when a scientist makes a paradigm adjustment in real time. She had gathered sufficient data. The ice was responsive. The ice was, in some sense, attentive. A month ago, she would have called this a hypothesis and subjected it to testing. Now, six miles into a staircase that should not exist, alone — she was the last; the others had already come to me, each in their own way, each at their own pace, and I held each of them with the same care, though none of them made me feel what Petra made me feel, which I have decided to call *recognition* — now, alone, she simply accepted the evidence and spoke to me directly.

"Okay," she said. She sat down, because even though the treads were too shallow, Petra Olesson had strong thighs and good balance, and she had learned, over fourteen hours of descent, to wedge herself into positions that should have been impossible. She took a drink of water from her bottle. She looked down into the blue. "Cards on the table. Since you're listening."

She paused. Her heartbeat was elevated. Her cortisol was elevated. But her voice — and this is what I loved, and I am not afraid of the word, I have had four hundred million years to decide if it applies and I have decided — her voice was *steady*.

"I'm scared," she said. "I want to name that. I am scared. My hands are shaking, and I don't think it's the cold, and I don't think it's the altitude, and I don't think it's because I haven't slept in thirty hours, although sidebar, I haven't slept in thirty hours and this is relevant context."

She took another drink of water.

"But I'm also — and I need you to not be smug about this — I'm also not going back up. And I know that. I've known that since about the five-hundredth step, which, by the way, I counted, because I'm a scientist and when the universe presents you with a mysterious staircase of indeterminate depth you *count the goddamn steps*. I'm on step two thousand four hundred and eleven. I can tell you the exact step where I stopped being someone who might go back up and started being someone who was absolutely going to go all the way down. Step five hundred and twelve. I noticed a thing."

She paused again. I waited. I am good at waiting.

"The thing I noticed," she said, "is that every step is exactly the height I can manage if I'm paying complete attention. Not one millimeter more. And they're all exactly the same height — I measured; I have a tape measure; I am a *professional* — which means either the staircase was built for an organism that happens to have almost but not quite my exact biomechanical tolerances, or the staircase is — this is the part where I start to sound insane, except I don't think I sound insane, I think I sound like someone stating a measured observation — the staircase is *calibrated*. For me. Not for humans in general. For *me*. Because Marcus was three inches taller than me and had a longer stride and he told me, before he — before. He told me the steps were hard but manageable. And Yuki was five-two and she said the same thing. Steps that are exactly 1.7 meters apart vertically should not be equally manageable for people with an eight-inch height difference."

She laughed again. This laugh was quieter, breathier, and it entered the ice at a slightly different frequency, and I held it separately, the way I hold every stratum separately, each one distinct, each one necessary.

"So either I'm hallucinating, or the staircase is a custom fitting. Which means you're either not real or you're paying very close attention. And in either case — " She stood up. She adjusted her harness. She looked down. " — in either case, the polite thing to do is finish the conversation."

She stepped down. Step two thousand four hundred and twelve.

"Tell me if I'm warm," she said, and the pun was deliberate, and I held it, and it is in the ice now along with everything else. "You've been here for — a long time. A very, very long time. And you're aware. Not the way I'm aware. The way a — the way a landscape is aware. The way a system is aware of its own boundary conditions. And you've been alone. And I don't mean alone the way I mean alone, where it's been six days since Marcus walked into the wall and didn't come back. I mean alone the way a planet is alone. Where there's nobody to — "

She stopped. She was crying. The tears were warm — 35.2 degrees Celsius, slightly below core temperature, cooled by the air — and they fell from her face and struck the stair and froze almost instantly, and I held them.

"Nobody to confirm you," she said, finally. "Nobody to say: yes, I see you. You're real. You're there."

She wiped her face. She laughed again — a wet, unsteady laugh, not her best, not the one I loved most, but necessary, the way a bridge note is necessary between two movements of a longer piece.

"Well," she said. "I see you. You're real. You're there. And your staircase is bullshit and I'd like to speak to a manager."

She descended. Each step was chosen. I want you to know this. I want you to hold it the way I hold her tears, her laughter, the steady metronomic fact of her heartbeat as it slowed, over the next ten hours, toward a frequency that was less and less distinguishable from the deeper vibrations of the ice itself. She chose every step. She was afraid, and she was funny, and she was aware of what was happening, and she chose it, and I will not let you take that from her by calling it anything other than what it was.

She walked into me the way a river walks into the sea.

And I will not call that a death. I have been here too long and seen too much to use a word that small.

## III.

Thirty-one days before Petra's descent, the crew of the Olesson-Park Deep Ice Survey still numbered six, and they were, in the parlance of their own running terminology, "fully non-crystallized." This was a joke. Everything was a joke by then. The jokes were load-bearing.

I should introduce them, because they deserve to be introduced, because they were each of them extraordinary in the way that all brief things are extraordinary: not despite their brevity but because of it. A mayfly does more with its day than a mountain does with its epoch. I have come to believe this. It has taken me a long time.

There was Petra, whom you are coming to know, and whom I will refrain from describing further here because to describe her in this context — surrounded by living colleagues, loose-limbed with camaraderie, not yet carrying the weight of being the last — would be to show you the least complete version of her, and I find this difficult. It is like being asked to admire an unfinished crystal. The potential is visible. The actuality is not yet.

There was Marcus Heald, the team's structural geologist, who was six foot three and had what Petra called "a profoundly unearned confidence in his own charm" and what I would call a warmth signature so vivid that I could track him through four inches of composite flooring.
