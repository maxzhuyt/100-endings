# Voices from the 2019 LaGuardia Closure

**Smithsonian National Museum of American History**
**Division of Political and Military History — Federal Governance Collection**
**Oral History Project: "Grounded: American Life During the 2018–2019 Government Shutdown"**

**Interview Subject:** Sandra Lehane, 51, management consultant, Brookline, Massachusetts
**Interview Date:** September 14, 2019
**Location:** Recorded at NMAH field office, New York, NY
**Interviewer:** Dr. Tomás Herrera, Project Historian
**Transcript Prepared by:** G. Meacham, Archives Division
**Accession No.:** OH-2019-0347

---

**[Recording begins.]**

You want to know a thing about me that will make you feel better about this whole enterprise? I almost didn't come. I got the letter — lovely letter, very official, the Smithsonian seal does real work on a person — and I thought, you've got the wrong Sandra Lehane. You want the one who was in the terminal with a congressional staffer, or the one who organized the line for the bathrooms, or the single mother with the twins, because *that* woman's story would make people cry in the right way. I was just a person whose flight didn't leave. I was business class to Boston, which — I realize — makes me exactly the kind of person no one wants to hear from in a story about a government shutdown. The comfortable woman. The woman for whom this was an inconvenience. I'm aware of the optics.

But you said — your letter said — you wanted range. Texture, I think was the word. That you already had the TSA workers who weren't getting paychecks, and the air traffic controllers, and the families, and you wanted civilians. Witnesses. And I thought, well, I can witness. I'm good at that. It's actually what they pay me for, in the loose sense — I observe systems and tell people what's wrong with them, and they pay me not enough to fix anything and too much to complain about it. So. Here I am. Witnessing.

January 25th, 2019. Terminal B, LaGuardia. I had been in New York for a client meeting — financial services, I'll spare you — and my 6:15 p.m. Delta shuttle to Logan was first delayed, then delayed further, and then, around 9 p.m., the gate agent made an announcement with the particular cadence of a person who knows they are about to be screamed at and has already left their body. The FAA had issued a ground stop. All departures suspended. Due to — and I'll never forget this phrasing, she read it off a sheet — "staffing considerations related to the ongoing lapse in federal appropriations."

Staffing considerations. The poetry of bureaucratic collapse.

So: no flights. Not just LaGuardia — Newark, JFK, Philadelphia, all in some state of stoppage. The terminal, which had been the usual LaGuardia special — desolate and overcrowded at the same time, which is a trick only LaGuardia can pull off — became something else. It became a place where people were going to stay. And there is a real difference, you know, between a room full of people who are about to leave and a room full of people who now understand they can't. The chemistry shifts. I watched it happen. It took about twenty minutes.

But this is the part you already know, isn't it? This was in all the coverage. The shutdown, day thirty-five, the longest in American history, the airports, the spectacle of it. You don't need me for the context. You have the context. So I suppose what you need me for is the — what did you call it? The texture.

Fine. I'll give you texture. I've got eighteen hours of it.

**[Pause in recording: 4 seconds.]**

The first thing you have to understand is the outlets.

I know that sounds ridiculous. But if you want to know what a society looks like when the veneer comes off — and I mean, the veneer is already gossamer-thin in an airport, we are already our worst selves in airports, this is well-documented — you look at how people behave around electrical outlets. There were, by my count, fourteen accessible outlets in the gate area of B26 through B31, which is the section where most of us ended up because that's where the Hudson News was, and the Hudson News was the only thing still open, manned by a woman named — I want to say Priti? I should be honest with you when I'm guessing — manned by a woman who had the look of someone who had not been told she was allowed to leave and was too conscientious to find out.

Fourteen outlets for — I don't know — two hundred people? Three hundred? It shifted. Some people left, found cars, found hotels. Some came in from other terminals. But let's say two hundred at the core, which meant roughly fourteen people with power and a hundred and eighty-six people in various states of negotiation, resentment, and grief.

The first man to figure it out — and I say *man* because it was, it was inevitably a man, early fifties, North Face vest, the uniform of a certain kind of Northeastern confidence — he'd brought a six-outlet power strip in his carry-on. Just had one. I don't know if he always traveled with it or if he was a prophet, but by 10 p.m. he was sitting on the floor by the pillar between B28 and B29, and he had *set up shop*. He'd plugged in his own phone, his own laptop, and then he had four outlets available, and he was — I'm not exaggerating — he was *granting audiences*. People would approach, and he'd look up, and there was this half-second assessment, this *weighing*, and then he'd either nod or say, "Give it about fifteen minutes, I've got someone at 80 percent."

He was running a utility. In a government shutdown. The irony was right there and nobody said it out loud, which I thought showed remarkable restraint.

Then there was the woman at the outlet near the women's restroom — the one outlet, singular, the one that everyone had to walk past and nobody wanted to commit to because sitting next to a restroom door in an airport is a specific kind of social death. She had claimed it early, maybe 9:30, and she had placed her coat — her good wool coat, camel-colored — over the outlet and the surrounding area, perhaps four square feet of airport carpet, and she sat on the coat with her phone plugged in and her eyes closed and her posture communicating, with the eloquence of the entire Western tradition of property law: *this is mine. I have improved this land. Do not test me.*

I respected her, honestly. That's how you survive the commons. You annex.

And then the trail mix. This is important. The Hudson News had maybe forty or fifty bags of trail mix — the kind with the clear window in the packaging so you can see the specific ratio of your disappointment — and there was no announcement, no rationing system, no one in charge, but around midnight a line formed. It just *happened*. People queued. Orderly, silent, one bag per person, and no one discussed it but everyone understood that this was the social contract and you were not going to take two bags under any circumstances. And each person who reached the register held their bag for a moment and made a kind of private topographical assessment — where are the cashews, where are the peanuts, am I getting a reasonable cashew distribution here — and some people put their bag back and selected another one, and no one judged this because the cashew question was *understood*. The raisins were a public fiction. A tax, as I said to someone — I can't remember who — a tax on wanting cashews. You had to eat the raisins in order to have deserved the cashews. That was the arrangement.

I bought my trail mix at — I'm going to say 12:30. I ate the raisins first to get them out of the way. Reader, I ate the raisins.

Sorry — not *reader*. Listener? Historian? What are you? You're the you in this, whoever hears this. I suppose you're the archive. I'm talking to the archive. That's either very grand or very sad and I'd rather not determine which.

Where was I. Right — the geography. So by about 1 a.m., the terminal had organized itself into zones. This is the part that actually is interesting, sociologically, if I'm allowed to say that without sounding like I think I'm writing a paper. Near the window bank at B26 — the view of the tarmac, the parked planes, the ground vehicles that had gone still — that's where the families collected. Parents with children. There was a resource-sharing logic to it: diapers, wipes, those squeezable applesauce pouches that all modern children seem to eat. Near B28, the business travelers, because that's where Power Strip Man had established his grid, and the business travelers were the ones who needed to send emails explaining their absence and they needed to do it with full batteries and an air of professional martyrdom. Between B29 and B30, a kind of no-man's-land, the miscellaneous, the people who didn't fit a category — a clump of college students who found each other the way college students always do, by gravitating toward the floor; a few solo travelers who stared at their phones and looked like they were going to be fine and also like they might be the loneliest people on earth. And then B31, which was the farthest from everything, the least lit, the most neglected, and which became — by a process I did not observe but whose results were unmistakable — the sleeping section. By 2 a.m. there were maybe thirty people prone between the seats and the walls of B31, some with actual travel pillows and blankets as if they had *foreseen*, and some just folded over armrests in positions that made my vertebrae ache to look at.

There was a man whose phone alarm went off at 2 a.m. and no one could figure out whose it was, and it played the default iPhone marimba tone for a full two minutes while people sat up and looked around with the particular fury of the almost-asleep, and someone near the charging station had made a nest out of three Hudson News bags and a raincoat, and a quiet man near gate B26 had folded his coat into a pillow and placed it under a stranger's sleeping child's head, and the Hudson News gate was half-down like an eyelid, and the whole terminal had that quality of light that doesn't know what time it is — fluorescent, eternal, the light of purgatory or a DMV, same thing.

You know, I read once that airports are technically non-places. Marc Augé — French anthropologist — he wrote about spaces of transience that have no identity, no history, no relation. Airports, highways, hotel rooms. I thought about that at about 3 a.m. while eating raisins. I thought: Marc, with all due respect, you're wrong. This place has a lot of relation happening. This place is practically *teeming* with relation.

But I'm getting ahead of myself.

The people? Yes, I — yes, I can talk about the people.

I should tell you about Dolores and George. Those aren't their real names. I don't know their real names. I'm going to call them Dolores and George because they looked like a Dolores and a George, by which I mean they looked like two people whose best years were behind them and who were not happy about it but had made a kind of accommodation with the fact, the way you make an accommodation with a landlord you can't afford to leave. They were both — seventies? Late seventies? He was tall and thin and had the bearing of a man who had been handsome and still held himself as if this were current information. She was small and had one of those short silver haircuts that says *I am done performing femininity for your benefit and you are welcome*. I admired that haircut.

They ended up next to each other. I don't know if it was chance or the logic of demographics — old people gravitating to seats, and there were only so many seats. But by 10 p.m. they were in adjacent chairs at B27, and by 10:15 they were fighting.

Not fighting. *Bickering*. There's a distinction, and the distinction matters. Fighting has stakes. Bickering is a recreational activity disguised as conflict. They bickered the way championship doubles tennis players volley — automatically, precisely, with an ease that suggested thousands of hours of practice, except they had never met before.

It started with the armrest. The shared armrest between their two seats, which is of course the most contested three inches in all of civilian life. He put his elbow on it. She looked at his elbow. She did not say anything. She looked at his elbow the way a woman looks at a spot on a blouse that she is going to have to take to the dry cleaner. Then she placed her own elbow — gently, deliberately — approximately one half inch from his. Not touching. Just *present*. Establishing a claim.

He said, "You've got your own armrest on the other side."

She said, "So do you."

He said, "I was here first."

She said, "Are you twelve?"

He said, "I'm seventy-eight, which means I have seniority," and she said, "I'm eighty-one, so by your own logic," and he removed his elbow with a kind of theatrical surrender that was, I swear, the most gallant thing I have ever seen a man do.

It went on. The temperature — he was too warm, she was too cold, and they discussed this as though the HVAC system of LaGuardia Terminal B were a matter of personal moral failure on the other's part. The overhead light — he wanted it on to read his newspaper, she wanted it off because it was *an assault*, and they compromised by him holding the newspaper at an angle that partially shaded her, which meant he was reading it six inches from his own face, which she told him would destroy his eyes, which he said were already destroyed, which she said was obvious from his shirt.

His shirt. She went after his shirt. It was a perfectly ordinary blue oxford and she implied it had been ironed by someone who did not love him. And he got very quiet for a moment and then he said, "Well, you'd be right about that. Nobody irons my shirts. My wife died in 2016."

And she said, "Mine died in 2014. My husband, I mean. Although he would have appreciated the —" and she sort of gestured at herself, at the ambiguity of the pronoun, and almost smiled.

They were quiet for about thirty seconds after that. I was pretending to look at my phone. I was not looking at my phone. I was looking at the space between their two chairs, which had changed. I can't tell you how it had changed. Nothing visible had shifted. But the quality of the air between them was different, the way the air in a room is different after someone has said something true.

Then he said, "The newspaper. Do you want the arts section."

He didn't phrase it as a question. He just held it out. And she took it without looking at him and said, "The crossword had better not be done," and he said, "I don't do crosswords, I'm not an animal," and she said, "No, you're a man who can't dress himself," and they were off again.

I watched them for hours. I'm not proud of this. Watching strangers doesn't require pride.
