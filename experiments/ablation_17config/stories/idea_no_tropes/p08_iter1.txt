# The Gobi Desert Ultramarathon Relay

Carlene taped the entry form to our case rack between a dog-bite advisory and a flyer about the blood drive.

This was last October. I remember because the blood-drive flyer had a cartoon turkey on it, even though it was a blood drive and not a Thanksgiving potluck, and I pointed this out to Carlene, and Carlene said, "Denise, nobody else notices things like that," which is something she says to me approximately once per delivery day and has been saying for six years, ever since they partnered us on Route 2214, which covers the east side of Kenmore and a strip of Akron proper that includes two schools, a dialysis center, and a man on Overlook Drive who has trained his beagle to accept Priority Mail through a slot in the fence.

The entry form was for something called the Gobi Desert Ultramarathon Relay. Two-person teams. Two hundred and fifty kilometers. Five stages over seven days. Across Mongolia. Carlene had highlighted the registration deadline in yellow and written *us???* in the margin with a ballpoint pen, which I knew was the pen chained to the express-mail clipboard because it left that specific shade of blue and also because the chain had left a small scratch across the word "ultramarathon."

I should say some things about Carlene. Carlene runs 50Ks for fun. She once described shin splints as "interesting." She eats lunch at 10:45 every morning — turkey on wheat, an apple, six almonds she counts out of a Ziploc — and she eats it while walking, which I have told her is a choking hazard and which she has told me is "efficient fueling." She is five-foot-four and has the cardiovascular endurance of something that migrates. She is the only person I have ever met who has opinions about sock fabric.

I should say some things about myself. I am forty-three. I have been a letter carrier for eleven years. I can tell you the walk time between any two houses on our route within a margin of four seconds. I do not run for fun. I run if a dog is involved, and even then I prefer a firm "No" and a change of direction.

I was married to Kevin for nine years. Kevin is an HVAC technician. We bought a house in Ellet. Then Kevin moved to his brother Gary's basement in Cuyahoga Falls, and I kept the house, which I could suddenly afford because one mortgage payment is easier than two people pretending the mortgage is the problem. The divorce was not dramatic. Kevin went one direction, I went another, the mail still gets delivered. We divided things fairly. He got the lawn mower and the good crockpot. I got the house and the knowledge that at Gary's Fourth of July cookout, approximately six weeks before he moved out, Kevin told Gary's wife, Tammy, that Denise — that I — "doesn't really have a thing."

I was in the kitchen doorway. I was holding a paper plate of potato salad. Tammy said, "Oh," in that specific way that means a person has been handed a piece of information they did not ask for but will absolutely remember and distribute. Kevin was facing the yard. The potato salad was from Costco, which I mention because I remember the weight of the plate and the particular way the mayonnaise was catching the light through the screen door while my husband explained, to a woman who sells Pampered Chef, that the defining characteristic of his wife was the absence of a defining characteristic.

I don't know what Tammy said next. I went back to the living room and watched Gary's kids play Mario Kart and ate the potato salad with a plastic fork that was slightly too small, and later Kevin and I drove home and I didn't mention it because what would I mention? He didn't say anything mean. He said something true in a way I wasn't supposed to hear, and the true thing was that I wake up and I go to work and I carry mail and I come home and I watch whatever's on and I go to bed, and if you graphed my life it would look like Route 2214 — a loop that starts and ends at the same place and covers the same distance every day and is useful to people but is not, by any reasonable definition, a thing.

Anyway, I signed the entry form the next morning. In the break room. With the pen chained to the express-mail clipboard. Carlene wasn't even there. I just signed it and left the form on her case rack with my half of the registration fee in a stamped envelope, because I know how to deliver things.

None of this matters, probably. What matters is that five months later I was standing in Mongolia looking at a desert that went in every direction there was, and I'd paid $4,200 I did not have, and my left knee already hurt, and Carlene was doing calf stretches against a jeep and smiling at the horizon like the horizon had said something nice to her.

---

Base camp was a staging area in the steppe that looked like someone had dropped a outdoor-gear catalog in the middle of nothing and let the wind arrange it. Sixty teams. Tents color-coded by nationality. A medical tent with a Red Cross flag and a Mongolian flag and a banner that said WELCOME RUNNERS in English and two other languages and featured clip art of a running man who appeared to be on fire, which I chose to read as a warning.

I stood at the registration table and took inventory. This is what I do. Every morning before I touch a single piece of mail, I stand at my case rack and I inventory what's there — flats, letters, parcels, SPRs — so I know the shape of my day. It's a habit. Carlene says I inventory social situations the same way, and I have told her that this is not a flaw, it is preparedness.

So here is what was there:

The Danes were already hydrating. Four of them, all blond, all silent, all wearing matching compression gear in a shade of blue that suggested both Scandinavian design and a deep institutional funding source. They moved with the coordinated calm of people who had peaked at the right time and knew it.

There was a team of Basque firefighters who looked like the kind of men who rescue people from cliffs and then decline interviews about it. There was a pair of New Zealand sheep farmers — women, mid-fifties, legs like bridge cables, skin like good leather — who were retaping their ankles with the speed and indifference of people who tape ankles between shearing seasons. There was a team from Japan that communicated entirely through nods. There was a British team that had already set up a small folding table and was drinking tea. Actual tea, from actual ceramic mugs they had carried into the Gobi Desert.

Then there were the ophthalmologists.

Dr. Todd and Dr. Brent. Scottsdale, Arizona. Matching jerseys — teal, with a logo that appeared to be an eyeball inside a sun, under which was printed *VISION QUEST RACING*, which I want to be clear is a name they chose on purpose. Matching sunglasses. Matching ease. They had the tanned, low-body-fat look of men who have money and time and a specific subscription to a magazine about obstacle-course racing. They were doing partner stretches that involved a lot of eye contact.

They saw us. They saw our shirts — standard gray USPS tees, because Carlene had said we should "represent" and I had not had the energy to argue. They saw our off-brand hydration packs from Dick's Sporting Goods. Dr. Brent lowered his matching sunglasses — Oakleys, the kind with the iridescent lenses that make the wearer look like a very fit insect — and he smiled the way a dentist smiles before telling you about a cavity.

"Oh," he said. "Are you two the cute ones?"

Carlene smiled. Carlene smiles at things. It's her default. She once smiled at a man on Berwin Avenue who told her the government was tracking him through his water bill, and after she smiled, he apologized and took his mail normally.

I did not smile. I looked at Dr. Brent, and I looked at Dr. Todd, and I inventoried them the way I inventory a route deviation — noted, categorized, filed under *obstacle*.

"We're the mail carriers from Akron, Ohio," I said. "If that's what you mean."

What Dr. Brent meant and what I meant were two different delivery zones, and we both knew it.

Behind the ophthalmologists, behind the tents and the medical station and the supply crates and the Jeeps, the Gobi Desert started. It didn't build up to anything. It didn't have a welcome mat. It was just suddenly there — flat, then not flat, then flat again, brown-gold-beige-khaki-dust as far as I could see and then farther. No trees in the meaningful sense. No structures. No addresses. A delivery area with no houses, no dogs, and no shade. The footing looked like the surface of a planet where footing was not a priority. There were mountains in the far distance that looked like they'd been left there by mistake and no one had filed the paperwork to move them.

Carlene was looking at it. Not the way I was looking at it — measuring it, clocking the gradient, estimating the distance the way I estimate how many steps between 4120 and 4122 Overlook Drive (thirty-seven, thirty-nine if Mr. Garrido's sprinkler is on). Carlene was looking at the Gobi the way people look at things they've been waiting to see. Like the desert was a place she already knew from a photograph and now it was real and being real made it somehow more than the photograph and the waiting combined.

She had that expression. I don't know how to describe it without making it sound like more than it was. She was just standing there, hand on her hip, head tilted about ten degrees, and she was *looking*, and whatever she was seeing, it was not a delivery area with poor infrastructure.

"It's really something," she said.

I told her the UV index was probably criminal and we should find our tent assignment.

---

They started us in waves. We were in the last wave, which felt correct.

The first two hundred meters were packed dirt, and I thought: fine. I know dirt. Dirt is half of every walking surface on our route between March and November. Then the packed dirt ended and the sand started, and the sand was not fine. The sand was an argument against forward motion. Each step was a negotiation in which I proposed to move forward and the ground countered with a lateral slide. My shoes, which the man at the running store in Fairlawn had promised were "trail-rated," immediately filled with grit. Within ten minutes, there was sand in my sports bra. Within twenty, there was sand between every two adjacent toes, and between several toes I had previously not known were adjacent. Within an hour, there was sand in areas where mail should not be sorted.

Carlene was five paces ahead. Always five paces. Not ten, not three — five, as if she'd measured it and decided this was the distance that said *I'm not leaving you but I am also not waiting for you.*

The first shared checkpoint was at kilometer twelve. The Danes were leaving as we arrived. They had a system: four identical bottles, which they filled from the communal water station to what I can confirm was exactly the 200-milliliter mark, because I watched. They poured. They capped. They drank. They looked at us the way you look at a package that has arrived at the wrong address — with polite, total disinterest.

"They're rationing like it's the last water on earth," I told Carlene.

"It kind of is," Carlene said. "Out here."

I drank my water. It was warm. Everything was warm. The air was warm. The sand was warm. My skeleton, I was fairly certain, was warm. I poured some water on the back of my neck and a Danish woman watched me do it with an expression I will describe as Scandinavian horror.

The blisters started at kilometer fourteen. Left heel first — a hot spot that went critical somewhere between one step and the next the way a dog on a route goes from sleeping to running: no warning, all commitment. Then the right arch. Then a creative one on the ball of my left foot that felt personal.

I had blisters on top of blisters. I had blisters in places where I had not previously been confident I had skin. I had what I can only describe as a blister ecosystem — a thriving community of blisters of various sizes and maturity levels, all contributing to the local economy of pain.

Carlene, five paces ahead, was running with a posture I can only call voluntary. Her arms were loose. Her stride was even. She was doing this on purpose. She had done things like this before — on purpose — many times.

"Can you slow down," I said.

"I'm going eight-minute pace," she said. "This is slow."

"Can you go nine."

"Nine is walking."

"Then I'd like to walk."

"Denise."

"I am blistered in places I can't name in public."

"You can walk the uphills."

"What uphills? It's flat. It's the flattest place on earth. It's what flat looks like when flat gets ambitious."

She slowed. Not to nine minutes. To something in between — a pace that acknowledged my suffering without fully endorsing it.

At kilometer eighteen, I asked her about the electrolyte tablets.

"You need to be taking them every forty-five minutes," she said.

"I've been taking them."

"You've been taking the flavored ones."

"Because the unflavored ones taste like licking a battery."

"The flavored ones have sugar alcohols that can cause GI distress."

"Carlene, I am in GI distress. I have been in GI distress since I bought the plane ticket."

"The unflavored ones absorb faster."

"The unflavored ones are a war crime against the mouth."

She turned around then. She was still moving — walking backward, which she could do in deep sand because apparently she had no physical limitations, just a turkey sandwich and six almonds and God on her side — and she looked at me with an expression I recognized. It was the expression she wore at mile four of our route, when the Berwin Avenue dogs started up and I started narrating my irritation to no one and she let me go for two houses and then said, gently, that the dogs couldn't help it.

"You're running angry," she said.

"I'm not running at all. I'm shuffling. But yes, I'm angry."

"At what, though?"

I looked at her. Behind her, the desert stretched out to where the desert ended and the sky started, which was not a clearly marked line. The Basque firefighters were half a mile ahead, moving in a way that suggested they had somewhere important to be. The ophthalmologists were a quarter mile ahead of the firefighters, which was offensive. A hot wind came across the flats and pushed sand into my eyes and my mouth and my future.

"Everything," I said. "The sand. The blisters. The Danes."

Carlene turned back around and didn't say anything. She didn't need to. The question wasn't about the Danes, and the sand was just the sand, and she already knew what I was angry at, and I already knew she knew, and neither of us said so because that is how a six-year partnership works: you carry the things you both know in the space between you like a parcel that neither of you signed for but that keeps showing up on the route.

She went back to five paces ahead.

I went back to the blisters.

---

The night checkpoint looked like the end of something. Not the end of the race — we weren't even halfway — but the end of the version of the day I'd been narrating to myself. The version where this was a story about two mail carriers from Akron who were in over their heads and that was the joke. That version had carried me through fifty kilometers of sand and sun and the Danish water protocols and Dr. Todd and Dr. Brent's matching sunglasses, and now it was dark and cold — genuinely cold, the temperature dropping so fast it felt like the desert was making a point about what it had been holding back — and the joke part was quiet and what was underneath it was not a joke.

Other teams had systems. The Japanese team had an efficient two-person bivouac that went up in four minutes. The British team had a thermos. The Basque firefighters had, I swear to God, a small portable stove on which they were heating soup. Dr. Todd and Dr. Brent had a battery-powered fan clipped to their tent pole, which was useless in the cold but which they left running anyway, and the small whirring sound followed me as Carlene and I found our allotted square of tarp and sat on it.
