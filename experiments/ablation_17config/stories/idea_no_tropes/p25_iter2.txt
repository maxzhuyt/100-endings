# The Butcher's Apologies

---

## I.

The pork loin had gone grey at its edges the way the sky goes grey before it commits to rain — not suddenly, not dramatically, but with a kind of tired permission, as though the flesh had simply agreed to stop pretending. Ernst Kessler held it under the light above his cutting board and turned it with both hands, the way one might turn a sleeping child to check for fever, and he said to it, quietly, "I'm sorry about this. I'm sorry about all of it."

He said this every morning. He said it to the pork and to the chicken thighs and to the small, mournful sausages he linked on Tuesdays. He did not say it loudly enough for anyone to hear, though this was an unnecessary precaution, because there was no one in the shop to hear it, and there had not been anyone in the shop since ten past nine, when old Margit Pfeiffer had come in for her weekly hundred grams of liverwurst and left a trail of melting snow from the door to the counter that Ernst had mopped up with the focus of a man rationing his tasks across an otherwise empty day.

The shop occupied the ground floor of a building on the Hauptstraße that had been a butcher's shop for three generations, and it wore this history the way the building wore its plaster — in layers, each one slightly more cracked than the last. The display case ran six feet along the front wall and was lit by tubes that had yellowed into the color of old teeth. Inside, small arrangements of cuts sat on beds of parsley that Ernst replaced every second day, though by any rational accounting the parsley was performing for no audience. A single fly strip hung near the back door. It was clean. Even the flies had found better places to be.

Above the front door, a brass bell on a curved spring announced visitors with a bright, surprised sound, as if it, too, had forgotten what it was for and was startled to be reminded. Ernst could go entire afternoons without hearing it. He had developed, over these quiet years, a relationship with the bell not unlike his relationship with the meat — he felt obscurely responsible for its silence, as though a more deserving man would have generated enough custom to keep it singing.

At four o'clock, an hour before the posted closing time, he began wiping down the surfaces. He did this methodically, moving from the block to the slicer to the scale to the counter to the glass of the display case, and there was in his movements the unhurried precision of a man who has learned to expand small tasks to fill large silences. His hands were large and clean and surprisingly fine — a butcher's hands, thick-palmed and scarred across the knuckles, but with tapered fingers that seemed designed for some instrument he had never learned to play. He was forty-three. He had his father's shop and his mother's soft, round face and a body that was built for labor but that labor had not, in recent years, had much use for — broad across the shoulders, going gentle at the waist, the body of a man who lifts heavy things tenderly.

His mother telephoned at four fifteen. She telephoned every day at four fifteen. She asked if he had eaten. He said he had, which was not true, but the lie was so small and so habitual that it had ceased to register as deception for either of them. She told him about a television program she had watched about the migration patterns of Arctic terns. She told him she was sending him on Friday to deliver a parcel of cuts to the Hofmann household — the father had been out of work for three months, and the family could use a good meal, she said, although everyone in the village understood that Anna Kessler's charity deliveries served a double and perhaps primary purpose, which was to force her son into contact with other human beings, to press him gently into the social body of the village the way a thumb presses a seed into soil, hoping something might take root.

Ernst agreed. He always agreed. He wrapped the grey-edged pork loin in brown paper and set it aside for his own dinner, though he would eat only a little of it, standing at his kitchen counter upstairs, looking out the window at the Hauptstraße as it emptied into evening.

He locked the register, which contained what the register always contained — enough to justify opening tomorrow if one did not think about it too carefully and Ernst was, in his quiet way, a man practiced in not thinking about things too carefully. He removed his apron. He folded it with the stained side in. He turned off the yellowed lights.

Through the front window, in the last of the afternoon light, he could see the Hauptstraße in its familiar winter composition: the bakery across the way with its lit windows, the pharmacy with its green cross, the small municipal parking lot where Josef Brenner's van sat in the same spot it had occupied since Josef's license was revoked in October. And Lena Weil — the schoolteacher, the widow — walking home from the primary school with her bag over one shoulder and her coat buttoned to the throat, passing directly in front of his window at the pace of someone who knows exactly how long the walk home is and has no reason to vary it.

She did not look in. She had never looked in, not in the particular way of choosing not to look, but in the simpler way of a person whose gaze had no reason to catch on this shop, this window, this man standing behind the glass with his folded apron in his hands.

Ernst watched her pass. He did not think of this as longing. He thought of it as looking, which was something he was allowed to do, which cost nothing and disturbed no one.

He locked the front door. The bell above it shivered once, faintly, from the vibration, but did not ring.

## II.

The knocking came at the back door on a Wednesday morning in February, and it had a quality that Ernst would later fail to describe to himself when he tried — not aggressive, not tentative, but rhythmic and unhurried, the knock of a man who has all day and finds all of it interesting.

Ernst wiped his hands on his apron and opened the door onto the alley that ran behind the Hauptstraße, where the bins were kept and where, in autumn, wasps gathered around the sweet rot of the bakery's discards. The man standing in the alley was tall and broad-chested and younger than Ernst by perhaps five years, with dark hair cut close and a face that arranged itself around a smile the way some faces arrange themselves around the eyes or the jaw — the smile was the organizing principle, and everything else followed from it.

"Kessler," the man said, not as a question.

"Yes."

"Martin Jaeger." He shifted the parcel he was carrying — something wrapped in white linen, held against his hip — and extended his free hand. His handshake was warm and encompassing, the kind that uses both the palm and the fingers, and Ernst felt his own hand disappear inside it briefly, not unpleasantly. "I've taken the Lindner farmhouse. Outside the village, on the eastern road."

Ernst said he knew the property. He said this with a small nod, because he did not know what else to say, because men did not often come to his back door, or his front door, and the surprise of it had sutured his conversational abilities into something rudimentary and stiff.

"I'm a hunter," Jaeger said. He said it the way another man might say *I'm a teacher* or *I'm a carpenter* — as a settled fact, a category of being rather than a profession. "The land out there — the woods past the Lindner property, the ravine, the old forestry roads — it's thick with game. More than I could possibly use." He looked at Ernst with an expression of frank, uncomplicated warmth. "It occurred to me that I'm new here and you're a butcher and there might be a natural friendship in that."

He held out the parcel.

"A gift," he said. "Neighborly. You'll know what to do with it better than I will."

Ernst took it. The linen was clean and white and wrapped with a care that he registered immediately — not bunched or knotted but folded in neat overlapping layers, tucked at the ends like an envelope, the way one wraps something one respects. He carried it to the cutting board and untied the cloth and opened it under the light.

The cut that lay inside was roughly the size and shape of a large roast, and the first thing Ernst's hands told him — before his eyes had finished their assessment, before the professional vocabulary that lived in the back of his mind like a reference library had begun to organize itself — was that this was extraordinarily fresh meat. Not recently killed, which is a different thing, because recently killed meat has a tautness, a resistance, the residual tension of a body that has not yet accepted its new condition. This meat had been killed and then rested, properly, at a controlled temperature, for exactly the right period, so that the fibers had relaxed into a state of — the word that came to Ernst's mind was *willingness*. The meat was willing. It wanted to be worked.

He turned it under the light. The color was deep red tending to burgundy, darker than beef but without the purple undertone of venison, and the marbling was extraordinary — not the thick white veins of a fatty cut but an intricate, almost capillary network of intramuscular fat, evenly distributed, so fine in places that it looked less like marbling than like a watermark held up to the light. He pressed his thumb into the surface and felt it yield and then recover, slowly but completely, the way good flesh does, the way flesh does that has been well-fed and well-muscled and has lived, for whatever span it lived, in a state of adequate health and regular motion.

He lifted it closer. The grain was long and fine, the fibers running in clean parallel lines, and where the cut had been made — cleanly, he noted, with a sharp blade and a confident hand — the cross-section revealed a layered architecture: a thin outer membrane, a subcutaneous fat layer of uniform thickness, the deep red muscle body, and at the center, near what would have been the bone line, a denser core of darker tissue that he could not immediately classify. He smelled it. Clean. Mineral. Faintly sweet, in the way that very fresh red meat is sweet before oxidation begins its slow, souring work.

He did not recognize it.

This was, for Ernst Kessler, an unusual experience. He had apprenticed under his father at fourteen. He had spent two years at the vocational school in Linz. He had broken down carcasses of cattle, pig, lamb, goat, horse, deer, rabbit, and, on one occasion, a wild boar so large that it had to be winched through the back door. He knew meat the way a librarian knows books — not only by title and author but by the feel of the spine, the weight of the pages, the particular smell of the binding glue. He could identify a cut by touch in a dark room. He could tell you, from a cross-section, what the animal had eaten, how it had moved, whether it had been stressed at slaughter.

And he could not place this.

He considered the possibilities. Some kind of game — the color suggested it, the leanness of the fat, the minerality. But the marbling was wrong for any wild animal he knew. Wild animals run and starve and fight and their muscles record these events in coarse grain and uneven fat distribution. This meat was from something that had lived well. Something raised, perhaps. Some heritage breed he hadn't encountered, a crossbreed, one of those experimental livestock programs he'd read about in the trade journals he still subscribed to and still read cover to cover, sitting upstairs in the kitchen with the journal propped against the salt cellar.

"What is it?" he asked, and was aware, as he asked it, that the question had come late — that he had been examining the cut for some time and that Jaeger had been leaning against the doorframe with his arms folded and his smile in place, watching Ernst work with what appeared to be genuine pleasure, the way one watches a man do something he is very good at.

"The woods out there are full of things," Jaeger said. He said this easily, lightly, the way one deflects a question not because the answer is dangerous but because the answer is less interesting than the question. "Old breeds. Animals that have been left alone for decades. You know how it is — the Lindner property backs up against federal forest, and no one's hunted it properly in years."

Ernst nodded, though he was not sure the explanation fully accounted for what lay on his cutting board. But Jaeger was already straightening up from the doorframe, already pulling on his gloves, already preparing to leave with the efficient purposefulness of a man who has many things to attend to, and the moment for follow-up questions — the specific moment when it would have been socially natural to ask, *No, but what species?* or *What animal has this muscle structure?* — passed in the way such moments pass, not with a closing door but with a gentle, mutual, almost imperceptible agreement that the conversation had found its shape and would not be improved by further pressing.

Jaeger stepped forward and placed his hand on Ernst's shoulder. The touch lasted precisely long enough to be warm and not long enough to be strange, and Ernst felt the weight of it — the broad palm, the firm fingers — settle against his body with a directness that startled him, not because it was threatening but because it was so uncomplicatedly physical. Ernst was not a man whom other men touched. He was not a man whom anyone touched, and the sensation of another person's hand on his shoulder traveled through him with a force entirely disproportionate to the gesture.

"I'll bring more," Jaeger said. "I always have more than I need."

He left through the back door, and Ernst heard his footsteps in the alley, confident and unhurried, and then he was alone with the cut of meat under the light, and the clean white linen folded on the counter, and the smell — mineral, sweet, the smell of something that had very recently been alive and was now, through the intermediary of a skilled hand that was not his own, becoming something else.

He trimmed the edges. He sectioned it. He worked with the careful, exploratory attention of a man learning a new text, letting the meat's own structure guide his knife, following the natural separations between muscle groups the way a woodworker follows grain. And he did not apologize to it. He did not notice that he did not apologize. He was too absorbed. The meat was, from a technical standpoint, the finest he had ever handled, and some part of him — the part that was still an apprentice, the part that still loved the work for its own sake — was simply, quietly, deeply glad.

## III.

There is a way that good fortune moves through a small village, and it does not move the way it moves through a city — in straight lines, through formal channels, impersonally. In a village of eleven hundred souls, good fortune moves the way warmth moves through water: slowly, by contact, expanding outward from its source in concentric, overlapping circles until even the farthest edge has felt a little of the heat.

Ernst labeled it *Wildspezialität*. He wrote this on small cards in his careful, upright hand and placed them in the display case beside the cuts, which he had portioned and priced after an evening of deliberation during which he'd stood in his kitchen with the trade journals open around him and a calculator and a glass of beer and an expression that his mother, had she seen it, would not have recognized — not worry, not resignation, but *concentration*, the focused, almost boyish seriousness of a man presented with a problem that interests him.

He could not price it as beef without inviting comparison he wasn't sure it would survive — not because the quality was inferior, because it was not, because it was by every metric he possessed vastly superior, but because the taste was its own category, and categories require names to become commerce. He priced it between venison and beef. He placed the first cuts in the case on a Thursday morning, on a fresh bed of parsley he'd bought the day before.
