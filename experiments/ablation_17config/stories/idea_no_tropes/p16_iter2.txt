# The Biography of Dr. Émile Voss

---

## I.

The clock on the wall behind her had no hands. She'd taken them off herself, she said, years ago — or no, that wasn't right, the clock had come that way from the shop in Annecy. Or maybe it was Grenoble. It didn't matter. She mentioned it only because the journalist was looking at it, and she wanted him to know she was aware of being looked past.

"You can put that away if it bothers you," she said, meaning the recorder. "Or leave it. It doesn't change what I'll say."

The recorder stayed. Its red light blinked on the kitchen table between them, next to a sugar bowl with a hairline crack running from rim to base and a brown bottle of chlorhexidine solution that she used, she explained without being asked, to wipe down the countertops every morning and evening. Hospital-grade. Well — veterinary-grade, actually, which was stronger. Hospitals were squeamish about concentration. Animals couldn't afford squeamishness. Neither could the people who cut them open.

"You wanted to hear about Dr. Voss."

She said the name the way one says the name of a disease one has survived: with neither affection nor resentment, but with the particular flatness that comes from having repeated it too many times in too many examining rooms of the mind.

"I'll start with the neck. No — I'll start with the village. No."

She pressed her thumb into the table's edge. The nail was short, squared off. A professional nail, though the profession was forty years gone.

"I'll start with the pigs, because that's what I remember first when I try to remember anything, is the smell. Which wasn't the pigs themselves but the mud they'd been kept in, which was not proper mud at all but a kind ofite — chalk marl —ite, do you see, mixed with dung and old straw until itite — no.Ite. What's the word. The calciumite compound.ite —ite —ite —"

She stopped. She pressed the thumb harder.

"ite. Why can't I — it doesn't matter. It was calcium-ite.Ite. A calcium compound in the soil. You could feel it when you knelt. It ate your knees. The pigs' hooves were in terrible condition because of it, and that's the first thing I noticed when I arrived, before I noticed anything else — I noticed the pigs' hooves, because that's what I was trained to do. You see?"

She looked at him as though he'd challenged her.

"I was trained to look at animals. I was very good at looking at animals. What happened later was not my — but I'll get to that."

Something behind her eyes shifted. A door opened, or failed to close.

"The morning I want to tell you about, with the neck — but I'm not ready for the neck. Let me do the village first. Let me do the village properly. Then the others. Then Voss. Then the neck. In that order."

She rearranged the sugar bowl, the chlorhexidine, the edge of her own sleeve.

"In that order," she repeated, as if rehearsing a suture pattern she'd tied ten thousand times and could still get wrong in the dark.

---

## II.

The village was called Sault-de-Vaucluse, though it appeared on only certain maps and on those maps was often misspelled. It sat in a depression in the hills southeast of — she waved her hand — somewhere past where the mail reliably arrived. She had come in the back of a cart in April of 1893 carrying a leather case of veterinary instruments and a letter of introduction to a man named Dussart, who kept sheep and whose sheep had worms. The letter was forged. The instruments were real. She was twenty-three and had been dismissed from her previous position for reasons she described as "a disagreement about dosage" and did not elaborate.

The village consisted of perhaps two hundred souls, a church with a cracked bell that rang in B-flat when it rang at all, forty or fifty stone houses arranged without visible logic around a central square that was not actually square, a communting trough green with algae, three drinking establishments of declining legitimacy, and a medical practice operated by a Dr. Ferreol, who was seventy-one and who, she learned later, had not physically examined a patient in six years, preferring to diagnose from his window by observing how people walked.

Dr. Ferreol died in May. Not of the sickness — of age, or spite, or the accumulated weight of never having touched another human body when he could avoid it. He died in his chair by the window with his spectacles on and a plate of cold lentils on the sill, and when they found him the lentils had a film on them that she said was the exact color and consistency of what she would later find lining the interior walls of the first cyst she assisted in opening, but she was getting ahead of herself.

"The drainage," she said. "I should tell you about the drainage."

The drainage was catastrophic. There was one well for the entire village and its water had a mineral taste — the same calcium compound — that suggested to her a shared groundwater source running beneath pens and cesspits alike. The lanes flooded in rain and the runoff carried everything. She had been trained in asepsis — limited training, veterinary training, but training — and she could see within her first week that Sault-de-Vaucluse was a textbook of contamination vectors. The pig pens had no proper floors. The root cellars where food was stored doubled as sick rooms when the houses were too small. The barn nearest the church — a large stone structure with good ventilation and a flagstone floor — was the cleanest space in the village, and she noted this silently, the way she would note the structural soundness of a room in which she might one day need to operate.

She did not yet know she would need to operate. She was there for sheep worms.

"The sickness started in June," she said. "Or it started earlier and was noticed in June. These are different things."

A woman named Clairaut — the baker's wife, or the baker's sister, she wasn't sure — developed a growth on her left forearm, midway between the wrist and elbow, on the ulnar side. The nurse remembered the side because she remembered thinking the placement was consistent with a lipoma or, in a dog, a mast cell tumor, and then being ashamed of the comparison, and then being ashamed of the shame, because the comparison was the only clinical framework she had and it was either that or nothing.

The growth was subcutaneous. It was firm but not hard. It was warm — warmer than the surrounding tissue by a degree or two, like a fresh bruise but without discoloration. Clairaut said it didn't hurt. She said it hummed.

"Hummed," the journalist said, the first time his voice was clearly audible on the recording.

"I didn't believe her either. Not then."

Within two weeks there were six more. A man with a growth behind his right ear. A child — the child's was on the chest, high up, near the clavicle — and the mother said it had appeared overnight, that she had checked the boy at dusk and he was clean and at dawn it was there, the size of a quail's egg, and warm. All of them warm. The skin over the growths was taut and faintly translucent if you held a candle behind it, and at night — at night, some of the afflicted said, and some of their families confirmed — the growths seemed to produce a faint light of their own. Not bright. Not like a lamp. Like a coal that remembered being a fire.

She described all of this in the tone of a woman reading postmortem notes. She did not raise her voice. She did not embellish. When she used the word "luminous" she pronounced it as though it were a measurement — a unit, not a quality.

"They grew faster when the patient slept," she said. "I was the first to document that. I measured. Every six hours. I used calipers I'd bought for measuring bone density in dogs. The growths increased in volume by as much as a third during sleep and remained stable during waking hours. I wrote this down. I wrote everything down. Voss later took credit for the observation protocol, but it was mine."

She paused. The recorder blinked.

"The village doctor was dead. The nearest hospital was in Apt, a full day's ride by cart over roads that were not truly roads. Thirteen people were affected, then seventeen, then twenty-three. No one had died yet. No one was exactly suffering. The growths didn't hurt. They were warm and they pulsed and they hummed and they grew while people slept, and the village was terrified not because anything felt wrong, exactly, but because something felt — inhabited. Like the body had taken in a lodger without being asked."

She picked up the chlorhexidine bottle, put it down, picked it up again.

"That's when they sent for Voss."

---

## III.

She had already been working for him, which she had neglected to say and which — she admitted with a flicker that might have been irritation or might have been something worse — was probably relevant.

Voss. Dr. Émile Voss. She said his full name deliberately, as though each syllable were a stitch she was placing.

He had been a field surgeon. He had been licensed, once, properly, in Lyon — or possibly Marseille — and had practiced in military hospitals during — she hesitated — a period she described only as "after his trouble." The nature of the trouble was something she approached and retreated from three times in quick succession. The first approach: "He had been accused of — ." The first retreat: "But that isn't what matters." The second approach: "There was an incident with a patient's — ." The second retreat: "I wasn't there. I don't repeat what I haven't witnessed." The third approach: "His license was not revoked. It was — ." The third retreat: a long silence during which she aligned the sugar bowl precisely with the edge of a tile.

"He came to Sault-de-Vaucluse because no one else would have him and no one else would have it. That's simplification. But it's not inaccurate."

When she had first encountered him, Voss had been performing veterinary work in a hamlet twelve kilometers from Sault — unofficial, unlicensed, technically illegal, but the hamlet had no veterinarian and its horses kept going lame. She was sent to assist. She was the only person in the district with formal training in animal medicine, and her credentials, forged letter notwithstanding, were real enough. They had worked together for four months on horses, cattle, a dog with a torsioned stomach, and once a goat whose horn had grown inward and was pressing on the skull. That last operation she described with sudden, vivid clarity — the exact angle of the bone, the way Voss held the saw, the goat's breathing under the ether — and then seemed to catch herself narrating the wrong life and stopped.

"He was precise," she said. "That's what I'm trying to say. His hands were precise. Whatever else — and there was else — his hands were the best I'd worked under. Better than my training vet in Dijon. Better than anyone's. He could suture in near-dark. He could find a bleeder by touch alone. I say this because you need to understand what we had and what we lacked, later. We had his hands. That was not nothing."

When the village officially summoned help — which is to say, when the prefect of the commune, a man named Portalis whose authority derived from the possession of a rubber stamp and a modest surplus of chickens — sent word that a medical crisis was unfolding and any qualified person should present themselves, the summons caught exactly four respondents. This was not because four was the preferred number. It was because four was the total number of people within a day's ride who could plausibly claim any proximity to the medical profession, and "plausibly" was doing significant labor in that sentence.

She listed them.

"First: Voss. Field surgeon, disgraced, working on animals. You know about him."

"Second: Luzhin." A dentist. She said the word *dentist* the way one might say *cartographer* in the context of a sea rescue — with acknowledgment of the credential and emphasis on its irrelevance. Luzhin was from Apt. He was competent. He was furious. He had been summoned on the basis that dentistry was a manual discipline involving blades and human tissue, which was technically true in the same way that carpentry and murder were both manual disciplines involving blades and a material that could be cut. Luzhin knew mouths. He knew the architecture of the jaw, the nerve pathways of the face, the proper administration of cocaine paste for local anesthesia. He did not know abdomens, shoulders, or the space behind ears. He announced this on arrival, repeatedly, to anyone in range, and continued announcing it for the duration of his time in Sault-de-Vaucluse, which lasted, she calculated, one hundred and twelve days.

"Third: Lesage." The midwife. She came from a village even smaller than Sault, a place the nurse described as "four houses and a grudge." Madame Lesage had delivered over three hundred children across twenty years. She had performed cesarean sections in farmhouses by candlelight. She had reached into living bodies and turned babies with her bare hands. She had more practical surgical experience than Voss and Luzhin combined, and none of it was recognized by any institution, and she was accustomed to this fact in the way that a person is accustomed to a chronic pain — it no longer surprised her, but it informed every gesture. She was short, the nurse said. She had a mole above her left eyebrow. She carried her own knives.

"Fourth: Ballard." The phrenologist. The nurse said this word with a specific, calibrated contempt that she seemed to have been refining for forty years, the way one might age a particular vinegar. Ballard was a traveling phrenologist. His science was fraudulent. His credentials were imaginary. His understanding of human anatomy was derived from a chart he kept folded in his breast pocket showing the regions of the skull and their supposed correspondence to moral and intellectual qualities. He was, in the nurse's assessment, "a confidence man with clean fingernails," and she had despised him from the first moment he opened his mouth about the "organ of amativeness" during a preliminary examination and she had continued despising him without interruption for the entire course of their acquaintance and beyond it into the present moment.

"But," she said. And stopped. And looked at the handless clock.

"His hands were steady. I'll say that for him. I disliked everything about the man except the one thing that mattered, which was that when he held a retractor his hands did not shake. Not once. Not even during — but I'm not at the neck yet."

She pressed her thumb into the table again, hard enough to blanch the nail.

"So. Four of them. Plus me. A field surgeon who'd lost his right to practice on humans. A dentist who only knew the mouth. A midwife who'd never been credentialed. A phrenologist who'd never been right. And a veterinary nurse whose entire experience of surgery was on creatures that couldn't speak. These were the people who were going to operate."

She almost smiled. It wasn't a smile.

"The prefect gathered us in the church because the church was where you gathered people, and also because it was the only room large enough for five adults and a table. Luzhin said immediately that he would not cut below the neck. Lesage said she would cut wherever needed cutting. Ballard said the growths were obviously manifestations of the organ of — I don't remember which organ, one of his nonsense organs — and should be treated with magnets. Voss said nothing. He was looking at his hands. The prefect explained that there were now twenty-seven people affected and the nearest real surgeon was in Marseille and would not come because Sault-de-Vaucluse could not pay and also because the description of the symptoms had been interpreted as either a joke or evidence of collective hysteria."

"So Voss looked up. And he said — I remember this exactly — he said: 'Then we'll need the barn.'"

"And that's how it started."

## IV.

She remembers the temperature differential: the growth was 2.1 degrees warmer than the surrounding tissue.
