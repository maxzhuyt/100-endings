# The Last Hand

## I.

I am not going to tell you my name because my name was never the point. I was the chaplain. I say this the way you'd say I was the plumber or I was the one with the clipboard—a function, not a person, and if you've ever been on a forward operating base you know the difference matters. My job was to be available. A twenty-four-hour service line with a divinity degree and a government-issue Bible I kept next to a copy of the Bhagavad Gita and a Quran with a cracked spine, because you cover your bases or you don't, and either way someone's going to bleed out calling for a God you haven't read up on.

I heard everything. That was the duty. People talked to me because I was the last option before silence, and silence on a FOB is the thing that kills you—not the mortars, not the heat, not the route clearance at 0430 with your balls in your throat. The silence. The part where you lie on your rack and the plywood ceiling is eleven inches from your face and you realize that no one in your life back home can hear what you're actually saying, and you have been screaming in a frequency only dogs and chaplains can detect.

So they came to me, and I listened, and I wrote nothing down, and I told no one, and I carried it. That was the architecture of the thing. I was a load-bearing wall in a building that was always on fire.

The story I'm going to tell you is about Sergeant First Class David Dorsey and a poker game that ran every Thursday night for seven months on FOB Copperhead, Paktika Province, 2009. I heard the story from Dorsey himself, across six sessions in my office—which was half a shipping container with a rug I'd bought at the bazaar in Sharana and a sign on the door that said CHAPLAIN in letters I'd stenciled with a Sharpie because the official placard never arrived.

There are two versions.

I used to know which one he told me and which one I built later, at night, on a cot, in the dark, revising his confession the way you revise a letter you'll never send—changing a word here, a gesture there, until the man in the story does the thing you needed him to do instead of the thing he did. I used to know. It has been fifteen years, and the seam where his version ends and mine begins has been walked over so many times that I can no longer feel the ridge under my feet.

I could lie to you. I could tell you the clean version, the one I rehearsed for the after-action review I was never asked to give. Or I could tell you the one that wakes me up. But I have been in the business of listening long enough to know that choosing for somebody else is the most violent thing you can do while sitting still.

So I'll tell you both. And you can do what I couldn't, which is choose.

## II.

Thursdays at 2100, the game.

Dorsey ran it out of the MWR tent, which was a generous name for a plywood box with a television that received four channels, three of which were AFN and one of which was, through some satellite miracle no one could explain, a Turkish cooking show. The television was always on. Nobody watched it. It was atmosphere. Dorsey said a card room needed background noise or it was just a bunch of men staring at each other, and that was called group therapy, and group therapy was my department, not his.

He called meetings of more than three people without cards a "vulnerability." He said this with a straight face.

The table was a sheet of plywood on sawhorses, covered with a green felt remnant that Dorsey had procured from God knows where—he said eBay, which meant someone's wife or mother had shipped it in a care package, which meant Dorsey had traded something for it, probably Rip Its or baby wipes or one of the favors he dispensed like a ward boss. The felt was stained with coffee rings and Tabasco and a brown mark in one corner that might have been blood and might have been dip spit and that Dorsey had circled with a silver Sharpie and labeled DEALER'S BLESSING, which was the kind of joke that only works at war, where everything is a little bit sacred because nothing is.

House rules. Dorsey had typed them. Single-spaced, twelve-point Times New Roman, printed on the operations center printer at 0300 when the battle captain wasn't looking, and then laminated—laminated—in the supply office, because Dorsey understood that lamination conferred authority. You do not argue with a laminated document. You obey it the way you obey a posted speed limit: not because you believe in it but because someone cared enough to make a sign.

Rule one: Texas Hold 'Em only. No Omaha, no stud, no "weird shit" (Dorsey's language, laminated).

Rule two: Buy-in is twenty dollars. Rebuy once. No credit.

Rule three: The buy-in goes in the can. The can was a Pringles tube, sour cream and onion, with the top cut off and HOUSE FUND written on the side in electrical tape. Where the fund went was never explained. People stopped asking.

Rule four: No rank at the table. "When you sit down, you're a player. I don't care if you're a private or you're Jesus Christ in multicam, you check your shit at the door." This was in the laminated rules. The word "shit" was laminated.

Rule five: The chaplain has a standing seat but is not required to bet. This was Dorsey's gift to me, or his way of keeping an eye on me, or both. I sat in most weeks. I folded a lot. I watched.

And the players. Let me tell you about the players, because you need to see the table full before you can feel it empty, and that is the whole point, if this story has a point, which I have spent fifteen years trying to decide.

Baker, the specialist, twenty-two, from Baton Rouge, who played every hand like it was his last—which was a metaphor he would not have appreciated and which, as it turned out, was not a metaphor. Baker bet with his jaw. When he had cards, his jaw relaxed. When he was bluffing, he chewed the inside of his cheek so hard you could see the muscle working through his skin. He didn't know he did this. Nobody told him.

Cortez, staff sergeant, thirty-one, on his fourth deployment, the kind of man who had been blown up so many times his body had a residual vibration, like a tuning fork that never stops ringing. Cortez played tight. Cortez played like a man who understood that the universe was a game of negative expected value and still sat down. He folded more than anyone. When he won, he didn't celebrate. He stacked his chips with the care of a man defusing a bomb, which he had literally done, twice, before he transferred to infantry because he said explosives were too predictable.

Little Nguyen—called that to distinguish him from Big Nguyen, who was in second platoon and never played poker because he said gambling was a sin, which I respected even though I thought he was wrong, not about gambling but about the taxonomy of sin, which in my experience is less a hierarchy and more a weather system. Little Nguyen was nineteen and played poker the way puppies play with shoes—no strategy, all enthusiasm, an endless willingness to be destroyed. He lost every week. He lost with joy. He once went all-in on a two-seven offsuit because, he said, "it felt right, Chap, it felt *right*," and when he was annihilated by Dorsey's full house, he laughed so hard he fell off his chair, which was a folding camp chair, which collapsed, which made everyone laugh, and for a moment—for one moment—the plywood box with the Turkish cooking show was the best room in the world.

Pettigrew, the medic, who shouldn't have been there because medics weren't assigned to the FOB's organic unit, but who had drifted in the way people drift in at war—he was covering for a medic who was covering for a medic who had been medevacked with shrapnel in his left kidney, and the covering kept extending until Pettigrew was just theirs, a borrowed man who became permanent through the alchemy of shared danger. Pettigrew played recklessly but knew the odds. He'd been pre-med before he enlisted. He had a calculator behind his eyes. He bet like a maniac because he was bored and because arithmetic, at a certain point, is its own kind of adrenaline.

Tomaszewski—Ski, always Ski—the platoon sergeant, who outranked Dorsey but deferred to him at the table because the table was Dorsey's country. Ski played one hand an hour. The rest of the time he sat with his arms folded and a dip in his lip and watched, running a parallel poker game in his head that he sometimes described aloud: "I would have bet there. I would have check-raised. You're dead." He was usually right. He never won because he never played, and this too was a metaphor he would not have appreciated.

And Dorsey.

Dorsey, who dealt with the speed of a man who'd worked card rooms before the Army, which he had—Atlantic City, two summers, dealing blackjack to tourists and learning that the house always wins because the house controls the architecture. Dorsey, who was thirty-four and looked forty-four and moved like a man held together by willpower and ibuprofen. Dorsey, whose Percocet habit was the worst-kept secret on the FOB—he palmed them openly, popped them mid-hand, chased them with Rip Its. He had a bad back, two bad knees, and a left shoulder that dislocated if he raised his arm above ninety degrees, which meant he threw grenades side-arm, like a pitcher with a torn rotator cuff, which terrified everyone in his squad because side-arm grenades are, aerodynamically speaking, a nightmare.

He was funny. I have to tell you he was funny because the rest of this will make no sense if you don't understand the specific quality of his humor, which was the kind that made you feel less alone in exact proportion to how dark the material was. He could say things about death that would have been monstrous from anyone else and from him were—I don't have the word. Not comforting. Not cathartic. *Accurate.* He was accurate about the horror, and accuracy, when you are surrounded by institutional euphemism, is a form of love.

He owed nineteen thousand dollars in gambling debt to various entities back home, a fact he shared with me in our first session and with the poker table in the second week, announcing it as though it were a credential: "You're playing with a professional loser, gentlemen. I have been losing at a level most of you will never achieve." Everyone laughed. The debt was real. The debt was destroying his marriage. But at the table—at the table—saying it out loud made it a chip in a game, a thing with known value, a problem that fit inside a structure. And a problem that fits inside a structure is a problem you can survive.

Both Dorseys did this. Both Dorseys ran the table, popped the pills, owed the money, made the jokes. Both Dorseys loved those men with the fierce, unspoken, operationally necessary love that the Army has no vocabulary for and that the chaplain's office is supposed to hold in trust.

At this point in the story, there is only one Dorsey.

You wouldn't feel the split.

## III.

Specialist Marcus Baker was killed on 14 March 2009 at approximately 1347 hours local time when the vehicle in which he was traveling, an M-ATV in the number-three position of a four-vehicle patrol, struck a pressure-plate improvised explosive device on Route Copper, vicinity grid 42S VD 1487 3902, Paktika Province, Afghanistan. Specialist Baker, who was seated in the right rear passenger position, sustained injuries inconsistent with continued life. He was pronounced dead at the scene by the patrol medic. He was twenty-two years old. His next of kin, a mother in Baton Rouge, was notified at 0215 Central time by a casualty notification team from Fort Polk, Louisiana. The team consisted of a captain and a chaplain. I know this because I was copied on the notification report, as I was copied on all notification reports, because that was procedure, and procedure was the rope you held in the dark even when you couldn't feel the texture.

Grid 42S VD 1487 3902. That's a piece of road about the width of a Walmart aisle. That's the amount of earth required to end a person. I have the grid memorized. I have all the grids memorized. They are the only prayers I say now that mean anything, because a grid coordinate is at least honest—it tells you where something happened, not why, and I have learned to prefer the where.

Thursday came.

The table was set. Dorsey had set it—he always set it, an hour early, aligning the chips, checking the felt for debris, slotting the Pringles can in its place on the folding table to the right of the dealer's position. Everything precise. Everything in order. Seven chairs arranged around the plywood, and one of them—Baker's chair, the chair on the south side—was empty.

This is where I lose the thread. This is where I need you to hold two things in your head at once and understand that I held them for fifteen years and that the weight has shifted over time, the way a load shifts in a rucksack on a long march, and what started on my shoulders now sits on my chest.

In one version:

Dorsey looked at the chair. He didn't sit immediately. The others had filed in—Cortez, Little Nguyen, Pettigrew, Ski, two others whose names I will not use because they asked me not to—and they stood or sat and they all looked at the chair and nobody spoke. The Turkish cooking show played on the television. A woman was julienning carrots. The generator hummed. Somewhere on the FOB a door slammed in the wind.

Dorsey stood behind his dealer's seat and looked at Baker's chair for a long time. Maybe thirty seconds. Maybe a minute. In a room full of soldiers, a minute of silence has the weight of an avalanche.

Then he reached into the cargo pocket of his pants—the right cargo pocket, the one where he kept the amber bottle—and he pulled out his Percocets. The bottle was almost full. He looked at it the way you look at a letter you've decided not to send. And he walked to Pettigrew, the medic, and he put the bottle in Pettigrew's hand and closed Pettigrew's fingers around it and said: "I'm done with these. If I ask for them back, tell me to go fuck myself."

Pettigrew looked at the bottle. Looked at Dorsey. Said: "Roger that."

And Dorsey sat down and shuffled the deck and said: "Blinds are up. Sit down or go home."

Or:

Dorsey looked at the chair. Same room, same men, same julienned carrots on the television, same thirty seconds or minute of silence that weighed the same in both versions because silence doesn't know it's being narrated two ways.

And Dorsey said: "Baker would've folded. Baker always folded. Man played poker like a nun at a strip club—technically present, spiritually horrified." A beat. "Pair of sevens. That was his hand. Every goddamn time, he'd chase a pair of sevens to the river like it owed him money." Another beat. "From now on, a pair of sevens is called Bakers."

Little Nguyen laughed first. It came out of him like a wound opening—not because it was funny but because the pressure had to go somewhere. Then Cortez laughed. Then everyone laughed, even Ski, who hadn't laughed in four months.
