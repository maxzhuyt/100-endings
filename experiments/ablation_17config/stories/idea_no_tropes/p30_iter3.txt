# The Own Goal

## I.

In the chapel of San José, the candles have a particular way of failing. They don't gutter or sputter like candles in movies. They simply become less, the flame thinning to a blue transparency, and then they are out and you're looking at a black wick in a pool of wax and wondering whether the prayer you were saying still counts. I have always believed it does. I have always believed God hears the whole sentence even when the light quits in the middle.

I kneel. The stone is familiar against my kneecaps — two specific points of cold I have known since I was seven, since my mother first brought me here with my shirt tucked in and my hair still wet from her comb. I know this stone the way I know the eighteen-yard box at the Atanasio Girardot: by feel, by pressure, by the particular resistance it offers to the body's weight. Some men pray with their hands. I have always prayed with my knees.

*Padre nuestro, que estás en el cielo —*

The morning light comes through the window above the altar in a column so clean it looks solid, and the dust inside it moves the way a defensive line should move: each particle independent, responding to its own physics, and yet the whole thing shifting as a single body, a coordinated breath. I have tried to explain this to teammates and they laugh. Alexis says I see football in everything. But it's the reverse. I see everything in football. The game is the language God gave me for understanding the world, and the world keeps answering in it.

I finish the prayer. I cross myself. I stand and my knees crack — thirty years old next year, and the knees are already filing their complaints — and I walk out through the nave, past the wooden pews with their smell of oil and age, past the font where I touch the water and bring it to my forehead, past the old woman who is always here, whose name I have never learned, who nods at me each morning with an expression that manages to be both distant and completely intimate, the way old Colombian women have perfected a kind of love that doesn't need to know your name.

Outside, Medellín is doing what Medellín does at six-thirty in the morning: becoming gold. The sun hasn't crested the eastern ridge yet, but the light is already pooling in the valley, catching the white facades of the buildings in Laureles, turning the tin roofs of the barrios up on the hillsides into a mosaic of amber and copper. I have been in cities that are beautiful. Bogotá is beautiful in its gray, severe way, like a nun. Buenos Aires is beautiful the way a woman is beautiful when she knows you're watching. But Medellín is beautiful the way a child is beautiful — with complete indifference to whether you're looking, with no knowledge that it could be otherwise. The mountains hold the city the way hands hold water: carefully, knowing it could spill.

I drive to training. The road from Envigado takes me past the new commercial district where buildings have gone up in the last three years with a speed that seems to violate physics. Glass towers, steel frames, facades that gleam like they've been polished by hand. The money for these buildings comes from — the money comes from places. Medellín is a city of enterprise. The road is good. The road was repaved recently, and the driving is smooth, and I am thinking about the back line, about the way we'll need to compress the space between the center-backs and the holding midfielder if we're going to contain Romania's counters, and the road is very smooth, and the buildings are very new.

At the training facility — also new, also gleaming, the pitches so green they look illustrated, the changing rooms tiled in white with the kind of fixtures you see in European clubs, everything paid for by the federation and its, by its supporters, by — I lace my boots. This is a ritual I have not altered since I was fifteen: left boot first, double knot, then right, double knot, then I stand and press each foot into the ground to feel the studs grip. The stud pattern is a language too. Six on the forefoot, four on the heel. Each one a point of contact between my body and the earth, and when I'm defending, when I'm reading the attacker's hips and shoulders for the feint before he knows he's going to feint, each stud is a fraction of a second's purchase against the turf, a fraction of a second in which I can change direction, and fractions of seconds are what defending is made of. A striker has time. A defender has fractions.

The grounds crew is already out. I know them. Hernán, who has a daughter my age and who cannot talk about her without his face becoming a different, softer face. Julio, who fought in the army and never says which years. Little Pipe, who is sixteen and runs the line-marking machine like he's driving a racing car, tongue between his teeth, absolute concentration. I greet each of them. My mother taught me this. She said: *The world is made of people who open doors and people who walk through them, and you will be the first kind or you will not be my son.* I have tried to be her son in everything.

She is home now, sitting in the kitchen with her rosary and her coffee — always in that order, the beads before the cup, because God comes before comfort, she says, though I suspect the coffee is also a prayer, the way she holds the cup in both hands and closes her eyes over the steam. I kissed her forehead before I left this morning. Her skin smelled of the cream she uses, something with cocoa butter, and her forehead was cool and dry and when I kissed it I felt what I always feel, which is that there is one place on earth where I am not Andrés Escobar the footballer but simply Andrés, her boy, and it is the square inch of skin between her eyebrows.

The squad arrives in pieces. Carlos Valderrama, unmistakable from any distance, the hair a kind of announcement, a herald preceding the man — he comes with his slow walk, his hands in his pockets, the posture of someone who has never been hurried by anything and does not intend to start. Freddy Rincón, all muscle and purpose, already running before he reaches the pitch, his body unable to accept the concept of stillness. Leonel Álvarez, who reads the game the way I read morning light in the chapel, as evidence of an underlying order. They are my brothers. Not metaphorically. There is a bond between men who have suffered through a qualifying campaign together that is not adequately described by the word *team*. We have bled on the same grass. We have won in Buenos Aires, which is like winning in a furnace, in a furnace that hates you, and we came out of it holding each other, and the holding was the thing itself.

Some of the boys arrive in cars I could not buy if I saved for three years. There is a Mercedes in the lot that belongs to — it's a nice car. There is a player, I won't name him, who has a new driver each week, and the drivers have a way of standing by the car with their arms crossed and their jackets unbuttoned that suggests the jacket is not the last layer. There are visitors sometimes at training, men with heavy watches and soft voices who stand at the edge of the pitch and watch and confer and leave without introducing themselves to anyone, and no one introduces them, and this is — this is the way of things. Medellín is a city of many interests, and football is of interest to many, and the support of the community is what allows us to have these pitches, these facilities, this chance.

I believe in this chance. I need you to understand that. I believe that when we take the field at the World Cup, we are carrying something more than a flag. We are carrying a counter-argument. The world knows one thing about Colombia: the thing I will not say, the thing that lives in the new buildings and the smooth roads and the cars in the parking lot. But we can make them know a second thing. We can make them know that this country also produces eleven men who can play the most beautiful football on earth, who can pass and move and defend and score and make the geometry of the game into something that the crowd in the stadium will remember when they've forgotten every headline, every photograph, every news report. The ball is clean. I believe that. I have to believe that. The pitch is a sacred space, a rectangle of grace, and what happens inside it is between the players and the game and God, and I will defend that rectangle with everything I have. This is not — this is not naivety. I have eyes. I know the shape of the world I live in. I know that the money paying for these boots has been through hands I would not want to shake in daylight. But my mother told me once: *Andrés, you can't choose the world. You can only choose what you do inside it.* And inside the lines, I am clean.

This is what I believe. This is what I take to the World Cup. This is the prayer I say on my knees in San José each morning, its candle burning or not, its flame whole or thinning: *Let the game be enough. Let what we do on the pitch be the truth of us. Let the ball be clean.*

## II.

America arrives as heat.

That is the first thing — the heat at the airport, a wet heat, a heat with weight, like walking into a wall made of warm water. In Medellín we have heat but it's dry, it sits on the skin, it's a companion. This American heat enters you. It fills the lungs. It makes the air into a thing you have to chew before you can breathe it.

We come through customs in our federation blazers, thirty-some men walking in a loose column, and the American officials look at our passports with an expression I will come to know well during our time here — a kind of polite suspicion, a smile with a question behind it, the look of a country that has been told something about your country and is trying to determine whether the something is standing in front of them at this moment. I smile back. I always smile. My mother says my smile is a room with the door open.

The bus to the hotel passes through a landscape that is impossible. I say this carefully: I have been to Argentina, to Ecuador, to Germany once for a friendly. I have seen large spaces, flat spaces. But America is a different kind of large. The highway out of the airport enters a flatness that seems to have no organizing principle — strip malls, billboards, parking lots the size of neighborhoods, everything spread out and low and repeating, as though someone took a city and pulled it apart like taffy until it covered nothing recognizably. The light is too bright. Even through the tinted windows of the bus, the light has a bleached, flattened quality, like a photograph left in the sun. Everything is overexposed. The signs are enormous — MOTEL, TIRES, EAT — the words themselves bloated, capitalized, demanding to be read from distances that would be absurd in Medellín, where signs are small and personal and often handwritten.

Valderrama, sitting across the aisle, looks out the window and says: *This is a country that is afraid of silence.* He means the signs, the noise, the constant visual shouting. But I feel it differently. I feel the silence *under* the noise — the vast, empty lots, the stretches of highway where nothing is happening, where nobody is walking because nobody walks, where the land itself seems to be waiting for something to be built on it or nothing, and it doesn't care which. In Medellín the land is occupied. Every hillside, every ridge, every square meter has a person or a house or a story pressed into it. Here the land is unoccupied and the noise is what rushes in to fill the vacancy.

The hotel is large and beige and could be anywhere on earth. The lobby has marble floors and a fountain that makes a sound like someone continuously clearing their throat. There are men in the lobby. There are always men in the lobby of a World Cup team hotel — press, federation officials, sponsors, embassy staff — but there are also other men, men who are not wearing credentials, men who sit in the leather chairs near the elevators and read newspapers they don't turn the pages of, men with phones who speak quietly and look up when you pass. They are — they are probably security. The federation has arranged security. It is a World Cup. There are always concerns.

My room is on the seventh floor. The bed is enormous, a white continent of sheets and pillows, and I sit on it and it accepts me in a way that feels less like comfort than consumption, the mattress so soft my body sinks into it and I feel, for a disorienting moment, that I am falling and the bed is the thing I'm falling into and there's no bottom. In Medellín my bed is firm. My mother's bed, which I slept in until I was six, was a thin mattress on a wooden frame that my father built, and it held you up. It supported you. This American bed does not support. It absorbs.

I call my mother. The connection takes several attempts — the international line clicking and buzzing, a series of tones like a mechanical rosary — and then her voice, small and far and absolutely clear: *Mijo.* My son. I tell her the flight was fine. I tell her the hotel is beautiful. I tell her the team is confident. I tell her Pelé has predicted we'll win and she laughs and says *Even Pelé doesn't know what God knows* and I laugh too and the laughter is the first thing since arriving that has felt like the right size for the space it's in.

I sit at the small desk by the window — the window looks out onto a highway and a parking lot and, beyond them, the foothills of something, scrub-covered and brown, not mountains, not even hills really, just the suggestion of elevation in a land that has otherwise abolished the concept — and I open my journal. I've kept a journal since I was twenty. I write about training sessions, about tactical ideas, about things my mother says, about the color of Medellín at particular hours, about prayers that felt answered and prayers that are still in transit. I write now:

*We are here. The tournament begins in three days. I have never felt a team so ready. We are going to show them.*

*The American light is strange. Too much of it. It flattens everything. In Medellín the light picks things out — a face, a wall, a leaf — and gives each thing its own shadow. Here the light comes from everywhere at once and nothing has a shadow and everything looks like a copy of itself.*

*We're going to show them the real Colombia.*

I write this and I believe it. I underline *real*. I put the journal away, and I am about to get on my knees to pray — the carpet is thin but the floor beneath it is hard enough, there is something to push against — when I hear, through the wall, which is not as thick as a hotel like this should have, a voice.

The voice belongs to — I know this voice. A teammate. I won't name him. The voice is speaking quietly but with the particular tension that turns quiet speech into something louder than shouting, the way a dog's growl is louder than its bark because the growl has intention in it. The voice is saying: *I understand. I understand. I understand.* Three times, each time higher, each time less like understanding and more like the word you say when you are pinned against a wall by a hand that is not visible but whose pressure is total.

Then silence.

Then, a few minutes later, I hear the door to his room open and close and I hear footsteps in the corridor — not my teammate's footsteps, which I know, which shuffle slightly because of his left knee, but other footsteps, hard-soled, measured, the footsteps of a man who is not in a hurry because men who make other men say *I understand* three times are never in a hurry.
