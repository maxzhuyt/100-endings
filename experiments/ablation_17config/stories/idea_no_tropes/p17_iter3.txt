# THE ANNOTATED TRANSCRIPT

## A Conditions-of-Access Document Prepared by the Office of Verification and Standards (Contractor), Regarding the Podcast Series *Bureau of Sleep*, Episodes 1–11 (Unfinished), Hosted by Maren Cask

---

**PREFATORY NOTE**

My name is Lena Guo. For fourteen months I served as the contracted fact-checker for *Bureau of Sleep*, an independently produced investigative podcast hosted and created by Maren Cask, distributed by Helix Media Group until its termination in March of this year. My role, per the terms of my engagement letter [see Appendix A, not included here because Helix Media Group's Legal Affairs division has denied my request to reproduce it, a denial I am formally noting], was to verify all factual claims made in pre-production scripts, flag potential defamation exposure, and review raw audio for discrepancies between recorded material and final edited episodes.

I have listened to every minute of tape.

I want to be precise about this because precision is the only thing I have to offer, and because Maren [I am going to use her first name; she used mine freely enough in production emails, usually misspelled] was not precise, and the absence of her precision is the reason eleven episodes of a podcast now sit on a locked server and five people who used to report to work at a GS-9 pay grade in a federal building in Dayton, Ohio, no longer do.

The Nightmare Appraisal Bureau — formally, the Bureau of Oneiric Revenue Assessment, Division 11-C of the Internal Revenue Service, Dayton Field Office — was a real place. It is, technically, still a real place. The office exists. The fluorescent lights are on. I confirmed this by telephone with the building's facilities manager on January 14th. He said the lights are on a timer he cannot override. He asked if I knew when someone was coming back because the fern in Room 6 was "getting out of hand."

No one is coming back. But the lights are on.

What the Bureau did — what it still, per the Federal Register, is *mandated* to do — is assess the tax implications of recurring nightmares under Section 1041(f)(iii) of the Internal Revenue Code, a provision inserted into the 1978 Revenue Act by a since-deceased congressman from Ohio whose legislative history I have reviewed and whose motives I cannot determine. [Maren, in Episode 1, claimed the provision was "buried in the tax code like a bad dream itself," which is the kind of sentence that sounds meaningful and means nothing. The provision is not buried. It is in Subchapter F. It is indexed. She did not check.]

Under this provision, a citizen experiencing a recurring nightmare — defined as any dream producing marked psychological distress that recurs with a frequency of no fewer than three episodes per calendar quarter — may file for a Nightmare Exemption Assessment, which, if approved, permits a modest adjustment to the filer's taxable income on the theory that recurring nightmares constitute an involuntary psychological burden with calculable economic impact (lost productivity, therapy costs, impaired sleep quality). The assessment is not automatic. A trained appraiser must conduct a structured interview, evaluate the nightmare against the Bureau's standardized rubric [see Form 11-C, "Recurring Nightmare Valuation Worksheet," which I will reproduce in part below, since it is a public document available through FOIA, despite Maren's repeated on-air insinuation that the Bureau operated in secrecy], and assign a severity grade that determines the size of the exemption.

Five people did this work. They did it in a building with a shared microwave and a parking lot with twelve spaces, only five of which were ever used. They did it for years. Then they stopped.

Maren told this story as a mystery. She used the word "vanished" in her pitch deck [timestamp: initial production meeting, raw audio file PM_001.wav, 00:04:12]. She used the word "sinister" in her first script draft. She used the phrase "What are they hiding?" as the tagline for the series artwork, which featured a stock photograph of an empty office chair that she purchased for $11.99 from Getty Images.

[I know the price because I processed the expense report.]

What Maren did not tell her listeners — what she cut from the raw tape, again and again, with a consistency I can only describe as *curatorial* — is that there was nothing sinister about the Bureau, and that the people who worked there were not hiding anything, and that what happened to them was not a mystery but something worse: it was legible, if you were willing to read the document instead of the narrative.

I was willing. I am a fact-checker. Reading documents is what I do.

What follows is an annotated transcript of *Bureau of Sleep*. I have included Maren's original narration as aired or as scripted for unaired episodes. My annotations appear in brackets. Where I have supplemented the podcast's account with material from the raw tape, production files, and documents obtained independently, I have noted the source.

I am not telling a story. I am correcting one.

[I should note, since I am in the business of disclosure, that I have been to the Dayton Field Office. Not as an appraiser. Not as a citizen filing a claim. I went once, three years before the podcast, when my mother — but that is not relevant to the corrections below, and I will not include irrelevant material. I am noting the visit for the sake of full transparency. I went. I sat in the waiting room. I did not file anything. I left.]

[That is all that needs to be said about that.]

### THE BUREAU AT FULL OPERATION: A RECONSTRUCTION

What Maren aired, in the first three episodes, was a portrait of the Bureau that she described in her pitch deck as "Parks and Rec meets Twin Peaks." I am not going to address this comparison except to note that it reveals more about her understanding of federal employees than about the Bureau.

What follows is my own reconstruction of the Bureau's daily operation, based on raw tape she did not use [camera files CAM_01 through CAM_09, mounted security footage, and production B-roll], internal documents obtained via FOIA [request #2024-F-00491], and the one interview Maren conducted that she deemed "too boring to air" [raw file INT_PH_001.wav, with Paul Haddad, Lead Appraiser, GS-9 Step 7, recorded six months before his departure].

The Bureau opened at 8:15 AM. This was not an official start time — the GSA lease specified 8:00 — but Paul Haddad, who had been with the Bureau longest and who held the only key to the supply closet where the blank Form 11-Cs were stored, did not arrive until 8:15 because he stopped each morning at a gas station on Wilmington Pike for a coffee that he described, on tape, as "objectively terrible but part of the load-bearing structure of my day." [INT_PH_001.wav, 00:03:22.] Maren cut this. She said it "didn't advance the narrative." I am restoring it because Paul Haddad drank that coffee every morning for eleven years, and it is a fact, and I am in the fact business.

The five appraisers were:

**Paul Haddad**, Lead Appraiser, 53, eleven years with the Bureau. Responsible for contested valuations and appeals. Maintained a desk that was, per the camera footage, the only clean surface in the office. Had a framed photograph of Lake Erie on the wall behind his chair, which he had taken himself. [He mentions this in INT_PH_001.wav, 00:07:41: "It's not a great photo. But it's mine."]. Spoke to citizens in a voice so steady it appeared, on the raw tape, to have no inflection at all, until you listened closely and heard the way it dropped a half-register when a citizen began to cry, lowering itself to meet them.

**Debra Keller-Luz**, Appraiser II, 47, eight years with the Bureau. Handled the highest volume of cases. Kept a container of mixed nuts on her desk that she ate from continuously and offered to citizens, a habit Maren described on-air as "quirky." [This is not "quirky." Debra Keller-Luz told a citizen, in footage Maren did not use — CAM_03, 10/14, 14:22:08 — "Sometimes when you're telling someone the worst thing your brain does to you, it helps to have something in your hands." She was talking about the nuts. It was the most precise description of trauma-informed practice I have ever heard, and it came from a GS-9 with no clinical degree, and Maren called it quirky.]

**Alan Moss**, Appraiser II, 38, five years with the Bureau. The youngest. Had strong opinions about the shared refrigerator. [I will address the refrigerator.]

**Constance Okafor-White**, Appraiser II, 44, six years with the Bureau. Responsible for Dreams of Deceased Persons (Category 7), a sub-classification requiring additional certification. Spoke the least on camera. In 340 hours of raw footage, I count nineteen sentences from Constance Okafor-White that are not direct interview questions addressed to citizens. Twelve of those relate to office logistics. Four relate to weather. Three I will address later.

**Yun-seo Pak**, Appraiser I, 41, four years with the Bureau. The most recent hire. Assigned primarily to intake and preliminary classification — the first person a citizen saw when they arrived with their recurring nightmare and their completed Form 11-C (Self-Report). ### HOW IT WORKED

I am going to describe the workflow because Maren did not describe it accurately, and because the workflow *is the story*, a sentence she would never have used because it contains no mystery and no billboard.

A citizen arrived at the Bureau with a completed Form 11-C (Self-Report), available at any IRS field office or downloadable from IRS.gov [it is still there; I checked on February 2nd; it has been downloaded eleven times since the Bureau ceased operations, which means eleven people filled it out and have nowhere to send it, and I do not know what to do with that fact so I am simply recording it].

The Form 11-C (Self-Report) asked the citizen to provide:

- **Section A:** Identifying information (name, SSN, filing status).
- **Section B:** Dream description. ("Describe the recurring dream in as much detail as you can recall. Use additional pages if necessary. Please print clearly.") [The instruction to "print clearly" appears on a form that asks people to describe the worst thing their subconscious does to them. I have no annotation for this. I am simply noting it.]
- **Section C:** Frequency and duration. ("How many times per quarter does this dream occur? How long has it been recurring? Check one: ☐ Less than 1 year ☐ 1–5 years ☐ 5–10 years ☐ More than 10 years.")
- **Section D:** Impact self-assessment. ("Rate the following on a scale of 1–5: Sleep disruption. Daytime anxiety. Impairment of work or daily function. Reluctance to fall asleep.")
- **Section E:** Prior treatment. ("Have you sought professional treatment for this dream? ☐ Yes ☐ No. If yes, attach documentation.")

Citizens arrived with these forms filled out in pencil, in ink, in handwriting that was sometimes neat and sometimes barely legible. [Raw footage shows Yun-seo Pak at intake, reading a citizen's Form 11-C and pausing on Section B. The camera was behind her. You could not read the form. She held it for a long time. Then she looked up at the citizen and said, "Thank you for writing this down." CAM_01, 09/22, 09:14:33. There was nothing in the training manual about thanking citizens for writing down their nightmares. I checked.]
