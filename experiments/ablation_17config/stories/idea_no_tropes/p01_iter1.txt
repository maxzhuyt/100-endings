# Maren Vik and the Tonga Trench

---

The granola bar was a Nature Valley Oats 'n Honey, which if you've ever eaten one you know produces more crumbs than actual bar, and it was floating past my face in absolute darkness at roughly 8,200 meters below sea level, ascending through the air pocket on the surface tension of the water that was, at that point, up to my sternum. I remember thinking: that's the most Nature Valley thing a Nature Valley bar has ever done. Disintegrate at the worst possible moment. Leave a mess. Provide no real sustenance.

My name is Maren Vik. I was upside down, then I wasn't, then I was sort of diagonal inside what had been the forward instrument bay of the *Leona K*, a 34-meter research-and-recovery vessel that was at that moment inverted and sinking through the hadal zone of the Tonga Trench with me sealed inside it like a prize in an arcade claw machine that the claw had fumbled.

Depth: approximately 8,200 meters and falling, though I'd lost the digital readout when the console shorted, so I was estimating based on pressure effects on the hull and on my own eardrums, which is to say I was guessing, but I was guessing with thirty years of professional experience, which is just guessing in a nicer dress.

Air supply: the pocket was roughly two meters by one-point-five meters by, at that point, maybe ninety centimeters of vertical clearance above the waterline. I'm not going to do the partial-pressure math for you because I was doing it then and it was making me feel terrible, but the short version: I had somewhere between forty-five minutes and an hour before the CO₂ in that space turned it into a coffin with ambiance. A little less if I panicked. A little more if I could slow my breathing to something a yoga instructor would approve of, which — I have never in my life done a single thing a yoga instructor would approve of. My ex-husband tried to get me into hot yoga once. I told him I already worked in a pressurized environment surrounded by things that could kill me and I didn't need to pay forty dollars to replicate the experience in Lycra.

The hull groaned. It had been groaning for a while, a low tectonic sound, the sound of metal being asked a question it didn't want to answer. I was talking. I don't know when I started. There was no one to hear me — I want to be clear about that. No radio. No comm. No dive buddy. No recorder running, unless you count the black box, and I didn't know yet whether the black box was still functioning, though it was — it was, that's how they found the audio later, which is how I know I said most of what I'm about to tell you I said, and also how I know I'm not embellishing, or at least not embellishing much, because the audio is out there if you want to check.

I was talking because quiet was not an option. Quiet meant doing the math. Quiet meant listening to the hull and interpreting its sounds as a structural engineer rather than as a woman who needed the hull to hold. Quiet meant the ocean, which at 8,200 meters doesn't sound like anything you've ever heard. It sounds like a body. Like a circulatory system with you lodged inside it.

So. Forty-five minutes. Give or take.

Let me tell you how I got here.

---

Six weeks before the *Leona K* decided to audition as a submarine, I was in Auckland, sitting in a borrowed office above a marine-supply wholesaler that smelled of diesel and neoprene and someone's microwaved butter chicken, looking at a sonar printout that was either worth two-point-three million U.S. dollars or worth nothing.

Polymetallic nodules. If you don't know what those are, imagine a potato made of money. A fist-sized lump of manganese, nickel, cobalt, and copper that forms over millions of years on the abyssal ocean floor, growing at a rate of roughly ten millimeters per million years, which makes them less a mineral deposit and more a geological exercise in patience. They sit on the sediment like God spilled a bag of dark marbles across the bottom of the world and forgot to pick them up. They are, pound for pound, some of the most valuable raw material on the planet, and they are functionally unreachable because they tend to form in places where the ocean is actively trying to kill you.

This particular field had been flagged by a survey vessel — New Zealand-flagged, university-affiliated, doing a routine thermal mapping run near a previously uncharted hydrothermal vent system along the northern wall of the Tonga Trench. The nodules were dense, high-grade, sitting on a relatively flat terrace at between 7,900 and 8,400 meters. The vent system — a cluster of black smokers venting superheated water at somewhere north of 350 degrees Celsius — was approximately 1.2 kilometers to the southwest, which in hadal terms is right next door. The vents meant mineral-rich water. The mineral-rich water meant the nodules were unusually concentrated, unusually pure, worth significantly more per unit than the stuff the big mining consortiums were pulling from the Clarion-Clipperton Zone at a fraction of the depth.

The vents also meant the geology was active. Tectonically, thermally, volcanically active. The Tonga Trench is a subduction zone — the Pacific Plate diving beneath the Indo-Australian Plate at roughly 24 centimeters per year, which is fast, geologically speaking. The fastest convergent boundary on Earth. The vent system was a symptom of that speed: magma close to the surface, crust under stress, the whole region a landscape of thermal instability that could rearrange itself on a timeline measured in hours rather than epochs.

Every risk assessment I ran came back the same color, which was red, and I ran four of them because the first three made me feel like a coward and I thought the fourth might tell me something different. It didn't.

I took the job anyway.

I could tell you this was a calculated decision — that I weighed the geological risk against the payout, factored in my equipment capabilities and crew experience, and determined that the margin was acceptable. And that would be true, technically, the way it's technically true that a person who bets their rent money on a hand of poker has "evaluated the odds." The deeper truth: I was forty-seven years old, divorced eighteen months, and the settlement had left me with the *Leona K*, a storage unit in Dunedin, and a reputation for deep-recovery work that was, at that exact moment, worth less than the fuel in my tanks because the insurance premiums on hadal operations had tripled in two years and every legitimate contract was going to the big outfits with ROV fleets and corporate backing and actuaries who could make the numbers sing.

The nodule field was a freelance job. Gray-market. A private buyer — minerals trading, Southeast Asian, very polite on the phone, very vague about end-use — who wanted a proof-of-concept retrieval: go down, collect a sample haul, prove the field was accessible, and bring back enough material to verify the assay numbers. If the sample checked out, there would be a larger operation. Bigger ship. Bigger crew. Bigger money. All I had to do was go first.

My ex-husband, Glen, would have called this reckless. Glen sold marine insurance for a Christchurch firm and believed the ocean was a series of actuarial tables with waves on top. He once told me, during the marriage, that my risk tolerance was "a form of self-harm dressed up as a career." I told him his risk tolerance was a form of embalming. We were both right, probably. But I was the one with the boat.

The sonar printout was grainy, second-generation, clearly pirated from the university survey. The nodule signatures were unmistakable — dense, regular, beautiful in the way that money is beautiful when you need it badly enough to see it at the bottom of the deepest water on Earth.

I said yes. I said yes the way you say yes to something you've already decided: with your body before your mouth, leaning forward over the desk in that diesel-smelling office, already calculating crew requirements, equipment needs, fuel costs, the number of dives it would take, and the particular physics of bringing heavy objects up from a place the sun has never touched.

---

The water was at my ribs now. I noticed this the way you notice the time at a party you're not enjoying — a peripheral awareness that sharpens into alarm only when you do the math.

Thirty-eight minutes. Maybe.

I was shivering. At 8,200-plus meters, the ambient water temperature is between one and two degrees Celsius, and the air in my pocket was not much warmer, because the pocket was shrinking and the water was winning and thermodynamics doesn't negotiate. I could feel the cold in my teeth, in my fingernails, in the bridge of my nose where it settled like a migraine with ambitions. My wetsuit was a 7mm Henderson — good for moderate depth, not designed for a scenario in which I was the filling in a metal-and-ocean sandwich headed for the most profound hole on the western Pacific floor.

I tried the torch again. The waterproof torch I kept clipped to my BC vest, which I'd already tried twice, and which had worked neither time on account of the bulb being shattered, probably during the capsize, probably when I hit the instrument console with the left side of my body at a velocity I prefer not to calculate. The torch did not work. The torch was a cylinder of anodized aluminum and broken promises and I put it back on the clip because even now, even *here*, I could not bring myself to discard functional equipment. Force of habit. The sea teaches you to keep your gear close. It doesn't teach you what to do when your gear is useless and you're keeping it close anyway, like a teddy bear for adults who are going to die in the dark.

I wasn't going to die in the dark.

I need to say that here because it's what I said then, out loud, to no one, in a voice that didn't sound like mine. "You're not going to die in the dark, Vik." And then I laughed, because it came out like a line from a movie, and not a good movie — one of those straight-to-streaming ones where the lead actress is always wet and always brave and never has to pee, which I did, incidentally, have to pee, and had been ignoring this fact for the better part of twenty minutes because there's a hierarchy of urgency when you're sinking in a metal casket and bladder management is not at the top.

But I was telling you about the crew.

---

First hire: Rana Tabakovi, Fijian-born, Tongan-raised, thirty-one years old, and the best sonar technician I'd ever worked with who also happened to be wanted for questioning in two countries for reasons she described, when I asked, as "a disagreement about frequency allocations." Rana had modified her own multibeam array — built it from parts she'd sourced from a decommissioned NOAA vessel and a telecommunications satellite that had, in her telling, "fallen off a truck in Nuku'alofa, which is impressive because Nuku'alofa doesn't really have trucks." The array could resolve features at hadal depth with a fidelity that the big commercial units couldn't match, because Rana had hand-tuned every transducer element and because she talked to the machine while it worked, a low murmuring monologue in a mixture of Fijian and what I think was profanity in at least three other languages, and the machine seemed to respond, which I know is not how sonar works but I also know what I saw on her screens and it was better than anything I'd seen from a unit costing ten times the price.

I found her in Suva, at a waterfront bar called the Blue Lagoon that had neither a lagoon nor anything blue about it. She was running a calibration test on a laptop held together with electrical tape and faith, eating a plate of kokoda with her free hand, and when I sat down across from her and said, "I need someone who can map a vent field at eight thousand meters," she looked at me the way a mechanic looks at someone who's just described their car making a sound — with a mixture of professional interest and personal pity.

"You're Vik," she said. "The one with the boat and the divorce."

Word travels in these waters. I confirmed both.

"How's the boat?"

"Functional."

"How's the divorce?"

"Less pressurized than the Tonga Trench."

She ate another piece of fish. "I charge double for anything below six thousand meters."

"Everyone charges double below six thousand meters."

"I charge double again if there's active volcanism."

"That's quadruple."

"I went to good schools," Rana said, and smiled with half her mouth, and I knew she was in because when someone is negotiating the price, they've already decided to go.

Second hire: Pita Latu. Harbor pilot. Tongan, sixty-two, built like a church — wide, solid, designed to outlast the weather. Pita knew the waters above the northern Trench the way he knew his own kitchen: where the shelves were, what was stored on them, which tiles were loose. He'd been piloting vessels through the Tongan archipelago for forty years, and I'd been told by four separate people in four separate ports that if I was taking a boat over the Tonga Trench, I should either hire Pita or I should not go. He had a face like a topographic map of a place that had experienced significant geological events, and he spoke in a bass register that I could feel in my molars.

I met him at the Nuku'alofa waterfront, near the fish market, where he was eating a coconut bun and watching the harbor with the serene attention of a man who has seen everything the ocean can do and has agreed to continue living near it anyway. He listened to my pitch. He ate his bun. He watched a container ship navigate the channel with a series of small course corrections that he narrated under his breath, finding fault with each one.

"The Trench," he said, when I'd finished, in a tone that suggested I'd proposed something indecent. "Vent field."

"Uncharted."

"They're all uncharted until they kill someone, and then they're very well charted." He finished his bun. He folded the paper wrapper into a precise square and placed it in his shirt pocket, and I understood later that this was how Pita processed risk: by organizing the nearest available object while his mind did the heavy lifting. "I've taken boats over the northern wall. Eleven times. I lost an anchor on the third trip and a depth sounder on the seventh and a deckhand named Simi on the ninth, though Simi turned up two days later in Vava'u with a woman he'd met on the radio and I consider that less of a loss and more of a redistribution."

"Will you go a twelfth time?"

"My wife will be upset."

"I'll pay enough to make it a productive argument."

He studied me. His eyes were the color of the deep water we were going to be floating over, and they had the same quality of revealing nothing about what was happening at depth. "You're the one Rana called about."

"What did she say?"

"She said you have a good boat and a bad plan and the kind of face that means you'll go regardless."

"That's generous of her."

"I'm editing."
