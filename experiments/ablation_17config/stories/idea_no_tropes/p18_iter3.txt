# The Desire Laundry

## I.

Every city has its fog, but the fog that settled over Carsophine each evening had the particular quality of a lie told so often it had begun to believe itself. It rolled in from the river at half-past five, thickened around the gas lamps like cataracts, and by seven had rendered the entire municipal district—its marble porticos, its monument to Civic Restraint, its fourteen identical banks—into a suggestion of itself, a rumor in stone. The fog was, in the official literature of the Tourism and Decency Bureau, "atmospheric." The fog was, in the private estimation of anyone who had ever tried to breathe while walking uphill, "a soup of coal-smoke, river-damp, and the collective exhalation of nine hundred thousand people trying not to be seen."

The effort of not being seen was, in Carsophine, the dominant industry.

This requires some explanation. Not because the reader is slow—we have the highest confidence in the reader; the reader is, we suspect, already several steps ahead of us and merely tolerating this preamble with the weary grace of a dinner guest who knows the soup course must be endured before the fish—but because the particular affliction of Carsophine was so thoroughly woven into the fabric of its daily existence that even its inhabitants sometimes forgot to find it remarkable. The affliction was this: everyone in Carsophine had a Wanting.

Not a wanting. Not the ordinary, lowercase sort—the mild hankering for a second cup of tea, the distant ambition toward a promotion, the human-scaled appetite that civilized persons were expected to manage with the same quiet competence they brought to digestion and personal grooming. No. A Wanting. Capitalized, corporeal, and catastrophically visible.

A Wanting was a creature. Or something so like a creature that the distinction was academic. It grew from a person as naturally as shadow, though considerably less obedient to the movements of its host. It had mass. It had luminosity—a cold, subcutaneous light, the color of which varied according to its nature and which could not be concealed by any number of cloaks, greatcoats, or strategically arranged scarves. It had, in many cases, what could only be described as *personality.* And it was, in every case, an externalization—visible, palpable, and mortifyingly specific—of the deepest desire its bearer carried.

The wealthy, naturally, found this arrangement intolerable.

A shipping magnate whose Wanting manifested as an enormous trembling orchid of pale violet light, its petals parting to reveal, at its center, a tiny replica of his mother rocking in her chair and telling him he had always been her favorite—this man could not be expected to attend the opera with such a thing draped over his shoulders. A judge whose Wanting took the form of a great coiling serpent, translucent and faintly amber, that wound between her legs and arched its neck toward anyone standing within six feet, its mouth opening to reveal not fangs but a tiny tongue on which was written, in luminous script, the word *more*—this woman could hardly be expected to preside over the Sixteenth Bench of the High Court while the gallery watched her creature strain and writhe.

And so: the laundries.

Desire laundries had existed in Carsophine for as long as Wantings had been considered impolite, which is to say, for as long as there had been a class of person with sufficient resources to be embarrassed by the human condition. The craft was ancient, its techniques refined over centuries, its practitioners organized into small workshops that operated— by tradition, by ordinance, and by the mutual agreement of all parties who preferred not to think about it—belowground.

Madame Grelch's establishment occupied the basement of a former tannery on Hesper Street, in the Nock District, which was the kind of neighborhood where the buildings leaned against one another like drunks sharing a confidence. The entrance was a service door, unmarked, set three steps below the pavement. Above it, a single gas lamp burned with the resigned flicker of a light that had long since given up hope of illuminating anything worth seeing.

Below that lamp, through that door, down a corridor that smelled of lye and anise and the faintly electrical tang of desire under pressure, seven people worked.

It was a Thursday, which at Madame Grelch's differed from a Monday in no meaningful respect except that on Thursdays Mossam brought biscuits. Mossam's biscuits were uniformly terrible— dry, burnt at the edges, and seasoned with what he insisted was cardamom but what the collective palate of the laundry had long since identified as pencil shavings. No one refused them. Mossam's Wanting, which manifested as a small fleet of luminous paper boats that circled his head in slow, hopeful formation, occasionally dipping down to nudge the shoulder or elbow of whichever colleague stood nearest, made refusal feel roughly equivalent to kicking a dog who had just learned to carry a newspaper.

"If you've put nutmeg in these again," said Sable, holding a biscuit at arm's length and examining it with the expression of a customs official confronted with an implausible passport, "I shall have to revise my theory that you are merely an incompetent baker and accept the more troubling hypothesis that you are actively at war with the concept of flavor."

"There is no nutmeg," said Mossam.

"There is always nutmeg," said Ivria, from the far end of the scrubbing trough, where she was elbow-deep in a solution that sent up occasional wisps of iridescent steam. "It's your signature. The way other artists have brushwork or a particular key, you have the inexplicable persistence of nutmeg in contexts where nutmeg has no business being."

"I believe," said Corla, who was the eldest of the seven and who spoke with the deliberate cadence of a person who had long ago decided that the world could wait, "that the word you want is *leitmotif.* The nutmeg is Mossam's leitmotif."

"The word I want," said Ivria, "is *assault.*"

This was the rhythm of the laundry. One must understand it to understand everything that came after.

The room itself was large, low-ceilinged, and damp in the way that all underground rooms in Carsophine were damp—not merely moist but *invested* in moisture, as though the walls had taken a philosophical position on the matter and were unlikely to be argued out of it. The central feature was the trough: a long stone basin, fifteen feet end to end, filled with a solution whose precise composition was a guild secret but whose primary ingredients—lye, witch hazel, rectified spirits, and a small amount of something Corla kept in an unlabeled bottle and referred to only as "the Persuasion"—could be identified by anyone with a functioning nose. Along the walls hung the tools of the craft: flat-irons of various sizes, rollers wrapped in chamois, fine-gauge wire frames shaped like human silhouettes, pressing boards padded with wool. And everywhere, on every surface not already occupied by equipment, candles—not for light, but because the process of laundering a Wanting generated a cold that could not be addressed by any furnace, and the candles, while practically useless, provided the psychological suggestion of warmth, which Madame Grelch considered sufficient.

The seven launderers worked the trough in pairs, rotating through a schedule that Drett maintained on a chalkboard near the door with the obsessive precision of a railway dispatcher. Drett herself was small, sharp-shouldered, and afflicted with a Wanting that took the form of an elaborate architectural model—a tiny, perfect house, glowing faintly amber, which balanced on her left shoulder and added rooms to itself at unpredictable intervals. At present it appeared to be constructing a conservatory. The house was, by any objective standard, a modest Wanting—certainly smaller and less obtrusive than, say, Pell's—but Drett regarded it with the unblinking vigilance of a woman who knows that houses have a tendency to grow.

"Client," said Junip, who was stationed at the entrance and whose primary duties were receiving arrivals and removing from them any items that might interfere with the laundering process, which in practice meant cloaks, hats, false beards, and on one memorable occasion a full suit of decorative armor.

The figure who descended the stairs moved with the particular gait of a man attempting to occupy as little space as possible while being, by every available metric, enormous. He was well over six feet, broad in the shoulder, dressed in a greatcoat of charcoal cashmere with the collar turned up, and wearing a hat pulled so low that the only visible feature of his face was a mouth set in the thin, determined line of a man enduring a medical procedure.

His Wanting preceded him into the room.

It was necessary, at moments like these, for the launderers to maintain what the guild called "professional aspect"—an expression of studied neutrality that communicated neither judgment nor interest, the facial equivalent of a waiting room. This was, in practice, the most demanding skill the profession required, far more difficult than the actual chemistry of suppression or the physical mechanics of scrubbing. Because the Wantings of the wealthy, the truly wealthy, the generationally and architecturally and legislatively wealthy, were—

Well. Let us say that the launderers of Hesper Street had seen a great deal, and leave delicacy to those who can afford it.

This particular specimen entered the room like weather. It was vast—easily eight feet at the shoulder, hunched to pass beneath the doorframe—and it possessed a complicated anatomy that the narrator will attempt to describe with clinical dispassion, though clinical dispassion has never been our strongest suit. The central mass was a great luminous node, the color of old gold mixed with something less wholesome—a bruised, feverish amber that pulsed with the man's heartbeat. From this node extended six limbs, each terminating in a shape that was almost a hand but was in fact a mouth—or, more precisely, was a hand whose fingers, when they closed around something, became lips, and whose palm, when it opened, revealed rows of tiny, perfect teeth. The whole apparatus was wreathed in tendrils of cooler light, a silver-white that might have been beautiful if the tendrils had not been in constant motion, reaching, testing, withdrawing, reaching again—performing the ceaseless reconnaissance of an appetite that had never once encountered the concept of *enough.*

"Lord Bracewell," said Junip, consulting her ledger with the same breezy competence she would have brought to receiving a delivery of soap. "Your usual Wednesday appointment has been rescheduled to Thursday. Madame Grelch sends her apologies."

Lord Bracewell said nothing. Lord Bracewell's mouth maintained its medical line. Lord Bracewell's Wanting extended one of its six limb-mouths toward Junip's face, and Junip, with the practiced ease of someone deflecting an unwanted compliment at a dinner party, tilted her head exactly three inches to the left and allowed the limb to pass by her cheek close enough to ruffle her hair.

"If you'll remove your coat, my lord."

He removed his coat. Beneath it, the Wanting was worse—brighter, more agitated, its tendrils wrapping and unwrapping around his torso in the manner of something that did not wish to be separated from its source. One of the hand-mouths had found the man's wrist and was gnawing at it gently, meditatively, the way a child gnaws a toy it has not yet decided whether to love or destroy.

Pell and Ivria were at the trough.

They had laundered Bracewell before—every Thursday, excepting holidays, for the better part of three years. The procedure was established. Ivria took the left flank, where the tendrils were denser. Pell took the right, where the limb-mouths were more active and where the risk of being bitten (it was not precisely a bite—more of a *suction*, a brief, startling draw on the warmth of whatever flesh it contacted—but "bitten" served as shorthand) was higher. Between them, working from opposite ends of the trough, they would coax the Wanting into the solution, submerge it, and begin the work.

"Breathe normally, my lord," said Pell, which was what she said to every client, though no client in the history of Madame Grelch's laundry had ever breathed normally during the procedure, because the procedure involved watching a stranger press the physical manifestation of your innermost desire into a basin of astringent fluid and scrub it like a stained tablecloth.

Pell's hands entered the solution. The cold was immediate and profound—not the cold of winter or of water but the cold of something being taken away, a thermal subtraction that began in the fingertips and climbed the forearms and settled, eventually, in the chest, where it would remain for several hours after the shift ended, a residual ache that the launderers referred to as "the chill" and treated with gin, proximity to the candles, and a refusal to discuss it.

She took hold of the nearest limb-mouth. It resisted. They always resisted. A Wanting did not wish to be diminished any more than a fire wished to be extinguished—the analogy was imprecise but emotionally accurate. Pell tightened her grip and pressed the limb beneath the surface. The solution hissed. The light dimmed. The limb-mouth opened wide, its tiny teeth chattering, and then—slowly, reluctantly, like a hand opening to release a coin—it went slack.

"First arm," said Pell.

"Noted," said Ivria, who was working her side with the grim efficiency of someone shelling peas while thinking about something else entirely. Her own Wanting, a complicated flickering aurora of deep green and violet that hung about her like a magnetic field, occasionally sending off flares that illuminated the hollows of her face from unexpected angles, was doing what it always did during laundering: intensifying. This was the occupational cruelty. Handling another person's desire made one's own desire more vivid, more present, more impossible to ignore. It was as though desire were contagious—not in its specifics, but in its *scale.* You could not spend eight hours scrubbing the Wantings of others and emerge with your own unaffected.

"He's clenching," said Ivria, meaning that Lord Bracewell's body was producing the involuntary resistance that made the work slower—a tightening of the musculature that anchored the Wanting to its source, as though the body knew, even when the mind had paid good money to disagree, that something essential was being reduced.

"My lord," said Pell, "I'll need you to think of something mundane. Laundry lists. The weather. Parliamentary procedure."

A small, strangled sound emerged from Lord Bracewell's medical mouth. It might have been a laugh.

The next forty minutes proceeded with the metronomic rhythm that was the hallmark of expert laundering. Limb by limb, tendril by tendril, Pell and Ivria submerged, soaked, scrubbed, and pressed the golden Wanting into manageable dimensions. The wire frames came out—Corla's department, for which her aged and exactingly steady hands were indispensable—and the diminished creature was shaped, molded, persuaded into a configuration that could pass, in adequate lighting and at sufficient distance, for something one might call "a minor enthusiasm."
