# The Bone-ite Story

## I.

The voicemail was from Darla Bevins at the Boone County Health Department, who was not calling on behalf of the Boone County Health Department, which she made clear twice in forty-one seconds. She'd gotten my number from a nurse at the VA in Huntington who'd gotten it from a patient I'd treated for sciatica in a Walmart parking lot in Ceredo. The referral chain itself was a diagnostic: nobody with real options ends up dialing a number they got from a man I needled between a shopping cart corral and a Dodge Durango.

What Darla said: there was a holler off Route 3, past Twilight, past the last cell tower, past the place where the power company stopped replacing poles after the ice storm of '09 and never came back. What Darla said: the CDC had sent a team in April and a second team in May and filed a report in June that she had not seen but that had resulted in jersey barriers across the access road and a laminated sign that read AREA CLOSED BY ORDER OF FEDERAL AUTHORITY, which was not a agency she recognized. What Darla said: calcification. She said the word like she wasn't sure it was the right one and also like she'd been saying it in her sleep for weeks. Living tissueiteiteite and then the message cut off because she'd hit the time limit, and she did not call back.

I knew what it was by then. You heard things. A paramedic in Mingo County had posted photos before his account went dark — a forearm, the skin gone to something betweenite andite lite lite lite. I did not look at the photos more than once. I am not describing them here.

I keep the van stocked for four-day runs. That's my circuit: parking lots, flea markets, the occasional church basement where someone's cousin has a frozen shoulder and no insurance and heard about the Chinese needle lady in the blue Econoline, which is what they call me despite the fact that I am from Wheeling and my acupuncture certification is from a program in Baltimore I completed on the GI Bill. I restock Sundays. This was a Thursday.

Inventory for the holler. Filiform needles: 200 count, 0.20mm gauge. 100 count, 0.25mm. 50 count, 0.30mm, which I use for deep tissue and for patients who flinch and need to believe the thicker needle is doing something the thin one wasn't, which it is not, but belief is a meridian I haven't found on any chart. 30 count, 0.35mm, which I have never had cause to use on a human being but which I packed anyway, in the way you pack a thing you cannot justify. Guide tubes. Alcohol swabs, 500 count. Moxa sticks, two boxes. Cotton balls. Sharps containers, three. Surgical tape. A copy of *Deadman's* for point location, which I do not need but which I carry the way a priest carries a Bible he's memorized — not for reference but for the weight of it in the bag.

Also: a case of MREs I still had from a life I do not inventory here. Also: a water filter. Also: a Mossberg 500 I keep under the passenger seat that has nothing to do with acupuncture and everything to do with road work in places where the roads have stopped being roads.

I left at 6 a.m. Friday. The van needed an oil change. I did not get an oil change.

## II.

Glen Kovacs had an office in a strip mall in Hurricane, between a vape shop and a place that did both tax preparation and dog grooming, which I mention only because the smell was relevant to the experience of sitting in Glen's waiting room. The waiting room had four chairs and a poster of the human spine that someone had annotated in blue ballpoint with handwritten labels — *THE ROOT*, *THE TRUNK*, *THE CROWN* — as though the spine were a tree, or a religion. Glen had added these himself. His license from the West Virginia Board of Chiropractic — revoked, 2021 — was taped to the wall behind the reception desk with Scotch tape that had yellowed and curled at the edges but held. He had not turned it to face the wall. He had not put it in a drawer. It was there, the way a scar is there.

When I walked in he was cracking his own knuckles, left hand working the right, a sequence he performed with the mechanical regularity of a man who does it sixty, seventy times a day. He looked at me and said, "Alignment issue?"

Peter Huang was a hypnotherapist with a practice in Charleston that was either thriving or collapsing depending on which of his two websites you found. The professional one listed credentials — a certification from a program I'd never heard of, a photograph where he wore a tie and looked like the concept of trust. The second website, which came up if you searched his name plus the word "breakthrough," described hypnotherapy as "the immune system of consciousness" and featured testimonials from people who had been cured of things that were not, in any medical framework I recognized, diseases.

He came to my van because I'd parked outside his office. He looked at the acupuncture signage. He went pale. 

"I have a thing about needles," he said.

"I know," I said. We'd met once, at a continuing education seminar in Morgantown where the accreditation was dubious and the coffee was free. He'd left during my demonstration. I'd noticed because people don't usually leave during demonstrations unless they're going to vomit, and he had the walk.

I told him what was happening in the holler. He listened with the particular attentiveness of a person whose profession is listening, which is different from the attentiveness of a person who is actually hearing you. When I finished, he said, "Consciousness can override cellular processes. The body follows the mind's blueprint. If the blueprint changes—"

"Can you come Thursday?"

"If the mind believes the tissue is calcium, the tissue has no choice but—"

"Thursday."

He could come Thursday.

The doula's name was Sadie Persinger and she was not recruited so much as she appeared.

I was at the Speedway in Danville filling the van's tank and organizing my kit in the back with the cargo doors open when a woman sat down on my rear bumper as if she'd been expected. She was mid-fifties, gray hair cut short, hands that looked like they'd been soaked in something their whole lives — water, or blood, or amniotic fluid, or all of those in the order that a life in her profession would produce them. She wore a canvas jacket with the pockets full. I did not ask of what.

She said, "You're going to the holler."

I did not answer. I had not told anyone where I was going. Darla Bevins did not know my van. I had spoken only to the four I'd already recruited, and none of them knew the specific holler, because I had not yet told them. I had said "Boone County" and nothing more.

Sadie said, "The progression's faster now. Three weeks ago it was extremities first. Now it's starting in the chest."

I put down the roll of surgical tape I was holding.

She said, "You can smell it before you see it. Like limestone after rain. Like a cave."

She knew the name of the holler. She said it — Burnt Cabin Branch — the way you say the name of a place you've been dreaming about, with the particular care of someone who has been pronouncing it to herself and wants to get it right for the first time out loud. She knew the progression rate. She knew things that were not in Darla's voicemail or on the paramedic's deleted posts or in any information I had been able to find in four days of looking.

I said, "How do you—"

She said, "I've delivered seven babies in that holler over twelve years. I know what the water tastes like. I know where the wells are. I know the families."

And the sentence I started — how do you know the progression rate, how do you know it's moved to the chest, how do you know what I can smell before I've smelled it — I did not finish. Because she was looking at me with an expression I could not chart, could not locate on any meridian, and I —

She said, "You'll need me," and she was right in a way I could not measure by gauge.

## III.

The checkpoint was a jersey barrier and a sign. The sign said what Darla had told me it said. The jersey barrier had been pushed to one side by something — a truck, or a flood, or the passage of enough time that even concrete slabs stop meaning what they meant. Wild-flowers were growing in the gap. I drove through. Nobody stopped us.

Glen rode shotgun. The others followed in Martin's car, a Lincoln Town Car that had clearly been more expensive in a previous life, the way Martin had been more licensed. We were a two-vehicle convoy entering a quarantine zone through a checkpoint secured by flowers.

The road went from asphalt to gravel three miles past the barrier. Gravel to dirt two miles after that. Dirt to something that was not a road but was where a road had been, the way a scar is where a wound was — you could see the memory of the path in the way the trees pulled back, but the surface was root and mud and stone and, increasingly, something else. A white mineral grit that crunched under the tires like sugar. Likeite ite ite.

The smell came first. Sadie was right. Limestone after rain. The deep mineral exhale of a cave, except we were on a hillside in open air and the sky was white and the trees were full of leaves that were the right color and the wrong texture — I saw this from the van and did not stop to confirm it, but the leaves looked *stiff.* They did not move in the breeze the way leaves move. They moved the way shingles move. They moved the way things move when they are still attached to a living structure but are no longer, themselves, entirely alive.

The holler opened below us like a cupped hand. Twelve houses. A church. A building that had been a store. Creek running through the center that I could see from the ridge was the wrong color — not polluted, not muddy, but *clear in the wrong way,* the way water is clear when it is running over limestone, that blue-white mineral transparency that means the water is calcium-saturated.

People were outside. That was the first thing. I had expected — I don't know what I expected. Doors shut. Curtains drawn. The barricaded posture of a place under siege. Instead: a woman was hanging laundry. A man sat on a porch step with a dog. Two figures stood in the yard of the third house, very still, and I did not determine from the ridge whether they were standing or whether they had stopped being able to stop standing.

We drove down.

It was finished. It was calcium from the wrist down, the fingers still curled around a clothespin in a grip that would outlast the clothesline, the shirt, the woman. She hung laundry with her left hand. She did not look at us when we parked. She pinned a shirt to the line and the pin slipped and she said "damn" in the exact tone of a woman whose clothespin has slipped, and that one word — *damn* — was the sound of a person who has integrated the end of her hand into the category of daily inconvenience, which is where human beings put everything they cannot survive thinking about.

The man on the porch step had a jaw that was locked. Open. I could see from twenty feet that the mandible had calcified in a position roughly thirty degrees past comfortable, the mouth a fixed oval, and he breathed through it with a sound like wind through a pipe, and the dog beside him was pressed against his leg in the way dogs press against the thing they are losing.
