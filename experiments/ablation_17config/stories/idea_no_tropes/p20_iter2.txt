# The Bone-Setter

## I.

The salt is the color of a cataract and she moves across it the same way she moves through everything now, which is by feel and by the faint electrical report of a body's future pressing against her palms like weather against a window.

"Kneel," she says.

He kneels. She can hear the quality of his kneeling — the hesitation in the left knee that tells her about the cartilage, the slight overcompensation in the right hip that tells her about the lumbar vertebrae, the breathing that tells her about the rest of it. She places her hands against his sternum. The bone speaks to her in its language.

"Nine years from now," she says, "you will fall from a gantry. The left femoral neck will fracture here" — she touches his hip through the heavy fabric — "along the intertrochanteric line. A garden-variety fragility fracture except that no one within two hundred kilometers will know how to set it and the bone will heal canted eleven degrees and you will walk with that cant for the remaining—"

*A kitchen with blue tile. Not blue. The memory of blue, because the tiles are white but the light through the water-jug gives them a cast that a child would call blue and she is the child, she is not the child, the water tastes of iron and limestone and someone is laughing in the other room with the particular helpless generosity of a person who has just been surprised by something genuinely—*

"—remaining decades, which will be three, during which the compensatory stress on the lumbar spine will produce a stenosis that—"

*The child's laughter stops not because the child is unhappy but because a dog has done something and the child, who is she, who is not she, is holding her breath the way children hold breath before the next wave of laughing, and the dog's ear is velvet, the specific velvet of a living ear with blood moving under it, warm—*

She presses. The bone offers its future to her hands like a confession, and she takes it, and the fracture that would have been unbraids itself from the causal sequence and ceases.

"Get up," she says.

He gets up. He is confused. She can hear his confusion — the particular shuffle of a man who expected to feel different and doesn't. He was brought to her across nine miles of irradiated flats by people who told him she could help and he cannot locate the help.

"I don't feel anything," he says.

"You won't. Not for nine years. And then you won't fall, or you will fall and the bone will hold, and you will not feel anything then either. You will feel nothing for the rest of your life where you would have felt pain and you will not notice the nothing. No one notices the nothing. That is the whole point of the nothing."

She remains on her knees on the salt. The crystals press small liturgies into her skin. She is aware that her hands are still buzzing with the kitchen, the laughter, the dog's ear. These will not leave. These have nowhere to go.

"What year is it?" she says.

He tells her.

She nods, the way a person nods when they have heard a number and must hold it next to other numbers to find where it belongs in a sequence they are no longer confident is sequential. Then she says: "Yes." And then, more quietly, as if confirming it for someone else, someone standing in a kitchen she has never entered, pouring water from a jug that casts blue light on white tiles: "Yes, all right."

The man walks away across the salt. She can hear him for a long time, the particular rhythm of a gait that has not yet learned it was saved.

## II.

Bridger was three Highway 93 overpasses and the will to believe they constituted a town. She arrived in the second year of its existence, when the concrete pylons were still being fitted with salvage-walls and the settlement's children still played in the median strips with the unselfconscious authority of creatures who had never known the road's original purpose. She was twenty-four. She could still see. She could still, on most days, tell you with confidence which memories were hers.

The child's name was Lena and she had been born under the northernmost overpass during a dust storm and she was, by every measure available to the settlement's healers, healthy. Four years old, hair cut close against the heat, a bruise on her shin from a fall that was already healing in the ordinary slow way that bodies healed when left to their own calendar.

"She's fine," the mother said. "Everyone says she's fine."

"Everyone is correct about what she is now," she said. She was kneeling in front of the girl, her hands not yet touching, held an inch from the child's ribs. She could feel it. The way a dowser feels water, except what she felt was time — the body's forward motion through its own unfolding, and inside that motion, the places where the unfolding would snag.

"There is a mass," she said. "It does not exist yet. It will begin as a single aberrant cell in the left cervical lymph node in" — she closed her eyes, read the body's clock — "four years and two months, give or take a season. Lymphoblastic. It will present with night sweats and a swelling here." She touched the left side of the girl's neck, gently, at the precise anatomical location. "Without intervention she will be symptomatic by nine. By eleven the marrow will be involved."

The mother made a sound.

"I can take it," she said. "The whole sequence. Collapse the causal chain before the first cell divides. She will never be sick. She will never know she was going to be sick. Do you understand what I am telling you."

"Yes."

"I need you to say that you do."

"I understand."

"Good. Hold her hand if she is frightened. She will not feel pain. I am the only one who will feel anything."

She did not say this to invoke sympathy. She said it as clinical orientation, because she was precise about her work and its costs the way a surgeon is precise about a tourniquet.

She placed her hands on the girl's chest.

*And Lena's life detonated through her.*

Not the four years until the cancer. The whole thing. The full unlived span that the cancer would have cut short, that was now being purchased from the body's future at the price of her knowing, her carrying, her bearing in her own flesh what this child would become. It came all at once and it came in the second person, the way dreams come, the way you are always you in a dream even when you are someone else, and she was—

—*you are eleven and the dust hasn't come this season and the air smells of something you vaguely remember is called grass, and your mother, who is standing behind you in this room watching a stranger lay hands on your chest, your mother is older now and her hands when she braids your hair pull too tight on the left side because her knuckle joints have thickened with work and you have never told her because her face while she braids is the most peaceful her face ever gets—*

—*you are nineteen and his name is — the name comes fast, too fast, she cannot stop it — his name is Arlen and his skin is dark and he is asleep and you are looking at the curve of his shoulder thinking about how strange it is that this is allowed, that you can simply lie in the warmth of another body and no one will come to take it from you, and his breathing has the particular unevenness of a man who is almost awake and pretending not to be so that you will keep looking at him—*

—*you are twenty-six and the pregnancy is early and you are sick every morning with a sickness that is also joy, both chemicals indistinguishable in the blood, and you stand outdoors to vomit because the smell of the cook-fire makes it worse and while you are bent over in the gray morning you see a single bird, a type you do not know the name of, cresting the overpass, and for no reason the sight of it makes you cry—*

—*you are thirty-three and twelve and forty-one and the drought is the worst since the collapse and the water ration is cut to half and your daughter, your daughter who does not exist yet, your daughter who will be born to a body this woman is touching in a concrete settlement under a highway overpass, your daughter is eight and she asks you if rain is real or if it is like the old stories about airplanes and snow and you say it is real, you have seen it, and she looks at you with the specific skepticism of a child who loves you too much to call you a liar—*

—*you are fifty-four and your hands look like your mother's hands and you braid your daughter's daughter's hair and you pull too tight on the left side—*

—*you are sixty-one and the fluid is in your lungs and each breath is a negotiation between your body and the air and you are not afraid, you are not afraid because Arlen is holding your hand and you can hear your granddaughter in the next room singing a song that has no tune, just a child's voice making sound because a child's voice must make sound, and the light through the window is amber and you are thinking—*

She gasped.

The salt flats. The overpass. The child standing in front of her untouched by time, by cancer, by any of it, blinking with the mild concern of a four-year-old watching an adult behave strangely. The mother gripping the child's hand. The settlement's noise — a cart, a voice, something metal ringing against something stone — entering her ears like a language she had briefly forgotten.

"Is she—" the mother began.

"She's clean." Her voice was steady out of practice, not out of calm. "The chain is collapsed. The cell will not divide. She will live to—"

She stopped herself. She always stopped herself here. She did not tell them the number. The number was hers now, along with everything else — the braiding, the bird, the breathing man's shoulder, the granddaughter's tuneless song.

"She will live," she said.

The child was fine. The child would always be fine. The child would grow up to love a man named Arlen and braid hair and watch birds and die at sixty-one with fluid in her lungs thinking about light. The child would do all of this without knowing that any of it had ever been in danger, and without knowing that a stranger in a settlement called Bridger had already lived her entire life in the space between one breath and the next and would carry it from this moment forward as a weight indistinguishable from memory.

She stood. Her knees ached. The light was too bright. For a disorienting moment she looked at her own hands and expected them to be older, the knuckle joints thickened, the skin of a woman who had braided granddaughters' hair in a drought.

They were her hands. She was twenty-four.

She counted. Lena made the thirty-first life she carried. She held each of them perfectly because the alternative was to let them blur and she had understood from the beginning that blurring was the first symptom of dissolution. So she kept the ledger precise: thirty-one complete lives, arranged in the order she had absorbed them, each with its full sensory catalogue.

Thirty-one people she had never been, intimately, in perpetuity, without consent.

Not their consent. They had all consented, or their families had. Hers.

She had not consented to know how Arlen smelled in sleep. She had not asked to carry the specific amber quality of light in a room whose coordinates she could pinpoint on the flats but which would not be built for another twenty years. She had not wanted to know how it felt to die at sixty-one with gratitude and fluid and the sound of singing.

But the alternative was the cancer. The alternative was always the cancer, the fracture, the embolism, the structural failure in a body's future timeline that she and she alone could see and reach and extract. And the price was always the same: the life she saved was the life she swallowed.

She walked to the edge of the overpass's shadow and sat with her back against the pylon and closed her eyes and Arlen's shoulder was there, warm and curved and not hers, and she pressed her actual hands against her actual face and breathed until she was only herself, mostly herself, a woman in the shadow of an overpass in a settlement called Bridger, twenty-four years old, and only borrowing.

## III.

*Come, and I will tell you of the Woman Who Heals What Hasn't Happened Yet.*

She came out of the salt in the years after the fire, when the world was still deciding whether to die. Where she came from, no one knows — some say the Eastern Wastes, some say she walked up from the ocean floor after the second boiling. She had hands like a surgeon's and eyes like a prophet's and when she touched you she could see the thread of your life unspooled all the way to its cutting, and where the thread had knots she would smooth them, and where the thread was frayed she would bind it, and the cutting would come later and cleaner and you would never know what she had saved you from.

She healed a warlord's daughter in the Red Settlements. She healed a well-digger in the Basin whose spine would have shattered in a collapse seven years hence. She healed a woman carrying twins whose cord was tangled in a future that had not yet arrived.

*The warlord's daughter. I will tell you about the warlord's daughter.*

*She was seventeen and she smelled of the lye her people used to clean their water and when I laid hands on her the future death was a childbirth, a hemorrhage, the baby transverse and the pelvis too narrow, fourteen years from the day I touched her. I collapsed the chain. I don't know how — I have never known how; my hands know and I follow my hands. The daughter sat up and looked at me with the simple blankness of a girl who has been told she was in danger and cannot locate the danger and suspects that the adults may be performing a kind of elaborate theater for reasons of their own.*

*She left. She was fine. She would be fine.*

*I was not fine. I am telling you what the stories leave out:*

*I still wake tasting copper.*

They say she walks the flats without rest, that the settlements send runners to find her and she comes to those in need with the patience of weather.

*They send runners. That part is true. And I go. That part is also true. But patience — what they call patience is something else. It is the inability to refuse. It is the knowledge that the child's lymph node is already dividing wrong in the future and I can feel it the way you can feel a sound too low to hear, a pressure in the jaw, an wrongness. And I cannot unlearn that. I cannot walk past a body whose future is catastrophic and choose not to reach in. Not because I am good. Because the reaching is compulsive, the way a cough is compulsive, the way a hand flinches from a burn. My body does it and I follow my body and then I carry the life.*

They say her healings are clean — a touch, a breath, a trembling, and then wholeness.

*Clean. Listen: when I took the warlord's daughter's death, I took her life. The whole life, unspooled, instantaneous, irrefutable. I know her children's faces. I know the name she would have given to the baby that would have killed her — she would have called him Soren, after a man I also know intimately because he is part of the life I swallowed, her husband, a quiet steady person who repairs water-pumps and who, in the privacy of their sleeping quarters — do you see?
