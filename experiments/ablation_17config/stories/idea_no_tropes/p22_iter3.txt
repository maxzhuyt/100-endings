# The Keen Film

---

**FEDERAL BUREAU OF INVESTIGATION**
**Behavioral Science Unit — Quantico, Virginia**
**Training Module 11-F: Case-Based Instructional Series**
**Date of Production: September 14, 1983**
**Classification: Internal Use Only — Not for Distribution**
**Viewer Clearance: GS-11 and Above**

---

What you are about to see is not entertainment.

This is the voice that begins it — dry, nasal, the consonants slightly over-enunciated in the manner of a man who has given depositions and knows that tape recorders do not forgive mumbling. There is a quality to the recording: a hiss beneath the words, the particular flatness of a room that has been soundproofed not for music but for testimony.

My name is Dr. Edwin Paulk. I am a clinical psychologist assigned to the Bureau's Behavioral Science Unit and have served in that capacity since 1974. The film you are about to view has been prepared at the request of the Office of Professional Responsibility and is intended for use as an instructional module in the recognition and management of what the Bureau has designated Investigative Identification Disorder — a term I coined, as it happens, though I take no particular pride in the coining. One does not take pride in naming a disease. One simply528 observes that the disease exists and that it needed a name.

A brief pause. The sound of paper being squared against a table.

The subject of this module is Special Agent Dolores Anne Keen, badge number 6241, assigned to the Louisville field office from March 1979 until her removal from active duty on October 3, 1981. Agent Keen was, by every available metric, an exemplary investigator. She graduated first in her class from the Academy. She received two commendations for undercover work in her first eighteen months. Her supervisory evaluations describe her, repeatedly, as — and I am quoting — "the calmest person in any room she enters." Special Agent in Charge DiMasi once noted in a performance review that Agent Keen had "the emotional thermostat of a surgical lamp." He intended this as praise. I include it because, in retrospect, it is the first exhibit.

The screen shows a photograph. It is a standard Bureau portrait: a woman in her early thirties, dark hair pulled back with the severity that was, in that era, a kind of uniform for female agents who understood that the uniform was the price of being left alone to do the work. The face is angular, handsome rather than pretty, with a quality around the eyes that might be described as attentiveness — the look of someone who is not merely seeing you but cataloging you, filing you in some interior system whose categories you will never be told.

Agent Keen was assigned to the case internally designated UNSUB 117-81, known in the media as the Vivisectionist, in August of 1981. By week four of her assignment, she was sleeping in her government-issued sedan in the parking lot of a Red Roof Inn outside Harlan, Kentucky, with a mason jar of pig knuckles on the dashboard. She told her partner, Agent Michael Logue, that the knuckles were "for reference." She did not specify what they were a reference to. Agent Logue did not ask. I note this because Agent Logue's decision not to ask is itself a clinical datum that I will return to later in this presentation.

I will ask the viewing agents to hold all questions until the conclusion of the film. I will also ask — and I recognize that this is an unusual request in a training context — that you resist the impulse to diagnose Agent Keen before I have finished diagnosing her. You will think you see what is happening. I thought I saw what was happening. We were both wrong, though in different directions.

The photograph of Keen dissolves. What replaces it is a slow lateral tracking shot across a bulletin board — the kind issued to field agents, a portable corkboard with a collapsible aluminum frame that could be set up in a motel room or a borrowed office. The left side of the board is papered with crime-scene photographs: bodies on steel tables, close-ups of incision patterns, the particular yellow-gray of flash photography in institutional spaces. Pinned alongside them, and increasingly interleaved with them, are hand-drawn sketches. Anatomical studies. The hand is confident, the line work precise — a draftsman's hand, or a surgeon's. The sketches annotate the photographs, extend them, *interpret* them, the way a scholar's marginalia extends a text. Moving right across the board, the sketches grow more elaborate and the photographs fewer, until, at the board's right edge, there is no visible distinction between the crime-scene documentation and Agent Keen's personal artwork. They have become the same thing.

Dr. Paulk does not comment on what the camera shows. He allows the pan to complete in silence, then:

We will begin with the crime scene.

---

The Vivisectionist had killed twice before Keen was assigned — or rather, he had killed twice that the Bureau had connected to a single actor. The first victim was a farrier outside Prestonsburg. The second was a taxidermist near Pikeville. Both men worked with animal bodies for a living. Both were found in their own workspaces, on their own tables, opened with their own tools. The local police treated them as unrelated. It was Agent Logue who noticed the connection: not the victimology, which was circumstantial, but the *technique,* which was not. In both cases, the thoracic cavity had been opened using a method that no coroner in the region recognized — a Y-incision variation that, upon Logue's consultation with a comparative anatomist at the University of Kentucky, turned out to be standard procedure for necropsies of large livestock. The killer was cutting people open the way a veterinary pathologist opens a horse. The Bureau was notified. Keen was assigned six days later.

She arrived at the third crime scene on the morning of August 9, 1981. A veterinary clinic in Ages, Kentucky — a town of perhaps three hundred people in the hills outside Harlan. I have been to Ages. It is the kind of place where the word *town* is itself a kind of aspiration. The clinic served a forty-mile radius. It was the only one.

The attending veterinarian, Dr. Carl Arnett, age fifty-one, had been found by his receptionist at 7:15 a.m. when she arrived to unlock the front door and discovered that she did not need to, because the front door was already locked — from the inside, with the deadbolt thrown and the chain engaged. She used her key. She entered. She described what she found in her police statement as follows, and I will read it verbatim because the language of a person in shock has a diagnostic precision that clinical language sometimes lacks: *He was on the table where we do the dogs. He was all open and everything was on the cart. It was on the cart like when he'd do a teaching thing for the 4-H kids, where he'd show them the parts. Everything had a label.*

Dr. Paulk pauses here. The pause is just long enough to register.

The labels were handwritten on prescription pad paper — Dr. Arnett's own prescription pads. Each organ had been removed, weighed on the clinic's postal scale, and placed on a rolling surgical cart in a sequence that the comparative anatomist later confirmed was consistent with the developmental ontogeny of the organs — that is, the order in which they form during embryonic growth. The heart first, then the liver, then the kidneys, and so on. The Vivisectionist — and we had begun using that name by then, though I should note that Agent Keen never used it; she referred to him only as "the subject" or, later, simply as "he," with a familiarity that I initially attributed to professional shorthand — the Vivisectionist had also left a note. It was written on a final sheet of the prescription pad, in a calm and legible hand, and it read: *I have put him in the order he was always in. You simply couldn't see it before.*

The crime-scene photographs appear on screen. They will not be described here beyond what has already been stated. The training-film audience is assumed to have the relevant clearance and the relevant stomach.

Agent Keen arrived at 11:40 a.m. She was accompanied by Agent Logue and by two members of the local sheriff's department. According to Agent Logue's deposition — and I should note that Agent Logue was deposed four times during the subsequent review, and his account remained consistent in all material respects, which is itself unusual and which I take as an indicator that what he witnessed was sufficiently vivid to resist the ordinary deformations of memory — according to his deposition, Agent Keen entered the examination room, stopped approximately four feet from the threshold, and did not speak or move for forty-seven minutes. Agent Logue timed it. He timed it because, as he stated, "I didn't know what else to do. She wasn't responding to her name."

Logue described her posture as "not frozen — more like *reading.*" He said her eyes moved. He said she examined the room the way — and this is his comparison, not mine — "the way you'd watch a movie you've seen before and you're trying to catch something you missed." He said the sheriff's deputies left after twenty minutes. He said he stayed because he was her partner and because he had the car keys.

At the forty-seven-minute mark, Agent Keen spoke. Her first words — and I have confirmed this with Logue's deposition, the sheriff's report, and Keen's own subsequent account, so we have rare triangulation — her first words were: "He's not wrong about the labeling. That *is* where the spleen goes, developmentally."

Agent Logue asked her what she meant.

She said: "The ontogenic sequence. He's got it right. Most anatomists would put the spleen later, but if you're going by the splenic anlage rather than the fully differentiated organ, he's placed it correctly. Which means he's not working from a standard textbook. He's working from primary embryological sources. Or from direct observation. Or both."

Dr. Paulk pauses again. A longer pause this time — the sound of him, perhaps, removing his glasses and replacing them.

I include this exchange in full because it is, in my professional assessment, the moment at which the process that would eventually be designated Investigative Identification Disorder began. I will also tell you — because honesty compels me and because this film is, among other things, an exercise in institutional honesty, or at least it has been directed to be — that when I first read this exchange in the case file, my marginal note was: *Impressive.* I wrote *impressive*. I wrote it because Agent Keen had, in forty-seven minutes of silence, extracted more actionable intelligence from a crime scene than the local authorities had produced in three days of processing. The observation about the splenic anlage was, in fact, subsequently used to narrow the suspect's educational background — it contributed materially to the resolution of the case.

So I want you, the viewing agents, to sit with that for a moment. Her first symptom was also her first breakthrough. The disease and the gift presented identically. This is, I have come to believe, the central problem — not just of this case, but of the work that this unit asks human beings to do. But I am getting ahead of myself. I will proceed chronologically.

---

In the second week of the investigation, Agent Keen began keeping a personal journal. This was not unusual. Many agents maintain investigative journals as a supplement to official case files. What was unusual — and what did not come to the Bureau's attention until substantially later than it should have — was the journal's evolution.

The journal was recovered from Agent Keen's motel room on October 3, at the time of her removal from duty. It had been seized pursuant to a warrant. I have the journal here.

A sound, faint but distinct: the creak of a binding being opened.

I will read selected passages. I have organized them chronologically for the purposes of this presentation, though I should note that Agent Keen's own internal chronology becomes, over the course of the document, increasingly — let us say *non-linear.* Dates are sometimes absent. Entries are sometimes interrupted mid-sentence and resumed on a later page with no acknowledgment of the gap. There are also drawings, which I will describe where relevant.

The sound of pages turning.

August 12. Three days after the Arnett crime scene. Entry reads: *Geographic cluster analysis: three victims within a 40-mile radius, all in counties with significant livestock populations. Suspect likely has legitimate access to veterinary pharmaceutical supply chain — surgical sedatives, specifically. Check DEA distribution records for ketamine, xylazine, and acepromazine. Logue skeptical but Logue is always skeptical. That's fine. That's his job. My job is something else.*

Paulk pauses.

I note the final sentence. *My job is something else.* At the time of recovery, I flagged this as the earliest sign of what I would subsequently describe as role diffusion — the agent's sense that her function within the investigation had begun to exceed, or perhaps to diverge from, standard investigative parameters. But it is, I acknowledge, ambiguous. Many agents describe their work in exceptionalist terms. It is not, in isolation, diagnostic. In isolation, nothing is diagnostic. That is the problem.

Pages turning.

August 15. *Reexamined photographs of Arnett scene, specifically the incision pattern on the left thoracic wall. The cut begins medially and sweeps laterally with a single motion — no hesitation marks, no correction. This is not a man who is following a diagram. This is a man who has done this enough times that the blade is part of his hand. I keep thinking about the word "fluency." When a person is fluent in a language they don't translate in their head anymore. The language thinks itself. His cutting is like that. The knife is thinking.*

Dr. Paulk clears his throat.

August 19. This is the entry I wish to draw particular attention to.

*Driving back from Evarts on 421. Dead possum on the shoulder, hit by a truck from the look of the pelvic displacement. I pulled over. I don't entirely know why I pulled over. I sat in the car for a while looking at it. The fur was mostly intact. The abdominal wall was ruptured on the left side, and I could see the intestines, and I thought: I don't actually know what a possum looks like inside. I know what a human being looks like inside. I have looked at Dr. Arnett's interior more carefully than I have ever looked at anyone's. But I have never looked at a possum. And it occurred to me that *he* would know. The subject would know what a possum looks like inside. He would know because he has made it his business to know, because for him the inside of a body is not a crime scene or an autopsy finding — it is a *landscape,* it is a *territory* he has been mapping for years, and I am trying to find him in that territory and I don't even have the most basic cartography.*

*I took it back to the motel. I used a steak knife from the Dixie Diner. It was serrated and not ideal, but it worked. I spent two hours. I learned things.*

Dr. Paulk stops reading. There is a silence of perhaps five seconds, which in the context of a training film is very long.

I will not read every entry in the sequence. I will summarize. Over the following eighteen days — August 19 through September 6 — Agent Keen's journal documents the roadside collection and subsequent dissection of a total of eleven animals. After the possum, a raccoon. After the raccoon, a groundhog recovered from a ditch outside Cumberland. Then two feral cats — she does not describe how she acquired the cats, and I did not, at the time, press the point. Then a sequence of smaller animals — squirrels, a rabbit — which she describes as "unsatisfying, anatomically — not enough complexity to be structurally analogous." And then, on September 1, a white-tailed deer.

The deer is the entry I am required to read in full. I am *required* to read it because the review board has determined that it is the entry at which, in retrospect, intervention should have occurred. I accept that determination. I will read it and I will let you decide whether, in my position, you would have intervened. I ask only that you decide honestly.

Pages turning.
