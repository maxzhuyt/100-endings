# The Natatorium

## I.

The notice had been pinned with a single red thumbtack through its laminated corner, slightly crooked, which meant Dennis from the permit office had done it. Dennis always used one tack. Madge had told him twice that the laminate curled under the fluorescents and that a single tack would not hold it flush to the cork, but Dennis operated under the theory—common among men who had never maintained a surface in their lives—that things, once placed, stayed.

She read it at 5:47 a.m. with her mop handle resting against her shoulder like a staff. The lobby was empty. The lobby was always empty at 5:47. It would remain empty at 6:47, and at 7:47, and for most of the hours that followed, because the Harmon Municipal Natatorium had not been full since the sardine cannery closed in 2003, and even then *full* had been a generous word for four lap swimmers and a woman named Jean Pfeiffer who came every Tuesday to walk the shallow end in a shower cap and talk to someone named Russell who was not, as far as anyone could determine, present.

The ordinance read:

*WHEREAS the Township of Harmon, pursuant to Section 14(c)(iii) of the Civic Organizations and Public Amenity Stewardship Act (revised), has determined that the continued allocation of community meeting space within municipally maintained facilities SHALL BE CONTINGENT upon demonstrated civic participation in the Annual Inter-District Water Polo Tournament (hereafter "the Tournament"); and WHEREAS all registered civic organizations currently utilizing public meeting rooms, multi-purpose halls, or ancillary gathering spaces within Township facilities are hereby REQUIRED to field a team of no fewer than seven (7) and no more than thirteen (13) registered members; and WHEREAS failure to participate shall constitute grounds for the immediate revocation of space-use privileges, effective sixty (60) days from the posting of this notice—*

There was a third paragraph involving indemnification clauses, the waiving of liability for "aquatic incident," and a phone number for a clerk whose extension, Madge already knew, would ring to a desk that had been unmanned since February. She read this paragraph too. Then she read the whole thing again.

Through the tall windows on the east wall, the morning entered gray and provisional. The town revealed itself in the order it always did: first the roofline of the Dollar General, then the collapsed pier slumped into the harbor like a man who has been told to sit down and has done so with too much obedience, then the seafront road with its boarded storefronts, their plywood darkened to the color of old tea. The diesel smell came in through the ventilation system. It had been coming in since the fuel depot expanded in 2011, and no amount of filter replacement had solved it, and at some point Madge had stopped noticing except when she was noticing, which was now, because the notice had made her notice.

She mopped around the base of the corkboard.

The floor here was a particular problem. The grout between the lobby tiles had been crumbling since before the last municipal resealing, which had been scheduled for 2017 and postponed until a fiscal quarter that had not yet arrived and showed no signs of arriving. Water pooled in the grout channels—not pool water but the ambient moisture that a natatorium breathed into its own bones. She worked the mop in the tight corners where the corkboard's aluminum frame met the wall. Beside the ordinance, a sun-bleached flyer for *AQUA AEROBICS – TUESDAYS AND THURSDAYS – 9 A.M. – INSTRUCTOR: GALE* had been hanging since sometime in the first year of the pandemic. Gale had moved to Tucson. The flyer had stayed. Madge had never removed it, because the corkboard was not her jurisdiction—the corkboard was Dennis—and because removing it would have felt like a statement about something, and Madge did not make statements. Madge mopped.

She moved from the lobby to the pool deck through the swinging door with the porthole window, and the air changed the way it always changed: immediately, completely, as though she had stepped across a border into a country with its own atmosphere. The chlorine was the first citizen of this country. It met her in the sinuses, familiar as her own name. Beneath it: the mineral note of old tile, the faint copper of aging pipes, the particular humidity that pools produce—not the wet heat of a sauna but something more institutional, a dampness that had been regulated into steadiness and had forgotten, over the decades, that it had ever been anything else.

The pool was still. Six lanes, twenty-five meters, depth graduating from three feet six inches at the south end to nine feet two inches at the north, though the painted depth markers on the east wall had faded so thoroughly that only Madge and the ghost of the man who had painted them knew the numbers for certain. The tile was Mediterranean blue, or had been. Forty years of chlorine had leached it to the color of an old airmail envelope. In the corners where the walls met the floor, the grout had darkened to something almost black, and in the underwater lights—only four of the original eight still functioning—this darkening gave the pool's edges the look of a manuscript whose margins had been slowly overtaken by mold.

Madge began her round. She walked the deck clockwise, as she had always walked it, her sneakers leaving brief impressions on the wet concrete before the humidity reclaimed them. She checked the skimmer baskets. She checked the chemical feeder. She ran the back of her hand along the starting blocks, feeling for the hairline cracks she had reported in a maintenance request last October and which the district had acknowledged with a form letter thanking her for her "ongoing facilities stewardship." The starting blocks remained cracked. The letter was in a drawer in the utility room beneath a bag of sodium bisulfate and a photograph of Ed, who had been her husband, and who had died in 2016 of a heart attack that the doctor described as massive and sudden, two words that, in the years since, Madge had come to understand were not synonyms but companions—a thing could be both enormous and instantaneous; a whole life could displace the same amount of space as a single stopped moment.

She did not think about Ed every time she opened the drawer. She did not think about him now. She thought about the skimmer baskets, which needed new gaskets, and the chlorine levels, which she would test at seven, and the notice on the corkboard, which she had already filed in the place where she filed all notices, ordinances, memos, and declarations that arrived in the natatorium's lobby: the broad, unhurried, thermally regulated middle depths of her attention, where things sank at their own pace and settled when they were ready.

Seventy years old. Five feet four inches. A body that had never been in the pool—not once, not in four decades—because Madge was the custodian, and the custodian's job was to maintain the medium, not to enter it. She knew the pool the way a meteorologist knows a storm system: through measurement, observation, and a respect born not of fear but of the professional recognition that the thing she tended was larger than the person tending it.

The surface of the water caught the fluorescent light and held it, trembling faintly from the circulation pump's vibration, and Madge watched it the way she watched it every morning, which was the way some people watch a sleeping animal: alert to any change, but expecting none.

## II.

They came on a Thursday.

The anarchist zine collective arrived first, at 2:15 p.m., which was not the time they had agreed upon with one another—they did not agree upon times, as scheduling was a coercive temporal framework—but was simply the time that three of their members happened to converge on the natatorium's entrance, having independently decided to check out the situation, a phrase that two of them used and the third rejected in favor of *reconnoiter the terrain of compulsory play*.

There were three of them. Sable, who was thirty-one and managed the digital archive for the collective's quarterly publication, *Ornery*, which had a print run of forty and a readership that was, in Sable's own assessment, theoretically infinite. Cal, who was thirty-four and built community tool libraries and spoke in paragraphs that began with *I think what's actually happening here is*. And Noor, who was twenty-nine and had the loudest voice of the three, a voice that entered the natatorium's lobby and immediately filled every corner of it, bouncing off the tile and the glass trophy case—empty since 1998—and the aluminum frame of the corkboard, so that by the time the three of them had crossed the lobby to read the ordinance, the building sounded like it was hosting six people rather than three.

Madge was behind the check-in counter, entering the day's chemical readings into a logbook that the district had asked her to switch to a digital spreadsheet in 2018 and that she continued to maintain in ballpoint pen in a college-ruled composition notebook because the computer in the utility room ran Windows Vista and took eleven minutes to boot and because the notebook had never crashed, been hacked, or required a software update. She watched the three of them the way the pool watched bodies from below: registering mass, velocity, the specific gravity of their disturbance.

They carried tote bags. Every tote bag bore a slogan. The slogans competed with one another. Noor's said NO GODS NO MASTERS NO LANE ASSIGNMENTS, which was either very old or very new, and either way suggested someone had been thinking about this situation before arriving, which meant the arrival was less casual than it was meant to appear.

"This is the pool," Noor said, after they pushed through the swinging door and stood on the deck.

"Yep," said Madge, who had followed them because people who had never been to a pool deck did unpredictable things around a pool deck, and these three had the specific postural uncertainty of people who had never been to a pool deck.

"It's—institutional," Cal said, in a voice that made the word carry the weight of a political analysis.

Sable said nothing. Sable was looking at the water.

They took seats in the east section of the bleachers. The bleachers were aluminum, six rows, and they amplified every shift of weight into a sound like someone slowly crumpling a car. Noor's voice, already loud, became, in the bleachers, a kind of civic event. Madge returned to the deck and began testing the water, dipping her strips, watching the color squares develop. The pool's pH was 7.4. The free chlorine was 2.0 ppm. Everything was where it should be. Everything was always where it should be. That was the job.

The neo-Luddite sewing circle arrived at 2:40 p.m. There were four of them: Bev, Gloria, Ines, and a woman everyone called Hutch, which was not her name but had become her name in the way that certain nicknames, after enough years, murder the original and take its place. They ranged in age from fifty-two to fifty-nine. They carried fabric bags—not tote bags, a distinction they would have been happy to explain if asked, which no one did, the difference being that a tote bag was a commodity that performed the aesthetics of refusal while being manufactured in the same facilities as the thing it claimed to refuse, whereas a fabric bag made by hand from reclaimed upholstery was an *object*, possessing history, weight, and the specific resistance of a thing that had been made slowly.

They entered the pool deck and immediately looked up at the fluorescent lights.

"Those are T8 tubes," said Hutch.

"They flicker in cycles of approximately four seconds," said Gloria, who had been in the building for less than a minute and had already timed the flicker. "That frequency is associated with cortisol increase in prolonged-exposure studies."

"The ones on the far end are T12s," said Madge, from behind the chemical testing station. "They're being phased out. Can't get replacements anymore."

Gloria and Hutch looked at Madge with the sudden, appraising interest of people who had found a fellow traveler in the gospel of planned obsolescence. Bev and Ines looked at the pool.

"It smells," Bev said.

"Chlorine," said Madge.

"Is it always like this?"

"Since 1981."

They took seats in the west section of the bleachers, as far from the anarchists as the geometry of the room allowed. They did not look at the anarchists. The anarchists did not look at them. The two groups occupied opposite ends of the same aluminum structure and communicated their mutual non-acknowledgment through a silence so pointed it was almost collaborative, like two soloists performing the same rest.

The libertarian survivalist chapter arrived at 3:05 p.m. There were two of them: Dale, who was forty-seven and wore tactical cargo pants with eleven visible pockets and, Madge suspected, at least three she could not see; and Kenny, who was forty-three and dressed like Dale's understudy for a role neither of them had been cast in. They entered the lobby with the specific alert posture of men who believed that any enclosed space was a scenario and that every scenario required an exit strategy. Dale's eyes went to the emergency exits. Kenny's eyes went to the locker room doors.

"Two points of egress from the deck," Dale said, not to Kenny, not to anyone, just narrating his own threat assessment the way some people narrated their thoughts at the grocery store. "Locker rooms are a choke point."

"Tile floor," said Kenny. "No traction. Bad footing in a situation."

"What kind of situation?" said Madge, who had come to the lobby to meet them because she had seen, through the porthole window, the way they were looking at her building, and she wanted it known, without saying so, that the building was hers.

"Any kind," Dale said. He looked at her directly. He had the courtesy, at least, of taking her seriously. "Ma'am, what's the structural load rating on this roof?"

"It's held up since 1979," Madge said.

"That's not a rating."

"No," Madge agreed. "It's a track record."

Dale considered this. He appeared to respect it. He and Kenny took seats on the south end of the bleachers, near the deep end, which Madge noted with the quiet interest of a woman who knew that the deep end was the furthest point from the water's nearest edge and that men who sat there, whether they knew it or not, had chosen distance.

The elderly Maoists arrived at 3:22 p.m.

There were five of them. They entered in silence.

Madge did not hear the lobby door. She did not hear footsteps. She became aware of them the way the pool became aware of a change in barometric pressure: not through any single sensory event but through a shift in the room's total equilibrium: a densification, as though the air near the bleacher entrance had become more serious than the air around it.

They were old. The youngest was seventy-three. The eldest was eighty-six and moved with the unsettling precision of a person who had either been a dancer or a soldier, and who regarded the distinction between the two as bourgeois. Her name was Fay. She wore a cardigan that was the color of nothing—not gray, not beige, not cream, but the specific absence of color that certain garments achieved after decades of washing in the service of more important concerns than appearance. She sat in the north section of the bleachers, closest to the deep end's edge, and she looked at the water with an expression that Madge could not read, which was unusual, because Madge could read almost anyone's expression around water, and which therefore interested her.

The other four arranged themselves around Fay in a formation that was either protective or geometric. They communicated in hand signals. Madge had been watching people gesture at one another in this building for forty years—swim instructors, lifeguards, parents shouting at children across the acoustic void of the pool—and she had never seen a gestural vocabulary this dense. The signals were small, economical, performed close to the body. One of them—a flat palm rotated ninety degrees and then pushed forward, thumb extended—was repeated three times by the man sitting behind Fay, and each time it produced a slight nod from the woman to his left and a sharp lateral headshake from the man to his right, which suggested that the gesture meant something upon which reasonable people could disagree.
