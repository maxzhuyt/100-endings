# The Tower and the Five

---

## I.

The letter was in her handwriting. That was the first thing, and the last thing, and the thing Maret could not stop returning to the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

She had found it on the table beside her mortar and pestle, tucked beneath a bunch of dried catmint as though it had always been there, as though letters routinely appeared in the homes of village herbalists who had not received mail in eleven years. The seal was unmarked wax the color of old honey. The paper was the kind they had used at the academy — not parchment but a fibrous, faintly warm sheet that the tower had grown from its own walls, a trick of architecture that she had not thought about since the century turned. She recognized it the way she might recognize the smell of a room she'd slept in as a child: completely, involuntarily, with a grief that began in the body and only later reached the mind.

She put it down.

She ground the catmint. She decanted the tincture she had promised old Veran for his joints, which were swollen again, which were always swollen, because tinctures were what she did now and tinctures were adequate and adequacy was the shape her life had taken and she did not regret it.

She picked it up.

*You will come back now. The halls have contracted around your absence long enough and the hearthfire remembers the specific temperature of your hands. I am not asking because I have already exhausted asking. I am telling you what you will do because I have seen you do it, because you are already doing it, because the part of you that left never arrived anywhere else.*

The phrasing was hers. Not approximately — precisely. It carried her particular cadence, the way she built sentences when she was too tired to soften them, the clipped maternal authority she used on patients who refused to rest. She would have written these words exactly, in this order, if she had been desperate enough. If she had been something she was not.

*Bring your hands. I have kept what they are owed.*

She put it down.

Her hands were steady. They had been steady for thirty-seven years, ever since she had walked out of the tower and sworn them to a smaller service. Herbalism. Poultices. The body's own slow repair. Not the other thing — the thing that happened when she laid her palms on ruined flesh and felt the wound's architecture, its load-bearing walls of pain, and dismantled it from the inside. She had sworn that off the way some people swear off drink: utterly, without drama, because the alternative was not moderation but annihilation.

Her hands remembered anyway. They had begun to warm the moment she touched the paper. A low, banked heat, like coals buried in ash, patient and total.

She picked it up.

She read it again. She read it as a reunion notice, because that was the only sane reading, because the alternative was that the tower — a building, an institution, a place made of stone and old magic and the memory of ten thousand lessons — had reached into her throat and pulled out her voice and used it to call her home. Buildings did not do that. Buildings did not have needs, or memories, or the particular cruelty of writing to someone in their own words and their own weakness.

She folded the letter and placed it in the pocket of her apron, against her hip, where it burned softly like a coal that would not go out.

She told Veran's daughter that she would be gone for a few days. She said it was a personal matter. Her hands, as she latched the door of her cottage, were trembling for the first time in thirty-seven years, and she thought: *I would have said it that way. If I were dying, I would have used exactly those words.*

She did not yet understand that this was the point.

---

## II.

She had walked this road every night for forty years in her sleep and knew that the third cobblestone past the broken fountain would be loose.

Solenne stepped over it without breaking stride. The dream and the waking had finally merged, and the feeling was not revelation but exhaustion, the particular tiredness of a woman who arrives at a destination she has been unable to stop visiting. She carried nothing. She had seen what she would need, and it was nothing.

---

Davan crossed the border at the place where the border had been.

There was no marker now. The river was the same river — the Sildt, low and brown, crossable on foot in late summer — but the kingdoms it had divided were gone. Torenmarch had collapsed into its own succession crisis forty years ago; what remained called itself a federation of communes and had no use for a war-caster's services or a war-caster's guilt. The other side, Vess, had simply stopped. No war, no famine, no dramatic fall: the last Vessine duke had died without issue and the people had shrugged and absorbed themselves into the neighboring provinces, and that was that. Two kingdoms, unmade not by conquest but by the quiet withdrawal of the energy that had held them in their shapes. Davan had fought for both sides at different times, which was the kind of thing you could do when your voice could shatter a cavalry charge at two hundred paces and your loyalty was to the institution that trained you rather than to any king who rented you.

He waded the Sildt. The water came to his thighs. It was cold, and his knees ached, and his boots — plain leather, a farmer's boots, which is what he was now, a farmer, a man who grew barley and spoke to no one in the register that could shatter walls — would take two days to dry.

On the far bank, he stopped and looked back at the country that was no longer a country. The light was the dull amber of late afternoon, and it lay on the water the way light lies on things that no longer matter. He had killed men in this river. He had killed a boy of perhaps seventeen very near this exact spot, a Vessine pikeman who had slipped on a wet stone and gone down on one knee, and Davan had spoken a word that collapsed the boy's breastplate inward like a crushed cup. He remembered the sound — not his voice but the metal. A high, surprised crumpling. He had not thought about it in decades and here it was, whole and precise, called up by the feel of river stones under his boots.

He climbed the far bank and walked south toward the academy. His letter, which was in his handwriting and contained phrases he recognized from orders he had given in the field — terse, imperative, the grammar of a man accustomed to being obeyed under fire — was folded inside his coat. He had not shown it to his wife. He had told her he was visiting an old colleague. She had looked at him with the expression of a woman who has been married to a quiet man for twenty-two years and knows exactly which silences contain lies, and she had packed him bread and cheese and said nothing.

---

Liss passed through Arenny on the second day and noticed it the way you notice a sound that should be present and is not — birdsong in a forest, the hum of bees near a garden. She noticed the *absence*.

There were children in the streets. Of course there were; Arenny was a market town, full of families, full of noise. Three girls were playing a clapping game near the tanner's shop. A boy was hauling water from the well with the exaggerated seriousness of a child performing adulthood. An infant howled from an upper window. Ordinary children. Adequate children.

None of them had it.

Liss had been an enchanter. Her gift had been beauty — the construction of illusions so persuasive they could make a hungry crowd feel fed, a frightened court feel brave, a dying king feel the warm pressure of legacy settling around his shoulders like a robe. She had built these beautiful lies for the court of Torenmarch, and the court had used them to pacify a province that was starving. She had made the starving feel full. She had made them grateful for it. When she understood what she had done, she left. She did not dismantle the enchantments; that would have required going back, and going back would have meant seeing the faces of the people she had lied to, and she was not strong enough for that, and the enchantments eventually frayed on their own, and the province eventually rioted, and she eventually heard about it and did not sleep for a year.

She knew what a child looked like when the gift was in them. A particular quality of attention — not intelligence but *porousness*, a way of being in the world that was less like standing in a room and more like being the room. She had seen it in herself at seven, in each of the Five at various ages, in students at the academy who arrived knock-kneed and frightened and radiant with potential.

The children of Arenny were not porous. They were solid. They were opaque in the way of a window painted over — the light should have come through, and didn't, and the window itself did not know what it was missing.

She counted them as she walked. Fourteen children in the market square. By the old distribution — roughly one in thirty born with some degree of magical vocation — there should have been perhaps one, perhaps none, and the absence of a gift in any given child meant nothing. But Liss was not counting one square. She had been walking for six days through villages and towns, and she had not seen it once. Not once in six days, in a countryside where she should have seen it a dozen times. The children were fine. The children were healthy and loud and grubby and ordinary, and they had been *emptied so gently they did not know they had ever been full*, and Liss walked through Arenny with her hands shaking inside her cloak and her letter — written in her handwriting and containing an enchanter's vocabulary of beauty, precision, and the precise shade of self-loathing that only a liar of genius can produce — burning against her breast.

---

By the time Maret arrived at the tower, the fifth of them was already inside.

She knew because the door was open. Not ajar — open in the way a mouth is open when it is mid-word, as though the tower had been speaking and had paused, waiting for the rest of its sentence to walk in.

The grounds were overgrown. The garden where they had practiced elemental theory was a thicket of blackthorn and wild rose, and the practice yard where Davan had learned to pitch his voice at stone was a lake of standing water reflecting a sky the color of tin. The tower stood at the center of it all, unchanged and entirely changed, the way a person's face is unchanged by grief but entirely different.

It was taller than she remembered. That was wrong — it should have been the same height, or shorter if anything, because things from childhood and youth are always smaller upon return. But the tower was taller. It rose into the low clouds and did not stop, or perhaps it did stop and the clouds had lowered themselves to meet it, and the effect was the same: a structure that no longer had a visible top, that extended upward into obscurity, that tapered and vanished the way a thought vanishes when you try to hold it.

The stone was dark. It had been pale once — a warm sandstone, golden in afternoon light — but now it was the color of a bruise in its later stages, a mottled gray-violet that seemed less like discoloration and more like circulation. Like blood moving beneath skin that was too thin.

Maret stood at the threshold. The door — oak, iron-banded, carved with the old sigils of the five disciplines — was open. The air that came from inside was warm. Not comfortably warm. Warm the way a body is warm: specifically, metabolically, with the implication of a process ongoing.

She heard footsteps behind her. She did not turn.

"You got one too," Davan said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"In your handwriting."

"Yes."

A silence. She could feel him standing three paces behind her left shoulder, exactly where he had always stood, because he was left-handed and his voice projected rightward and they had learned in the first year of their intimacy to arrange themselves so that his power angled away from her. Thirty-seven years gone, and the body remembered. The body always remembered.

"Mine told me to bring my voice," he said. "It said the halls had been too quiet."

"Mine told me to bring my hands."

Solenne came around the far corner of the tower as though she had been there for hours, and perhaps she had. She looked at them with the calm, flattened expression of a woman who had foreseen this morning ten thousand times and was tired of it.

"The third cobblestone past the fountain," she said. "I told you about that, Maret. Twenty years before the fountain was even built, I told you a fountain would be built and a stone would come loose."

"I remember," Maret said, though she didn't.

Liss arrived last among the four of them, half-running, her cloak muddy, her face carrying something Maret did not recognize until she looked more closely: not fear but the specific pallor of someone who has been counting a loss and has only now arrived at the total.

"The children," Liss said. "On the road. In the towns. Did any of you—"

"Yes," Solenne said. "We know."

"You *know*?"

Solenne said nothing. She looked at the open door.

"Oren is already inside," she said. "He arrived three days ago. He has been speaking with the hearthfire."

"Speaking with—"

"We should go in."

Maret crossed the threshold first because her hands were already reaching for it, and the warmth that rose up through her boots at the first step was so familiar that she had to stop and press her palm against the wall, which yielded slightly under her touch, like skin over something breathing, and she said, very quietly, to no one: "Oh. You're alive. You're still alive."

The wall pulsed once under her hand.

## III.

The corridors had changed.

Not in the way buildings change through renovation or decay — through *intention*. The hallway that had once connected the entrance to the great study was narrower now, the walls closer together, and the narrowing was not uniform but organic, as though the passage had contracted like a throat around a swallow. The ceiling was lower. The stones, which had once been fitted with mathematical precision, had shifted subtly, adjusting to some new structural logic that had nothing to do with architecture and everything to do with anatomy.

Maret walked with her hand still on the wall. She could not bring herself to lift it away. Beneath her palm, the stone was warm and faintly textured, not rough but *grained*, the way living tissue is grained, with a directionality that suggested purpose, flow, the movement of something essential through hidden channels. She felt, in the tips of her fingers, a rhythm she did not identify as a pulse until the third repetition.

"It's slower than it should be," she said.

"For a building?" Davan asked, from behind her.

"For anything alive."

They moved inward. The light was wrong — not torchlight, not magelight, but a dim bioluminescence that seemed to emanate from the walls themselves, a faint amber glow that shifted as they moved, brightening ahead and dimming behind, so that they walked always at the center of a small halo of visibility while the corridor behind them sank into a darkness that felt less like absence of light and more like the closing of an eye.
