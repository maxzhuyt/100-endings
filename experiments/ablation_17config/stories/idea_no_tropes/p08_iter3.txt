# The Nothing at the Edge

## I.

The ground here turns to light.

I need you to understand this first, before the schedule, before Maret, before any of it. I need you to see the planet as it is. At the perimeter of Settlement Koreva — two hundred and eleven souls, thirty-one years of habitation, grid layout of ninety-six polymer shelters arranged in eight rows of twelve — the earth begins to glow. Not suddenly. Not like a fire catching. The way a word you've said too many times starts to lose its meaning: gradually, then completely. First the soil brightens, as though something beneath it has woken up for the first time and is shining upward through a thinning skin. Then the texture goes. Dirt that your boot could grip becomes smooth, then smoother still, then not quite solid. Then translucent. You can see through it the way you can see through a pane of amber, and what you see beneath is more light, deeper light, a luminescence that has no floor. And then the ground isn't ground anymore. It is radiance without source, brightness without heat, a shining so total it becomes a kind of absence. You reach the place where your foot would pass through what was earth and meet nothing — not a hole, not a void, but a nothing so bright it burns after-shapes into your vision for hours.

The edge is beautiful. I want you to hold that.

Settlement Koreva sits on a diminishing island of solid ground. The dissolution moves inward at a rate that no one has ever reliably measured — I will return to this — and we live in the shrinking center, on the matter that remains, arranging and rearranging ourselves according to a schedule that has governed every waking hour of our collective life for three decades.

The schedule is etched into polymer boards that hang at every third shelter junction. I could recite it the way I was taught, which is the way everyone was taught, which is the way the elders speak it at the start of each new cycle: without inflection, without emphasis, as though the sequence of tasks were not a human invention but a property of the universe, as fundamental and as inarguable as the fact that the ground is disappearing.

Cycle begins at first brightening — the planet has no sun; the sky lightens on its own, sourceless, the way the ground lights at the edge, and no one speaks about this parallel, though I hear them think about it in the hours when they cannot sleep. At first brightening: filtration crews to stations one through six. Water is drawn from the deep wells at the settlement's center, where the ground is oldest and most solid, and it is passed through polymer screens that remove the luminous particulate — tiny motes of brightness that appear in the water as though the planet is dissolving from inside as well as from the edges. Filtration is a six-person task. Maret has served at Station Three for nine years.

Following filtration: structural assessment. Six teams of four walk the grid, testing the polymer joints of each shelter with calibrated torsion instruments. The shelters do not often fail. The assessment is performed anyway, every cycle, without exception, because the schedule requires it and the schedule is not a set of practical responses to material conditions. The schedule is the settlement's theory of the universe. This is what I need you to understand. The schedule is our answer to the light.

The elders — there are four, two of the original settlers, two appointed by consensus a decade ago — determined in the first year of habitation that the dissolution of the ground was responsive to collective human behavior. Not to any measurable input. Not to weight or pressure or chemical interaction. To *behavior*. To the synchrony and diligence with which the settlement performed its tasks. The claim, as Elder Panu articulated it in Year One, was this: the planet's matter persists in proportion to the coherence of the community living on it. Disunity, idleness, arrhythmia in the collective pattern — these accelerate the dissolution. Precision, coordination, shared effort — these slow it.

There was no evidence for this. I say it plainly because I have always known it plainly and because the plainness of it has never diminished its power over two hundred and eleven minds. There was no experiment. There was no control. In Year One, the dissolution was slow, and the settlers were diligent, and Elder Panu, who had the kind of mind that could not tolerate an unexplained phenomenon the way a tongue cannot leave a broken tooth alone, drew a line between the two facts and called it causation. By Year Three, the schedule was codified. By Year Five, it was compulsory. By Year Ten, it was no longer experienced as compulsory. It was experienced as *true*.

After structural assessment: polymer maintenance, nutrient allocation, waste processing, soil monitoring at the mid-perimeter, child instruction, tool repair, second filtration, third filtration, communal meal, post-meal calibration review, evening monitoring, cot time. Every hour named. Every transition marked by a low tone generated from the polymer resonance posts the elders installed at the grid's four corners. The settlement breathes in and out on this rhythm, ninety-six shelters contracting and expanding like a single lung, and the ground beneath it continues — with neither haste nor hesitation, with neither malice nor mercy — to become light.

I have described this the way it was taught to me because this is how it lives in me: as catechism, as the first and most persistent architecture of my mind. Before I knew what I know, before the shard, I was a child learning the schedule the way a child learns the sounds that make up its own name, and some part of me still recites it in the hours before I sleep, the way someone might finger a smooth stone from a place they can no longer return to. I do not believe it. I have not believed it for a very long time. But I cannot stop hearing it in the voice it was first given to me — the voice of my mother, who did believe, who believed with such totality that her believing was the most solid thing I knew in a world where solidity itself was the exception.

The ground turns to light. We respond with order. The order does not slow the light. We maintain the order anyway. This is the settlement. This is everything I have to tell you before I tell you about her.

## II.

When I was seven years old, I swallowed a piece of the planet's heart.

I tell you this now the way I might tell you about a wound that never healed but that I have long since learned to carry — with a kind of practiced steadiness that should not be mistaken for ease. I was at the mid-perimeter, where children were not permitted, during the ninety minutes of tool repair when my assigned minder, Gessel, had fallen asleep over a polymer wrench. I had walked to the edge because children walk to edges. The ground at the mid-perimeter was already thinning — you could see the brightness working through it, veins of light threading the soil the way lightning threads a cloud from inside — and at one point where the surface had cracked and peeled back, there was a shard. I have no better word for it. A splinter of the planet's luminous substrate, the layer beneath the layer beneath the ground we built on, exposed and shining with a light that did not flicker or pulse but simply *was*, with a constancy I had never seen in any other thing. It was the size of my smallest finger. It was warm.

What I did next I cannot fully explain to you, and I will not falsify an explanation to make the telling more comfortable. I did not think: *I will eat this*. I did not think anything articulable. I was seven, and the shard was bright, and my hand was already reaching, and then it was in my mouth — warm, and sharp enough to cut, and tasting of nothing I can compare to any other taste, though if you made me try I would say it was like the taste of a word you know is true before you understand what it means — and I swallowed it whole, and it cut me going down, and I bled from the inside for two days, and I told no one because the blood was not red but faintly luminous, and even at seven I understood that luminous blood was not something that would be met with comfort.

The perception began on the third day.

I will try to be precise. What the shard did to me — what it does to me, present tense, twenty-three years later, with no diminishment and no acclimation — is this: I perceive the interior lives of every person within a radius I have never been able to determine because I have never been far enough from other people to find its limit. I perceive them constantly. Not as thoughts in language, or not only. As *states*. As weather. As the full, churning, unedited tumult of what a person silently is while they are publicly being something else. I hear the fear beneath the competence. The resentment beneath the obedience. The question beneath the certainty. I hear it the way you hear a room full of people all speaking at once — not each voice distinctly at all times, but the totality always, and any single voice available if I turn my attention to it, the way you might isolate a single instrument in an orchestra if you knew what to listen for.

For twenty-three years, I have heard Settlement Koreva thinking.

I have heard Elder Panu's successor, Elder Venn, rehearse in the small hours of every morning a defense of the schedule that she delivers to no one — a monologue addressed to a version of herself that she imagines asking *but what if it doesn't work?*, and the monologue is detailed and persuasive and slightly different each time, as though she is honing an argument for a trial that is always approaching and never arrives. I have heard Torrin, who maintains the polymer resonance posts, sing fragments of a song from whatever world his parents came from, a song he does not remember learning and cannot complete, and I have felt the particular grief of the incomplete — the way the melody breaks off and leaves him standing in a silence that is worse than any sound. I have heard children dream. I have heard the dying clarify. I have heard, in the aggregate, the full and unspeakable weight of two hundred and eleven people's private bargains with a reality that is dissolving beneath them, and I have said nothing about any of it, to anyone, ever.

This requires explanation.

I did not stay silent out of nobility. I did not stay silent out of fear, though I am afraid, and have been for as long as I can remember. I stayed silent because what I perceived, from the very beginning, was that the settlement's coherence — its capacity to get up each morning and perform the schedule and maintain the shared fiction that the schedule was doing something — depended entirely on the privacy of each person's doubt. The doubt was universal. I knew this by my eighth year. Every person in Settlement Koreva harbored some version of the suspicion that the schedule was meaningless. And every person believed themselves to be nearly alone in this suspicion. And it was precisely this belief — *I am one of the few who doubts, and therefore I must keep doubting quietly and comply, because if the others believe, I should not be the one to break what sustains them* — that kept the system intact. The settlement was a circle of people each protecting the others from a knowledge they all already possessed. If I had spoken — if I had said, *everyone doubts, the doubt is universal, the schedule is a shared performance that no one fully believes but everyone maintains for the sake of everyone else* — the circle would have broken. And I did not know what would happen then. And I was seven, and then eleven, and then nineteen, and then thirty, and at no point in those years did I find the courage or the certainty to break a thing that, whatever else it was, kept people waking up in the morning.

So I became the settlement's silent witness. Its living record. The one who knows what every face conceals and reports nothing.

And when I tell you about Maret — when I recount what she did and what I perceived in her and what it meant — I tell it the way someone tells the story of a saint or a catastrophe, because I am not able to tell it any other way. I have carried the sound of two hundred and eleven minds for most of my life, and in all that time, until Maret, I never heard anything like silence.

## III.

Let me show you what I hear.

Not on the morning Maret stopped. Before that. During the long, indistinguishable cycles that preceded it, the years of pattern, the texture of ordinary time in Settlement Koreva. I want you to hear the chorus before you hear the silence, so you understand what the silence broke.

A cycle. Any cycle. The same.

Elder Venn rises fourteen minutes before the brightening tone. She is sixty-three. Her knees articulate with small sounds she has decided not to investigate. She dresses — polymer-weave tunic, polymer-weave trousers, boots that she resoled herself last quarter because the cobblers' queue is long and she will not ask for priority, because asking for priority would be an admission that her body is failing, and her body cannot fail because she is the schedule's primary voice and the schedule requires her to stand at Post One at first brightening and her standing there is — she believes this, she *has to* believe this — part of what keeps the ground solid.

And beneath this, in the part of her mind she visits only when she is alone and the brightening tone has not yet sounded: *If the ground dissolves regardless. If thirty-one years of rising early, of standing straight, of enforcing every deviation with calm and measured correction, have been thirty-one years of pantomime, then what she is, is a woman who spent her life maintaining a fiction so thoroughly that she became the fiction, and there is nothing beneath the role, no self that exists outside the schedule's structure, and the dissolution that matters is not the planet's.*

She does not complete this thought. She has never completed it. She routes around it the way water routes around stone — automatically, at the level of reflex, the thought shunted aside before it can reach its conclusion. I have watched her do this thousands of times. The reflex is so practiced it is nearly invisible even to me. Nearly. Not entirely. There is a tiny hitch, a momentary stutter in the flow of her interior life, like a skipped heartbeat, and then she is thinking about the cycle's task allocations and the hitch is gone and Elder Venn is Elder Venn again, upright and certain, the schedule's keeper, her doubt bundled so tightly and shelved so deeply that it functionally does not exist. Until tomorrow. When the same fourteen minutes will play out again. With the same hitch.

Torrin, at Resonance Post Four, testing the tone generator's polymer membrane with a small padded mallet. Twenty-eight. Born in the settlement. Has never seen the edge up close because his mother told him, when he was four years old, that the light at the edge calls people, and that the call sounds like the thing you want most, and that you must never listen. He believed her then. He believes her now. Not rationally — his rational mind, which is precise and mechanically gifted, understands that the edge is a physical phenomenon and does not *call* — but in the deeper place where childhood warnings live, in the place where knowledge and belief are not the same system. He tests the membrane. The tone is correct. He logs it. He does this every cycle. The tone is always correct. He tests it anyway.

And beneath: *If I walked to the edge. If I just walked there and listened. Would I hear it.* Not a desire. A curiosity so carefully sealed inside its own containment that it has the quality of an artifact — something preserved under glass, looked at occasionally, never touched. Torrin is not a rebel. Torrin is not even particularly restless. Torrin is a man with a single forbidden thought that he maintains the way he maintains the resonance posts: with diligence, with routine, by visiting it at the same time each cycle and confirming that it is still there and still contained and still not leaking into anything actionable. I have listened to Torrin tend this thought for years. It has not grown. It has not diminished. It sits in him like the shard sits in me.

Kanna, at Shelter Row Six, waking her children. She is thirty-four. Her son, Emit, is twelve and performs structural assessment with a diligence that makes the team leads smile. Her daughter, Lunn, is eight and is beginning to ask questions about the edge that Kanna cannot answer. Kanna gets her children up. She checks their tunics. She hands them their ration bars and watches them eat and reminds Emit to report to Assessment Team Two and reminds Lunn to stay with the instruction group and she does this with the same mechanical tenderness she does everything, which is to say with love, with genuine love, and also with a terror so deep and so architectural that it has become the foundation of her love rather than its contradiction.
