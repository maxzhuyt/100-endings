# The Parlor Game

## I.

Look, I'm doing the whole Agatha Christie thing, and I need you to just go with it.

I understand the impulse to resist. You've been burned before—someone opens a story by winking at you, and by page three they're so busy being clever about storytelling that they forget to tell the story, and you're left holding a narrative that keeps gesturing at its own reflection like a cockatiel that's discovered a mirror. I get it. I do. That's not what this is. Or maybe it is, but I'm going to be useful about it. I'm going to find who did it. I'm going to sit them in a wingback chair and I'm going to look them in their lying face and I'm going to say, *I know what you've done*, and it's going to be magnificent. You're going to want to be here for that.

But first: competitive pub trivia is a sport.

Shut up. It is.

I will argue this point to your face. I will cite the hours of preparation—the flashcard systems, the signal-calling, the division of knowledge into sectors the way NATO divides airspace. I will cite the strategy: when to wager, when to sandbag, when to sacrifice a round in a lesser category to protect your lead going into the final. I will cite the injuries, because Marcus once dislocated his thumb slamming the answer buzzer during a speed round about the moons of Jupiter, and he still has the medical bill, which his insurance categorized under "recreational sports incident," so actually Blue Cross Blue Shield agrees with me and you can take it up with them.

My team is called the Feral Almanacs.

We have matching shirts. They're heather green, which was a group decision I opposed because heather green is nobody's color and several of us have complexions that skew toward the olive family, but democracy is democracy and I believe in the process even when it makes my teammates look mildly jaundiced under pub lighting. The shirts say FERAL ALMANACS across the chest in a serif font, and underneath, in smaller letters: *We know things. Unpleasant things.* Jordan designed them. Jordan designs everything. Jordan once spent thirty-five minutes selecting a font for a grocery list, and the grocery list had four items on it, and two of the items were "milk."

We have finished last in our league for nine consecutive seasons.

Nine. Consecutive. Seasons.

I want you to sit with that number. I want you to feel its texture. Nine is not a streak you stumble into. Nine is a commitment. Nine is a lifestyle. Nine is the kind of number that makes you wonder whether the universe is trying to tell you something and then makes you realize you don't care because it's Tuesday and you've got somewhere to be. Nine seasons of bottom-of-the-standings. Nine seasons of watching Quiz Pro Quo—yes, that's the rival team's name; yes, they think it's clever; no, it isn't—collect their trophy while we sit in the back left corner of the Ploughman's Rest and eat curly fries we didn't earn.

But season ten. Season ten was going to be different.

Season ten was different, actually, in the same way that getting hit by a bus is different from getting hit by a car. Technically a new experience. Not the kind you'd seek out.

Someone on my team leaked our championship-round answers to Quiz Pro Quo. Someone sitting at my table, wearing a heather-green shirt with the serif font, took the questions we'd war-gamed and the answers we'd battle-tested and handed them to a team of polo-shirt-wearing, craft-beer-sponsorship-having, smugness-exuding pub trivia mercenaries who would not know loyalty if loyalty sat on their faces.

I'm going to find out who.

I've already rented the wingback chairs.

Two of them, from a theatrical prop company in Swindon. Eighty quid for the week, plus a forty-pound delivery surcharge, plus whatever my dignity is worth, which at this point is a negative number so arguably they owe me. They're burgundy. They smell like dust and old velvet and someone else's production of *The Mousetrap*. They are perfect. They are the most perfect things I have ever rented.

I have a notebook. I have a pen—a fountain pen, because commitment to a bit is a moral position. I have index cards on which I have written my suspects' names and beneath each name a single question tailored to crack them open like an egg.

What I do not have is a magnifying glass, because I looked it up and Poirot never actually uses one and I'm not about to confuse my Christies.

So. Here's what happened.

Oh—one more thing. You should know that trivia night is my Tuesday thing. My anchor, I mean. The thing I do on Tuesdays now instead of the thing I used to do on Tuesdays, which was different and which I don't do anymore and which isn't relevant to the investigation so let's move on.

Ready?

Good. I need you.

## II.

The Ploughman's Rest Quiz League Championship takes place the third Tuesday of November. It has taken place the third Tuesday of November for eleven years, ever since Pete Reeves—the quizmaster, the institution, the only person in four counties who can pronounce "Eyjafjallajökull" correctly on the first try *and* tell you its eruption date *and* hold a room of forty drunk people in complete silence while he reads the question—since Pete Reeves founded the league out of what I believe was a combination of genuine intellectual passion and a desire to make his pub the only establishment between here and Cheltenham that does reliable midweek traffic.

The format: five rounds of ten questions each. Cumulative scoring. Categories announced in advance for rounds one through four so teams can strategize. Round five—the Championship Round—is a wildcard. No advance category. No prep advantage. Just whatever Pete Reeves has decided to pull from the cabinet of his gorgeous, encyclopedic, wrinkly-in-a-Sean-Connery-way brain.

Here's how the night was supposed to go.

Weeks before the championship, we'd identified our four prep categories: British monarchs (strong—that's Priya's domain, she can recite the full line of succession backward while doing the washing up), Olympic host cities (solid—Marcus has a mnemonic system involving body parts that I will not describe to you because I respect your appetite), twentieth-century inventions (covered—Jordan, obviously, who knows everything about everything and will tell you about it at a length that makes you question both the concept of friendship and the heat death of the universe), and rivers of the world (mine—I'm rivers, I've always been rivers, the Danube runs through my veins and the Yangtze is my spinal column and if you come at me with a question about tributaries I will end you). That left round five as the wild card: unprepped, instinct only, the chaos round, the round where Lena's weird knowledge of 1970s cinema and Tomasz's photographic memory for flags usually saves us or at minimum prevents total humiliation.

We were ready. We were so ready. I had flashcards. Marcus had his body-part mnemonics. Jordan had prepared a seven-page document—seven pages, typeset, with footnotes and a bibliography—covering the complete history of the incandescent lightbulb, because Jordan had a "feeling" about the lightbulb, and I'd long ago learned that Jordan's feelings were worth trusting because Jordan's feelings were not feelings at all but a pattern-recognition algorithm housed inside a person who once forgot to eat for two days because they were researching Baltic shipping routes.

The night of the championship, we arrived at the Ploughman's Rest at 6:45 p.m.—fifteen minutes before doors. Standard operating procedure. You want table position (back left corner, sight lines to the scoreboard, proximity to the toilets for nervous-bladder management), and you want ten minutes of quiet before the room fills up, to run final-round drills and make sure nobody—nobody—has had more than one pint before Round 1. One pint is the threshold. One pint loosens the associative cortex. Two pints loosens the tongue. Three pints is how you end up answering "What is the capital of Mongolia?" with "Is it Jeff?" which happened in Season 4 and which I will never forgive Marcus for even though technically it was my fault for not enforcing the threshold.

We settled in. We were calm. We were focused. We were a well-oiled, heather-green machine.

Then Quiz Pro Quo walked in.

You need to understand Quiz Pro Quo. I need you to really see them. They are six people—always six, because league rules cap rosters at six and some teams carry four or five because not everyone can commit to every Tuesday, but Quiz Pro Quo always fields the full six because they have the kind of lives that allow for total schedule control, by which I mean they are the kind of people who have never had to choose between pub trivia and a double shift or a sick kid or a broken boiler. They wear matching polo shirts—not homemade, not screen-printed on a budget, but *actual embroidered polos* from some corporate fulfillment service, navy blue with their team name stitched in gold thread on the breast pocket. They have a *logo*. It's a gavel crossed with a quill. It looks like an emblem for a law firm that defends war criminals. Their captain is a man named Gavin who introduces himself as "Gav" and has never, in the entire recorded history of the Ploughman's Rest Quiz League, bought a round for anyone outside his own team. Not once. Not even on Christmas quiz night, when Pete Reeves wears the Santa hat and everyone—*everyone*—gets into the spirit. Not Gavin. Gavin sips his half-pint of low-alcohol lager and smiles the smile of a man who peaked in middle management and found it satisfying.

Quiz Pro Quo has won the championship four of the last five years. They are good. I hate them. Both of these things are true simultaneously and I will not apologize for either.

Round 1: British monarchs. We'd prepped this. Priya was locked in—she was answering before Pete finished reading, which is technically illegal but Pete lets it slide because he respects conviction. We went 9 for 10. Missed one on the exact year of Edward VIII's abdication—Priya had it as '36, which was right, but she second-guessed herself and wrote '37 because Marcus made a face, and I swear to God, Marcus, if your face ever interferes with Priya's instincts again I will remove it from your skull and hang it on my wall.

9 for 10. Strong. One point back of Quiz Pro Quo, who went perfect. Fine. Fine. They always start strong. We'd mapped this.

Round 2: Olympic host cities. Marcus. His domain. His body-part system firing on all cylinders. He touched his left kneecap for Seoul, his right ear for Munich, his sternum for Mexico City—I told you I wouldn't describe this and I'm already describing it, I'm sorry, it's disgusting and effective. We went 10 for 10. Perfect round. Tied with Quiz Pro Quo at 19 apiece.

And this is where it started.

Round 3: twentieth-century inventions. Jordan's round. Jordan had prepped this the way other people prep for surgery—meticulous, redundant, no margin for error. We had anticipated the lightbulb, the transistor, the polio vaccine, the microchip, velcro, the internet-precursor question that always shows up because Pete loves asking about ARPANET, the nylon question, the penicillin question that's technically not twentieth century but which Pete always frames as "mass production of" to make it fit.

We had the answers.

Quiz Pro Quo had the same answers.

Not similar answers. The *same* answers. Word-for-word on three of the ten questions—not paraphrased, not arrived at independently, *identical*, down to Jordan's habit of writing "Fleming (Alexander, not Ian)" in parentheses after the penicillin answer because Jordan once lost a half-point on a technicality and has carried that wound ever since.

Do you understand what I'm telling you?

"Fleming (Alexander, not Ian)" is not a phrase that exists independently in nature. It is a Jordan phrase. It is a fingerprint. It is DNA. And it was on Quiz Pro Quo's answer sheet.

We went 10 for 10. They went 10 for 10. But the air had changed. I could feel it. Priya could feel it. Jordan was staring at the scoreboard with the expression of someone who has just discovered a window open in a room they locked.

Round 4: rivers of the world. My round. My rivers.

Reader, they had my rivers.

Not all of them. But the hard ones. The ones I'd specifically drilled because they were *my* obscure picks, my edges, the questions I'd flagged in our prep document as "likely Pete selections based on his documented fondness for African geography and his tendency to cycle back to questions about confluences." They knew the Kasai River was a tributary of the Congo. They knew the Irrawaddy's historical name. They knew—and this is the one that cracked me open—they knew the answer to a question about the Tigris-Euphrates confluence point, and they gave the exact answer I had written in my prep notes, including the parenthetical "(also called Shatt al-Arab)" which is *my* notation, *my* spelling preference, because I spent twenty minutes deciding on the transliteration and I chose that one and it was mine and they *had* it.

We went 10 for 10. They went 10 for 10. Tied going into the Championship Round.

Between rounds, there's a ten-minute break. Pete announces it, people get up, use the toilet, buy drinks, mill around. Standard chaos. I went to the bar for a soda water. When I came back to our table, my prep binder—a three-ring binder, blue, with a label on the spine that says "SEASON 10 — CLASSIFIED" because I am who I am—was on the table where I'd left it. But the pages were in the wrong order.

I keep my binder organized by round, tabbed with color-coded dividers. Rivers is green. Monarchs is red. Inventions is yellow. Olympic cities is orange. They were in order when I left the table. When I came back, yellow was in front of green. Someone had opened the binder, removed pages, and replaced them—but in the wrong sequence.

This is not paranoia. This is tab-color forensics. The tabs do not lie.

Round 5: wildcard. Pete chose Greek mythology, which is nobody's strong category and Lena's chaotic wheelhouse, and we went 7 for 10, which in any other circumstance would have been respectable, survivable, even triumphant given that Lena pulled the answer on the Twelve Labors of Heracles from what she described as "a dream I had once, maybe"—but Quiz Pro Quo went 9 for 10. They knew the name of Odysseus's dog. They knew the specific punishment of Tantalus with phrasing that didn't match any standard reference I could find. They were reading from a script.

Final score: Feral Almanacs, 46. Quiz Pro Quo, 48.

Two points. Season ten. The season that was supposed to be different.

I sat in the back left corner of the Ploughman's Rest and did not eat curly fries. I sat there and I looked at my teammates and I thought: one of you did this. One of you wearing this heather-green shirt, this serif font, this stupid little motto—one of you took the thing we built and handed it to Gavin.

And I'm going to find out who.

Let me tell you about the suspects.

There are five of them. Six-person team, minus me—because I am not a suspect, I am Poirot, I am the detective, I am the center, and no, I do not think it's possible that I'm unreliable, thank you for asking, please sit down.
