# The Six-Mile Gap

Nine feet of snow was coming.

The storm that carried it had been born four days earlier in a place no one in the Nebraska Territory had a name for, a compression of Arctic air that had slid south across the Saskatchewan plains gathering mass and velocity until it became a thing visible from a hundred miles: a black wall, ruled straight along its base as though drawn with a carpenter's edge, rising to a height that abolished the concept of sky. It was visible now, on the northern horizon, at seven-fourteen in the morning, as the Marshal led her three deputies northwest out of the garrison town on horses whose ears were already pinned flat against the wind.

Nine feet. The snow would fall for nineteen hours. It would bury fences, root cellars, the shorter varieties of barn. Cattle that survived the initial whiteout would be found in the spring in standing positions, freeze-dried in mid-stride, their eyes open and white as church candles. Three people in Dawes County would die. This the narrator knows now, at the outset, as one knows the final page of a book already read. The Marshal did not know the number three. She suspected it was higher. She had ridden out to make it lower.

Her silence was the silence of organization. She rode at the front of the four-person column, and the set of her spine, the particular way she held the reins in her left hand and let her right hand rest on the saddlebag at her hip, communicated a single continuous thought so clearly that it was almost language: *sequence, sequence, sequence.* The saddlebag held a map she had drawn herself on butcher paper in an ink she mixed from walnut husks because the mercantile had been out of the commercial kind since October. On the map, every homestead in her jurisdiction was marked with a small, precise X. Next to each X, in handwriting so tidy it looked like an act of prayer, she had noted the family name, the number of occupants, the number and type of livestock, the distance from town, and a single letter — A, B, or C — representing her assessment of the structure's ability to survive what was coming. The map contained, in aggregate, the lives of forty-one people spread across approximately two hundred square miles of open grassland. She had ordered them by a calculus that accounted for distance, vulnerability, and a variable she did not have a name for but which she calculated instinctively, the way a carpenter calculates load: the likelihood that the occupants, upon seeing her, would do as she asked.

Behind her rode Aldersen, whose silence was his profession worn to a polish. He had been a deputy for nine years in three territories and his relationship to catastrophe was the relationship of a farrier to a horse — he saw the problem from the underside, where the work was. He did not look at the black wall on the horizon. He had looked at it once, in town, and had recorded it as a fact the way he recorded all facts: with total acceptance and absolutely no emotional response. His horse was the best of the four. He had seen to that.

Behind Aldersen rode Pell, whose silence was philosophical. He was the youngest and had read books, and the books had given him a tendency to arrange experience into shapes that meant something, and his mind was currently engaged in the pleasant, useless work of comparing the black wall to various things — a curtain, a judgment, the closing of a Bible. He was not unserious. He was, in fact, deeply serious, and his seriousness expressed itself in this compulsive search for metaphor, as though by finding the right comparison he could fit the storm inside a frame and hold it at the distance that a frame provides. He had not yet found the right comparison. The storm resisted framing.

Behind Pell rode Dunleavy.

Dunleavy's silence was built on top of a scream he was perpetually not screaming, had been not screaming for the better part of four years, and the scream had nothing to do with the weather.

Dunleavy was afraid of horses.

He was not mildly uncomfortable with horses, not vaguely nervous around horses, not the kind of man who might say, if speech had been possible, that horses made him "a little uneasy." Dunleavy was afraid of horses the way a rational person is afraid of standing on the roof of a tall building and feeling the wind push: the fear was specific, physical, rooted in the body's honest assessment that the situation was objectively dangerous, that he was sitting on top of a twelve-hundred-pound animal that could, at any moment and for any reason, decide to end the arrangement. The horse beneath him was named Brick. Brick had done nothing to earn Dunleavy's fear and was, by all measurable standards, a placid and well-mannered gelding who would not have harmed Dunleavy for any inducement short of fire. Dunleavy knew this. The knowing did not help. The knowing had never helped. The fear lived in his hands, which wanted to grip the reins too tightly, and in his thighs, which wanted to clamp against the horse's sides in a way that would communicate his panic to the animal and potentially cause the very disaster he feared, and every moment of every ride was an exercise in overriding his own body's instructions with a set of counter-instructions so detailed and constant that they amounted to a second, silent career. *Soften the fingers. Release the thigh. Breathe from the belly. You are not falling. You are not about to fall. The horse is not thinking about you. Soften the fingers again.*

He was, by a significant margin, the best rider of the four.

This was not a paradox. This was the inevitable consequence of a man paying more attention to the act of riding than any person who merely enjoyed it would ever think to pay. His posture was a masterpiece. His hands were a clinic. The Marshal, who knew about the fear because the Marshal knew about everything in her jurisdiction that might become a structural problem, had watched him ride for four years and had never once seen the scream. She had seen only the not-screaming, and she regarded it as one of the more impressive things a human being had ever done on a horse.

The wind was now strong enough to push a hat sideways on a head. It had been strong enough to steal speech since before they left town. The last words any of them had spoken had been the Marshal's, at the hitching post outside the office, when she had looked at each deputy in turn and said, "You know the list." They had nodded. She had mounted. That was the last human speech any of them would hear for some time.

They rode northwest into the wind, toward the farthest homestead, because the Marshal understood a principle so basic it was almost an insult to state it: you go to the farthest point first and work your way back, so that every subsequent stop brings you closer to safety rather than farther from it. You do not save the nearest and hope to reach the far. You run the math in reverse. She had explained this to the territorial governor once, in a letter he had not answered, using the phrase "the logic of retrieval." The governor was six hundred miles away in a building with a stove. The Marshal was riding into the only part of the arithmetic that mattered: the distance between the farthest X on her map and the advancing black wall that would, when it arrived, make all distances theoretical.

She touched the saddlebag. She did not open it. She had memorized the map so thoroughly that touching it was not reference but ritual, the way a priest touches the altar — not to check that it's there, but to receive something from the contact. Her hand stayed on the leather for three seconds, and in those three seconds the narrator, who sees such things, watched her run the full sequence one more time: the Proulx homestead first, nineteen miles out, two adults and an infant; then the Mikkelsen claim, fourteen miles, one adult male who would not want to leave his sod-breaking work; then the Fassbinder place, twelve miles, where an elderly woman lived alone with a cow; then the Hardings, ten miles, two adults and four children including the youngest, who was seven; then the Obrist homestead and the Lundeen homestead, which were close enough together to be treated as a single stop; then the long ride back. Each stop would cost time. How much time depended on variables she could estimate but not control — the willingness of each household to leave, the speed at which they could be loaded, the condition of the wagon road, which was not a road but a set of ruts that the first inch of snow would erase.

She was, in the quiet of her own calculations, already worried about the Fassbinder place. She did not yet articulate why, even to herself, but the letter on her map next to Fassbinder's X was the only D she had ever assigned in two years of the system. She had considered the standard grades — A through C, readiness to compliance — and found them insufficient. The D stood for a word she had not written down.

The black wall was patient. It neither advanced fast nor slow. It came at the speed of itself, which was the speed of something that had no interest in arrival, only in occupation, because it was going to fill every inch of whatever it touched and there was no particular hurry in that. It was the most democratic thing in the territory. It did not distinguish between the Marshal's jurisdiction and the jurisdiction of air. It did not care about maps drawn in walnut-husk ink. It was, at this moment, approximately four hours from the Proulx homestead, and the Marshal was approximately two hours from the Proulx homestead, and the arithmetic of these two numbers was the arithmetic on which everything that followed would depend.

They rode.

The grass was already flat. It had been flattened by the advance wind that precedes all great storms, pressed down against the earth as though a giant hand had smoothed it, and the horses moved across it in a landscape so horizontal and so stripped of the vertical that the riders themselves were the tallest things in any direction. They were the only things that stood upright between the ground and the black wall, and the narrator, who was above them now, looking down in the manner of something that loved them for reasons it was not obligated to explain, saw them as what they were: four small mammals moving on top of four large mammals across an enormous plane of brown grass, heading toward a catastrophe they had chosen to ride into rather than shelter from, because four of the forty-one people on the Marshal's map were at the end of the farthest line and someone had to go get them.

The humor of this was the kind that does not produce laughter.

The Proulx homestead appeared first as a smudge, then as a shape, then as a sodhouse with a lean-to barn and a wagon parked at an angle that suggested it had been shoved sideways by last week's wind and no one had straightened it. Carl Proulx and his wife, Ada, were outside. They had seen the black wall. They had been standing in their doorway looking at it with the expressions of people who have been told a theoretical thing and are now watching it become actual.

The Marshal dismounted in a single motion that managed to communicate, through its efficiency, both urgency and calm. She did not rush. Rushing would have told the Proulxes that there was reason to panic, and panic was the one variable her arithmetic could not accommodate. She walked to them. She pointed north, at the wall. She pointed south, toward the direction of town. She touched her badge — not dramatically, not the way a man would have, as though drawing a sidearm, but with two fingers, lightly, the way you touch a thing to remind someone it exists. Then she made a motion with her right hand that was unmistakable in any language and any wind: *Move. Now. South.*

Carl Proulx looked at his wife. His wife looked at the wall. The wall was closer than it had been an hour ago, and this fact, visible and unarguable, performed the bulk of the Marshal's persuasion for her.

Ada Proulx went inside. She came out forty seconds later carrying a bundle that contained the baby and a second bundle that contained something the narrator could see but will not inventory, because every person who has ever been forced to leave a home quickly has made the same calculation about what fits in their arms, and the specific objects matter less than the fact of the choosing, which is always a grief in miniature. Carl Proulx was already moving toward the lean-to to get whatever he needed from it. The Marshal held up a hand: *Stop.* She pointed at the wagon. She pointed at the horses. She made a pulling motion. *Hitch the team. Nothing else.*

This was the moment that cost time at every homestead: the moment between the decision to leave and the physical act of leaving, during which a person's mind, already in motion, doubles back to retrieve something — a tool, a document, a keepsake — that is not essential but feels essential because it is a piece of the life they're being asked to carry away from. The Marshal had budgeted four minutes per homestead for this doubling-back. Carl Proulx used three. He hitched the team with the competence of a man who had hitched teams in worse weather, loaded his wife and the bundles, and looked at the Marshal for what came next.

She pointed south. She made a gesture that meant *follow.* She remounted.

While this had been happening, Dunleavy had been standing next to Brick, holding the gelding's reins in his left hand and the reins of Pell's horse in his right, because Pell had gone to help Proulx with the hitch. The standing was worse than the riding. When he was riding, the constant motion provided a structure for his counter-instructions — each stride was a unit of time he could manage. But standing still, holding reins, feeling the horse breathe and shift its weight for reasons he could not predict or decode, he was alone with the full architecture of his fear, and the only thing between it and the surface of his body was the face he had built over four years, the face that said nothing and meant nothing and was, in its blankness, the most eloquent performance of courage occurring in the Nebraska Territory at seven minutes to nine on a Tuesday morning.

Brick shifted his weight from his left hind to his right hind. This was a thing horses do. It meant nothing. It meant that Brick's left hind leg was slightly tired of bearing weight and his right hind leg was now bearing it instead. The shift produced a lateral movement of approximately two inches at the point where Dunleavy's hand held the reins, and those two inches traveled through the leather and into Dunleavy's fingers and up his arm and into the center of his chest, where the scream lived, and the scream rose exactly the way it always rose — sudden, hot, total — and Dunleavy did not scream. He stood. He breathed. He softened his fingers, which had clamped shut like a trap. He told his thighs nothing, because his thighs were not on the horse. He was winning a war no one could see against an enemy that lived in his own sternum, and the narrator, who saw it, who saw all of it — the clamped fingers, the released fingers, the breath that was a millimeter too controlled to be natural — regarded Dunleavy the way one regards a man building a cathedral with his bare hands: not with pity, and not with amusement, but with the kind of admiration that is available only to those who understand what the work costs.

Pell's horse yawned.

Dunleavy did not flinch. But the ghost of the flinch passed through him like a current, and he turned his face into the wind so that the cold would give him a reason for the expression that briefly crossed it, and by the time Pell returned for the reins, Dunleavy's face was the same face it always was: a monument to the art of not-screaming, indistinguishable from peace.

The party moved south, now five adults, one baby, and a wagon. The Proulx homestead disappeared behind them with the speed of all things on the plains — not gradually, but suddenly, as though the land had swallowed it. The wind was against them for the lateral ride to the next homestead but would be partially behind them for the long run south, and the Marshal had accounted for this in her arithmetic, and the accounting was correct, and the correctness was the kind that matters only until the first variable changes.

The black wall was closer. A person could see, now, that it had internal texture — not the smooth opacity of a painted surface but the boiling, roiling motion of a thing that was made of a billion small things in violent conference with one another.
