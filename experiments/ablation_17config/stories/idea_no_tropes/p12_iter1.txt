# One-Boot Beauregard

## I.

Fort Perdition sat on the alkali flats of the New Mexico Territory like a thing that had been dropped there by a God who had lost interest in aiming. It was not so much built as *accreted* — wall upon wall, watchtower upon watchtower, each addition the brainchild of some new commanding officer who had looked upon the existing fortifications and found them insufficiently paranoid. By the time of this account it possessed thirty-one cannons, which was, by conservative arithmetic, thirty-one more than any rational strategist could justify, given that the fort's primary function was the containment of between nine and fourteen men at any given time, depending on the season, the prevailing winds of territorial justice, and whether the Doña Ana circuit judge had been drinking. The walls stood forty-two feet high. The nearest settlement of any consequence lay a hundred and six miles to the southeast. The nearest settlement of no consequence — a stageline relay consisting of one building, one man, and one mule, the three of them locked in a private misery that is not our concern — lay eighty. The surrounding terrain offered nothing that might motivate an attacking force: no water, no silver, no timber, no grass, no reason. Fort Perdition defended its little parcel of nothing with the grim totality of a man guarding an empty safe.

There were, at the time that matters to us, eleven prisoners, forty-six guards, one commandant, one cook who was also the barber, one barber's cat, and a vagrant chaplain who arrived on the third Sunday of alternating months and performed services that no one was compelled or, evidently, inclined to attend. The ratio of armed men to confined men was thus slightly better than four to one, which in practical terms meant that each prisoner, should he elect to make trouble, could rely upon the focused professional attention of four soldiers who had nothing else to do and nowhere else to be and who had developed, in their boredom, a certain enthusiasm for the minor dramas of confinement.

It is into this masterpiece of disproportionate effort that we must now, with some reluctance, lower our gaze — past the watchtowers, past the parapet with its absurd row of nine-pounders aimed at the empty desert like a firing squad assembled for the execution of the horizon, past the yard where the prisoners took their daily constitutionals in a circuit so well-worn it had become a trench, and down to a particular cell, or rather, to the patch of hardpan just outside a particular cell, where a man was squatting on his heels and drawing in the dirt with a mesquite stick.

He had one boot on.

The other foot was wrapped in a series of rags that had been layered, compressed, and re-bound so many times they had achieved a kind of dense felt-like permanence, a textile fossil, the rags having long since forgotten they had ever been anything other than a boot-shaped accretion on the end of a man's left leg. The boot that remained — the right — was of no particular quality or distinction. It was, in the taxonomy of frontier footwear, a plain pull-on of middling leather with a cavalry heel, the sort of boot a man acquires not by choice but by proximity, which is to say it had probably belonged to someone else first and would, in the fullness of time, belong to someone else again, the boot being destined to outlast any individual owner the way a river outlasts any individual fish.

The man's name was Beauregard. Not his Christian name — if indeed he had been Christianed, a matter on which the available evidence was inconclusive — but the only name by which anyone at Fort Perdition knew him. He had arrived eleven months prior, delivered by a territorial marshal who had ridden away with the visible relief of a man returning a borrowed horse he suspected of being rabid. The charges were prospecting without a license, destruction of a sluice belonging to the Carmody Mining Concern, and — this last was the one that had earned him Fort Perdition rather than a mere county lockup — the theft and subsequent accidental detonation of fourteen cases of blasting powder, an event that had rearranged a modest portion of the Sangre de Cristo foothills and could be heard, according to three independent accounts, from as far away as Taos.

He was drawing in the dirt. He drew with the concentration of a man for whom the dirt was not dirt but vellum, and the stick was not a stick but the instrument by which the architecture of his freedom would be drafted into existence. He drew lines. He drew circles. He drew what appeared to be a small rectangle with an X through it, which represented — though we are getting somewhat ahead of ourselves — the guardhouse at the fort's northeast corner.

What Beauregard was drawing, though he had no word for it and would not have used the word if he had, was a plan.

It was not his first.

It would not be his last.

## II.

The first escape attempt occurred on a Tuesday, or possibly a Wednesday — the narrator confesses some imprecision on this point, the days at Fort Perdition being distinguishable from one another only by the direction of the wind and the mood of the barber's cat, neither of which followed any system discernible to rational inquiry. Beauregard had noticed that the drainage channel running beneath the east wall, through which the fort's modest sewage made its way to a pit beyond the perimeter, was braced by timber frames set at intervals of approximately ten feet, and that between these frames the earth was of a sandy, friable composition that a man could move without tools if he did not object to moving it with his fingers and did not mind where he put it once moved.

He minded neither.

What Beauregard did not notice, or noticed and assigned insufficient weight, was that the drainage channel, having been repaired the previous spring by a detail of soldiers who had been given ambiguous instructions, did not terminate beyond the east wall but rather curved — gently, almost apologetically — back toward the fort's interior, surfacing at last in a grated opening beneath the floor of the commandant's personal quarters.

It is possible to imagine the scene without the narrator's help, but let us be precise: Beauregard emerged headfirst from a gap in the commandant's floorboards at approximately eleven o'clock in the evening, coated in a substance that need not be catalogued in detail, directly beneath the commandant's writing desk, at which the commandant was at that moment seated, composing a letter to his sister in Vermont in which he was, by coincidence, describing the general docility and low cunning of the prisoner population. The commandant looked down. Beauregard looked up. What passed between them in that interval of mutual recognition — the commandant's ink-stained fingers frozen above the page, Beauregard's face framed by splintered pine like a portrait of filth in a rustic frame — was not a conversation, because no conversation was had, but it was, in its compressed and silent way, a complete exchange: surprise, followed by comprehension, followed by something on the commandant's side that approached, without reaching, admiration, and something on Beauregard's side that approached, without reaching, embarrassment. Then the commandant placed two fingers in his mouth and blew a whistle that required no object, being produced entirely by the geometry of his hand, and four guards arrived, and Beauregard was extracted from the floor like a bad tooth.

Days passed. The precise number is uncertain but was fewer than the fingers on a man's two hands. Then Beauregard attempted the second escape.

The disguise was — the narrator takes a deliberate breath here and chooses his words with the care of a surgeon selecting an instrument — *ambitious*. Beauregard had acquired, through channels that remain opaque, a military tunic of approximately the correct color but catastrophically wrong size, being cut for a man of roughly twice his width and two-thirds his height, so that he resembled, when wearing it, not a soldier but a child's drawing of a soldier, or perhaps a scarecrow that had been dressed by someone who had once had a soldier described to them by a third party who was working from memory. He had completed the effect with a hat — not a military hat but a hat nonetheless, a brown felt affair of civilian character — pulled so low over his brow that his face was largely hidden, which was the intention, and his ability to see where he was walking was entirely eliminated, which was not.

He made it eleven feet from his cell before he walked into a wall.

Not a wall in the metaphorical sense. A wall of actual adobe, which he struck with his face, and from which he rebounded in the manner of a man who had been expecting open air and received instead the full solidity of the physical world. The guards who observed this did not laugh, or if they laughed, the narrator did not hear it, which is another way of saying the narrator prefers to believe they did not laugh, because it is more dignified for all involved, and dignity is a resource in short supply at Fort Perdition and must be conserved where possible.

He was returned to his cell. He sat on his cot. He wrapped and re-wrapped the rags on his left foot with the tender precision of a man adjusting the sails of a very small, very doomed ship.

The next morning he was in the yard again, drawing in the dirt.

It should be said — and this is the point at which the reader must begin to pay a different kind of attention — that none of what has been described so far involved a single spoken word. Beauregard did not announce his tunnel. He did not explain his disguise. The commandant did not exclaim when a man surfaced beneath his writing desk. The guards did not issue verbal commands when they extracted the prisoner from the floor. All of these events occurred in the medium of the physical world — of gesture, of posture, of the mute eloquence of a body in motion and a face in reaction — and if the reader has not yet remarked upon this silence, that is because silence, at Fort Perdition, was not an absence but an atmosphere, the way heat was an atmosphere, the way dust was an atmosphere. It was the element in which all action was conducted. It was not that men could not speak. It was that the territory, by some accident of culture and geography and the particular type of man drawn to its emptiest reaches, had developed a relationship with language that was, to put it generously, conservational, and to put it accurately, functionally nonexistent.

Men communicated. Of course they did. But their communications were conducted in a vernacular that had nothing to do with the throat or the tongue.

Consider the hat.

At Fort Perdition, and in the broader civilization of the alkali West from which its residents had been drawn, the angle of a man's hat brim relative to his brow was a document as legible as any written text, provided one had been educated in its orthography. A hat worn level and low, brim parallel to the horizon, signified a general contentment with the present arrangement of the world and an unwillingness to alter it. A hat pushed back on the head, exposing the forehead, communicated openness — to suggestion, to collaboration, to schemes of a nature the schemer had not yet specified but wished to signal his willingness to entertain. A hat tilted forward at thirty degrees meant caution. At forty-five degrees, commitment. At forty-six degrees — and here the distinction was critical, the difference between salvation and catastrophe — it meant betrayal. Specifically: *I will report you to the commandant before sundown, not from any personal animosity but because the reward for informing is an extra ration of tobacco, and I would rather have the tobacco than your friendship, which has been, if we are being honest with each other through the angle of our respective hat brims, no great asset to me in any case.*

There were further refinements. A hat removed entirely and held against the chest signified something so solemn that the narrator has encountered it only twice in all his years of study and is not confident he understands it fully. A hat placed on the ground, crown down, was a question. Crown up, an answer. Which answer depended on additional variables — the position of the hat relative to the man's boots, the cardinal direction the brim faced, whether the hat was placed gently or dropped — that cascaded into a combinatorial richness rivaling, in the narrator's estimation, the tonal systems of certain East Asian languages, though this comparison would have meant nothing to the men who employed it and so is offered here purely for the reader's orientation.

Tobacco spit served as punctuation: a long stream to the left for emphasis, a short drop to the right for conditionality, a dry empty motion of the jaw — the spit mimed but not produced — for negation, which is to say *no*, which is to say *I hear what your hat is telling me and I decline*.

Boot-stomps, belt adjustments, the speed at which a man rolled a cigarette he would not smoke, the specific finger used to push a hat brim up or down, the angle of a squint into the sun when the sun was not, in fact, in one's eyes — all of these were vocabulary, and taken together they constituted a language of adequate complexity and startling nuance, and it was in this language that Beauregard planned his escapes, recruited his confederates, and absorbed the news of his failures, and it is in this language that the business of the story, from this point forward, is conducted.

## III.

The reader may be wondering how a fort of such exaggerated proportions came to exist on a flat of such comprehensive worthlessness, and the narrator is not only willing but eager to explain, having always found the history of Fort Perdition more interesting than any individual event that occurred within it, in the same way that the biography of a theater is often more interesting than any particular play staged on its boards.

Fort Perdition was founded in 1854 by Colonel Erasmus Whit, a man whose distinguishing characteristic — acknowledged by his superiors, his subordinates, his wife, and, in a letter discovered after his death, by Whit himself — was a capacity for suspicion so comprehensive it resembled, at its highest expression, a kind of philosophy. Whit suspected everyone of everything at all times. He suspected his officers of insubordination, his enlisted men of desertion, his wife of correspondence with a surveyor in Santa Fe (she was in fact corresponding with a milliner in Saint Louis, but the principle, Whit felt, was the same), his horse of malingering, and the land itself of harboring intentions it had not yet disclosed.

He built the fort to house a single prisoner: a man named, or believed to be named, Calaveras — a prospector, or a bandit, or a former soldier, or possibly all three in uncertain sequence — who had been accused of stealing a government mule and, in a separate but thematically related incident, setting fire to a customs house near the border. Whit considered Calaveras so dangerous that the standard frontier stockade would not suffice. He requisitioned materials for walls of twenty feet. He requisitioned cannons. He requisitioned a second set of walls outside the first set. By the time the first laborers arrived and began cutting adobe bricks, Whit had revised the plans to include a moat — a theoretical moat, as there was no water with which to fill it, a difficulty Whit acknowledged only in the most provisional terms, having submitted a separate requisition for the diversion of a river that was, at its nearest point, forty-seven miles away and flowing in the wrong direction.

Calaveras died three weeks before the fort was completed.

He died of natural causes, in a county jail in Mesilla, while awaiting transfer to the facility that had been built exclusively for his confinement. The news reached Colonel Whit by courier on the same day the last cannon was mounted on the parapet. Whit received the news — a slip of paper handed to him by a rider who did not dismount — and read it, and folded it, and placed it in his breast pocket, and looked up at the fort he had built, and adjusted the angle of his hat, and the adjustment said nothing more or less than: *A fort exists now. I will not un-build it. Prisoners will be found.*

Prisoners were found. The territory, cooperating with the logic of the situation, began producing men whose crimes were deemed severe enough, or whose persons were deemed inconvenient enough, to justify transportation to the most remote and over-fortified installation in the entirety of the New Mexico Territory. Whit expanded the walls to their present forty-two feet. He added watchtowers. He requisitioned additional cannons with the steady, joyless persistence of a man who has found, in architectural paranoia, a purpose sufficient to organize a life.
