# The Gospel According to the Golden Seat

## I.

You have to understand that every public restroom in New York City is a church.

I don't mean that metaphorically, or I do, but only in the way that all metaphors are just truths too ugly to say plainly. I mean that when you are a person who walks this city on foot — not cabbed, not car-serviced, not Ubered between climate-controlled boxes — but actually *walks* it, block by block, with your gut doing what guts do and your body keeping its own vulgar schedule, then the public restroom is the only institution that will save you without asking what you believe. A bodega won't let you use theirs. Starbucks put locks on the doors in 2018 and never took them off despite what they told the newspapers. The church on the corner, the actual church, has a sign that says RESTROOM FOR CONGREGATION MEMBERS ONLY, which tells you everything you need to know about what congregation means to people who mount it on a placard.

But the public restroom — the Parks Department john, the library basement, the subway station facility with its biblical smell and its fluorescent buzzlight and its toilet paper so thin you could read scripture through it — that restroom will receive you. That restroom don't ask for a receipt, a membership, a minimum purchase, a code texted to your phone after you buy an eight-dollar oat milk latte. That restroom simply says: *You are a body. Come in.*

This is a story about what happens when that church gets privatized. When someone puts a lock on the door and a card reader on the wall and a thing in the pipes that I still can't fully explain without my hands shaking, a thing that I will try to explain anyway because Desmond walked through the door and someone has to tell it.

My name is Claude Jeffords. I am forty-four years old. I drive a bus for the MTA, the Bx19 route, Fordham to East Tremont, five days a week, which means I know every public restroom on that corridor the way a priest knows every station of the cross. I have reviewed two hundred and thirty-seven public restrooms in the five boroughs of New York City, each review written longhand in composition notebooks and later transcribed to our website, thronereviews.nyc, which at its peak had eleven thousand monthly visitors, most of them real. I am — I was — a Throne Reviewer, which means I was part of something that had rules and purpose and a name for what it loved, even if no one outside of us could hear that name without laughing.

I need you to not laugh. I need that from you before I go any further.

The Throne Reviewers started in 2019, before the plague, which is important because the plague almost killed us in the way it almost killed everything that required people to be in rooms together. We were founded by a woman named Patrice Delacroix who worked the front desk at the Brooklyn Central Library and who understood, because of her position, that the most important room in any library is not the reading room. Patrice had an idea that was so simple it sounded stupid until you sat with it: that public restrooms were the last truly democratic spaces in the city, and that someone ought to be documenting them — their condition, their accessibility, their dignity or lack thereof — with the same rigor and reverence that the Michelin Guide applied to restaurants where a single entrée cost more than a bus driver makes in a shift.

So she started reviewing them. Online, at first. Short paragraphs, no ratings, just observations. *The second-floor women's room at the Prospect Park branch has a lock that sticks but a window that opens onto the garden, and if you time it right, you can hear the children in the story hour singing while you wash your hands. Three stalls, two functional, one permanently occupied by a mop bucket wearing a sign that says OUT OF ORDER but means WE GAVE UP.* Like that. And something about the way she wrote — not joking about it, not holding her nose, just *seeing* it — drew people in.

By the time I found the website, there were nineteen regular contributors, and we'd started meeting monthly at a Dominican restaurant in Washington Heights called La Reina Juana that had, not coincidentally, one of the finest public-access restrooms north of 125th Street. Four stars. Cedar toilet seat. A framed picture of the Virgin Mary above the sink in which the Virgin appeared to be smiling specifically at whoever was washing their hands, as if to say: *I see you. This too is holy.*

I found them because I was lonely in the specific way that bus drivers are lonely — surrounded by hundreds of people a day, not one of them wanting to see you. And because I had spent twenty years noticing bathrooms with a weird devotion I could never explain to anyone, cataloguing them in my head like a birdwatcher catalogs warblers, and suddenly here was a flock of people who spoke my language.

But I didn't stay because of Patrice, or La Reina Juana, or even the eleven thousand monthly visitors.

I stayed because of Desmond.

## II.

The first time I saw Desmond Lyle Cross was at the October 2019 meeting, which was the meeting where we voted on whether to include private-but-publicly-accessible restrooms in our purview — meaning hotel lobbies, department stores, that kind of thing — and the vote was contentious because it touched on the central question of what *public* meant and who got to define it. I voted no. I was wrong, but I voted no.

Desmond voted yes, and the way he voted yes told me everything I would ever need to know about him.

He stood up — and you need to understand the physical fact of Desmond to understand its effect, because he was six foot four and built like a man who had been moving heavy things since before his body was done growing, broad in the shoulders and thick in the hands, with a face that looked like it had been designed for seriousness but kept getting ambushed by kindness. He was forty-one. He was dark-skinned in the way that, in America, means people make assumptions before you open your mouth, and he had a voice that was low and unhurried and precise, the voice of a man who had learned that if he spoke too fast or too loud, certain rooms would stop hearing the words and start hearing only the volume.

He stood up and he said: "The question isn't what counts as public. The question is what happens when a person needs to shit and can't."

There was laughter, but not the kind I asked you not to do. This was the laughter of recognition, the ha of people hearing something they already knew but hadn't been able to say.

"I clean bathrooms for a living," he said. "Port Authority, mostly. Night shift. And I can tell you that the people who come into that bathroom at two in the morning are not there because they wanted to visit Port Authority. They're there because every other door between 34th Street and 42nd Street is locked. The hotel lobby bathroom has a keycard now. The Marriott. The Hilton. Used to be you could walk in like you belonged and nobody stopped you. Now there's a man at a desk whose whole job is to stop you."

He let that sit.

"So yes, we should be reviewing them. We should be reviewing every bathroom in this city that a person can or cannot get into, because the thing we're really documenting isn't plumbing. It's access. It's who this city thinks deserves to sit down."

Patrice was nodding before he finished. The vote carried 23 to 6. But what I remember is not the speech, not exactly. What I remember is what happened after, when the meeting broke up and people drifted toward the bar, and Desmond stayed behind to stack the chairs. I watched him do it. He stacked every single chair, including the ones that other people had been sitting in, and he did it with the quiet efficiency of a man who had been cleaning up after other people so long it had become a kind of reflex, and when he was done he wiped down the table with a napkin from his pocket and nodded at the owner and left.

He didn't know I was watching. That's the thing. He wasn't performing. He was just a man who saw a room that needed to be put back together and put it back together.

I followed him out. Not in a strange way — I was leaving too, we were both heading to the 1 train — and on the platform I introduced myself and told him I drove the Bx19 and that I'd been a Throne Reviewer for six months and that his speech was the best argument I'd ever heard for anything.

He looked at me with this expression I would come to know well, an expression that was 60 percent genuine warmth and 40 percent carefully calibrated disbelief that anyone was paying attention to him.

"Claude," he said, shaking my hand. "You drive the Bx19."

"Every weekday."

"I know that route. Fordham. You pass the McDonald's on the Grand Concourse, the one where the bathroom has been closed since 2017."

"Since 2016," I said.

He smiled. It was the first time I saw Desmond smile, and I am not exaggerating when I say it rearranged my understanding of what a smile could do. It was not a big smile. It did not show teeth. It was a contraction of the muscles around his eyes and a slight lift at the left corner of his mouth that communicated, somehow, both sadness and delight simultaneously — sadness that we lived in a world where a McDonald's bathroom had been closed for three years and delight that someone else had noticed and cared enough to know the exact year.

"Since 2016," he repeated. "You're right. I stand corrected."

We rode to 59th Street together, where he transferred to the A to get back to Far Rockaway, which is, if you don't know, about as far from Washington Heights as you can get without leaving the city, and which tells you something about Desmond's commitment that he'd ride ninety minutes each way for a monthly meeting about bathrooms. On the platform he told me he worked nights at Port Authority, custodial, and that he had a daughter named Elise who was seventeen and wanted to go to Spelman and that he was trying to figure out how to make that happen on $37,000 a year, which he said not with self-pity but with the flat factual tone of a man stating the dimensions of a problem he intended to solve.

I learned later that Desmond had been a Throne Reviewer longer than almost anyone — he was the fourth person to register on the site after Patrice, and he had written more reviews than anyone in the group. Eighty-seven at that point. Each one was meticulous. He reviewed the way I imagine a surgeon operates: systematically, with attention to the thing everyone else would overlook. He noted the angle of the toilet relative to the stall door. He noted whether the soap dispenser was mounted at a height accessible to someone in a wheelchair. He timed the hot water. He checked whether the baby-changing station had been installed in the men's room or only the women's. He noted the graffiti and sometimes quoted it, not for laughs but because he believed that what people wrote on bathroom walls when they believed they were alone was a kind of civic record.

His review of the second-floor restroom at the Bronx courthouse remains the finest piece of criticism I have ever read in any genre. It was four pages long. It addressed the water pressure, the tile work, the light quality, and the smell, but it also addressed the experience of being a Black man in a courthouse bathroom, the way the room's institutional coldness felt different when you were there not by choice but by summons, the way the mirror above the sink was mounted at a height that cut off anyone over six feet so that Desmond could wash his hands but could not see his own face doing it, which he described as "the architecture of a building that does not expect you to see yourself as whole." He ended the review with a single line: *Two stars. Functional. Bring your own dignity.*

The group voted that review the best of the year, and Patrice created a title for it that we'd never had before: the Golden Seat. It was a joke, sort of. Except that when she said it — when she stood up at the December meeting and said, "The Golden Seat goes to Desmond Cross" — nobody laughed. People applauded. And Desmond stood up and received it with the solemnity of a man accepting a knighthood, because he understood that the joke and the seriousness were the same thing, that we were all standing in the place where the profane and the sacred met and shook hands.

He won the Golden Seat three years in a row. By the third year, people had stopped calling him Desmond at meetings. They called him the Golden Seat, or just Golden, and he accepted this without protest in the way that certain people accept names given to them by communities — not because they chose it, but because the community saw something in them and needed a word for it.

I loved him. I want to be clear about that. Not romantically — I have an ex-wife and a theoretical interest in women that has not resulted in anything practical since 2015 — but in the way that one person recognizes another person as the better version of the thing they both are. Desmond was what I would have been if I'd been smarter, braver, more articulate, and more willing to stack chairs when nobody was watching. Desmond was the best of us.

I need you to know that he was the best of us before I tell you what happened to him.

## III.

By 2022, the Throne Reviewers had thirty-one active members, and I loved every miserable beautiful one of them in the specific way that you love people who share your particular damage.

There was Patrice, our founder, who had retired from the library and now devoted herself full-time to the website and to her secondary passion, which was making pimento cheese sandwiches for every meeting, sandwiches so aggressively seasoned that eating one was itself a gastrointestinal event that would, within hours, require the very facilities we reviewed. There was Roland Marsh, who was seventy-three and had driven a cab for forty years and who claimed, plausibly, to have urinated in every zip code in Manhattan. Roland reviewed with the terse authority of a man who had seen too much and forgiven nothing. His reviews were rarely longer than three sentences. His review of the Times Square public restroom in its entirety read: *No. God, no. Three stars because the door locks.* He was the only reviewer who had ever given three stars to a bathroom he'd described as an abomination, and when questioned about the inconsistency, he said, "A door that locks is a door that locks," which was the closest Roland ever came to a philosophical statement.

There was Yolanda Kim, who was twenty-six and worked as a paralegal and who had Crohn's disease, which meant her relationship to public restrooms was not academic. Yolanda reviewed with a ferocity that bordered on the religious. She kept a laminated card in her wallet listing every accessible restroom within a ten-block radius of her office, updated quarterly, and she had been known to weep — actually weep, tears on her face — when a listed restroom was closed for renovation without notice. She was also the funniest writer in the group, capable of describing a broken toilet in terms that made you feel you were reading Joan Didion on the subject of moral collapse. Her review of a Whole Foods bathroom in Union Square included the line: *The toilet senses your presence and flushes preemptively, as if to say, I already know what you are, and I am not impressed.*
