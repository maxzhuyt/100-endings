# The First Successful Appendectomy West of the Missouri

---

## I.

Gentlemen, if you will indulge me.

I have dined well tonight, as have you all, and the hour is late enough that a man may be forgiven for trading the caution of the consulting room for the candor of the banquet hall. I have been asked — by our distinguished territorial secretary, whose generosity with the brandy I trust will extend to his judgment of my oratory — to say a few words about the night in question. The night that several of you have been kind enough to call historic. I will not argue the point. I lack the constitution for false modesty, and besides, the brandy is very good.

It was the autumn of 1876. Deadwood Gulch. I had been practicing there some months, serving the miners and merchants of that young and unruly settlement in whatever capacity a physician might, which is to say: constantly, and with little thanks, and with the sort of patients who regard a medical degree as roughly equivalent to a farrier's license and considerably less useful. I do not complain. I had chosen the frontier. The frontier had not yet chosen me. That distinction, gentlemen, was conferred by the evening I am about to describe.

The man they brought into Dooley's saloon was called Elias Frye. Some of you may know the name. A road agent of middling reputation and considerable personal odor who had been terrorizing the Cheyenne stage route with more enthusiasm than competence, and who had, on the night of October the fourteenth, presented himself at Dooley's bar to drink away the proceeds of his latest enterprise. I was told later that he'd been suffering for days — the pain beginning, as it does, at the umbilicus, migrating with the patience of a church deacon to the right lower quadrant, until what had been discomfort became agony and what had been agony became, at approximately nine-fifteen that evening, a perforation.

A burst appendix, gentlemen. In a mining camp. In 1876.

You know what that means. Even now, in our fine hospitals with their boiled linens and their ether cones, a peritonitic abdomen is a death sentence delivered by committee and executed by sepsis. In Deadwood, with sawdust on the floor and kerosene in the lamps and not a single qualified surgeon within four hundred miles — that is not a death sentence. That is death itself, arrived, wearing no particular expression, waiting by the bar.

I will not pretend I was not afraid. I will pretend — because vanity has its prerogatives — that my fear was better concealed than it probably was. The men who carried Frye to the back room and laid him across a door propped on two flour barrels were his associates, which is a polite way of saying that they were armed, drunk, and operating under the conviction that if their companion died, the doctor who let him die would follow at a conversational interval. I understood their position. I sympathized, even. A man wants to believe that someone in the room knows what to do.

I laid out my instruments. I had a fair kit — I had always had a fair kit, even when my experience was less fair than my equipment, because I believed then, as I believe now, that a physician's tools are the visible scripture of his competence, and they should be read clearly. My Liston knife. My tenaculum. A set of artery forceps I had purchased in Philadelphia from a German instrument-maker whose name I cannot now recall but whose craftsmanship I blessed that night with a fervency bordering on the devotional. I boiled water. I cleaned the surface as well as a man can clean a door that has spent its useful life separating the weather from a saloon's back office. I rolled my sleeves.

And then, gentlemen, I opened him up.

I will not burden you with the full procedural account, though I have written it up for the *Western Medical Gazette* and am told it will be published in the spring edition. Suffice it to say: the peritoneum was a swamp. The appendix had burst with the thoroughness of a man who does nothing by halves, and the resulting inflammation had turned the abdominal cavity into a landscape that bore less resemblance to Gray's illustrations than to the mud flats of the Missouri in spring flood. I found the stump. I ligated it. I irrigated the cavity with a carbolic solution I prepared from my own stores and drained what could be drained, and then I faced the problem that has defeated better surgeons than I in better conditions — to wit, how to close a contaminated abdomen in a manner that permits continued drainage while preventing further insult.

It was here that I improvised — and I use the word with full awareness of its implications, for in surgery, improvisation is either genius or catastrophe, and the only difference is the outcome. I had no proper surgical suture. What I had was catgut — the ordinary catgut of the frontier, such as any saddler might carry — and a curved needle I adapted from a harness-maker's tool by heating and rebending it over the lamp. With these, I fashioned a running suture of sufficient delicacy to close the peritoneum in layers, leaving a wick of boiled cotton at the inferior margin for drainage. The technique was not in any textbook. Permit me a moment of pride: it is now. Dr. Elmore Case of Chicago has since described a nearly identical method in his monograph on abdominal closure, and I have written to him to suggest that priority, while a tedious subject, is not an irrelevant one.

Frye lived. I say this plainly because the plainness is the miracle. He suppurated, of course — he seethed with fever for nine days, and on the fourth night I was quite certain he would die and spent an hour composing, in my mind, the remarks I would make to his armed companions, which I had decided would be brief and delivered from a considerable distance. But on the tenth morning the fever broke, and on the fourteenth day he sat up and asked for whiskey, and on the twenty-first day he walked out of Dooley's saloon under his own power and was subsequently hanged in Yankton for an unrelated matter, which I mention only to illustrate that medicine is a discipline of incomplete victories and a physician must find his satisfactions where God allows.

Gentlemen, I do not tell you this to boast. I tell you this because — forgive me — because I think it matters. I think it matters that in a place no one had yet thought to call civilization, on a table no one had thought to call sterile, a man who was going to die did not die. I think it matters that the hand that held the knife did not shake. That is — I think it matters that the work was possible. That somewhere in the country of the sick there is always a narrow passage through, if the man who goes looking for it is — if a man has the nerve, and the knowledge, and the —

The nerve.

Gentlemen. Your health.

## II.

The applause is the thing I remember, and I have always hated myself for how much I remember it. Not the surgery. Not Frye's face or the sound he made when the knife first parted the skin. The applause. Its specific texture, the way it began at the front tables and rolled back through the room, the way it permitted me to set down my glass and lower my eyes in a performance of humility so practiced I could feel the gears of it turning behind my face. I had told the story perhaps two dozen times by then. I knew where the laughs fell — *considerable personal odor* — and where to pause so the gravity could settle — *that is death itself, arrived.* I knew the rhythm of it, the architecture. I had built it like a man builds a house: room by room, joint by joint, until you could walk through it and never see the nails.

But I have promised myself — should I say to whom? to God? to the page? to the woman whose name I have been circling for two sections now like a man afraid to touch a wound? — I have promised that I would go back, and so I will go back. Not to the beginning. To the first moment after.

The surgery was finished. Frye was alive — that part was true, that part was always true. There was a boy sitting with him, some associate's nephew charged with watching his breathing through the night, and I had given such instructions as I could and stepped out through the back of Dooley's into the alley. It was perhaps two in the morning. October in the Black Hills, which means the cold has a particular quality, thin and mineral, as though the air itself has been mined. I wanted to vomit. I did not vomit. I stood against the wall and breathed and felt the trembling in my hands that I had somehow held at bay for ninety minutes finally arrive with the comprehensiveness of a debt collector.

She was already there.

Jessamyn Lightfoot was washing her hands in a horse trough, methodically, the way she did everything — with a precision that made the ordinary act of cleaning look like surgery and surgery look like prayer. She had rolled her sleeves to the elbow. There was blood on her forearms that the water was turning to a pale rust, and she worked at it with the focus of a woman who knew that the blood of other people's patients was the most dangerous substance she carried, not for reasons of infection but of evidence.

She did not look up. She said: "He'll suppurate. Four days of hell and then the fever will either break or it won't. Keep the drain clean. Change the wick twice daily. If you use carbolic on the wick, dilute it by half or you'll burn the tissue and give yourself a fistula you'll have no way to explain."

I should note — in fairness, and I use that word with full awareness of its insufficiency — that it was she who spoke first about the arrangement. I want to be clear about this. I did not propose it. I did not suggest it. She understood the situation before I had finished understanding that there was a situation to understand.

"You know what happens now," she said. She had finished with her hands and was drying them on a cloth she carried in her coat. Her coat was a man's coat, heavy canvas, too large for her. Later I would learn she had taken it from a dead freighter whose wound she had cauterized too late to save him, and that she wore it the way a knight wears armor taken from a vanquished enemy, which is to say: without sentiment, because it fit.

"They'll ask who did it," she said. "The men in there. The camp. And then the newspaper — there's a newspaper now, did you know? — and then the territorial medical society, and eventually someone from the *Gazette* or the *Lancet* will write a letter asking for particulars, and you will have to answer it."

"We could —"

"We could not."

She said it without anger. That was the thing about Jessamyn that I could never convey in the telling, not even to myself: her absence of anger, which was not peace but efficiency. Anger was a luxury, and she counted luxuries the way she counted sutures — not by their beauty but by their cost.

"I cannot practice, Dr. Creech. We both know this. I have no license. I have no degree because no school would have me, and I have no school because — but you know why. You have eyes and you are not, contrary to recent evidence, stupid. If I am named, I will be prosecuted. Not for saving a man's life — that is not a crime — but for practicing medicine while being inconvenient to the licensing boards of three territories and the great Commonwealth of Pennsylvania, which has the further objection that I was, at the time of my application, in possession of a uterus."

"I did not —"

"You did very little, Dr. Creech. That is the point. You held the knife when I told you to hold it, and you put it down when I told you to put it down, and you made one incision I had to correct and one ligature I had to redo, and for twelve minutes in the middle of the procedure you held a retractor with the trembling dedication of a man carrying his firstborn, which is the one thing you did that I could not have done alone, and for which I genuinely thank you."

I said nothing.

"So here is what will happen," she said, and her voice shifted — became, if anything, more courteous, more carefully assembled, the way a surgeon's hand becomes steadiest at the moment of greatest danger. "You will take credit. Not because you deserve it — desert has nothing to do with any of this; desert is a Sunday-school word and we are in Deadwood — but because you can *use* it and I cannot. You will be the doctor who saved Elias Frye. You will write it up. You will describe the procedure, and you will describe it accurately, because that procedure — my procedure — works, and other doctors need to know it works, and they will not learn it from a woman's name on a page they will never read."

She folded her cloth and placed it in her coat. Her motions were unhurried. She might have been tidying a parlor.

"In exchange," she said, "you will do nothing. That is the whole of my price. You will not name me. You will not seek me out. You will not tell anyone what happened in that room. And when my patients — the ones who come to me because they cannot come to you, or because you cannot help them, or because they are women, or they are Indian, or they are Chinese, or they are any of the other varieties of person that your degree does not oblige you to see — when those patients need something I cannot provide without a man's name on a requisition, you will provide it. Laudanum. Carbolic. Suture silk. The things I cannot buy because the suppliers want a license number. You will be my requisition. That is the arrangement."

"And if I decline?"

She looked at me for the first time. Her eyes were dark, not brown but some deeper color, the color of old surgical instruments, of iodine, of the tinctures she brewed from plants whose names I did not know. She was not beautiful. She was something else entirely, something for which there was no word in my vocabulary, which was the vocabulary of a middling Eastern education and a life spent in rooms where the hardest object was an opinion.

"You won't decline," she said. "You are trembling, Dr. Creech. You have been trembling since you stepped outside. Your hands are shaking so badly you could not thread a needle if your life depended on it, and one hour ago you were elbow-deep in a man's abdomen because I talked you through it the way one leads a horse across a river — with my voice, and with the absolute conviction that if I stopped talking, you would stop moving. You will not decline because you have just had the most important night of your life, and we both know that you were its least important participant."

I accepted. I told myself it was pragmatism. I told myself — I am trying to be precise here — I told myself that the arrangement was hers, that its architecture was her design, that I was merely the façade she had chosen for structural reasons, and that a façade is blameless. I was the front of a building. She was the building. I was blameless.

I have been telling myself this for three years, and the telling has not once achieved the weight of belief. But it has achieved the weight of habit, and on most nights I have found the two to be interchangeable, and on the nights I have not, there is brandy, and applause, and the deep and certain knowledge that I am the answer to a question no one in that banquet hall thought to ask: *Who held the knife?*

Everyone held the knife. Only one of us knew what to do with it.

## III.

But you want the surgery. Of course you want the surgery. The toast gave you the surgery in miniature, framed and captioned and hung on the wall of my best rhetoric, and now I must take it down and show you the wall behind it — the stained wall, the cracked plaster, the place where something leaked.

The plank they'd laid him on was a door, as I said. That part was true. It had been the door to Dooley's back office, pulled from its hinges by two of Frye's associates with the efficient brutality of men who have dismantled things before and do not distinguish between furniture and obstacles. They'd set it across two flour barrels. The lamp was on a crate to the left, and it was the only light — a single kerosene lamp whose flame shuddered with every gust through the wall gaps, so that the shadows in the room breathed.
