# The New Madrid Job

---

## I.

For the record — and I want to be clear from the outset that this is the record, this is *the* record, the one I am submitting to Supervisor Aldeen and whoever at the Office of Emergency Management is still taking statements at this point — my Monday was on track.

My Monday was, by every measurable metric, a normal Monday. I had eleven check-ins scheduled, two warrant-status updates to file, and a 2:30 with a man named Gerald Potts who owed Shelby County four hundred hours of community service and had completed, to date, nine. At 7:46 a.m. I had a cup of coffee from the machine on the third floor that has never once, in my eleven years with the Tennessee Board of Parole, produced anything I would describe as coffee but which I continue to drink because the alternative is the machine on the first floor, which dispenses what I believe to be heated river water with a memory of beans. I was not thinking about the 2013 Bass Pro Shops case. I was not thinking about seismic fault lines. I was not thinking about Fabergé eggs, radiological materials, or the structural integrity of downtown Memphis. I was thinking about Gerald Potts and his four hundred hours and whether the Methodist church on Poplar still counted aluminum-can sorting as a qualifying activity.

Let me establish this now, while the chronology is still clean, because nothing that follows is clean, and if I don't build the frame here it will collapse under the weight of what I'm about to describe.

I manage a caseload of sixty-three active parolees within the Western Tennessee district. Of those sixty-three, five are linked to case file 13-CR-00847, which the media called the "Bass Pro Pyramid Heist" and which I have, in my own notes, referred to simply as "the fish thing," because the first forty minutes of the robbery took place, for reasons no one has ever satisfactorily explained to me, in and around the saltwater aquarium on the retail floor of the Bass Pro Shops Pyramid in Memphis, Tennessee.

I'll introduce them the way they exist in my files, because that's how they existed to me before this weekend — as files. As folders. As recurring calendar entries and urine-test results and mildly irritating phone calls.

**Gary Alan Phelps**, DOB 5/14/1979. Wheelman. Served forty-two months at Turney Center Industrial Complex, paroled September 2017, currently employed as a driver's education instructor at Germantown High School, which is the kind of detail that, when I first noticed it, made me close the file and stare at the wall for about fifteen seconds. Three years without a violation. Married. Two kids. Coaches their soccer team, the under-eights, called the Thunderbolts and apparently terrible. During our check-ins Gary speaks almost exclusively in driving metaphors. Life is a highway. He's staying in his lane. He's got both hands on the wheel. I have written in my notes, more than once: *Phelps — stable. Annoying, but stable.*

**Curtis Ray Waylon**, DOB 2/22/1975. Muscle. Listed in the original indictment as the "entry specialist," which I am told means he was the one who broke things. Served the longest stretch — six years at Riverbend Maximum — on account of a prior. Paroled March 2020, right into the pandemic, which he spent, according to his own account, "getting right with the Lord and also my cholesterol." Currently employed part-time at a church in Whitehaven — Greater Calvary Missionary Baptist — doing maintenance and, apparently, running their food pantry. Has never missed a check-in. Has never tested positive. Has never done anything, in my professional experience, more alarming than calling me "Sister Sandra" and asking if I've been saved, which he does every single time, with a warmth so total and so genuine that I have to actively remind myself he once ripped a Diebold safe off a wall using a winch and a stolen F-250.

**Dennis Wayne Moody**, DOB 8/9/1981. Lookout. Served thirty months, paroled 2016, currently lives with his mother in Bartlett and works at a FedEx sorting facility. Dennis is, and I say this with the clinical detachment my position requires, the most anxious human being I have ever supervised. His check-ins are twenty-minute marathons of hypothetical worry. Can he travel to Mississippi? He's not planning to, he just wants to know. What if his employer does a new background check? They haven't said they're going to, he's just asking. What if someone at work finds out? Finds out what, Dennis? Anything. Everything. Dennis's file contains more of my handwritten marginalia than anyone else's, most of it variations of: *reassured Moody. Again.*

**Tamara Dawn Birch**, DOB 11/3/1983. Safecracker. The only woman in the original crew, and, based on everything I've read and been told, the only one of them with anything I'd describe as technical competence. Served thirty-six months at the Tennessee Prison for Women, paroled 2017. Currently lives in Cordova, works as a locksmith — and yes, I flagged that, and yes, it was approved, because her license is legitimate and her employer is aware of her record and because, as I wrote in my recommendation memo, "Birch's vocational skills as applied in a lawful context represent a textbook reintegration pathway." She is the only one of the five who has never, in any check-in, mentioned the 2013 case. Not once. She talks about her daughter. She talks about her continuing-education credits. She talks about weather. The 2013 case is a door she has closed, locked, and — I suspect — welded shut from the inside.

**Marcus Deshawn Webb**, DOB 7/27/1980. The Rat. The beats nickname themselves over the course of a career, and Marcus earned his not because it was ever proven at trial — the government's cooperating witness was sealed, the plea deals were labyrinthine, and the sentencing disparities alone could fill a semester of law-school discussion — but because everyone else on the crew *decided* Marcus was the snitch, and in the economy of prison reputation, consensus is conviction. Marcus served twenty-eight months — the lightest sentence — which did nothing to quiet the rumors. Paroled 2016. Currently lives in East Memphis, works as a night-shift security guard at a self-storage facility, and has a fourteen-year-old daughter involved in competitive cheerleading, which means Marcus Webb, the Rat, alleged betrayer of his co-conspirators, spends most of his weekends in high-school gymnasiums watching his kid do back handsprings. During check-ins he is quiet, polite, and radiates the low-grade tension of a man who has been looking over his shoulder so long his neck has forgotten any other direction.

That's the five. Five folders, five calendar entries, five cups of bad third-floor coffee per quarter. They are not, as a group, my most troubled parolees. They are not even my most interesting. They are, in the taxonomy of my professional life, mid-tier — people who did a stupid thing, went away for it, came out, and have been doing the unremarkable, grinding, unglamorous work of not doing the stupid thing again. I have watched them age through their files. I have watched Gary's kids appear in his emergency-contact updates. I have watched Curtis's blood-pressure medication get added to his medical disclosure. I have watched Tamara's daughter go from a footnote in a pre-sentencing report to a middle-schooler in the gifted program. These are not masterminds. These are not Ocean's Eleven. These are people who once, a decade ago, tried to steal what they believed were Fabergé eggs from a Bass Pro Shops, and who have been paying for it, in overlapping and non-overlapping ways, ever since.
