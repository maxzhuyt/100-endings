# Marla Huang vs. The Mold

## I.

The mold had been eating Chicago long before there was a Chicago to eat.

Three hundred million years before the first surveyor drove a stake into the lake-bed mud, before the Potawatomi named the wild garlic, before the retreating Laurentide ice sheet left its gift of flat clay and poor drainage, the spore-loss of an organism with no name in any human taxonomy settled into the Silurian dolomite beneath what would become, with the stacked improbabilities of geological time, a studio apartment at 3404 West Evergreen Avenue, Unit 2R, monthly rent $1,150, last inspected never. The organism did not sleep in the way that dormant things sleep. It metabolized. Slowly, patiently, at the rate of molecular fractions per epoch, it dissolved the boundary betweenite andite,ite the stone. It ate distinctions.ite It was, in the language of a mycology that would not exist for an eon, a living argument against the concept of a wall.

The ceiling stain was the size of a dinner plate and the color of a bruise that had given up on healing.

Marla Huang lay on a futon that cost $240 from Wayfair and was worth, at this stage of its structural life, about $240 less than that, and she stared at the stain the way she stared at most catastrophes, which was with the sustained, glassy attention of a woman running seventeen simultaneous anxiety threads and refusing to fully commit to any of them. There was the mold, yes, black and vaguely fractal at its edges, but there was also the missed call from the staffing agency (CustomerFirst Solutions, which she had not called back because calling back would require both hope and a charged phone and she had neither), and there was the $11.40 in her checking account that was earning, according to her banking app's cheerful dashboard, zero-point-zero-one percent annual interest, which she had calculated once during a bout of insomnia came out to approximately one-tenth of one cent per year, which felt like the banking industry's way of personally insulting her, and there was the thing where her ex, Kevin, had posted an Instagram story of himself and a woman named—Marla had checked—Britt, short presumably for Brittany but possibly for Brittle, which would explain the vibe, at a rock-climbing gym in Logan Square, both of them laughing in that performative way that said *we are so present and so alive and so over the people who were not enough for us*, and Kevin's forearms in the climbing harness looked good, she had to admit that his forearms looked objectively good, and she hated that she had to admit it, and she hated that she was lying on a biologically compromised futon at 1:47 a.m. evaluating the forearms of a man who had once asked her, in complete sincerity, whether the sun and the moon were the same object at different temperatures—

The mold did not care about Kevin's forearms.

The mold had been dissolving the gypsum board behind the latex paint for eleven weeks, converting calcium sulfate dihydrate into a lattice of filaments so fine that no visual inspection would detect them until the structure had already been compromised past remediation. It worked the way grief works: invisibly, then all at once.

Marla picked up her phone. She typed *black mold ceiling removal cost Chicago* into Safari, because she used Safari, because she had never downloaded Chrome, because downloading Chrome felt like a commitment to optimization and she was not optimizing, she was surviving, and Safari had been there since the beginning and was therefore grandfathered into her entropy. The first result said $1,500–$6,000 depending on severity. She closed the tab. She opened Instagram. Kevin and Britt were still climbing. Britt had tagged the gym. The gym had an inspirational quote in its bio: *The only way out is up.*

"Cool," Marla said to no one. "That's so helpful. Thank you."

She looked at the ceiling.

The stain had grown by a quarter inch since Tuesday. She knew this because she'd placed a small piece of painter's tape at its edge on Tuesday, a diagnostic gesture she'd seen in a YouTube video titled "IS YOUR MOLD TOXIC? (5 Signs You NEED to Know)," and the stain had now overtaken the tape like a slow-motion tide consuming a sandbag. The video had also said to test for *Stachybotrys chartarum* using a home kit available for $39.99, and she had added it to her Amazon cart, where it now sat alongside a $7 phone charger, a book about dopamine she'd never read, and a replacement filter for a Brita pitcher she no longer owned, a shopping cart less like a list of intended purchases and more like a museum of good intentions, a diorama of the woman she kept meaning to become.

Did her renter's insurance cover this? She had renter's insurance. She was sixty percent sure she had renter's insurance. She had signed something when she moved in, and the landlord—Mr. Porchevsky, who communicated exclusively through text messages that contained no vowels and no warmth—had said something about liability, and she had nodded, and there had been a PDF, and she had maybe signed the PDF or maybe just opened the PDF and then closed it when her phone buzzed, which would mean she had been living uninsured for nineteen months in a building that the city of Chicago had last inspected during the second Obama administration, but that was a question for a version of Marla who had not just seen her ex's forearms in a climbing harness, and that version of Marla was unavailable at this time, please leave a message after the existential dread.

She pulled the blanket to her chin. The blanket smelled faintly of something she was choosing to classify as "apartment smell" and not "the exhalation of a parasitic organism digesting the substrate between her and structural collapse."

The mold spread in the dark, as it had always spread, with the patience of something that had never needed to rush because the things it consumed could not leave.

"I should deal with you," Marla said to the ceiling.

She did not deal with it.

She opened TikTok and watched a woman in Portland organize a pantry for forty-five minutes until she fell asleep with her mouth open and her phone resting on her sternum like a headstone, the screen dimming, the algorithm still offering its luminous suggestions to no one, the mold above her growing in its fractal patience, a black map of everywhere it intended to go.

## II.

At 2:14 a.m. on a Wednesday—five days after the painter's-tape experiment, three days after her phone charger died and she'd had to borrow one from the bodega owner downstairs who charged her a dollar and a conversation about his niece's wedding, one day after she'd noticed the stain had reached the light fixture and thought, with the detached clarity of someone watching their own life from the outside, *Huh, that's probably not load-bearing*—Marla decided to fix the problem.

She did not have the correct tools. She had rubber gloves from a box of Playtex Living that she'd bought to dye her hair six months ago and never used. She had a Swiffer WetJet, which was designed to clean floors and was being asked to perform well above its pay grade. She had, in lieu of the N95 respirator recommended by every mold remediation guide she'd bookmarked and not read, a bandana folded into a triangle and secured to her face with a hair tie that had lost most of its elasticity, a mask that would protect her from approximately nothing but that made her feel, in a way she could not defend intellectually, like she was doing something.

She stood on the futon. The futon groaned. She raised the Swiffer like a jousting lance and pressed its wet pad against the ceiling stain.

The mold did not wipe off. It *smeared*. The wet pad came away streaked with black and something else—a faint iridescence, like oil on water, that should not have been there and that Marla filed immediately under *weird but not my problem right now* alongside the noise her knee made when she climbed stairs and the fact that her upstairs neighbor appeared to be moving furniture at all hours despite never having visibly moved in. The smear on the ceiling looked worse than the stain. It now resembled less a dinner plate and more a Rorschach blot, and the Rorschach blot looked, if she tilted her head, like a map.

A map of what, she did not ask, because asking would have been the first step toward knowing, and knowing was a luxury reserved for people who could afford the $1,500–$6,000 remediation.

"You," she said, jabbing the Swiffer at the stain, "are literally the worst thing in my life, and I once dated a man who didn't believe in pillows. Not like, didn't prefer pillows. Didn't *believe in them*. Thought they were a conspiracy by the sleep-industrial complex. And you"—jab—"are worse than Kevin no-pillows Hartley, which is a bar so low it's underground, which is where you came from, probably, you off-brand nightmare, you ceiling-eating bastard, you absolute—"

She stopped.

Something in her head had moved.

Not moved the way a thought moves—Marla knew her own thoughts, knew their rhythms, their associative leaps, the way they pinballed from anxiety to joke to anxiety with the manic regularity of a metronome set to *millennial panic*. This was different. This was a sentence that arrived fully formed, syntactically ornate, and utterly foreign, laying itself across her thinking like a transparency placed over a page:

*The porosity of load-bearing membranes determines the rate at which one state of matter concedes its architecture to another, which is to say that a wall is only a wall for as long as both sides agree to the terms.*

Marla blinked.

"What the fuck," she said.

She stood on the futon with the Swiffer extended and the bandana-mask sliding down her nose and an expression that, had anyone been filming, would have read as either the face of a woman having a minor stroke or the face of a woman receiving an unwanted software update. The thought sat in her mind like a stone dropped into a pond, heavy and undeniable and absolutely not hers. She had never, in thirty-one years of thinking, thought the phrase *concedes its architecture*. She had barely passed AP Physics. She did not think about membranes. She thought about Kevin's forearms and staffing agencies and the remaining battery life on borrowed phone chargers. She thought in fragments and jokes and run-on sentences that her college writing professor had once described, not kindly, as "breathless." She did not think in subordinate clauses that nested inside each other like Russian dolls made of dread.

"Am I having a stroke," she said, to the stain, which glistened.

She lowered the Swiffer.

"Or did I absorb something from that HGTV marathon. Because I watched nine consecutive episodes of *Love It or List It* last Tuesday, and I'm not ruling out some kind of Hilary Farr–induced psychotic break."

The thought was gone. Or not gone—*receded*, the way a wave pulls back from the shore but leaves the sand darker, changed, carrying less of itself than before.

Beneath Unit 2R, in a sub-basement that did not appear on any blueprint filed with the City of Chicago Department of Buildings, the organism extended its filament network through the poured-concrete foundation and into the clay till of the Illinois glacial deposit. It did not think in the way that thinking things think. It processed. It distinguished between materials by dissolving the distinction—iteiteite—calciumiteite giving way toite silicate giving way to the warm carbon-wet of the water main, the water main giving way to the water, the water carrying spore-fragments south along the trunk sewer, east toward the drainage canal, north toward the lake. In seventeen locations across a four-block radius, the boundary between the built environment and the geological substrate was softening to the consistency of wetite chalk. In time—not geological time, not anymore, but human time, weeks, perhaps less—the grid of streets and foundations and basements and water mains that constituted the Humboldt Park neighborhood would begin to lose its Euclidean certainty. Corners would soften. Right angles would become suggestions. The organism did not want this; want was a concept that required a boundary between self and desire, and the organism had spent three hundred million years eating boundaries. It simply *did* this. It was a process, not an entity. A digestion with no stomach. A hunger with no mouth.

Its nearest edge was now eighteen inches from the copper pipes that supplied Marla Huang's apartment with water.

Marla dropped the Swiffer.

She climbed off the futon. She pulled the bandana off her face. She went to the kitchen—three steps, the apartment was very small—and poured herself a glass of water from the Brita pitcher that no longer had a working filter and drank it standing up, the way you drink water when you are trying to convince yourself that everything is fine, that the thought about membranes was just a weird glitch, a synapse misfiring, the cognitive equivalent of a check-engine light that you drive past by turning up the music.

"I need sleep," she said.

She went back to the futon. She looked up at the ceiling. The stain was the same size. (It was not the same size. It had grown by three millimeters during the ninety seconds she had been standing on the futon, which was too slow to see and too fast to be ordinary, which is the speed at which most true catastrophes travel.)

She pulled the blanket up. She closed her eyes.

In the dark, behind her eyelids, a sentence assembled itself with the unhurried precision of a hand writing in ink on wet paper: *All architecture is temporary. All membranes are generous. Everything you have built is an invitation to be unbuilt.*

Marla did not hear it. Or—she heard it the way you hear a refrigerator hum: constantly, and therefore never.

She slept.

The mold grew.

## III.

The video was filmed at 2:17 a.m. on a Wednesday by a twenty-four-year-old named Declan Moray, who was not supposed to be in the building and who was absolutely not supposed to still have a key, but who still had a key because Marla's roommate—former roommate, technically, Yesenia, who had moved to Pilsen three months ago to live with a ceramicist named Beau and who had left behind a succulent, four hundred dollars of unpaid electric bills, and Declan—had never asked for it back, and Declan sometimes stopped by the building to use the laundry machines because his own building's machines had been broken since the Biden administration.

Declan was carrying a basket of darks past Unit 2R when he heard the shrieking.

He did what any person of his generation would have done: he held up his phone.

Through the gap between the door and the frame—Marla's door did not fully close, another item on the list of things she had been meaning to address—he captured ninety-three seconds of footage. In the footage, a woman in underwear and rubber gloves stood on a futon and attacked a ceiling stain with a Swiffer WetJet while delivering a monologue that would later be described by *Vulture* as "if Fleabag had a lease dispute with Cthulhu." Her bandana mask was around her neck. Her hair was in a configuration that could not be called a bun or a ponytail but occupied a third, more desperate category. The stain on the ceiling glistened under the overhead light with a dark wet luster, and at certain angles—angles that Declan did not consciously notice but that the camera faithfully recorded—it appeared to *pulse*.

"—and the thing is," Marla was saying, jabbing the Swiffer, "the thing *is*, Mr. Porchevsky texts me back 'wll lk nto t' like he's paying by the vowel, like vowels cost money, which—maybe they do?
