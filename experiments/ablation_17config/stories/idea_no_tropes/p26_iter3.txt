# The Ash Street Lofthouse

## I.

The bird's name was Nettie, and she was eleven minutes late before anyone said so.

Up on the roof of 2416 Ash Street, the June light had gone the color of a healing bruise — purple and yellow above the Elevated, gray where the towers stood. Six people sat in five folding chairs and one overturned bucket, their faces turned east toward the training-toss line, which ran forty miles out to a parking lot behind a Wawa in Hammonton, New Jersey, where that morning Curtis Blay had opened the crate and watched thirty-one birds wheel up in a clap of wings and chalk-blue feathers and resolve themselves into a formation that, within eight seconds, found its compass and aimed itself home. Thirty had returned. They were in the coops now, behind the plywood walls that Manny Ferreira had rebuilt last April with marine-grade screws because Manny did not know how to build a thing halfway. Their cooing was the only sound on the roof. It sounded like an apology.

"She's working a thermal," said Tommy Wojcik, to no one, and shifted his weight on the bucket so that the plastic bowed and complained beneath him. Tommy was sixty-three and built like a filing cabinet — square, dense, full of things no one asked for. He had come to the roof in 1994, the year Lesniak Welding folded, and in the twenty-nine years since he had missed exactly four club days: one for each knee replacement, one for his mother's funeral, one for the birth of a grandson he now saw on alternate Thursdays by court arrangement. He knew Nettie was not working a thermal. A thermal would have added four minutes, not eleven. But Tommy had learned decades ago that the interval between the moment you know a thing and the moment you say it is the last free space a man has, and he intended to live in it as long as possible.

Beside him, not in the adjacent chair but in the one after that — because there was always a chair between them, a gap so habitual it had become invisible, the way a limp becomes a walk — Earl Tate cleaned his fingernails with a toothpick and did not look at the sky. Earl had stopped looking at the sky four minutes ago, which was when he had known. His body, a long assemblage of patient joints and economy of motion, was perfectly still in the aluminum frame of the chair, and inside that stillness was a sound like a dial tone — the flat, continuous noise his mind made when it was refusing to process what it had already processed. Nettie was his bird. Not in the sense of ownership — the club's birds were the club's — but in the sense that he had bred her, paired the parents, watched the egg, fed the squab with a syringe when the mother died on day four, and trained her on progressively longer tosses until she could hold a heading in crosswind like a nail driven into the sky. She was a blue-check hen, three years old, with a white flight feather on her left wing that made her visible at distances where other birds were just particulate. She had never been late. She had never been late, and Earl Tate was cleaning his fingernails because his hands needed occupation that was not prayer.

At the other end of the roof, as far from Earl as the tar paper allowed, Vincent Tate stood at the parapet with his forearms on the ledge, his chin on his forearms, his eyes on the middle distance where the skyline dissolved into signal towers. Vincent was forty-nine to Earl's fifty-four, and where Earl was still, Vincent was merely stopped — a body perpetually in the act of not yet moving, coiled with the particular restlessness of a man who had spent his adult life arriving at places moments after the useful moment had passed. He could see the towers from here. Everyone could see the towers from here. They had gone up the previous autumn, four of them in a line along the old Norfolk Southern right-of-way, skeletal and blank-faced, studded with gray rectangular panels that looked like the closed eyes of something pretending to sleep. The city had sent mailers. The mailers had used the words *connectivity* and *underserved* and *investment corridor.* The corridor ran directly across the birds' return line from the east — a wall of invisible frequency that, if you believed the forums (and the forums were all they had, because nobody who published peer-reviewed research gave a damn about a pigeon club in Kensington), scrambled the magnetite crystals in a bird's upper beak the way a magnet scrambles a compass. Vincent believed the forums. He believed them the way a man in a hospital bed believes the stain on the ceiling is shaped like something meaningful: because the alternative was that the world was random and owed him nothing, and he had already accepted that in too many other departments to accept it here.

Curtis Blay sat in the best chair — the one with both armrests intact — because Curtis always sat in the best chair, and because nobody contested it, not out of deference but out of the same instinct that makes people leave an empty seat next to the loudest person on a bus. Curtis was fifty-seven, slender, dark-skinned, with a goatee he maintained with a precision that his former colleagues at Dewey, Mack & Epstein would have recognized and that the Pennsylvania Bar Association would have described, if pressed, as characteristic. The disbarment had been eleven years ago. The specifics were a matter of public record and private mythology: a trust account, a decimal point, a client's money in a place it should not have been for a duration that could not be explained as clerical. Curtis had never admitted wrongdoing, which was either the mark of an innocent man or the mark of a guilty man trained in the law, and the people on this roof had long ago decided it didn't matter which. What mattered was that Curtis kept immaculate records, argued beautifully, and had once written a twelve-page letter to the Bureau of Licenses on the club's behalf that had resulted in a zoning variance so favorable the city clerk had called it "a goddamn poem." He sat now with his phone in his hand, the club's tracking spreadsheet open, Nettie's row still blank, and his face composed in the expression he had worn in courtrooms — neutral, calibrated, a face that gave nothing to opposing counsel. But his thumb was pressing the edge of the phone case hard enough to whiten the nail, and the omniscient fact of his interiority was this: Curtis Blay was the one who had suggested the Hammonton toss line in the first place, over Earl's preference for the safer Cherry Hill route, because the Hammonton line avoided a Red-tailed Hawk corridor near Voorhees, and the calculus had seemed sound, and now he was doing the math on whether the Hammonton line had put Nettie exactly fourteen degrees closer to the tower corridor on her inbound heading, and whether that fourteen degrees was the distance between a bird coming home and a bird flying in circles until her body gave out over some horse farm in Burlington County where nobody would find her.

Tommy said, "Might be she put down somewhere. Resting up."

Nobody answered.

Manny Ferreira stood by the coops with his hands in his pockets and his mouth set in a line that was the topographical record of forty years of swallowed commentary. Manny was the youngest club member at forty-six and the best builder among them, a welder by training and a perfectionist by pathology, and the coops he had constructed were clean, plumb, and insulated with rigid foam he had cut and fitted himself. The landing board — a narrow plywood shelf extending from the coop's entrance, painted white so the birds could see it from altitude — was empty. Manny was looking at it the way he had looked at the gate of Lesniak Welding on the day the padlock appeared: with the blankness of a man to whom the world has just demonstrated, for the fifth or sixth or twentieth time, that care is not a defense against loss.

And Doris Wellman, who was sixty-eight, who had served lunch to nine thousand children over thirty-one years at Conwell Middle School and had been given, upon retirement, a sheet cake and a gift card to Boscov's, who had come to this roof because her husband had been a founding member and her husband had been dead for eleven years and someone had to fill his chair or the chair would have to be acknowledged as empty — Doris sat with her purse in her lap and her eyes on the horizon line and knew, in the way she had always known things, which was quietly and earlier than anyone else, that Nettie was not coming back. She had known it at minute seven. She had known it the way she had known, in 1989, that the school was going to cut the hot lunch program, and in 2003, that Arthur's cough was not a cough, and in 2012, that the two Tate boys were never going to speak again. Doris's gift was not intuition. It was the ability to see what was already visible and decline to pretend otherwise. It was the least rewarding gift a person could have.

At minute nineteen, Earl stood up. The toothpick snapped in his fingers. He dropped both halves, walked to the roof door, and descended the stairs without speaking. His footsteps rang in the stairwell — a building that had once been a millinery warehouse, before it was a garment sweatshop, before it was subdivided into apartments so narrow you could touch both walls from the center of any room. The stairwell smelled of mildew and Fabuloso and the particular calcite scent of old plaster surrendering to gravity. Earl's footsteps faded.

Vincent, at the parapet, listened to them fade. He did not turn around.

"Well," Curtis said, and locked his phone screen.

Tommy got up from the bucket. Manny pulled his hands from his pockets and checked the water dispensers in the coops, because checking the water dispensers was what Manny did when there was nothing to do of any use. Vincent left by the far stairwell — the one that exited onto Ash Street rather than the alley — maintaining the geometry of separation so precisely that an observer would have thought the two brothers had rehearsed it. Curtis folded his chair and leaned it against the parapet wall at a specific angle, because Curtis was a man to whom disorder was personally offensive, and then he left, too.

Doris stayed.

The sky was fully dark now, the orange of the sodium lights competing with the purple remainder of dusk, and the towers along the right-of-way had begun to blink — red, then dark, then red — the steady pulse of an obstruction light that existed to warn aircraft but that looked, from the roof of 2416 Ash Street, like a heartbeat. Doris regarded the blinking for a long time. Then she opened her purse and removed a square of white cotton — a dinner napkin, pressed and folded, the kind of thing she kept on hand because she had spent a professional lifetime being prepared to clean up after others — and she crossed the roof to the landing board and laid the napkin over it, folding the edges down so the wind wouldn't take it, pressing the fabric flat against the plywood with the heels of her hands.

She stepped back.

It looked like what it was: a small white cloth over a surface where something was expected and would not arrive.

Doris picked up her purse, took the far stairs, and locked the roof door behind her with the key she'd carried since Arthur died.

On the landing board, the cloth shifted once in the breeze and was still.

## II.

The standings were published on a Tuesday, which was obligatory cruelty, because Tuesday was the day the club gathered informally at Patsy Giordano's shop on Frankford Avenue — a store whose legal name was Giordano Feed & Seed but whose inventory had contracted over the decades to consist primarily of pigeon supplies, rat bait, and a cooler of Tastykakes that Patsy's widow, Angela, restocked weekly because the men and Doris expected them and Angela was not a woman who disappointed on the small things, even if the large things — the rent, the insurance, the marriage — had disappointed her serially and without remorse. The standings came by email from the Combined Philadelphia & Tri-County Racing Pigeon Cooperative, which was the kind of organization that sustained itself on bylaws and carbon-copy paperwork and the conviction, held by its officers with the fervor of minor prophets, that pigeon racing was a sport in the formal sense and would be recognized as such by some governing body, someday, if only the records were sufficiently meticulous.

Curtis read them aloud. He didn't have to. Everyone had a phone. But Curtis read things aloud because Curtis had a voice built for reading things aloud — a baritone that still carried the cadences of oral argument, the slight pause before a damning figure, the modulation that could make a number sound like an indictment.

"Chestnut Hill Flyers," Curtis said. "Combined average velocity, twelve hundred eighteen yards per minute. Season points, eighty-four." He paused. The pause was architectural. "Ash Street Lofthouse. Combined average velocity, nine hundred forty-one yards per minute. Season points, thirty-nine."

"Out of what," Manny said.

"Out of any number you like, Manny. Pick a number. We're under it."

Tommy was leaning against the counter by the Tastykake cooler, arms crossed, his face expressing nothing, which for Tommy meant he was expressing everything but had locked the door. "They got that new loft," he said. "That climate-controlled thing."

"They got three lofts," Curtis said, scrolling. "One for breeding, one for training, one for race birds. Thermostat-regulated. Positive-pressure ventilation. The monthly electric bill on their setup would pay my rent through Easter." He scrolled further. "They're running GPS ankle bands — the Finnish ones, the Trackipigeon 4s. Three hundred dollars a unit. They've got a drone on a timer that does raptor-simulation flyovers at dawn to condition the birds to evasive patterns. They have a *nutritionist.*"

"For the birds," Doris said.

"For the birds."

A silence moved through the shop. It rearranged the dust.

"Well," said Vincent Tate, who was sitting on an upturned five-gallon bucket in the doorway — not fully inside, not fully outside, occupying the threshold the way he occupied most spaces in his life — "at least our birds got personality."

"Our birds got *missing* is what our birds got," Curtis said.

"Nettie had personality," Tommy offered.

"Nettie had a white feather and a sense of direction, and now she has neither. What she's got now is a zip code in somebody's field we'll never find. What personality has to do with any — "

"Chestnut Hill," said Manny, and the way he said it — quietly, with the specificity of a man identifying a species of beetle that has infested his home — stopped Curtis's sentence as effectively as a hand over his mouth. "Chestnut Hill. You know what they got out in Chestnut Hill? You know what they got?" He was not asking. "They got lawns. They got that nice stone.
