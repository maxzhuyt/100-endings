# The Familiar Catalogue of Dolores Kijek

---

The detective's pen clicks eleven times before he asks the first question. I count because counting is what I do when she is about to lie— I count the small things, the things with edges, the things that don't yet know they're evidence. Eleven clicks. The fluorescent tube above the table hums at sixty cycles but wavers, every four seconds, to fifty-eight, and in that waver the light goes the color of old milk. The laminate tabletop has a burn mark in the shape of a dog's ear where someone left a cigarette in 1974. Dolores's left hand is on the table and her thumb is pressing into the pad of her index finger, rolling the skin back and forth over the knuckle in that way she has— that way they all have— which means the lie is already forming behind her teeth like a second tongue and she hasn't decided on its shape yet.

I have been watching this hand, or hands like it, for longer than this city has had a name.

The detective's name is Gurley. He has a moustache that does not suit him and a wedding ring he touches when he thinks he's caught someone. He hasn't touched it yet. He thinks this is a simple case. He thinks Dolores is a simple woman. He has a file in front of him thick as a knuckle and he believes the file is the whole story, and I would feel sorry for him for this smallness of imagination except that he is, after all, correct— not about the file but about the outcome. She's going to give it to him. She always does.

Newark in October smells like diesel and leaf rot and the particular exhalation of old brick buildings losing their heat, and through the window— barred, third floor, the kind of glass that turns everything outside into an impressionist's headache— I can just hear the 27 bus pulling away from the stop on Washington Street, and the sound of its brakes is the sound of every bus that has ever pulled away from Dolores while she stood on the curb holding the wrong transfer, which is to say it is the sound of her entire life, a series of departures she could have prevented and chose to watch.

She could unmake the lock on this door. She could do it between one breath and the next. The detective would look down and his file would be in a language he didn't recognize. The tape recorder— boxy, beige, its reels turning like small dull eyes— would develop a sudden and permanent amnesia. She could walk out of here and leave nothing behind but a faint smell of ozone and the detective's bewildered sense that he'd been sitting alone in this room for an hour, interviewing a chair.

But she won't.

She never does.

"Mrs. Kijek," the detective says, and he pronounces it *Ki-jek*, hard j, the way they always do, and something in that mispronunciation— the flattened vowel, the clumsy American jaw failing the consonant cluster— splits me open like a seed and I am not in this room anymore, I am—

---

The kitchen on Piotrkowska. 1936 or 1937— time is less precise when I look backward, the years furred at their edges like old peaches. Ruta Kijek, Dolores's mother, standing over the stove in the apartment with the cracked window that let in a draft from the east that smelled of the dye works on Ogrodowa Street, and she is rendering fatback, and the fat is popping in the pan in a rhythm I can still feel in whatever I have instead of a body, and the oilcloth on the table is printed with yellow flowers that are not any flower that has ever existed, some factory's idea of a flower, and Ruta is muttering over the beet soup.

Not muttering. Working.

Her lips are moving and what she's saying is older than Polish, older than any of the languages Polish ate to become itself, and the soup is turning a color that beet soup cannot naturally achieve— a red so deep it is almost a sound, almost a low hum at the bottom of hearing— and what she is doing, with a precision that would make a watchmaker nervous, is folding the building inspector's attention into the broth. Specifically: she is convincing the inspector's perceptual field that the load-bearing wall in the stairwell, the one her husband took a sledgehammer to in order to widen the corridor so the Malinowski family's grandmother could get her wheelchair through, has always been an archway. Has never been a wall. The inspector will come on Thursday. He will see an archway. He will note it on his clipboard and move on to the Wiśniewski flat on the fourth floor, where the real violations are, and Ruta's beet soup will taste faintly of plaster for a week but the Malinowski grandmother will keep rolling her wheelchair through without hitting her elbows and no one will be fined and this is— this is what it is. This is what it has always been.

I watched Ruta through the steam and I thought: this woman could restructure the geometry of this entire building. She could convince the walls they were doors and the floors they were ceilings and the building inspector that he was a pigeon. She has the deepest probability-Loss field I've tasted in three generations. She could—

She could rename matter.

And she was fixing a building code violation with soup.

I knew Ruta's mother. I knew Ruta's mother's mother. I have known them back to the one who didn't have a kitchen, who had a clearing in trees that are not trees anymore, and the power has always been like this— enormous, stupid with potential, directed at the smallest possible target with the most exquisite possible care, and I have never understood it and I have never stopped watching it and I have never—

The detective clears his throat.

---

I am back in the room. The fluorescent tube flickers. Dolores's thumb is still working her index finger. The detective has asked a question I missed— something about a policy number, a date— and Dolores is nodding slowly, the way she nods when she's buying time, her chin dipping in that metronome rhythm that means the lie is almost ripe, she's almost got its shape, just one more second—

"The policy was taken out in 1971," the detective says. "That's what you're telling me."

"That's what I'm telling you," Dolores says.

"And the claim was filed—"

"In seventy-three. After the water damage."

"And the water damage—"

"The pipe," Dolores says. "In the bathroom. It burst."

The pipe did not burst. The pipe was *persuaded.* I was there. I am always there. Dolores stood in the bathroom of the apartment on South 10th Street at 2:47 in the morning, wearing the robe with the coffee stain on the left lapel, and she laid her hand against the copper pipe behind the toilet and she spoke to it in a language that copper understands, which is not a language of words but a language of temperature and crystalline structure, and she told the pipe that it was tired. That it had been holding itself together for thirty years and it had done enough and it could rest now. And the pipe— I felt it happen, felt the molecular decision like a sigh translating through the wall— the pipe *agreed.* It let go. Water everywhere. A legitimate-looking disaster. $4,200 from Prudential.

The things she could do with copper alone. The symphonies of atomic persuasion she carries in her fingertips. And she used it to burst a pipe in a bathroom in Newark for forty-two hundred dollars, which she spent on rent and a winter coat and a secondhand television and sixteen weeks of breathing room before the next crisis, and this is the part where I would scream if I had a throat, but I don't have a throat, I have only this— this watching, this endless attendance at the bonfire of Kijek potential.

---

"We've been looking at your claims history, Mrs. Kijek." The detective opens the file. He is enjoying this. The moustache twitches. He has arranged his evidence like a man arranging silverware for a dinner party— everything in its proper place, the forks of accusation, the knives of documentation.

I could give you the catalogue myself, Detective. I could save you the trouble. My catalogue is longer than yours and more detailed and I have been compiling it not for months but for decades, each entry annotated with the specific quality of light in the room and the particular grade of Dolores's desperation and the exact distance between what she did and what she could have done.

Let me tell you about the gas meter on Hawthorne Avenue.

1967. Dolores was living above a hardware store run by a man named Sabo who charged her eleven dollars too much each month and dared her to complain. The gas meter was in the basement, a brass-faced thing with dials like small clocks, and Dolores went down there on a Tuesday night— I remember the night, the specific cold of it, November air coming through the basement window sharp as a word— and she laid both hands on the meter and she *convinced* it. Not broke it, not tampered with it. Convinced it. She introduced a subtle theological doubt into the meter's mechanism. She made the dials believe they had been counting too fast, that they owed her a correction, that the gas flowing through the pipe was lighter and less consequential than they'd been reporting. The meter became, overnight, an instrument of gentle fiction. She saved nine dollars a month for fourteen months. One hundred and twenty-six dollars total. I could feel the hex humming every time I passed that basement, a low domestic frequency like a refrigerator that knows it's being listened to.

She could have— but no. Not yet. Let me finish the catalogue.

The landlord on South Orange Avenue. Bielik. A man built like a filing cabinet, all hard corners and small drawers. He came to Dolores's door on a Friday in March of 1969 with an eviction notice he'd typed himself on a typewriter with a broken *e*, so every *e* in the document floated slightly above the line, giving the whole thing the look of a ransom note written by someone with aspirations. He stood in her doorway and told her she had thirty days and Dolores stood there in her housecoat and looked at him and I felt it— the way I always feel it— the probability field around her tightening like a fist, the air getting denser, the possible futures narrowing—

She didn't hex him in the doorway. She waited until he was on the stairs. She waited until his back was turned because even in her rage she was precise, she was *architectural* about it, the way Ruta was architectural about the beet soup, and what she did was: she spoke to his knees. Specifically to the cartilage. She told the cartilage a brief and convincing story about age and calcium and the specific betrayals of a body that has climbed too many stairs collecting rent from people who can't pay it, and Bielik's knees locked up on the second-floor landing and stayed locked for thirty-one days— one day longer than the eviction notice, a petty specificity that I loved her for, God help me— and he told the neighbors it was gout and Dolores got three more months in the apartment before she moved anyway, to the place on South 10th with the pipe she would later befriend, and Bielik's knees never fully recovered and he sold the building in 1972 to a man who was, if anything, worse, but that's not the point, the point is never the outcome, the point is the *scale*—

Do you see? Do you see what I'm telling you?

The woman sitting across from Detective Gurley with her thumb working her index finger and her jaw set in that way that means she's calculating not magic but *odds*, human odds, the grubby arithmetic of what she thinks she can get away with— this woman could disassemble a building at the level of its molecular grammar. She could convince gravity to take a holiday. I have watched her, in unguarded moments, in the kitchen late at night when she thinks no one is watching (and she is right, in a sense— I am no one, I am the permanent audience of one that she has never acknowledged, never spoken to, never once glanced at with recognition even though I am always *here*, in the corner of her eye, in the thickening of air behind her left shoulder)— I have watched her, absently, while waiting for water to boil, make a spoon spin on the countertop by looking at it with mild interest, and the spoon spun like a compass needle seeking north, except the north it found was Dolores, everything finds its north in Dolores whether she wants it to or not—

And she uses this to forge insurance claims.

She has a gift, the detective might say, for paperwork. For the small grift. For the con that fits inside an envelope.

Let me tell you about the brooch.

1961. Autumn. The brooch was amber— not the amber of the word *amber* but the amber of actual amber, the fossilized tears of ancient trees, and this particular piece was old, old in a way that made my attention itch, and it was set in a silver bezel that Dolores had acquired from a woman on Ferry Street who was either a Kijek cousin or a Kijek enemy or both, because with the Kijeks these categories have never been mutually exclusive. The brooch was cursed. Not by Dolores— it came pre-cursed, carrying a hex so old its syntax was in a dialect I could barely parse, something from the eastern forests, something that tasted of pine resin and spite. Dolores cleaned it, reset the hex to her own specifications (I watched her do it at the kitchen table, working with a pair of tweezers and a candle and a focus so intense the candle flame bent toward her like a flower toward light), and she fenced it.

She fenced it to a man named Potocki on Market Street, a man with soft hands and hard eyes who dealt in estate jewelry and didn't ask questions. She got forty dollars for it. Forty dollars. The brooch, as Dolores had reconfigured it, would bring its wearer a steady, reliable, almost undetectable current of good luck— not lottery-winning luck, not destiny-altering luck, but the small luck of finding parking spaces and avoiding head colds and having the bus arrive just as you reach the stop. It was, in its way, a masterpiece of hexcraft. A tiny engine of probabilistic grace, hand-built by a woman who understood that the best magic is the magic no one notices.

Potocki sold it for three hundred. The buyer was a dentist's wife from Montclair. She wore it every day for six years and never caught the flu and always found street parking and never once suspected that her good fortune was manufactured. It was, by any measure, a perfect con. An invisible gift disguised as a transaction.

Forty dollars.

And the psychic storefront. I haven't told you about the psychic storefront.

On Broadway, between a bodega that sold plantains and a shoe repair that had been closed for longer than it was ever open, Dolores rented a narrow space in 1970 and put a neon hand in the window— the kind of hand that means *palm reading*, the universal signifier of retail mysticism— and she set up shop. MADAM DOLORES. READINGS. ADVICE. SPIRITUAL CONSULTATION. Behind a curtain of glass beads that clattered like teeth in the draft from the street.

Here is what happened behind the beads: women came. They always come. They came in headscarves and housedresses, in winter coats too thin for the weather, in shoes worn down to the geometry of their particular sorrows, and they sat across from Dolores at a card table draped in velvet she'd bought at a remnant sale, and they put their hands out, and Dolores looked at their hands and she *read* them. Really read them. Not the lines— the lines are nothing, the lines are decoration, the body's doodling— but the probability fields, the weight of possible futures hanging off each woman like coats on a rack. She could see which futures were heavy, likely to fall. She could see which were light, barely there, needing encouragement. She could see the tumors forming, the husbands leaving, the children getting better, the jobs appearing, the money arriving, the money not arriving. She saw everything.

And she charged too little. She charged three dollars, or five, or sometimes nothing if the woman across from her was the particular kind of broke that Dolores recognized in her own bones, the kind of broke that is not an absence of money but a *weather system*, a climate you live inside where every decision is made under atmospheric pressure and nothing is ever dry. She couldn't help it. She couldn't charge a desperate woman what the reading was worth because the reading was worth — how do you price the truth about a person's future?
