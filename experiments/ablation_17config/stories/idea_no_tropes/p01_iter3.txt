# The Dominant Vocal Specimen

## I.

One develops, in the practice of social commentary for a readership of eleven hundred souls—fewer if one discounts the illiterate, the deceased who remain on the subscription rolls through administrative inertia, and Mr. Fennwick, who employs the *Harlow Creek Clarion* exclusively as kindling—a certain facility for euphemism. The ungenerous reader might call it dishonesty. I prefer to think of it as *architectural*: one constructs a sentence the way one constructs a doorway, so that the truth may pass through it without scraping its shoulders.

I mention this by way of credentials.

My name is of no consequence; my column is called "Notes on Decorum," and it appears every Thursday in the space between the land-office advertisements and the report on alkaline content in the municipal water supply, which has been holding steady at levels the Territorial Health Board describes as "regrettable." I have written "Notes on Decorum" for six years. I have adjudicated the correct deployment of napkin rings at altitudes above four thousand feet. I have arbitrated the question of whether a woman may remove her gloves to accept a plate of elk at a standing supper (she may, provided the elk is braised and the company is not Presbyterian). I am, in short, a person of no particular importance who has made a modest living from the precise management of tone.

I say all of this so that you will understand the nature of the instrument through which the following events were perceived, and make your own allowances for its distortions.

Now: the splinter.

It arrived on a Tuesday, six weeks before the events I mean to relate, falling from a sky so cloudless and still that the air itself seemed to have opinions about being disturbed. I was crossing the alkaline flats east of town on my way back from the Perdue homestead, where I had been researching a column on the etiquette of childbirth announcements when the child's paternity is, let us say, a matter of active municipal speculation. The splinter was perhaps three inches long, metallic, warm to the touch, and possessed of a faint iridescence that shifted when I tilted it—not like mother-of-pearl, which knows what colors it contains, but like something that was *deciding*. It had embedded itself in the hard-pan with the authority of a thing that had fallen from a great height, though it weighed almost nothing. I picked it up because I am a columnist and therefore constitutionally incapable of leaving anything alone, and I put it in my breast pocket because one does not discard what one does not understand. That is not etiquette. That is simply sense.

The following morning I woke to the sound of Dorothy Keller thinking about her husband's feet.

I do not mean I heard Dorothy Keller speaking about her husband's feet. I mean I heard the thought itself, arriving in my mind with the greasy, unwelcome intimacy of a stranger's warmth on a recently vacated chair: *If he does not trim those nails I will wait until he is asleep and I will do it myself and if I cut him it will be his own fault and God will know it was an accident even if I do not entirely mean it as one.*

I sat up in bed. The thought continued. It was joined by others.

Within the hour I understood three things: first, that the splinter had done something to me that I lacked the vocabulary to describe and the theology to accommodate; second, that the range of this affliction extended at minimum to the edges of town and with concentration somewhat beyond; and third, that the interior lives of my neighbors were, without exception, *significantly worse* than I had imagined, and I had imagined them to be quite bad.

I wish to be precise about the nature of this condition, because precision is the only dignity available to me. I do not "read minds" in the manner of a carnival act. I do not choose a subject and plumb their depths. What I experience is closer to weather: a constant, indiscriminate, ambient saturation of thought, every waking mind within range producing its ceaseless, self-regarding noise, and all of it arriving in my perception without hierarchy or mercy. I can, with effort, attend to a single stream the way one might attend to a single conversation at a particularly terrible dinner party, but the rest does not stop. It pools. It presses. At night, when most of the town is asleep and only the insomniacs and the guilty remain conscious, the noise thins to something almost navigable, and I can nearly remember what silence was. Nearly.

In six weeks I have not slept more than two hours consecutively. I have developed a tremor in my left hand that I conceal by keeping it in my pocket or wrapped around a coffee cup. My column has, I am told, improved markedly. I attribute this to the fact that I am no longer speculating about the inner lives of my subjects but *reporting* them, filtered through enough circumlocution to preserve deniability. When I wrote, two weeks ago, that "Mrs. Halloran's commitment to her Wednesday Bible study is exceeded only by her commitment to the private devotions she conducts on Thursday afternoons at the Leward Hotel, which devotions appear to require the spiritual counsel of Mr. Ambrose Pike and a quantity of sherry that would be considered medicinal only by the most liberal interpretation of pharmacy," I was not being wry. I was being *conservative*. What Mrs. Halloran actually thinks about during those Thursday afternoons would constitute an emergency if translated into print, and not merely a social one.

But I have been circling. The columnist's vice: approaching the subject as though it were a horse that might bolt. Let me come to it.

The judge.

I cannot set down his name. This is not discretion—I have precious little left—but something closer to the superstition one feels about naming a storm while still inside it. The town calls him "the judge" and the town's minds call him worse. He presides over the Third District Territorial Court, which in practice means he presides over everything, because in a town of eleven hundred souls a man who can destroy you with a sentence does not need a jurisdiction.

I knew him, before the splinter, as everyone knew him: by reputation, by anecdote, by the particular quality of silence that entered a room when he entered it, the way sound is said to behave in the presence of certain very large predators. He was tall, though not unusually so. He was not physically imposing. What he possessed was a *voice*—not in the sense of volume, though he could fill a courtroom the way a church organ fills a nave, but in the sense of *instrument*. He had, over a career of some thirty years, refined the art of public speech into something that I can only describe, with the full weight of professional judgment, as a *practice of cruelty so elegant it constituted a civic aesthetic*. He did not shout. He did not bluster. He *constructed*. His sentences were architectural; his pauses were *load-bearing*. He could, with a single dependent clause, so thoroughly dismantle a man's self-regard that the man would not realize he had been insulted until three hours later, at which point the shame would arrive all at once, like a bill.

I had heard the stories, naturally. Everyone had. The attorney from Santa Fe who wept on the courthouse steps—not from the ruling, which was just, but from the language of the ruling, which was *surgical*. The defendants who accepted conviction rather than face cross-examination. The editor of the rival paper in Dawson who had published an unflattering profile and received, in return, not a lawsuit but a public letter so precisely, lovingly devastating that the editor sold his press and moved to Oregon, where by all reports he now builds furniture and does not speak often.

These were the stories. What I heard, after the splinter, was worse.

The judge's mind—and I must describe this carefully, because the inadequacy of my description is itself a kind of data—did not sound like other minds. Most people think in a sort of rough, looping improvisation, a jazz of anxiety and desire and half-finished sentences that circle back on themselves like dogs settling into sleep. The judge thought in *drafts*. Even his idle ruminations had structure: premise, development, conclusion. When he looked at a person, I could hear him *composing* them—finding the precise word for their weakness, filing it, sharpening it against the stone of his attention with the leisurely care of a man who knows he may not need the knife today but enjoys the sound of the whetstone.

On the Sunday I mean to begin with—the last ordinary Sunday—I sat on the porch of the Clarion office with my coffee and my tremor and I listened to the town think. It was three o'clock. The light had that particular quality of late Western afternoon that makes everything look like evidence: sharp-edged, without charity, every surface testifying against itself.

From the Keller house: Dorothy, again on the subject of feet, with a subsidiary thread concerning a roast she suspected had turned but intended to serve regardless, in the spirit of what she internally characterized as "economy" and what I would characterize as "aggression."

From the Mercantile: Harold Goss, calculating in exact and damning figures the degree to which he had been shortchanging the Paiute women on flour and the degree to which he believed this was not actually shortchanging because, and I will reproduce the thought with the fidelity it deserves, *they don't use it the way we use it, it's different when they buy it, it's practically a favor.* Harold Goss's mind was a place I visited as seldom as possible, not because it was unusually cruel but because its cruelty was so *ordinary*, so absolutely without the dignity of deliberation, that it made me want to lie down in the alkaline flats and simply *stop*.

From the church: Reverend Mosely, concluding his sermon, thinking—and I must ask the reader to hold two things at once here—simultaneously about the grace of God and about the ankles of Alice Weekes in the second pew, and managing, with a mental dexterity that I confess impressed me, to feel no contradiction whatsoever between these two streams. The soul is a versatile instrument.

And from the courthouse, steady as a pulse, thinking about nothing in particular and yet thinking about it *better* than anyone else thought about anything: the judge. He was in his office. He was reading. The text was, I believe, Cicero—he kept a Latin library and the original texts were the only things I ever heard him think about with anything resembling tenderness. His mind moved over the sentences with the appreciative attention of a craftsman examining another craftsman's joints. Occasionally he would pause and hold a phrase up to his inner light and turn it, admiring the engineering, and then—this is the part that never failed to unsettle me—he would *improve* it. Silently, privately, with the reflexive ease of a man straightening a picture frame, he would reconstruct Cicero's rhetoric and move on, as though the alteration were merely a courtesy he was extending to the dead.

That was Sunday.

The town was as it always was. Small. Cruel in the ordinary ways. Full of people performing goodness in public while their minds seethed with the magnificent, unedited obscenity of actual human thought. I sat on my porch and I held my coffee with both hands to hide the one that shook, and I breathed, and I listened, and I set none of it down in my notebook because there are limits to what a person can do with information, even—especially—when the information will not stop.

## II.

They came from the east, which was wrong.

Let me explain. The road from the east crosses thirty miles of alkaline flat before it reaches Harlow Creek. There is nothing out there. The flats are white and level and merciless, and in the late afternoon they produce mirages of such hallucinatory specificity that teamsters have reported seeing not just water but *particular* water: a lake they visited as children, a horse trough outside a specific saloon in a specific town they have been trying to forget. The flats are a canvas for the mind's projections, and this is why, when the figures first appeared on the eastern road at approximately four o'clock on a Monday afternoon, no one was alarmed. Mirages were common. Movement on the eastern road was not.

I was in my office. I felt the town notice before I saw anything myself—a ripple in the ambient noise, like a stone dropped in the middle of that endless psychic lake. Lottie Groves, who kept the post office, was the first to form a coherent thought: *Someone coming. From the east. That's peculiar.* Peculiar was correct. The road from the east connected to nothing. It was a road in the way that a scar is a road: it recorded a passage that had occurred once and was not expected to recur.

I stepped onto the porch.

The figures were still distant—a quarter mile, perhaps—but they were resolving out of the heat-shimmer with an insistence that mirages do not typically display. Mirages flicker. They negotiate with the eye. These figures simply *advanced*, with a steadiness that suggested not urgency but *appointment*, the unhurried confidence of guests who know they are expected even when they are not.

There were five of them.

I will do my best with the description, though I confess that what I am about to set down is not entirely what I *saw* but rather what I was able to *retain*, which I have come to understand are different things when the subject possesses certain properties. They were tall. They moved on what I must, for lack of a better term, call legs—two of them—but the locomotion was slightly wrong, as though they had learned bipedal movement from a reliable source that had nevertheless omitted some crucial footnote. Their surfaces—I cannot call it skin without misleading you—had a quality I associate with certain insects: not chitinous, exactly, but *finished*, the way a lacquered thing is finished, the way a thing that does not intend to be penetrated is finished. They wore something. It may have been clothing; it may have been themselves, differently. In the late light, crossing the white flats, they were the color of a bruise in its last days: that uncertain yellow-violet that means the damage is old but not gone.

I am aware that this description is inadequate. The inadequacy is, I think, accurate.

What struck me—what struck the town, because I felt the collective intake like a held breath held by a hundred lungs—was not their strangeness but their *deportment*. They walked in a formation that was legible as *courteous*: a lead figure slightly ahead, the others arranged behind in a pattern that suggested deference without subservience. When they reached the first buildings at the edge of town—Keller's feed store, the old assay office—the lead figure *stopped*. It turned to its companions. There was a brief pause that had the unmistakable quality of consultation. And then it—I will use "it" until language fails me more completely than it already has—turned to face the street, and it *waited*.

It waited the way a person waits at a door they have knocked on. It waited with its hands—analogous to hands, I should say; they were more articulate than hands, possessing what appeared to be additional joints that lent their resting position a quality of *calligraphic* repose—folded in front of it, at approximately the height where a waistcoat would be if it were wearing a waistcoat, which it was not but which its posture somehow *implied*.

The town's collective mind, which I will attempt to render as faithfully as I can:

Harold Goss: *Oh Christ. Oh Christ oh Christ. Are those. What are those. My flour. Who will buy my flour.*

Dorothy Keller: *They'll walk on the garden. They always walk on the garden. Everything walks on the garden.*

Reverend Mosely, and I record this with neither judgment nor surprise: *Lord God Almighty, protect us. Also, I left the candles burning. If the church burns down while I am being killed by whatever those are, that would be—is it vanity to worry about the candles? It is vanity. Lord, forgive my vanity. But the candles.*

Alice Weekes, with a clarity of perception I had not previously associated with her: *They're waiting for permission. Look at them. They're waiting.*

Alice Weekes was correct.
