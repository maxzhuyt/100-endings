# The Varick Street Interpreter

---

From March of 1940 to January of 1951, I served as a contract interpreter, Series GS-1040, at the Immigration and Naturalization Service intake facility at 201 Varick Street, New York, New York. My working languages were Polish, Hungarian, and German, with a secondary certification in Czech that the Bureau invoked when it suited them and forgot when it did not. I am told that the intake logbooks from that period survive, in part, among the Records of the Immigration and Naturalization Service held at the National Archives facility in College Park, Maryland, Record Group 85, though I have not gone to look for them. I would not know what to do with my own handwriting now.

What follows concerns a Tuesday morning in February 1951 — my last month at the Bureau, though I did not yet know it would be. I offer it not as testimony, which has a legal definition I spent eleven years learning to respect, but as an account, which has no definition at all and is therefore honest.

The Bureau's 1951 procedural manual for contract interpreters — I kept my copy; it is on the shelf behind me as I write this, its blue cover gone the color of a vein — contains the following instruction in Section 4(b):

*The interpreter shall render the statements of the alien in the first person, preserving the declarant's meaning without addition, omission, or editorial modification. The interpreter is not an advocate, a counselor, or a witness. The interpreter is a instrument of the record.*

*An* instrument. I have remembered the typographical error for thirty years. I do not think I have remembered anything in my life as precisely as that missing article, that small bureaucratic admission that even the manual that governed my tongue could not govern its own.

---

The Varick Street facility occupied the lower floors of a federal building that had been built for other purposes and converted, the way all holding spaces are converted, with the minimal effort required to make human processing possible and the minimal expense required to make it legal. The intake floor was on the second level: a room the size of a modest ballroom partitioned by counters into lanes, each lane ending at a window where an examiner sat with a stamp and a stack of forms, each form a different color for a different outcome. I-130, white. I-129, green. The denial form — I do not remember its number, only its color, which was yellow, and which I still cannot see in any context without feeling a specific sensation in my stomach, the way a surgeon might flinch at a particular shade of red.

The benches were oak and had been installed, I believe, during the building's previous life as something else — a meeting hall, perhaps, or a courtroom — and they bore the smooth depressions of decades of sitting that had nothing to do with us. The overhead lights were fluorescent, installed in 1946 to replace the original fixtures, and they gave the room the complexion of an operating theater: everyone looked slightly ill, which was useful, in a way, because many of the people on those benches were slightly ill, and the light made this fact seem like a property of the room rather than a property of their lives.

I arrived each morning at seven forty-five. The examiners arrived at eight. The doors opened to the public at eight-thirty, but the line began forming outside on Varick Street by six, sometimes earlier, and through the windows of the intake floor you could hear it before you could see it — a murmur that was not quite language, the sound of many people talking quietly in many tongues simultaneously, which produced an effect less like conversation than like weather, a low-pressure front of human sound that pressed against the glass.

By nine o'clock the room would be at capacity. The benches full. The lanes backed up to the door. Four or five interpreters working simultaneously at adjacent windows, so that a sentence in Hungarian overlapped with a sentence in Italian overlapped with a sentence in Yiddish, and the air in the room became a kind of palimpsest, layers of meaning scraped and overwritten in real time. I learned to hear selectively — to pull my language out of the layered sound the way a violinist hears her own part in an orchestra — but in the early years I would sometimes lose my thread, and a phrase in Italian would drift into my ear and lodge there, and I would have to stop and find my way back to the Hungarian or the Polish the way a reader finds her place on a page after glancing up at a noise.

The smell was wool and paper and the particular institutional soap the Bureau stocked in its lavatories, which had no scent of its own but which functioned, through years of association, as the smell of bureaucracy itself — the olfactory signature of waiting. On wet days, which February provided in abundance, the wool smell intensified and mixed with the steam from the radiators, which clanked in the walls like prisoners signaling between cells, and the room took on the warm, damp, bodily atmosphere of a cabin on a ship, which I sometimes thought was not accidental, not designed but rhyming — these were people who had recently been in the holds of ships, and here they were again in a warm, enclosed, rocking space, waiting to learn the next thing that would happen to them.

On this particular Tuesday, February sixth, the weather was cold and clear. I remember this because the light from the windows on the south wall was direct rather than diffused, and it fell across the intake lanes in columns that caught the dust and lint suspended in the air, so that each lane appeared to have a pillar of illuminated particulate matter standing in it, a slow-turning column of bright debris, and the people waiting in those lanes walked through these columns without noticing them, the way one walks through the obvious things.

I took my position at Window 7, my usual station, adjacent to Maria Fontana at Window 6.  Maria interpreted Italian and Spanish. She had a voice like a cellar door — deep, heavy, hard to open — and I could hear it even when I was not listening for it, a basso continuo beneath my own work. She would retire six months after I did, and she would die in 1964, and I mention her here only because she was the other set of professional ears in the room that morning, and because she heard everything I heard, and because she never mentioned any of it afterward, not once, which I took as proof that she had heard it all.

---

The Hungarian woman's name — I will call her by her initial, Mrs. K., because the logs in College Park contain her full name and I would not want anyone to locate her there and confuse this account with the record — arrived at Window 7 at approximately nine-twenty. She was accompanied, in the procedural sense, by her husband, whom I will call Mr. W., an American citizen from Bethlehem, Pennsylvania, who had petitioned for her admission under Section 4(a) of the War Brides Act as amended, though they were not, strictly speaking, war brides in the conventional sense, because they had not met during the war. They had not met at all.

Mr. W. had married Mrs. K. by proxy in September of 1950, through a legal proceeding conducted in Budapest by an attorney who stood in the groom's place and repeated the groom's vows while the groom himself was in Bethlehem, Pennsylvania, working, I would later learn, in a machine shop that manufactured parts for locomotives. The marriage was legal under Hungarian civil law. The Immigration Bureau recognized it provisionally, contingent upon the in-person interview, which was the interview I was about to interpret.

What the proxy marriage meant, as a practical matter, was this: Mr. W. and Mrs. K. were husband and wife, had been husband and wife for five months, and had never been in the same room.

They had corresponded. He had written first — he told me this during the interview, offering it as though it were a credential, a proof of seriousness — in response to a listing in a church bulletin circulated among Hungarian Reformed congregations, a bulletin that matched American men of Hungarian descent with displaced women seeking sponsorship. He chose her name, he said, because it was the same as his mother's name. She wrote back because he was the only one who wrote back. The letters had continued for a year and a half, through the bulletin's network and then directly, and then through the attorney, and then through the Bureau, and now through me.

The examiner, a Mr. Linden — his name I will give in full, because he was a federal employee operating in his official capacity and his actions are a matter of public record — read the standard intake questions from the I-130 spousal petition adjudication form.

"State the date and place of marriage."

I rendered this in Hungarian. Mrs. K. answered. I rendered her answer in English: "September fourteenth, nineteen-fifty. Budapest. The Sixth District civil registry."

"Has the petitioner met the beneficiary in person prior to this interview?"

This question I translated slowly, because I knew the answer, and because I knew that the answer, spoken aloud in a federal intake room at nine-twenty on a Tuesday morning, would perform a small act of violence against the word *marriage* that no one in the room would acknowledge. The Bureau asked the question not to learn the answer but to place it on the record. The record required it. The record was the point.

"No," Mrs. K. said.

"No," I said.

Mr. W. was standing on the other side of the counter, in the petitioner's lane, approximately four feet from his wife. He was a short man with large hands who wore a brown suit that fit him well enough in the shoulders but whose sleeves were slightly too long, so that his hands appeared to be emerging from hiding. He had removed his hat when he entered the building and was holding it in front of him with both hands, turning it slowly by the brim, and I could see that the inner band of the hat was dark with old sweat, the stain of years of wearing the same hat to the same job, and the hat was the most honest thing about him — it had no idea it was at an immigration hearing.

Mrs. K. was thin and had dark hair and wore a coat that had been good once, good in a European way, with a cut that implied a city and a life before this, and she held her documents in both hands against her sternum, the way one holds a hymnal, with a grip that suggested not reverence but the fear that someone would take them.

When Mr. Linden finished the preliminary questions and began the verification of Mrs. K.'s biographical data — her date of birth, her municipality of origin, her father's name — there came a pause in which Mrs. K. was required to present her documents to the examiner. She had to set them on the counter and slide them through the opening at the base of the window, a gap of perhaps four inches between the counter's surface and the glass partition, designed for the passage of forms.

She slid the documents through.

Mr. Linden picked them up and began reviewing them.

And in the silence of that review — a silence that lasted perhaps forty-five seconds, during which the examiner read and the fluorescent lights hummed the note they always hummed, which I had once, in a moment of boredom, identified as a slightly flat B — Mrs. K. and Mr. W. looked at each other.

I do not mean they glanced at each other. I do not mean they made eye contact of the kind that occurs between strangers in a room. I mean they looked. They took the measure. Each face was the answer to a question the other had been asking for a year and a half through letters and photographs — had been asking, in fact, since before they began writing, since the moment each of them had decided that writing to a stranger was preferable to whatever they were otherwise facing, which in her case was a displacement camp near Salzburg and in his case was a house in Bethlehem with a kitchen table where one person ate.

The look lasted the duration of Mr. Linden's review, perhaps forty-five seconds, perhaps a minute. It was not dramatic. Neither of them smiled. Neither of them moved. They stood on opposite sides of the counter and the partition and entered with their eyes the new fact of each other, and the length of the look was itself a kind of language — a duration that would have been uncomfortable between strangers and was therefore a declaration, because they were not strangers, they were married, they had simply never before had the occasion to be married in the same room.

When Mr. Linden looked up from the documents and asked his next question, Mr. W. made a small sound — not a word, not in any language — a brief exhalation, a compression of the chest, as though he had been holding his breath and had only now remembered that breathing was among the activities one was expected to continue during an immigration hearing. And his hands, which had been turning the hat, stopped.

I translated the next question: "Can you state the petitioner's full legal name?"

Mrs. K. said the name. She said it in the Hungarian way, surname first, and she said the surname correctly — she had, after all, been writing it on envelopes for eighteen months — but the given name she said with a slight displacement of the first vowel, the vowel pushed forward in the mouth and given a length it did not possess in English, so that the name — I will not write it here, but it was a common American name, two syllables, the kind of name one hears shouted across a factory floor or called in for dinner — the name was rendered, in her pronunciation, a fraction more tender and a fraction more foreign than the man himself had probably ever heard it.

He did not correct her.

Mr. Linden noted the name on the form. I noted the name on the form. The record was satisfied.

---

The second couple arrived at my window at approximately ten-fifteen, though *couple* is not the word the Bureau would have used, and the documents that governed their appearance at intake were not petitions of marriage or sponsorship but a bail bond, Form I-352, posted by one party on behalf of the other.

The man was a Greek merchant sailor — I will call him Mr. D. — who had jumped ship in Baltimore six months earlier and been apprehended in New York, where he had been living, in violation of his crewman's landing permit, in a rooming house in Astoria. The woman was the owner of that rooming house. I will call her Mrs. P. She was also Greek, a naturalized citizen, and she had posted his bond — five hundred dollars, which in 1951 was serious money for a woman who let rooms — and she was present at intake as his surety, meaning the person financially and legally responsible for ensuring that he appeared at all future hearings and did not, as the Bureau's language put it, "abscond from the jurisdiction."

My Greek was not certified. I should say this plainly. When Greek-language cases came to my window, which happened rarely, it was because the Bureau's Greek interpreter, a man named Stavridis, was occupied elsewhere, and I was asked to manage with what I had, which was a reading knowledge acquired in college and approximately two hundred words of spoken Greek acquired from a woman I had known in another context, in another decade, whose name is not part of this account. I could follow most of what was said. I could render the essential meaning. I could not hear the nuances that a native interpreter would have heard, and I say this not as an apology but as a fact that bears on what follows: I was listening harder than usual, and when one listens harder than usual, one hears things that have nothing to do with language.

The examiner for this case was also Mr. Linden — the same window, the same morning, the same flat B-natural from the fluorescent tube above us — and the questions were different in kind from the spousal petition. These were bond-verification questions. They had the texture of an audit.

"State your relationship to the respondent."

Mrs. P. answered in English. Her English was good — accented, quick, the English of someone who had been using it for business for many years and did not have time for it to be pretty. "He is my tenant. He lives in my house. I am posting for him."

"How long has the respondent resided at your address?"

"Six months. Since August."

"Are you aware that as surety you are financially liable for the respondent's compliance with all terms of release?"

"I am aware."

Mr. D. stood beside her and said nothing, because the bond-verification questions were directed at the surety, not the respondent, and because his English was poor, and because — I understood this gradually, over the course of the interview — he had been instructed by her to say nothing, not because she doubted his ability to speak but because she doubted the Bureau's ability to hear.

He was a large man who occupied his body with the particular economy of someone accustomed to confined spaces — the posture of a man who had spent years in engine rooms and below decks. He stood with his shoulders slightly forward, his hands at his sides, and he wore a white shirt and a dark jacket that did not match his trousers but that had been pressed with visible care, the creases sharp, the collar starched to a state of military rigidity that I recognized immediately as the work of someone other than the man wearing it.

The collar, in fact, was the first thing I noticed, because it was wrong. Not in the sense that it was poorly done — it was, as I have said, meticulously pressed — but in the sense that it was *fighting him.*
