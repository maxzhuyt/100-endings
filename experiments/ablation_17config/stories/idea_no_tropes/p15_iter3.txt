# The Floor Between Them

## I.

The body arrived at 3:47 a.m. on a Tuesday, which was late for a weeknight transfer but not unusual for St. Raphael's, where the living clung on with a tenacity that meant the dying often waited until the small hours to be noticed. The gurney came down in the service elevator, accompanied by a transport orderly named Marco who had already radioed ahead, and by a manila envelope containing the preliminary paperwork: Edith Callow, 74, congestive heart failure, expired 2:12 a.m., attending physician Dr. Singh, cause of death natural.

Dr. Lena Maric signed the intake form and peeled back the sheet.

The woman beneath was small, the way the dead are always smaller than the living expect. Her skin had the mottled pallor of someone whose circulation had been failing for months. Her hands were curled inward, the nails blanched, the knuckles swollen with arthritis that would never bother her again. A hospital bracelet circled one wrist. An IV bruise marked the crook of her left arm—fresh, the purple still saturated, which meant the line had been placed or adjusted within hours of death.

Lena noted this. She noted the bruise's position, its diameter, the slight asymmetry that suggested reinsertion. She noted the chart's record of medications: enalapril, furosemide, metoprolol, potassium chloride supplements. She noted the time of death and the time of the last recorded nursing check, which was 1:40 a.m.—thirty-two minutes before death, an unremarkable gap on a night shift with twelve patients and two nurses.

She noted all of this the way she noted everything: with the compressed, habitual attention of a person who had received over four thousand bodies in eleven years and had learned that the difference between what was worth flagging and what was not often came down to the size of a needle mark or the timing of a potassium dose. Most of what she noted, she then set aside. The body was consistent with its paperwork. An elderly woman with a documented cardiac history had died of cardiac failure. The world was full of such deaths, and they came to Lena's basement and she certified them, and they moved on.

She pulled the sheet back over Edith Callow's face and turned to the intake log.

And then she stopped.

It was not a dramatic pause. It was not the intake of breath that precedes revelation. It was the kind of stop that happens when a person's hand, reaching for a pen, simply fails to close—when some subroutine of the brain, operating below the level of conscious analysis, sends a signal that is not yet a thought but is no longer nothing. She stood with her fingers above the log and looked at the entry she had written two shifts ago: Gerald Patton, 68, post-surgical embolism, Ward 6, expired 1:58 a.m. And above that, six days prior: Miriam Yee, 81, renal failure secondary to diabetes, Ward 6, expired 2:24 a.m.

Ward 6. Small hours. Natural causes.

Lena blinked at the log. Then she picked up the pen and signed Edith Callow in, and the moment passed—or rather, it did not pass but sank, the way a stone sinks in turbid water, dropping below the surface of her attention but not out of existence. She would spend the rest of the shift in the ordinary routines of her work: labeling samples, filing, checking the cold storage temperatures, writing the brief and colorless reports that constituted her professional speech. She would drink two cups of coffee from the machine in the hallway. She would hear, faintly, the building's mechanical heartbeat—water in the pipes, the elevator's distant hum, the ventilation system pushing recycled air through ducts that ran, like a circulatory system, through every floor.

She would not yet call what she had noticed a pattern. She was not yet ready for the word.

---

Three floors above, and forty minutes earlier, a man in a white coat stood at the nursing station of Ward 6 and made a small adjustment to an IV pump.

His name was Carl Anderton. He had been the chief pharmacist at St. Raphael's for twenty-two years—longer than most of the current attendings, longer than two of the three administrators, longer than the current wing of the building, which had been added in 2011 as part of a renovation he had helped plan. His badge photo, taken at the beginning of his tenure, showed a younger face, thinner, with the anxious smile of someone not yet certain of his place. The face he wore now was settled. The anxiety had been replaced by something that looked like patience but was more precisely described as *fit*—the deep, structural comfort of a person who has been in a building so long that his routines have become part of its architecture.

He checked the pump's display. The drip rate was correct. The medication—a potassium chloride supplementation prescribed for a patient in bed 4, a seventy-year-old man named Arthur Pollard who had been admitted two days ago for management of an irregular heartbeat—flowed from bag to line to catheter with the steady, transparent logic of modern medicine. Carl made a note on the MAR. He initialed it. He returned the chart to its slot and stood for a moment in the hallway, listening.

The ward was quiet. Room 3's monitor beeped in its slow, steady rhythm. The nurses' station was empty—Denise was in room 7 helping a patient to the bathroom; Ray was on his break, eating a sandwich in the lounge at the end of the hall. The overhead lights had been dimmed to their nighttime setting, which cast the corridor in a sallow half-light that made everything look provisional, as if the building were holding its breath.

Carl walked to the supply room. He unlocked it with his key—not his badge, his physical key, one of three copies in the hospital, which he had carried on his person every shift for twenty-two years. He entered. He checked the stock. He noted a discrepancy in the fentanyl count that was, in fact, a rounding artifact from a partial-vial discard earlier in the day, and he corrected it. He locked the room behind him.

He walked back down the hallway, past the rooms where patients slept or didn't sleep, past the window that looked out over the parking lot where the shift-change cars were beginning to appear, past the elevator that went down to the basement where the morgue was. He did not press the elevator button. He had no reason to go to the basement. He continued to the pharmacy office, sat at his desk, and began checking the next day's dispensation orders.

He was methodical. He was quiet. The fluorescent light above his desk hummed at a frequency that was below most people's hearing but that he had long ago absorbed into the texture of his concentration. His hands moved through the paperwork with the practiced, unhurried competence of a man who understood his institution the way a surgeon understands anatomy—not as a set of rules but as a living system, with pressures and tolerances and points of vulnerability that could be managed, maintained, kept in balance.

Three floors below, Lena Maric was still looking at her intake log.

She did not know about Carl Anderton. She did not know that the building above her head contained, in its ordinary rhythms, a man who had killed eleven people in the last fourteen months. She did not know that Edith Callow was the twelfth. She did not know that the stone sinking in her mind had a name, or that the name was correct, or that her correctness would be the thing that destroyed her.

She knew only that Ward 6 had sent her three bodies in sixteen days and that the coffee was bad and that her shift ended in four hours and that something about Edith Callow's IV bruise was still sitting in her chest like a breath she hadn't finished exhaling.

She set down the pen and went back to the body.

---

## II.

The fourth body arrived on a Saturday.

Thomas Gregoire, 77, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Ward 6, expired 3:08 a.m. The paperwork was clean. The attending's signature was legible. The nursing notes documented a gradual decline over twelve hours: increasing respiratory distress, morphine administered for comfort at 11 p.m. and again at 1:30 a.m., family notified, patient transitioned to comfort-care status at 9 p.m. the preceding evening. A textbook death. A good death, if such a language meant anything—the kind where the living had been given time to gather, to hold hands, to say what needed saying before the machines were silenced and the body was allowed to do what it had been trying to do.

Lena signed the intake. She placed Thomas Gregoire in cold storage drawer 7. She returned to her desk.

She pulled out the intake log.

She had begun, over the past week, to do something she could not yet have described as investigation. It was closer to a tic—a compulsive re-reading, the way a person re-reads a sentence that contains an error they can feel but not identify. Each night, after the shift's work was done, she opened the log and read backward through the entries. Not all of them. Only the ones from Ward 6. Only the ones from the overnight hours. Only the ones coded natural.

There had been, in the past five weeks, six deaths from Ward 6 between the hours of midnight and 4 a.m. This was, by any standard metric, a statistical anomaly—but not, by any standard metric, an impossible one. Ward 6 was a medical-surgical unit. Its patients were old, sick, and dying. People died on Ward 6. That was the nature of the floor. If you put the frailest people in a building together and waited, some of them would die at night, because night was when the body's defenses ebbed and the monitors were watched by fewer eyes and the slow arithmetic of organ failure reached its conclusions in the dark.

Lena knew this. She understood the base rates. She had written her doctoral thesis on postmortem interval estimation and had spent the first two years of her career in a county office where the dead arrived in such volume that statistical anomaly was a permanent condition. She was not a person who startled at clustering. Clustering was the signature of reality, which did not distribute its events with the even spacing of a metronome but dumped them in heaps and then went quiet, because that was what randomness actually looked like when you stopped expecting it to look orderly.

And yet.

She opened her desk drawer and took out a legal pad on which she had begun to write dates. Not a chart, not a spreadsheet—she had not yet granted the project the dignity of structure. Just dates, scrawled in her compressed handwriting, each followed by a name and the barest notations: *ward, time, cause, attending, nursing gap*.

Nursing gap. This was the detail that had snagged her, though she had not yet given it the weight of a pattern. In each of the six cases, the interval between the last documented nursing check and the recorded time of death was between twenty-five and forty minutes. This was not unusual. Nursing checks on night shift were conducted hourly at best; on a floor with twelve patients and two nurses, a forty-minute gap was not a lapse but a reality. The human body could not be in twelve rooms at once. The dead did not wait for witnesses.

But the consistency bothered her. Not its presence—its *uniformity*. Six deaths, six gaps, all falling within the same narrow window. If the deaths were truly random, she would have expected at least one to coincide with a nursing check—one case where the nurse was in the room, one where the gap was five minutes or sixty. Instead, the deaths were nestled into a pocket of unwatched time as if they had been scheduled there.

She wrote this on the legal pad. Then she put the legal pad back in the drawer and closed it and sat in the fluorescent quiet of the morgue for a long time, listening to the building's pipes.

---

She was not sleeping well. This was not new. The night shift had its own relationship with sleep—a fractured, adversarial one, in which the body was asked to be alert at 3 a.m. and unconscious at noon, and responded by being neither, reliably, at any hour. She had worked nights for seven of her eleven years at St. Raphael's, and her circadian rhythm had long since collapsed into something that resembled not a cycle but a landscape: flat, gray, punctuated by erratic peaks of alertness that came and went with no connection to the clock.

But this was different. She was not merely failing to sleep. She was lying in her apartment in the late morning light with the blackout curtains drawn, staring at the ceiling and seeing Edith Callow's IV bruise. Seeing the intake log's column of dates. Seeing—not thinking about, but *seeing*, the way one sees a face or a wound—the twenty-five-to-forty-minute gap, over and over, like a pulse.

She went back to the hospital early for her next shift. She arrived two hours before she was needed, which required her to use the back entrance because her badge was coded for shift-start access only at the main doors. She went to the records room—not the morgue's records, which she had already exhausted, but the hospital's central patient records, which she could access with her institutional credentials because the system had never been designed to distinguish between a medical examiner checking a death and a medical examiner checking a *pattern* of deaths. She pulled the charts for all six patients.

She spread them across the records room table under bad lighting and read them the way she read bodies: systematically, from the outside in, beginning with the gross features and working toward the tissue.

The gross features were unremarkable. Six patients, ages 68 to 84, all with serious underlying conditions, all admitted to Ward 6, all deceased during the night shift. The attending physicians varied—three different names, none of them unusual, none of them the same for more than two cases. The nursing staff rotated according to the standard schedule. The admitting diagnoses were varied. The medication regimens were varied.

But the medication administration records—the MARs—were not varied. Not entirely. In each of the six charts, there was a pharmacist's initial on the MAR within eight hours of death. The initial was the same: *C.A.* The notation was always routine—a dosage adjustment, a supplementation order, a compatibility check triggered by a new prescription. In one case, Edith Callow's, it was a potassium chloride supplement authorized to address a borderline electrolyte reading that had come back from the lab at 6 p.m. In another, Gerald Patton's, it was an anticoagulant dosage recalculation after a weight change was recorded. Each intervention was justified. Each was documented. Each bore the pharmacist's initials in the same small, precise hand.

C.A.

Lena did not know who C.A. was. She had worked at St. Raphael's for eleven years, but the morgue existed in a different gravitational field than the rest of the hospital. She knew the transport orderlies. She knew some of the pathology lab staff. She knew the security guard, Bill, who sometimes stopped by on his rounds to microwave his dinner in her break area because the basement microwave was better than the one on his floor. She did not know the pharmacists. She had no reason to know the pharmacists. The dead did not need prescriptions.

She wrote *C.A.* on the legal pad. She underlined it.

She put the charts back and went down to the morgue and began her shift. A body arrived at 1:15 a.m.—a cardiac patient from Ward 3, unremarkable, no connection to anything. She processed it. She filed the reports. She drank her coffee.

She did not open the drawer with the legal pad again that night.

But the pad was there. And the underlined initials were there. And the stone that had sunk in her mind two weeks ago had reached the bottom and was now resting on something solid, something that would not let it sink further, something that felt—if she were honest, which she was not yet ready to be—like the floor of certainty.

---

Three floors above, on the same night, Carl Anderton was conducting a routine inventory of the Ward 6 medication dispensary.

He moved through the shelves with precision, checking each box against the computerized log, noting the counts, initialing the reconciliation form. The dispensary was a locked room the size of a walk-in closet, lined floor to ceiling with shelving, organized alphabetically and then by concentration. He had designed this system himself, seventeen years ago, when the old pharmacist—a man named Duvall who had been competent but disorganized—had retired and Carl had inherited not only the position but the opportunity to remake the infrastructure.
