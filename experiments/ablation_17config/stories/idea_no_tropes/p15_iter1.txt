# DEX'S WEEKEND

## Recovered Media from the Personal Device of Dexter Ramírez, Licensed Waste Systems Technician (NAWT Cert. #07734), Contracted to Solstice Sounds Music Festival, Ridgecrest CA. Device recovered Sunday 06/22, 14:20 PST, from a storm drainage channel approximately 300 meters southwest of the festival perimeter. Media files arranged chronologically.

---

## FRIDAY

---

**VIDEO — 06:47 AM**
*Duration: 4:12. Location data: 35.5984°N, 117.6731°W. Camera faces outward from the driver's seat of a white Ford F-350, service rack visible through the rear window. A hand — brown, scarred across the knuckles, nails bitten ragged — adjusts the phone mount on the dash. The desert is flat and blinding and featureless except for an insane grid of turquoise rectangles reaching toward the mountains.*

"Friday. Six forty-seven. Call it entry one, for tax purposes, posterity, whatever. This is my content now."

*The truck rolls forward. The turquoise rectangles resolve into porta-potties — hundreds of them, arranged in clusters of twelve across the festival grounds, connected by service roads of packed alkali dust. Behind them, still skeletal, the main stage scaffolding rises like a half-built cathedral.*

"So. Solstice Sounds. Ridgecrest, California. Three-day electronic music festival out on the flats where God goes to feel dehydrated. Fifty thousand tickets sold. I am the guy — I am *the* guy — contracted to keep their sanitation grid from collapsing into a Superfund site by Monday morning. That is one hundred and eighty independent units across six service clusters, a centralized vacuum collection system, a ten-thousand-gallon holding tank with chemical treatment, and a pumping schedule that, if anyone actually follows it, should keep the whole thing exactly one step above a humanitarian crisis."

*The truck stops. Dex climbs out. The phone, now handheld, pans across the nearest cluster — Unit Row East-1 through East-12, freshly unloaded, their doors hanging open to air out the chemical smell. The blue of the deodorizer is vivid, almost beautiful, inside each shadowed interior.*

"One hundred eighty units for fifty thousand people. Industry standard is one per fifty for an eight-hour event. For a seventy-two-hour camping festival, you want one per forty, minimum, with service every four hours at peak. I asked for two-twenty. They gave me one-eighty. I asked for service support — at least one additional tech for overnight rotations. They said the budget was committed."

*A dry laugh.*

"The budget was committed. Four hundred dollar weekend passes and the budget was committed. Sure."

*The camera tilts down. Dex's boots crunch on white dust. A clipboard is visible under one arm.*

"Anyway. Pre-gates check. Every unit, every tank, every seal. The fun part."

*Cut.*

**PHOTOS — 07:15 to 08:40 AM**
*A series of twenty-three images. Service documentation. Each photo captures a unit's interior — tank level, seat seal integrity, chemical color. The blue deodorizer in the tanks is consistent across the first nineteen images: a deep, uniform cobalt, exactly the hue specified for glutaraldehyde-based treatment fluid. Photo 20 (Unit W-7, West Cluster) shows a tank where the fluid is darker, purpled, like a bruise spreading from the center outward. Photo 21 is a closer angle, flash on. In the flash, something in the fluid appears to shift — not ripple, shift — though this could be a camera artifact from the reflective surface. Photos 22 and 23 show what appear to be faint, threadlike filaments along the underside of the seat rim, pale against the plastic. They could be cleaning-chemical residue. They could be cobwebs. In the harsh light, they are almost iridescent.*

Probably a contaminated chemical batch or some reaction with the tank polymer. I'll flag it for the coordinator if she ever calls me back. Moving on."

**VOICE MEMO — 10:07 AM**
*Duration: 3:22. Background: two voices, one Dex's, one a woman's — clipped, professionally impatient, with the specific tone of someone who manages logistics for a living and considers every conversation a delay.*

"— saying the contract specifies a four-hour peak rotation. I can *do* four hours solo if you limit concurrent access per cluster, but that means line management, that means signage, and that means two units per cluster held in reserve for overflow."

"Dex, we sold fifty-two thousand tickets. We are not restricting bathroom access."

"Then I need another tech."

"We don't have budget for another tech."

"Okay, then you need to hear what I'm telling you, Mariana. At capacity with a four-hour solo rotation, I can maintain about a hundred and forty units in continuous service. That leaves forty units cycling off-grid at any given time for pumping. If people slam the east clusters during headliners the way they did last year at Thermal—"

"Last year at Thermal wasn't our event."

"Last year at Thermal three units *ruptured*. Sidewall blowouts. Forty-degree heat, overfull tanks, pressure from the chemical off-gassing. You know what it looked like?"

"I can imagine."

"No. You can't. I was on the remediation crew. I have photos. Do you want to see—"

"Dex, I hear you. I do. But gates open in four hours and I've got seventeen vendor contracts that aren't signed. Do your best. Flag anything critical. I'll check in tonight."

*A door closes. Silence. Then Dex, to the phone:*

"She will not check in tonight."

**VIDEO — 02:22 PM**
*Duration: 1:41. Gates have opened. The first wave of attendees flows through the entrance corridor — a river of bucket hats, hydration packs, body glitter, bare skin already pinking in the sun. Dex films from the service road, leaning against the truck. The angle catches the crowd streaming past a row of porta-potties that nobody will touch yet because it's early and they haven't started drinking and the very idea of a porta-potty is still abstract.*

"Look at them. Fresh. Hydrated. Optimistic. They think they're here for music. They don't know yet that by tomorrow morning, this entire grid is going to be a theater of biological warfare. Every one of those water bottles is future urine. Every one of those açaí bowls is a future emergency. I process the downstream. I am the last person in the chain. Everything that passes through fifty thousand digestive tracts becomes *my problem*."

*A pause. He turns the camera on himself for the first time. Dex is mid-thirties, sharp-featured, dark circles under dark eyes, sunburned across the nose, wearing a sweat-stained gray T-shirt with the sleeves cut off. A name patch on a lanyard reads RAMÍREZ — WASTE SYSTEMS. He is smiling, and the smile is the kind that has been seasoned by years of finding genuine amusement in conditions that would make most people reconsider their career.*

"Fifty-two thousand bladders. One of me. Welcome to Solstice."

*Cut.*

**VOICE MEMO — 06:38 PM**
*Duration: 0:47. Background: bass. Deep, concussive, the kind that travels through dirt. The festival is alive now.*

"First peak service run complete. East clusters are predictably the heaviest hit. Four units already at sixty percent. Pumped and treated. West Cluster seven — the one with the weird readings this morning — I checked it again. The discoloration is actually worse, which shouldn't be possible since I swapped the chemical charge six hours ago. New chemical, same reaction. And the filaments I saw on the seat rims? They're back. I wiped them this morning. They're back and they're longer. I'm going to pull a sample tomorrow if it persists. Probably some bacterial thing — the heat out here is stupid. Everything's growing."

*A pause.*

"Also, and I know this is just the desert cooking my brain, but when I opened the lid on seven, the smell was different. Not worse. *Different*. Sort of… sweet. Which, when you spend eight hours a day servicing porta-potties, is the one smell that actually scares you. Sweet means anaerobic decomposition. Sweet means something is breaking down that shouldn't be. Anyway. Back to it."

## FRIDAY NIGHT

---

**VIDEO — 09:14 PM**
*Duration: 6:53. The phone is propped against the lid of a Pelican case on the ground, angled upward. A work light casts a harsh white circle into a repair trench — a three-foot-deep, six-foot-long cut in the alkali soil where a lateral sewage line connects the east cluster to the central vacuum system. The line, a four-inch PVC pipe, has a lateral crack along a seam joint. Dark fluid weeps from the crack. Dex is visible from the chest down, crouching in the trench, gloved hands working the joint.*

"The universe said: you know what Solstice Sounds needs? A cracked lateral on cluster East-3 at peak flow on opening night. Because I was getting complacent. I was getting comfortable. I was starting to think maybe I'd get dinner."

*He shifts his weight. The trench floor is wet — not with water. The repair has required isolating the line, but residual waste still pools in the cut, dark and viscous, reflecting the work light.*

"This is a pressure crack. These lines are rated for the vacuum pump cycling, but somebody — and I'm not naming names, Mariana, because this is going on my professional record and not a wrongful death suit — somebody ordered the lightweight schedule-twenty PVC instead of schedule-forty because it saved eleven cents per linear foot. Eleven cents. Per foot. And now I'm standing in—"

*He turns to reach for a coupling wrench on the trench lip. His boot slides. The footage captures the exact sequence: the left foot goes, the right knee buckles, a hand grabs for the trench wall and finds only loose alkali that crumbles under his fingers, and then Dex goes down and back like someone yanked his legs out, and there is a thick, concussive splash that the phone microphone distorts into a crunch of static.*

*Silence. Two seconds. Three.*

*Then a sound that is not quite a scream and not quite a retch — it is the sound of a person trying to inhale and finding their mouth full of something that should never be in a mouth.*

"FFFF— *gkkk* — oh *fuck* — oh FUCK—"

*The work light, knocked sideways, now illuminates the trench at a low angle. Dex is sitting — not crouching, sitting, fully seated — in the pooled waste. It comes up to his mid-chest. He is drenched. The fluid is dark, opaque, and in the raked light there is a faint opalescent quality to it that the standard chemical treatment does not produce. Dex thrashes, tries to stand, slips again, goes back under to the shoulders. More cursing, wet and strangled. He gets a hand on the trench wall, hauls himself halfway out, slides, catches, hauls again.*

*It takes forty-one seconds to get out of the trench. The phone captures all of it.*

*Dex stands on the trench lip, dripping. The fluid runs off him in slow sheets — slower than water, slower than waste should, clinging to the fabric of his clothes and the skin of his arms with a quality that is less like wetness and more like reluctance. Like it does not want to let go.*

"Oh my God. Oh my *God*."

*He stands there, arms held out from his body, dripping, breathing. The bass from the main stage thuds in the distance like a second heartbeat.*

"This is — I need — this is a full-submersion contamination. This is a reportable incident. I have to — there's no shower out here. There's *nothing* out here. The nearest — "

*He turns in a full circle, dripping. The desert is dark beyond the work light. The festival glows on the horizon, a line of colored light, impossibly festive.*

"I am covered from the neck down in the combined sewage output of nine thousand people."

*A pause. A long, shuddering breath.*

"Content."

*Cut.*

**VIDEO — 09:48 PM**
*Duration: 3:07. Interior of a porta-potty. The phone is wedged into the paper-towel dispenser bracket, filming downward. Dex is stripping off his coveralls. Underneath, the T-shirt is soaked through, plastered to his body, and in the blue chemical glow of the unit's tank, the staining on the fabric is visible — a purplish-brown that does not match standard waste discoloration. Dex's forearms, now bare, are coated in a thin, clinging film that catches the light.*

"I can't — it won't come off. I've used half a bottle of hand sanitizer and it won't — look at this."

*He holds an arm toward the camera. The film is translucent, almost gelatinous, and in the blue light it has a faint pearlescence. He rubs at it with a wad of toilet paper. The paper comes away stained, but the film remains, thinned but present, like a second skin.*

"That is not sewage. Sewage washes off. I've been doing this for nine years. Sewage washes off. This is — I don't know what this is. Some kind of chemical reaction between the treatment fluid and — I don't know. Skin oils. Body heat. Something."

*He scrubs harder. His skin underneath is reddened, irritated.*

"It burns a little. Like a mild chemical burn. Which is consistent with glutaraldehyde exposure on broken skin, and I definitely have micro-abrasions from the fall, so that's — that tracks. That's normal. That's a known occupational hazard."

*He stops scrubbing. Looks at his arms. The film gleams.*

"I'm going to need a real shower. And a tetanus booster. And possibly a new career."

*He pulls on a spare shirt from his work bag, gathers the contaminated clothes into a garbage bag, and pauses. His hand goes to his stomach.*

"Oh, no."

*Another pause. A low groan.*

"No, no, no. Not now."

*Cut.*
