# The Wantings of Mudlarch

## I.

In Mudlarch, you were read at sixteen.

Not your palm, not your stars, not the bumps on your skull—though the city had tried all three in centuries past and found them wanting. What was read was your *Wanting* itself: that creature which had been growing alongside you since birth, invisible to everyone but you in childhood, increasingly visible to others as puberty thickened its substance, until by sixteen it walked beside you like a second shadow, dense enough to be touched, shaped enough to be named.

And the naming was everything.

The Bureau of Intimate Classifications occupied the largest building in the Threshold District, a structure of white stone and brass fittings that managed to look simultaneously like a cathedral and a post office. Citizens of Mudlarch had debated for generations which impression was more accurate, and the Bureau—wisely—had never clarified. Inside, the Classification Hall rose four stories to a domed ceiling painted with the Seventy Recognized Forms of Desire in their approved configurations, each rendered in pigments derived, it was said, from the shed skins of ancient Wantings. Whether this was true or merely the sort of institutional mythology that bureaucracies cultivate the way other buildings cultivate ivy, no one could say with certainty. But the ceiling was, everyone agreed, very fine.

On the morning in question—a Tuesday in the month of Greyfall, the light thin and civic—eleven youths stood in the Classification Hall in a queue that moved with the calm regularity of a well-funded government program. Their Wantings pressed close to them, still half-translucent with adolescence, mutable with nerves. Families watched from the Public Gallery. A woman sold roasted almonds from a cart in the vestibule, doing brisk business, because the ceremonies tended to run long and parents got hungry.

The Lord Taxonomist—not the Chief Taxonomist herself, who rarely attended routine readings, but her third deputy, a man named Clodden with a brass monocle and the practiced gentle voice of a person who measured the intimate lives of children for a living—operated the primary instrument. It resembled a harpsichord mated with a weather vane: keys, strings, a rotating armature of polished sensors that turned toward a Wanting the way a compass turns toward north, and a narrow brass horn that emitted a tone corresponding to the Wanting's essential classification. The tone was then matched against the Registry of Seventy Forms. The form was entered into the ledger. The youth was assigned a caste, a district, a set of permissions and prohibitions that would govern their intimate life until death or reclassification hearing, whichever came first. Reclassification hearings were granted roughly once per century.

And who among us does not want, at sixteen, to be told what our wanting means?

The first youth was a boy named Pell, whose Wanting—when Clodden's instrument swung toward it, when the sensors locked and the brass horn hummed—emerged in the unmistakable shape of a long-necked heron, blue-grey, still-legged, its beak pointed at some invisible middle distance with an intensity that suggested depth of feeling and a certain inflexibility regarding whom that feeling would be directed toward. The horn sang a clear D-sharp. Clodden smiled. "Subclass Ardea, variant constans," he announced. "Caste of Devoted Distances. Welcome to your life, young man." The crowd applauded. Pell's mother wept. His Wanting settled onto his shoulder with the visible relief of a creature that had finally been told what it was.

The second was a girl, Tarsa, whose Wanting manifested as a red fox—quick, clever, low to the ground, already eyeing the exits. F-natural. "Subclass Vulpes, variant errans. Caste of Roving Ardors." Tarsa grinned. Her father looked mildly concerned but rallied. The fox wound between her ankles and nipped at the air with proprietary delight, and Tarsa walked out of the Hall with the particular swagger of someone who has just been officially authorized to break hearts.

Third: a shy, enormous boy whose Wanting turned out to be an equally enormous and equally shy bear, its fur the amber of late afternoon, its posture apologetic. The crowd murmured approval. B-flat. "Subclass Ursus, variant tenerus. Caste of Gentle Enormities." There was always something moving about the large-and-tender classification, a sense of desire proportional to the body that housed it, and the boy carried his bear-Wanting out of the Hall with his eyes bright and his hands shaking and the unmistakable look of a person who has been given permission to be exactly the size of his own need.

A system that works is a beautiful thing. Even—perhaps especially—a system whose work is the naming of the unnamed, the organizing of the unruly, the transformation of the raw, terrifying, shapeless fact of human want into a form that can be addressed, accommodated, paired with a complementary form, and housed in an appropriate district with adequate plumbing. Mudlarch had been classifying for three hundred years. The system was mature. The system was refined. The system produced, in the aggregate, a city whose citizens could articulate their desires in a shared language, could find partners through a rational process of caste-compatibility matching, could resolve intimate disputes by consulting the Regulatory Concordance rather than resorting to the chaos of unmediated feeling. The divorce rate was low. The satisfaction surveys were promising. The almonds were excellent.

Jessamine Fleck was ninth in the queue.

She was not, by any visible measure, remarkable. Sixteen, thin-wristed, the particular kind of pale that comes from institutional living rather than constitution—she had been raised in the Matchward Foundling Home since infancy, origin unknown, parentage unrecorded, her file notable only for its thinness. She wore the foundling's grey pinafore. Her hair was the color of wet straw. She stood with her shoulders drawn inward, the posture of a person accustomed to occupying as little space as possible, and her Wanting—

Her Wanting was pressed against her back.

This was not unusual. Many adolescent Wantings were shy before classification, half-formed, reluctant to show themselves before the instrument gave them permission to exist fully. Clodden had seen thousands of them. He adjusted his monocle. He activated the sensors. He depressed the keys in the standard introductory sequence—three chords meant to coax a Wanting forward the way one might coax a cat from under a bed, with patience and a show of disinterest.

Jessamine's Wanting detached from her back. It moved into the space between her body and the instrument. It did not take a shape.

Or rather—it took a shape, and then it took another, and then it took a third. It flickered. It spasmed. For one half-second it was a heron—blue-grey, long-necked, almost identical to Pell's—and the horn began to hum D-sharp, and Clodden's pen moved toward the ledger. Then it was not a heron. It was a fox. F-natural sang from the horn for a quarter-beat before the tone sheared sideways into something atonal. The fox collapsed into a shape that was briefly ursine, then briefly serpentine, then briefly nothing at all—a smear of color and heat in the air, like light through a prism that would not hold still.

Clodden's pen stopped.

The sensors spun. The armature rotated wildly, hunting for a lock, finding one, losing it, finding another. The horn produced a sound that was not a note but a chord, and not a chord but a cluster, and not a cluster but white noise, the acoustic equivalent of every classification playing simultaneously.

"Hold, please," Clodden said, though Jessamine was not moving. She was standing very still, in fact, with her hands at her sides and her face emptied of everything except the particular concentrated blankness of a person trying very hard not to exist.

Her Wanting kept shifting. It had no preference. It moved through the Seventy Forms the way a dreamer moves through rooms—without logic, without loyalty, each shape as real and as temporary as the last. For three seconds it was a moth with human eyes. For two seconds it was a glass horse filled with smoke. For one lurching instant it was something that corresponded to no form in the Registry at all, something with too many limbs and a sound like singing, and a woman in the Public Gallery stood up and then sat down again very quickly.

Clodden consulted the Concordance. He recalibrated the instrument. He played the introductory sequence again, then the advanced stabilization sequence that was used for difficult cases, then—his hands uneasy on the keys—the emergency taxonomic override that he had learned in training but never deployed.

The Wanting would not hold.

Other officials arrived. Clodden was joined by his supervisor, then by the supervisor's supervisor, then by a quiet woman from the Archival Division who brought a very old book and opened it to a page whose margins were crowded with annotations in a nervous hand. They conferred in low voices while Jessamine stood in the Classification Hall and watched her own Wanting betray her, over and over, in forms she had never asked for.

She was not defiant. She was not proud. She was sixteen and an orphan and the thing that was supposed to give her a name and a place and a caste and a home and a future—the thing that every other youth in the queue had received as a birthright, the thing the system existed to provide—was instead making her into a spectacle. She could feel the eyes of the gallery. She could hear, or imagined she could hear, the roasted-almond woman craning for a better view.

"Can you make it stop?" Clodden asked her, not unkindly. His confusion was genuine. He had classified over four thousand youths and had seen reluctant Wantings, deceptive Wantings, oversized and undersized and inconveniently timed Wantings, but he had never seen one that simply *would not resolve.*

"I'm trying," Jessamine said. Her voice was very small.

She was trying. She was trying the way you try to stop a sneeze or a blush—with the interior clench of a body attempting to override its own involuntary processes through sheer force of social embarrassment. It did not work. The Wanting surged. It became, for five terrible seconds, something luminous and vast, something that pressed against the walls of the Classification Hall and made the painted ceiling hum—and then it contracted to the size of a walnut and sat, trembling, in her cupped hands, and was briefly, almost, a sparrow.

"Sparrow," Clodden said quickly, and his pen touched the ledger.

The walnut unfurled into a vine. The vine grew teeth.

The quiet woman from the Archival Division closed her very old book. She leaned close to Clodden's supervisor's supervisor and said something. The supervisor's supervisor said something back. The quiet woman nodded. She produced a form—not from the standard stack, but from a separate case she had brought with her, a leather case with a lock.

The form was grey. It had very few boxes. The largest box, at the top, was already stamped with a single word in red ink.

Jessamine watched the form being passed from hand to hand. She watched Clodden decline to sign it, and his supervisor decline to sign it, and his supervisor's supervisor sign it with a pen that seemed to weigh a great deal. She watched the quiet woman countersign. She watched the form travel back across the room toward her with the inevitability of weather.

UNCLASSIFIABLE.

Below it, in smaller type: *By order of the Bureau of Intimate Classifications, pursuant to Regulatory Provision 7.16.4(c), the above-named individual is remanded to the custody of the Bureau's Department of Desire Correction for an indefinite period of therapeutic containment and rehabilitative classification assistance.*

The sentence came from a form. Nobody made a speech. Nobody was cruel. A functionary in a clean uniform took Jessamine's elbow—gently, because the Bureau trained its functionaries in gentle elbow-taking—and guided her toward a side door she had not noticed before, a door that was the same white as the walls and fitted with the same brass as everything else in the building, a door that looked, in fact, exactly like the main exit except that it led in the opposite direction.

In the gallery, the roasted-almond woman was already packing up. The ceremony was over. The remaining youths would be rescheduled.

Jessamine's Wanting, as she passed through the door, was a flicker of grey light that matched her pinafore exactly, as if it were trying, at last, to be invisible, to be nothing, to be the precise color of not being seen.

The door closed behind her with a sound like a form being filed.

## II.

Now: a locked room.

The Lord Registrar's private chambers occupied the top floor of the Bureau's administrative tower, accessible only by a staircase whose key he wore around his neck on a chain of such slender links that he sometimes forgot it was there, which was, if one were inclined toward metaphor, appropriate. The chambers were large, well-appointed, and decorated in the manner of a man who had achieved the highest office in a system built entirely on the taxonomy of desire: portraits of previous Lord Registrars on the walls, each painted beside their Wanting in full ceremonial display; a mahogany desk the size of a small country; a window that overlooked the Threshold District with the proprietary gaze of ownership.

The Lord Registrar's name was Aldous Bream.

He was sixty-one. He had held the office for nineteen years. Under his administration, the Bureau had expanded the Registry from Fifty-Eight to Seventy Recognized Forms, established the Concordance Courts for inter-caste dispute resolution, and achieved a classification accuracy rate of 99.4 percent, which was the highest in recorded history and which he mentioned, in conversation, roughly as often as a new parent mentions their child, which is to say at every opportunity and some that were not, strictly speaking, opportunities at all.

His public Wanting was a stag.

It was magnificent. It walked beside him through the corridors of the Bureau with an antlered authority that made junior officials step aside and senior officials stand straighter. It was the color of dark oak. Its hooves rang on marble. When Aldous Bream entered a room, his Wanting entered first, and the room rearranged itself around the fact of it—chairs shifted, spines aligned, the ambient temperature of deference rose by several degrees. He had been classified at sixteen as Subclass Cervus, variant imperialis, Caste of Sovereign Appetites, and the classification had carried him through a political career of uninterrupted ascent, because the system, naturally, favored those whose Wantings most resembled its idealized forms, and the stag was the system's own self-portrait: noble, virile, dominant, and vegetarian.

Aldous Bream locked his chamber door. He drew the curtain. He removed the chain from his neck and placed the key on the desk.

The stag dissolved.

It did not dissolve dramatically—there was no shimmer, no sound, no theatrical evaporation. It simply stopped being maintained, the way a held breath stops when you are finally alone, and in its place was—

A mouse.

It was grey. It was very small. It had pink ears that were too large for its body and eyes that were black and wet and round with the particular kind of gentle alertness that is the opposite of authority. It sat on the desk beside the key and cleaned its whiskers with both paws, a gesture so precise and so earnest and so thoroughly, irredeemably meek that it could not possibly have belonged to the most powerful man in Mudlarch.

But it did.

"Hello, precious," Aldous Bream said. His voice was entirely different now—soft, almost musical, stripped of the baritone command he deployed in council chambers. He opened a desk drawer and removed a cloth napkin. Inside the napkin: crumbs.
