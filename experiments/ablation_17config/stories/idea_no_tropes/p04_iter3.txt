# Bros Before Roads

### A Streamable Original — Eight Episodes — Rated TV-MA for Language and Structural Collapse

---

## I.

Here's what you need to know.

Hank Mellis is fifty-three years old, six-foot-one, built like a man who was once in excellent shape and then made a series of reasonable decisions about beer. He sells HVAC systems in the greater Dayton, Ohio area. He has been divorced once, amicably, and once more in a manner that the Franklin County Family Court described as "notably less so." He cries at gas station sunsets. He has used the phrase "no, but listen, listen, the *light*" at least four documented times while pointing at an otherwise unremarkable sky over an otherwise unremarkable Pilot Travel Center, and every time he said it, he meant it.

Dale Kendrick is fifty-two. He is five-foot-ten in theory, though the practical number is somewhat different now and has been for a while. He has a beard that he maintains with the seriousness of a man who has decided that this one small territory is his to govern. He was a building inspector for Cuyahoga County for nineteen years, which means he has opinions about load-bearing walls the way some men have opinions about the 1985 Bears, which is to say: loudly, without invitation, and with the absolute conviction that you are worse for not sharing them. His wheelchair is a Ti Lite ZRA, titanium frame, matte black, with aftermarket wheels he ordered from a place in Colorado. It is in every shot from the first frame forward because it is where Dale sits, and Dale is one of the two people this show is about.

They have been best friends for thirty-one years.

The premise is simple, the way all good premises are simple, the way "two guys walk into a bar" is simple and everything after it is where the money is: Hank and Dale are going to drive — well, Hank is going to drive, and Dale is going to sit in the passenger seat and say the driving is bad — across eleven states in nineteen days, revisiting every place that mattered to their friendship. Every diner, every motel, every accidental landmark, every site of every catastrophe and every stupid, holy, unrepeatable night that became a story they have told so many times the words have smoothed over like river stones.

That's the show. Two men. One van — a rented Mercedes Sprinter, retrofitted with a wheelchair lift that will malfunction in Episode 3 and again in Episode 5 and a third time that we don't have time to get into but that produces language the FCC would take an interest in. Eleven states. Nineteen days. Thirty-one years of friendship, redriven.

It is, in every measurable way, a show designed to perform adequately in the 10 PM Tuesday slot for adults 35-54 who have exhausted the true crime vertical and aren't yet ready to commit to subtitles. It is content. It is two guys in a van who like each other more than they're comfortable saying. It is, or it appears to be, the warm bath of American male friendship: long silences, shared food, the insult as endearment.

Roll the title card.

Here's Hank in the pre-trip interview, shot in what appears to be his living room, which has the energy of a place recently and somewhat performatively tidied. He's sitting forward. His hands are doing something they don't usually do, which is moving.

"There's this thing that happens," Hank says, and he pauses, and you can see him choosing between the honest version and the broadcastable version, and what he chooses is somewhere in the middle. "You go through your life, and the places — the actual physical places — they're like... they're holding the memory for you. Right? You don't have to remember it perfectly because the place is *there.* The booth is there. The parking lot is there. The stain on the ceiling from when Dale threw a full beer straight up because the Browns — anyway. You can go back. That's the thing. You can always go back." He looks at the camera with the expression of a man who has recently learned that this is not, in fact, the thing.

And here's Dale, in a separate interview, shot in a room that is either an office or a room that wishes it were an office. He looks directly into the lens with the steady, unblinking gaze of a man who has been asked to participate in something he finds faintly ridiculous and has decided to participate anyway for reasons he is not currently sharing.

"Hank called me and said, 'They want to put us on TV.' And I said, 'Who's they,' and he said, 'A streaming thing,' and I said, 'What kind of streaming thing,' and he said, 'The kind where two guys drive around and go to places,' and I said, 'That's every show,' and he said, 'Yeah, but this time it's us.'" Dale pauses. He has the timing of a man who knows what a pause is for. "And I said yes. Because it's Hank. And Hank's the kind of guy who — once he gets an idea about something sentimental, you either get on the bus or he'll cry at you until you get on the bus. And I've known him thirty-one years. I don't have the energy to watch him cry anymore."

He doesn't smile. The beard shifts in a way that suggests he might be smiling underneath it, in a region the camera can't reach.

The Sprinter pulls out of Hank's driveway at 6:47 AM on a Tuesday in early October. The light is doing something absurd and golden through the maples on either side of the street, the kind of light that would make a cinematographer weep and that makes Hank, predictably, point at it and say, "No, but *look,*" and Dale, predictably, says, "It's light, Hank. It happens every day. It's one of the main things that happens."

They pull onto I-70 heading east. Hank turns on the radio. Dale turns it off. Hank turns it on again. Dale turns it off again. This continues for eleven minutes. We have the footage. All of it. The full, uncut, eleven-minute war of attrition over a radio tuned to a classic rock station playing "Ramblin' Man" by the Allman Brothers, which, if you believe in the storytelling powers of the universe, and Hank does, is almost too perfect. If you don't believe in the storytelling powers of the universe, and Dale doesn't, it's just a song on a radio that he would like to be off.

This is the show.

These are the guys.

Let's go.

## II.

The first stop is a diner.

Or: the first stop *was* a diner. The first stop is now a SmartStor+ self-storage facility, its cinder-block face painted the pale, medicinal green of a building that knows it contains nothing anyone loves. There is a sign. The sign says: CLIMATE CONTROLLED UNITS FROM $49/MO. The sign does not say: THIS USED TO BE THE STARLIGHT DINER, WHERE IN 1994 TWO TWENTY-THREE-YEAR-OLDS ATE PANCAKES AT 3 AM AND ONE OF THEM LAUGHED SO HARD THAT COFFEE CAME OUT OF HIS NOSE AND THE OTHER ONE STILL REMEMBERS THE SPECIFIC SOUND OF IT.

The sign just says the thing about the units.

Hank stands in the parking lot — a paved, efficient, joyless rectangle where the gravel lot used to be — and he stares at the building with the expression of a man arriving at a funeral to find the church has been replaced by a Jiffy Lube. His mouth opens. Closes. Opens.

"Huh," he says.

Dale, from the van's open door, his chair not yet lowered because the lift is taking its time, says: "Well. That's that."

"It was *right here,*" Hank says, pointing at the SmartStor+ the way you would point at a magician's empty hand. "The counter went — the counter was along that wall. That wall right there. And the booth — our booth — would've been..." He walks toward the building, stops, recalculates. "Inside a storage unit, I guess."

"Climate controlled, though," Dale says. "Your memories are between fifty and eighty degrees. That's something."

Hank laughs. It's a good laugh. It just isn't a complete one.

They move on.

The second stop is a bar in Wheeling, West Virginia: the High Water Tavern, where Hank and Dale spent an entire weekend in 1997 during what Dale, in the pre-trip interview, called "the situation we don't talk about but that Hank will definitely talk about." The bar is gone. It is now half a bar — the front room survives as a vaguely operational establishment called BRICK'S, which appears to serve food in the sense that there is a menu and a kitchen and an optimistic relationship between the two — and half a barbershop, the dividing wall apparently erected by someone whose familiarity with dividing walls was primarily theoretical. The floor tilts. There is a smell.

Hank walks in. He looks around. He locates the pool table — or rather, the space where the pool table was, which is now occupied by a barber's chair bolted into the floor at an angle that suggests the floor is losing an argument with gravity.

"The pool table was here," Hank says.

"The pool table was *not* here," Dale says, rolling through the door that is technically wide enough for the chair if he takes the doorframe's feelings into account, which he doesn't, and a small piece of trim pops off and lands on the floor. "The pool table was against the far wall. By the window."

"There is no window."

"I'm aware. There was a window."

"The pool table was *here,* Dale. I remember because I'm the one who scratched on the eight ball from *this exact position*—"

"That was a different bar."

"It was *this* bar."

"It was a different bar, and it was a different game, and you scratched on the eight ball because you were looking at the waitress, and we left because you were embarrassed, and *that* is why we ended up at the Starlight Diner, which is now—"

"A storage facility. I know."

"Climate controlled."

"I know."

They stand in the half-bar, half-barbershop, in the spot where a pool table may or may not have been. The floor creaks. It creaks again. It is the kind of creak that Dale, in his professional capacity, would have flagged. He doesn't flag it. Hank shifts his weight and a floorboard gives a quarter inch and makes a sound like a question.

"We should probably—" Hank starts.

The floor doesn't give way. It just *threatens* to. Which is, in its own way, worse, because it introduces the idea.

They leave.

The third stop: a motel in rural Pennsylvania. The Lamplighter. Two stories, all exterior corridors, the kind of place that peaked aesthetically in 1974 and knew it and stopped trying. Hank and Dale stayed there once, maybe twice, maybe four times — the number varies depending on which one of them is telling the story and how much they've had to drink. The motel is still standing, which is itself a kind of punchline, because it is standing in the way a man at last call is standing: technically, improbably, and with visible resentment toward the forces of physics that insist upon it. Half the rooms are boarded. The office is open but unmanned. There is a cat on the front desk who looks like he has been in charge for some time and is tired of the responsibility.

Hank reaches for the railing on the exterior staircase.

The railing detaches from the wall with a sound like a bone leaving a socket — that specific wet, structural pop that means something has been pretending to be attached for a long time and has decided to stop pretending. Hank stumbles. Doesn't fall. Catches himself. The railing swings out and hangs there, bolted at only one end now, a metal arm reaching toward nothing.

"Jesus," Hank says.

"Failed fastener," Dale says, not moving from his chair, looking at the railing with the appraising calm of a man who spent nineteen years looking at things that were about to stop being things. "Corrosion on the lag bolts. That's been going for years. You can see where the wood's delaminated around the—"

"Dale."

"I'm just saying, if I were inspecting this place—"

"You're not inspecting this place. We're *visiting* this place."

"You can visit and inspect. I've been doing both my whole life. The two are not mutually exclusive."

"Can we just—" Hank stops. He looks at the motel. He looks at the swinging railing. He looks at the boarded windows. He says, very quietly, not to Dale but to the building or to something near the building: "I thought it would still be here."

"It *is* still here," Dale says. "That's the depressing part."

They don't go inside.

The fourth stop: gone. A restaurant in Altoona, replaced by a medical office complex. Hank stands in the parking lot. He says nothing. Dale says nothing. The narrator notes the name of the restaurant — Martinelli's — and the year of its last health inspection: 2011. The silence is long enough that you can hear the Sprinter's engine ticking as it cools.

The fifth stop: gone. A campground outside State College, now a solar farm. Rows of panels stretching across what was once a field where, according to Hank, they stayed up until 4 AM and Dale saw a shooting star and made a wish and refused then, and refuses now, to say what the wish was.

"Some things are private," Dale says.

"We're on a television show," Hank says.

"Some things," Dale repeats, "are private."

The panels catch the afternoon light. They are functional and useful and clean. They are also the opposite of a campground, in the same way that a spreadsheet is the opposite of a poem: accomplishing more and meaning less.

Hank sits on the Sprinter's bumper. He puts his hands on his knees. He looks at the solar farm the way you'd look at a letter you've been carrying around for years, only to open it and find the page blank.

"Five for five," he says.

"Five for five," Dale confirms.

"Every place."

"Every single place."

Hank makes a sound. It is not a word. It's the pre-verbal form of a word, the sound a word makes before it decides what it wants to be when it grows up. It could become a laugh. It could become something else. It hovers.

"You good?" Dale says.

"Yeah," Hank says, in the specific key of a man who is not good but who has made a decision about how the next few minutes are going to go. "Yeah. Next stop."

They get back in the van.

Here's what's funny — and it is funny, that's the thing, that's what makes this show work, in the way that it works: each demolished or gutted or barely surviving location follows the same rhythm. Hank builds it up — in the van, in the miles before arrival, he talks about it with the reverence of a man describing a cathedral, his hands moving, his voice climbing — and then they arrive, and the place is gone or ruined, and the air goes out of him, and Dale says something dry and exactly right, and the van fills with the specific silence of a joke that is not quite a joke, and then they drive to the next one. Setup. Reversal. Button. Setup. Reversal. Button. It is good television. The rhythm is reliable. The contrast is sharp. Hank's earnestness, Dale's commentary, the demolished landscape between them.

It is, perhaps, a little too reliable.

But we'll come back to that.

## III.

Let's talk about the show for a moment. Not the show you're watching — the show *behind
