# The Lanai

Mrs. Plimpton is dead, and she was right about everything, and I did nothing soon enough.

My name is Deshi Carrington. I am — I was — a home health aide employed through BrightCare Staffing of Kissimmee, Florida, assigned to Unit 14-C of The Palms at Lago Vista, a retirement community on the southwest shore of Lake Toho, where the entry gate is flanked by royal palms and the monthly HOA runs four thousand dollars and the residents die at a rate that is, I now understand, statistically anomalous, though no one has yet performed the calculation. I was hired to manage Margo Plimpton's medications, her meals, her hydration, her decline. Instead I read every journal she ever kept — forty-one composition notebooks, college-ruled, spanning 1974 to 2019, stored in a filing cabinet in the second bedroom she still called her office — and I learned that someone in The Palms at Lago Vista was killing people, and that Mrs. Plimpton knew it before anyone, and that her knowing it was structurally indistinguishable from her losing her mind, and that I sat on this lanai and did nothing until it was too late to matter.

I am sitting on this lanai now. I have been sitting on this lanai for seven months, first as Margo's aide and now as whatever I have become — her executor, her translator, the keeper of her evidence, the person who waters her bougainvillea because no one else will and the HOA has already sent a compliance letter about the state of the planter boxes. The lanai is screened. It faces the interior courtyard of Building 14, which means I can see the walking path that loops around the retention pond, the bench where Ed Forchetti sat every morning until he didn't, the parking lot where the BrightCare van drops me off at seven and collects me at seven, the pool house with its terracotta roof and its perpetually locked utility closet. I can see, if I lean forward and angle my head past the bougainvillea, the lanai of Unit 14-A, where Dr. Lewis Kendrick lives with his oxygen concentrator and his collection of Hummel figurines and his medical bag, which he has not been licensed to carry since the state of Florida revoked his privileges in 2016 but which he still brings to neighbors' units when they call him instead of calling 911, because he is a doctor, because he has always been their doctor, because at The Palms at Lago Vista you do not call strangers when you have Dr. Kendrick two doors down.

I can see everything from this lanai. I have always been able to see everything from this lanai. That is the nature of my confession and the shape of my guilt, and I am telling you now so that you understand what kind of story this is. It is not a story about whether someone was murdered. People were murdered. It is a story about the particular way that knowing a thing and being unable to make anyone else know it can turn you into furniture. I was furniture. I was a brown man in a white wicker chair on a white woman's lanai in a white community in central Florida, and I held in my mind the complete architecture of a crime, and no one ever once asked me what I thought about anything more substantial than whether Mrs. Plimpton preferred her Ensure warm or cold.

She preferred it cold. She preferred everything cold. She kept the air conditioning at sixty-four degrees and wore a cable-knit cardigan indoors in August and referred to the thermostat as "the only man in this building who does what I tell him." She was eighty-one years old and she had been, for forty-three years, a licensed sex therapist with a practice in Coral Gables, and she had retired to The Palms at Lago Vista in 2013, and she had been diagnosed with Lewy body dementia in 2021, and she had spent the last three years constructing, on her kitchen wall, a document of extraordinary precision and devastating unreliability that explained exactly how and why four of her neighbors had been killed.

I need to tell you about the wall.

---

The wall occupied the full east-facing side of Margo's kitchen, from the edge of the refrigerator to the corner where the counter met the pantry door. It had been white once. A landlord's white, the flat matte they use in rentals because it's cheap and takes a primer coat without complaint. By the time I arrived in January, it was a palimpsest.

The base layer was photographs. Margo had collected them from every source available to a resident of The Palms at Lago Vista: the quarterly newsletter (*Palm Fronds*, full color, printed on cardstock, featuring a "New Neighbors" column and an "In Memoriam" sidebar that Margo had scissored out with surgical regularity); the bulletin board in the community room where candids from holiday parties and talent shows were pinned under the heading MAKING MEMORIES AT THE PALMS; the memorial service programs, which at The Palms at Lago Vista were produced with a consistency and professionalism that suggested either a deep well of communal grief or an institutional comfort with death that should have alarmed someone earlier than it alarmed me. She had also, apparently, printed photographs from Facebook — the accounts of residents' children and grandchildren, mostly — and from a website called OsceolaTribune.com that published local obituaries with headshots. The photographs were arranged not alphabetically, not chronologically, but *spatially* — mapped, roughly, to where the subjects lived within the community. Building 12 in the upper left. Building 16 in the lower right. The pool house, drawn by hand in blue ballpoint, at the center, like a heart.

The connections were yarn. Red for romantic entanglements. Blue for financial connections. Green — and I did not learn this until I found the legend she had written on the inside of a cabinet door — for "therapeutic relationships," meaning people she had treated, or counseled, or listened to in the capacity that blurred, in a retirement community where your neighbors are also your clients are also your bridge partners, into something without a clinical name. Black for the dead.

There was a great deal of black.

I will walk you through it. I have earned that right, or I have forfeited the right to remain silent about it, which may be the same thing.

In the upper left quadrant, Building 12: Arthur and Dot Schneider, married fifty-four years, connected by red yarn to each other and by a second strand of red from Arthur to a man named Alan Muñoz in Building 15. The red yarn between Arthur and Alan was annotated with a Post-it in Margo's firm, clinical hand: *A. Schneider — presented 2003 w/ performance anxiety and guilt re: same-sex attraction. Referred to Dr. Paulson for individual. Relationship w/ A.M. began approx. 2005. Dot aware by 2009. Accommodation reached.* Below that, in what I came to think of as Margo's *other* hand — the handwriting that arrived when the disease moved through her like weather — a second Post-it: *Arthur is DEAD. Ask Dot about the morning. She called Kendrick NOT 911. WHY.*

Arthur Schneider died on March 3, 2023. I verified this in the *Palm Fronds* "In Memoriam" sidebar. Cause of death listed as cardiac event. He was eighty-seven. There was nothing, on the surface, unusual about an eighty-seven-year-old man dying of a cardiac event. Margo's annotation did not make it unusual. Margo's annotation made it a data point. One of many. All of which shared a single feature that I did not notice until I had been studying the wall for three weeks: in every case Margo flagged, the deceased's survivors had called Dr. Lewis Kendrick before calling 911. In every case, Kendrick had arrived first. In every case, the death was attributed to natural causes. In every case, the black yarn led back, eventually, through intermediary connections, to the hand-drawn pool house at the center of the wall.

I want to be precise about what I verified and what I could not.

I verified the romantic connections. Not all of them — Margo had mapped thirty-seven relationships across the community, some of which dated back to the 1990s and involved residents who were themselves now dead — but the ones I could check, I checked. I cross-referenced names with the journals. Margo's clinical records were meticulous: dates, presenting complaints, treatment modalities, outcomes. She had disguised her clients' names in the early notebooks with initials, but by the 2000s she had grown either careless or confident, and the full names appeared. I found Gloria Reeves. I found Ed Forchetti. I found Alan Muñoz, and Dot Schneider, and a woman named Patrice Okonkwo-Bell who had moved to The Palms at Lago Vista in 2011 and who had died in her sleep on August 19, 2023, six months before I arrived, and whose death was one of the four that Margo had circled in red marker and connected to the pool house with black yarn and annotated with a Post-it that read, in her deteriorated hand: *THE SAME PILLS. THE SAME PILLS. THE SAME PILLS. WHO HAS THE KEY TO THE POOL HOUSE.*

I verified the financial connections where public records existed. Property transfers. Liens. Two residents who had named Dr. Kendrick in their wills — this I found not in public records but in Margo's journals, where one of the residents had mentioned it during a session about estate anxiety.

What I could not verify — what I still cannot verify, sitting here on this lanai with the bougainvillea brushing the screen and the sprinklers cycling on across the courtyard — was whether the pattern was a pattern. Whether four deaths among elderly residents over two years, all attributed to natural causes, all attended first by a retired physician who lived in the community, constituted evidence of murder or evidence of proximity. Whether Margo Plimpton, career sex therapist, owner of a mind trained to detect hidden connections between people's bodies and their lies, had uncovered a conspiracy or had constructed one — had seen the signal in the noise or had become the noise.

That was the wall. That was the document. That was the thing I stood in front of every morning while Margo slept and her coffee maker cycled through its routine, and I read her annotations like scripture, and I could not tell the holy from the mad.

---

I should tell you about the day Gloria Reeves came.

It was February, a Thursday. I know it was a Thursday because Margo's daughter, Laine, called on Thursdays, and I had already lied to Laine that morning — told her Margo was sleeping well, eating the protein shakes, showing no new behavioral concerns — and the residue of the lie was still in my mouth when the doorbell rang. I set two glasses of iced tea on the tray the way Margo liked, with the long spoons and the raw sugar in the small ceramic bowl she'd brought back from Oaxaca in 1989, because Margo had, even in her dissolution, an absolute intolerance for artificial sweetener and for the receiving of guests without ceremony. I carried the tray to the lanai. I set it on the glass-top table between the two wicker chairs. I retreated to my chair in the corner near the sliding door, the chair that was nominally there so I could monitor Margo's hydration and medication schedule but that was actually there because it was where the furniture sat, and I was the furniture.

Gloria Reeves was sixty-nine years old. She had been at The Palms at Lago Vista for six years. She was a former school principal from Dade County, a tall woman with close-cropped gray hair and a posture that communicated, even in the act of sitting down, that she had spent a career maintaining order in rooms full of people who did not want to be orderly. She wore linen pants and a blouse with a botanical print and earrings made of small green stones. She smelled of a perfume I could not name but that I later found referenced in Margo's journal entry for November 14, 2017: *G.R. — same fragrance as always, vetiver and something floral, she says her daughter buys it for her, she does not know it is the same fragrance Helen wore, or perhaps she does and that is the point, see notes from 11/2.*

"Margo, honey," Gloria said, settling into the wicker chair with the caution of a woman who has been warned, by someone at the gate or on the walking path or in the community room, that this will not be easy. "I brought you scones from that place on 192. The cranberry ones you like."

Margo was in the opposite chair. She had dressed herself that morning, which she could still sometimes do, and she had chosen well — a navy blouse, white slacks, the reading glasses on their beaded chain. She looked, in the slanted February light that came through the screen, like a woman you would trust with your secrets. She looked like someone who had spent four decades being trusted with secrets and who had never once betrayed that trust — except now the trust and the betrayal had become neurologically indistinguishable, and she could no more stop herself from surfacing what she knew than she could stop the plaques from thickening in her temporal lobe.

"Gloria," Margo said. Her voice was clear. I noted this. Clear days were not rare but they were unpredictable, and they were dangerous, because they gave Margo access to the full architecture of her knowledge without the executive function to modulate its deployment. A clear day meant the filing cabinet was open and the files were intact but the librarian was gone.

"How are you feeling?" Gloria asked, and looked at me for the first time, the briefest flick of eye contact that contained the question every visitor asked without speaking: *How bad is it today? What should I be prepared for?* I did not answer. I never answered. I was the furniture.

"I want to ask you about Patrice," Margo said.

The change in Gloria's body was immediate and total. It was the kind of change I imagine a therapist of Margo's caliber would once have catalogued with precision — the microexpression taxonomy, the postural shift, the sudden engagement of the orbicularis oculi. Gloria's hands, which had been resting on the arms of the wicker chair, moved to her lap. Her shoulders drew inward by perhaps half an inch. Her smile remained but it became structural rather than communicative, a load-bearing smile, a smile that was holding something up.

"Patrice passed last summer, Margo. We talked about this."

"We talked about it and you told me she died in her sleep and I am asking you now what I asked you then, which is who was with her."

"No one was with her, honey. She went to bed and she didn't wake up. It was peaceful."

"That is what you said. That is what Ed said. That is what Kendrick told the family. She went to bed and didn't wake up, like that's a thing that just *happens*, Gloria. People don't just stop. Something stops them."

"Margo —"

"You were there that week. I know you were there because you told me in October that you'd been bringing her dinners. You were in her unit. You saw her the evening before. What did she look like. What was on the counter. Were there pill bottles that shouldn't have been there. Was there a glass. Was there —"

"Margo, I brought her a casserole. A sweet potato casserole. She ate some and she said she was tired and I left. That's all."

"That's not all. I have your intake form from 2017. You told me about Patrice in our third session. You told me what happened between you and Patrice in the pool house after Ray's memorial, and you told me it was the first time you'd touched a woman since Helen, and you cried for forty minutes — you *cried*, Gloria — and now you're telling me she just died and that's that and nobody looked at what was on the counter —"

"You cannot — Margo, that was *private* —"

"Everything is private until someone dies and then it's evidence. I need you to tell me about the pills. Patrice was on metoprolol and donepezil and a statin, she told me herself, and Kendrick had been adjusting her metoprolol for months without consulting her cardiologist, and I need to know —"

"I don't know anything about her pills!"
