# The Booth

---

## I.

---

**STAFFORD'S ALL-NIGHT DINER — SHIFT LOG**
**Employee:** Dara Birch, overnight (11 PM–7 AM)
**Date:** Thursday, November 14
**Weather:** Cold rain. The kind that doesn't commit to being anything else.

---

Booth 6 is occupied.

Coffee initiated at 11:04 PM. Black, no sugar. Counterclockwise stir, seven rotations, handle turned to two o'clock before first sip. First napkin pulled from dispenser at 11:07. Written on. Folded. Torn in half. Torn in quarters. Quarters placed beneath saucer.

Second napkin at 11:23.

He is wearing the brown corduroy jacket, which means it is a weekday. (Saturdays: olive field coat. Sundays and federal holidays: the grey cardigan with the elbow patch on the left side only, the right elbow having worn through sometime around year six.) The jacket has achieved a state of material sympathy with the booth's vinyl — both cracked in corresponding locations, as if they have been ageing together, which they have.

Booth 6 seat cushion: compressed to roughly 40% of original thickness on the left side, 70% on the right. This corresponds to his habit of leaning on his left elbow while writing. The table's laminate has a pale half-moon where eleven years of saucers have bleached the surface. When I wipe the table around his cup — I do not move the cup, I have never moved the cup — the rag catches on a groove in the laminate where his pen presses through the napkins.

Coffee consumption tonight as of 2:15 AM: four cups. Running total since my employment began (six years, two months): approximately 2,340 cups, at my best estimation. Linda, who had the overnight before me, kept a tally on the walk-in freezer door in grease pencil. Her count, assuming a consistent pace before my time and correcting for the two-week period in 2016 when the diner closed for the gas leak: approximately 4,017.

Linda's grease pencil mark is still on the freezer door. Management has not removed it. Management has also not removed the man from Booth 6. Both seem to be regarded as permanent features of the refrigeration and seating infrastructure, respectively.

The bookshop across the street closed at 9 PM. Its window display is lit. From here I can see a book propped on a small wire easel, facing outward toward the road. I have never paid attention to which book. There is always one.

Napkin count at end of shift: eleven. All destroyed.

He said nothing tonight.

**Seating Area — Anomalous Finding, Booth 6:**

Vinyl upholstery on bench seat (east-facing) exhibits wear pattern inconsistent with standard customer rotation. Left-side compression measured at approximately 1.3 inches below original seat plane. Right side within normal range. Wear pattern suggests sustained single-occupant use over extended period. Table surface shows localized discoloration (approx. 4" semicircular bleaching, consistent with repeated placement of ceramic dishware in fixed position). Laminate scoring observed at table center, consistent with repeated pen-pressure application.

Booth frame shows no structural deficiency. Floor tile beneath booth exhibits minor but measurable concavity (est. 0.5mm below surrounding tile plane), likely attributable to static load over time. This is not a code violation. It is noted here because I have been conducting inspections of this establishment for nine years and the same individual has been seated in this booth during every visit. The previous inspector, R. Canales (retired), noted the same in his final three reports.

This is not a health concern. There is no regulation governing maximum booth-occupancy duration. I am noting it because the form asks me to flag anomalous conditions, and a man-shaped depression in a vinyl bench seat, visible to the naked eye from across the room, is anomalous.

**Food Temperature Compliance:** All items within acceptable range.

**Ambient Conditions, Booth 6 Vicinity:** Temperature consistent with rest of establishment. Odor: coffee, paper, and something faintly mineral that I associate with old library books, though I cannot identify the source. The bookshop across the street was open during my inspection. Its window display featured a hardcover book on an easel, angled toward the diner. I mention this only because the man in Booth 6 was looking at it when I passed his table, and he did not appear to be reading it so much as *declining* it, the way you might shake your head at a waiter offering dessert — a refusal that still acknowledges the existence of the question.

**Overall Rating:** Satisfactory.

**Recommended Follow-Up:** Standard (12 months). No action required re: Booth 6. Whatever is happening there is not within my jurisdiction, and I suspect it is not within anyone's.

**STAFFORD'S ALL-NIGHT DINER — SHIFT LOG (ADDENDUM)**
**Employee:** Dara Birch
**Date:** Thursday, November 14 (cont'd)

---

The health inspector's report is pinned above the time clock. Jerry highlighted the part about the exhaust hood. Nobody highlighted the part about Booth 6. Nobody ever does.

I want to state for the record — though there is no record, this is a shift log that no one reads, which is why I write in it honestly — that the man in Booth 6 is not a curiosity to me. He is not a "character." The regulars who come in at 4 AM and perform their personalities for an audience of hash browns, they are characters. The man in Booth 6 is a *condition.* Like the freezer that hums in B-flat, or the way the door sticks in August. You do not wonder about these things. You accommodate them.

He is in the booth the way the booth is on the floor.

3:40 AM. His coffee is cold. I will refill it in four minutes because that is when he lifts the cup, finds it unsatisfactory, and sets it down in a way that adjusts the handle from two o'clock to four o'clock, which is the signal. He does not know it is a signal. I have never told him. There are entire languages in this diner that only I speak.

The bookshop window is still lit. The book on the easel is still there. From this angle, I think it has a blue cover. I don't know why I noticed that. I've never noticed before.

## II.

---

**STAFFORD'S ALL-NIGHT DINER — SHIFT LOG**
**Employee:** Dara Birch
**Date:** Saturday, November 16
**Weather:** Clear. The kind of clear that feels accusatory.

---

Tonight I am going to do something I have never done in this log, which is reproduce, from memory, the contents of his napkins.

I should explain. He writes them and destroys them. This is his practice and I respect it the way you respect any religious observance you don't share. But I have been working this shift for six years. I clear the saucers. The quarters of napkin are sometimes legible before I bus them. And I have — I will be honest — I have occasionally, in the early hours when the diner is empty and he is between drafts, unfolded a piece under the counter. Not to invade. To *understand the equipment.* Like reading the manual for that freezer.

I stopped doing it after the first year. Not because I felt guilty, though I did. Because they were too good. They made me feel like I was holding something alive that was meant to be buried, and if I kept reading them, I would become responsible for knowing what was in them, and I am a waitress, not a priest.

But I remember.

---

**NAPKIN (RECOVERED FRAGMENT — APPROXIMATE DATE: YEAR 3)**

*Today I will walk across the street and I will open the door and the bell above the door will make a sound and she will look up and I will*

---

That's where it stops. The pen line continues past the last word, a long trailing stroke that runs off the napkin's edge. I remember the geometry of the tear — he ripped this one in thirds, not quarters. Thirds means agitation. Quarters is standard. Halves only is a calm night. Once, in my first month, he tore one into confetti. I never found out what it said.

---

**NAPKIN (RECOVERED FRAGMENT — APPROXIMATE DATE: YEAR 5)**

*The problem is not courage. I have considered this from every angle and courage is not the variable. The problem is that I have sat here long enough to become the man who sits here, and the man who sits here is not the man who walks in there. If I stand up I will have to be someone I have not been in five years, and that person may no longer exist. She would be opening her door to a ghost. Ghosts do not buy books. Ghosts haunt the places where they*

---

Torn in quarters. Standard disposal. Placed under saucer. I found this one at 4:12 AM. It was the best writing I have ever read, and I put it in the bus tub with the cold fries and the cash-register tape from the night's receipts, and I did not fish it out. I thought about fishing it out. I thought about it for two hours.

---

**NAPKIN (RECOVERED FRAGMENT — APPROXIMATE DATE: YEAR 7)**

*Dear*

---

That's it. Just "Dear." The rest of the napkin was blank. He held it for forty-five minutes before tearing it.

---

**NAPKIN (RECOVERED FRAGMENT — APPROXIMATE DATE: CURRENT YEAR)**

*REASONS TO CROSS THE STREET*

*1. She is there.*
*2. I have imagined the conversation 11,000 times and in 4 of those imaginations I did not immediately humiliate myself.*
*3. The distance is 34 feet. I measured it by counting ceiling tiles reflected in the window and extrapolating. 34 feet is not a meaningful distance. People cross 34 feet to reach a restroom.*
*4. The book in the window today was*

*REASONS NOT TO CROSS THE STREET*

*1. She may not remember me.*
*2. She may remember me.*
*3. I am not a person who walks into places like that.*
*4. What if she didn't mean it.*
*5. Eleven years is an explanation I cannot give standing in a doorway.*
*6. The coffee here is*

---

That one he didn't tear. He folded it very small — smaller than usual, tight as a note you'd pass in school — and put it in his jacket pocket. The corduroy one. I have never seen him pocket one before. I noted it at the time as a deviation. I am noting it again now because I have begun to suspect that there is a pocket somewhere in that apartment of his — he must have an apartment, he does leave during the daytime hours, I am told — filled with the ones he could not bring himself to destroy. An archive of the almost-sent.

I changed the menu board tonight.

The daily special had been Meatloaf (Jerry's call, Jerry always defaults to meatloaf). I erased it. I wrote:

**TODAY'S SOUP: LUKEWARM AMBIVALENCE**

I watched him from the counter. He looked up at the board. He read it. His hand tightened on his coffee cup — a visible thing, the knuckles shifting — and he held that grip for a count of six. Then he looked back down at his napkin. Then he pulled a new napkin and began writing again.

I don't know what I expected. Laughter, maybe. Recognition. Some crack in the structure.

The menu board is my only channel. It faces the room. He reads it. Whatever I write there, he *reads.* It is the closest thing to a conversation we have ever had, and he does not know we are having it.

Napkin count at end of shift: fourteen. All destroyed except the pocketed one.

He said "thank you" when I refilled his cup at 2:30 AM. That's the fourth time this month.

## III.

---

**STAFFORD'S ALL-NIGHT DINER — SHIFT LOG**
**Employee:** Dara Birch
**Date:** Tuesday, December 3
**Weather:** Overcast. Temperature: 28°F. The window has frosted at the edges, which means his view of the bookshop is framed, which means this will be a difficult night for him, because a frame makes a picture, and a picture is easier to look at than a street.

---

2:47 AM.

He is going to stand up.

I know the signs the way you know the signs of weather — not because anyone taught you but because you have stood outside long enough. The counterclockwise stir happens first. Seven rotations. Standard. Then the cup goes down, but not at two o'clock. At twelve. Straight ahead. The handle pointing at the bookshop window like a compass needle.

Then his palms flatten.

They go down on the table edge, both of them, fingers splayed, and his weight shifts forward. I can see it from behind the counter — the architecture of intention assembling itself in his shoulders, his arms, the straightening of his spine. He stops being a man slouched
