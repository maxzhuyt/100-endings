# The Hall of Proper Losing

---

## I.

The floorboards remembered.

Forty-six years of bare feet had worn channels into the hinoki planks — not grooves exactly, but suggestions, the wood's patient record of where generations had pivoted, lunged, retreated. Hayashi Yoshiko knew them the way a blind woman knows her own kitchen: the soft dip at the center where her grandfather had drilled *kirikaeshi* until his students wept, the raised grain near the eastern wall where humidity crept in every June, the one board by the entrance that sang a half-tone higher than the others when you pushed off it with your right foot.

She stood barefoot at the center of the hall, 5:47 in the afternoon, and waited.

Golden hour came to the Yamamoto Kendo Hall through three tall windows on the western wall. The glass was original, fitted in 1971 by her grandmother's husband, and it had warped in the way old glass warps — slowly, over decades, thickening at the bottom so that the light passing through it bent and pooled, arriving on the floorboards not as clean rectangles but as something more like spilled honey, amber and heavy and slightly wrong. She had loved this light since she was five years old. She loved it now with the fierce, unsentimental attention of someone who understood that love required no justification but did require *presence* — you had to stand in the thing you loved and let it be what it was.

Above her, the ceiling stain spread. It had been the size of a dinner plate in March. Now — September — it was the size of a futon. Brown at the edges, darker at the center, shaped like a country on no existing map. She had placed a bucket beneath it during the June rains. The bucket was still there, dry now, a plastic orange accusation in a room of wood and tradition.

"Sensei! We're starting?"

Doi-san stood in the doorway. Seventy-three years old, retired postmaster, bogu tied with the careful looseness of a man who had learned to dress himself in armor at sixty-eight and still found the whole enterprise faintly amusing. His *men* — the helmet — sat slightly crooked. It always sat slightly crooked. She had corrected it perhaps two hundred times.

"Your *men* is crooked," she said.

He reached up, adjusted it in the wrong direction, and beamed at her through the metal grille.

Behind him, the others arrived in their customary disorder. Tanaka-san and Ōba-san came together, as always, because Tanaka-san drove and Ōba-san had surrendered his license after the incident with the post box. Murakami-san appeared from the changing room already in full bogu, his *kote* — the gloves — on the wrong hands, an error he made approximately every third session and corrected with the cheerful indifference of a man who had survived stomach cancer and considered all subsequent problems minor. Shimizu-san arrived last, winded from the hill, carrying a plastic bag of mikan oranges that he deposited by the entrance with the quiet ceremony of a temple offering.

Six men. Six names on the enrollment sheet tacked to the wall by the door, written in her own hand because her grandmother's had trembled too badly by the end. The sheet had space for forty. Her grandmother had once filled it.

"Line up," Yoshiko said.

They lined up. Not well. Doi-san stood too close to Tanaka-san, which meant Tanaka-san stepped sideways into Ōba-san, who grunted. Shimizu-san was still in his socks.

"Shimizu-san."

"Ah." He peeled them off, embarrassed, and tucked them into his *hakama* ties, where they hung like small white flags of surrender.

She raised her *shinai* — four bamboo slats bound with leather, the *tsuka* wrapped in the pattern her grandmother had preferred — and the room changed. It happened every time and she never tired of it. Six old men, variously breathless, variously tilted, variously present, and yet when the *shinai* came up they straightened, or tried to, and something passed across their faces that was not quite seriousness but was adjacent to it: an acknowledgment that within the borders of this room, for the next ninety minutes, certain things mattered. The angle of the left foot. The position of the hands. The quality of the *kiai*, even if it emerged as more wheeze than thunder, even if Tanaka-san's sounded like a teakettle and Murakami-san's like a man surprised by a spider.

"*Mokusou*," she said. Close your eyes. Meditate.

They closed their eyes. She did not close hers. She watched them — six men, the youngest sixty-eight, the oldest seventy-nine — standing in borrowed armor in a hall that was falling apart, in a town that was falling apart, trying to hold their spines straight, trying to remember what she'd taught them about breathing from the belly, trying to be, for this brief window, *students* rather than retirees, widowers, men whose children had moved to cities and who came here on Tuesdays and Thursdays not because they loved kendo — most of them had taken it up within the last five years, after their retirements, after their losses — but because this was where they could stand in a line and be *corrected*, and correction, she had come to understand, was a form of care that did not require anyone to say the word.

She watched the light move across them. Amber bending through warped glass. The wood breathing beneath their feet.

"*Mokusou yame*. Open your eyes."

Practice began.

It was, by any competitive standard, terrible. Doi-san's *men* strike landed on Tanaka-san's shoulder. Tanaka-san's counter was a full second late and accompanied by an apology so elaborate it constituted its own *kata*. Ōba-san and Shimizu-san engaged in what appeared to be a polite disagreement conducted through shinai — each waiting for the other to strike first, neither wanting to seem aggressive, the two of them advancing and retreating with the cautious courtesy of men merging into traffic. Murakami-san struck well but forgot to *kiai*, producing a strike that was technically a point but spiritually silent, a sword cut with no soul in it.

She moved among them. Watched. Let most errors pass — the ones that were merely aesthetic, that came from age and limitation and the irreducible comedy of old joints in heavy armor. But certain errors she could not forgive. When Doi-san dropped his left heel during a lunge, she was there, the tip of her *shinai* pressed gently against his Achilles tendon.

"Here," she said. "Your weight lives here. If it lives here, you can go anywhere. If it doesn't, you've gone nowhere."

He nodded with the solemnity of a man receiving philosophical instruction, then immediately dropped his heel again.

In the corner where the space heater stood — the one that worked only on its highest setting, so that in winter the choice was between hypothermia and a four-foot radius of equatorial intensity — the *nafuda-kake* hung on the wall: the name plate rack where students' wooden tags were displayed. Forty hooks. Six tags. The empty hooks held dust.

Above the *nafuda-kake*, her grandmother's calligraphy. Four characters, brushed in a single breath at age eighty-one, the year before she died:

正しく負ける

*Tadashiku makeru.*

To lose properly.

The men practiced for ninety minutes. She corrected and demonstrated and stood in to receive when they needed a target. At 7:17, Shimizu-san's left knee announced its opinion, and she called the session. They bowed — to each other, to the *shinzen* at the front of the hall where a small wooden shelf held a photograph of her grandmother and a dry vase that had once held flowers and now held a single *shinai* *tsuba*, the hand guard, placed there by Doi-san for reasons he had never explained and she had never asked about.

They changed slowly, with the careful movements of men for whom dressing and undressing had become acts of negotiation with the body. She heard them through the thin wall of the changing room: Tanaka-san asking if anyone had watched the Hanshin Tigers game, Ōba-san complaining about the new vending machine outside the post office that accepted only digital payment, Murakami-san saying nothing, which was how Murakami-san said most things.

They left in ones and twos. Doi-san last, lingering at the door.

"Sensei. My wife made *tsukemono*. I left a jar by the shoe rack."

"Thank you, Doi-san."

He stood there a moment longer, as if he had something else to say. He did this sometimes — hovered in the doorway with the look of a man composing a difficult letter. She had learned to wait.

"The hall feels good today," he said finally. "The light."

"Yes."

He left.

She was alone.

The hall held the silence differently when it was empty — not absence but *presence* reversed, the way a bell's cavity holds the shape of the sound. She could hear the floorboards still ticking, expanding in the cooling air. She could hear the water stain growing, or imagined she could — a slow capillary creep behind the plaster.

She took her position at the center of the hall. Bare feet in the channels. *Shinai* in her hands, not raised — held at her right hip, point angled down, the posture called *sage-tō*, the resting sword.

Then she began.

Solo *kata*. The formal exercises. Patterns her grandmother had taught her at age seven, her grandmother having learned them from her own teacher, Yamamoto-sensei, the man who had built this hall and whose name it bore, who had died two years before Yoshiko was born but whose movement still lived in her body the way light lived in wood: absorbed, transformed, released in altered form.

She moved through the first three alone, then the fifth, which was her grandmother's favorite and the one she found most difficult — a pattern that required you to step *into* the opponent's strike, not away from it, to close distance at the moment of greatest danger, a movement that violated every instinct of self-preservation and replaced it with something her grandmother had called *shin* — trust, but not in the opponent. Trust in the geometry. Trust that if you were where you were supposed to be, disaster could not reach you.

The amber light dimmed. The windows went from gold to grey. She did not turn on the fluorescents. She practiced by feel, by memory, by the floorboards' familiar text beneath her feet, until the hall was dark and she was nothing but movement and breath, a body performing a conversation with a partner who was not there, who had never needed to be there, because the *kata* contained both roles — the one who strikes and the one who receives — and to practice alone was not loneliness but *completeness*, the closed circuit of a form that needed nothing outside itself.

She stopped. Stood in the dark. Sweat ran down the channel of her spine beneath her *keikogi*.

Outside, a car passed on the road below the hill, its headlights sweeping briefly through the windows and across the wall where her grandmother's calligraphy hung. The characters flared white and vanished.

*To lose properly.*

She bowed to the empty hall. The floorboard by the entrance sang its half-tone as she stepped off it.

## II.

The town of Miyoshi had 4,300 residents in the year Yoshiko was born. It had 2,107 now, and the number declined with the steady, impersonal regularity of a tide going out. The elementary school had consolidated with the one in Kawano two valleys over; the building remained, its playground equipment rusting in postures of arrested play, a yellow slide going nowhere. The Lawson convenience store had closed and reopened as a community center that was open Mondays and Wednesdays and served as a polling station twice a year. The bus to Osaka ran four times daily, down from seven, the schedule posted at every stop in the clean, cheerful typography of the regional transit authority, font size large enough for elderly eyes, the departure times reading less like transportation and more like a standing invitation: *You can still leave. You can always still leave.*

The council meeting was held in the community center on a Tuesday evening, which meant Yoshiko arrived still damp from practice, her hair pulled back in the severe ponytail she wore under the *men* and had not bothered to undo. She sat in the third row. The metal folding chair was cold through her *hakama*, which she had also not changed out of. She was aware that this constituted a minor statement — showing up in kendo training clothes to a meeting that would discuss the future of her kendo hall — and she was aware that the statement would be read, by everyone in the room, as exactly the kind of stubbornness they expected from her, which made it both protest and confirmation, a gesture that reinforced the narrative it intended to resist. She sat in it anyway.

Chairwoman Ogata spoke with the particular gentleness reserved for decisions that had already been made. She was sixty-one, a former nurse, universally liked, effective in the way that the best rural administrators were effective: by making every predetermined outcome feel like a group discovery. She used a projector. There were slides. The slides showed an architect's rendering of the proposed Miyoshi Wellness and Community Activity Center, which in the rendering appeared as a clean, light-filled space with blond wood floors, yoga mats in neat rows, and large windows through which an improbably beautiful sunset poured. The rendering bore no resemblance to the current structure. This was because the architect had rendered not a renovation but a replacement: new framing, new floors, new windows. The only element retained from the original building was its footprint on the hillside.

"The prefecture's regional revitalization grant covers eighty percent of construction costs," Ogata-san said, advancing to a slide that showed a pie chart in agreeable pastels. "The remaining twenty percent would come from municipal reserves. Operating costs are projected to be offset within three years by usage fees and wellness tourism revenue."

Wellness tourism. Yoshiko let the phrase sit in her mouth like an unripe *umeboshi*.

"The current structure," Ogata-san continued, and here she paused to adjust her reading glasses with the practiced hesitation of someone preparing to be kind, "has been assessed by the prefectural engineer as requiring significant remediation before it can be certified for public use beyond its current limited status. The roof, in particular—"

"The roof leaks," said Yoshiko. "I know the roof leaks."

The room did not turn to look at her. They didn't need to. Everyone knew who she was, where she sat, what she would say.

"Hayashi-san, of course, and we all appreciate so much the work you've done maintaining the hall—"

"My grandmother maintained the hall. I maintain my grandmother's hall."

Ogata-san smiled. It was a beautiful smile, warm and full and absolutely impenetrable.

"The committee acknowledges the hall's cultural significance. We've included a heritage display area in the new design — a wall for historical photographs and artifacts. And we've proposed naming the main activity room the Yamamoto Room, in honor—"

"The main activity room."

"—in honor of the hall's history."

The Yamamoto Room. Where people would do yoga. Where the floor would be blond engineered wood that remembered nothing and bore no channels and would never learn the shape of anyone's practice because yoga mats would separate every foot from every plank.

She did not say this. She looked at the rendering on the projected screen — the clean space, the impossible sunset, the absence of everything that made the building the building — and she felt, not for the first time, the particular claustrophobia of being understood. Everyone in the room understood her. Ogata-san understood her. The room understood that she would object, that her objection would be noted, that the minutes would record her dissent in a single line, and that the conversion would proceed because the math was the math and the math did not factor in the sound a floorboard made when you pushed off it with your right foot.

Afterward, Doi-san found her in the parking lot. He had attended the meeting in civilian clothes — pressed trousers, a cardigan — and without his bogu he looked like what he was: an elderly man with kind eyes and a stoop he was losing the battle against.

"It's not decided yet," he said.

"It is decided, Doi-san."

He didn't argue. He stood beside her in the parking lot and they looked out at the town below — the streetlights marking the main road, the dark gaps where closed shops made absences in the light, the distant glow
