# The Cutlery Creek Block Party Massacre

## Being the Complete and Unabridged Personal Account of Mrs. Geraldine Mosby (née Yates), Sole Surviving Resident of Cutlery Creek Estates, Regarding the Events of June 13, 1987

---

**Editor's Note**

The following document was recovered in 2019 from the personal effects of Mrs. Geraldine Anne Mosby (1932–2018) following her death at the Sunrise Palms condominium complex in Tucson, Arizona. Mrs. Mosby had lived at Sunrise Palms since relocating from the Dayton, Ohio, metropolitan area in late 1987.¹ The manuscript, handwritten in blue ballpoint on seventy-three sheets of yellow legal pad paper, appears to have been composed in a concentrated period between September and November of 1987, approximately three to five months after the events described. Several pages bear water stains consistent with glass rings. One page (here designated p. 47) contains a marginal drawing of what appears to be a bird feeder, rendered in considerable detail, beside a paragraph describing the death of Margaret Ohlsen.

The text is presented here without significant editorial alteration, though obvious misspellings have been silently corrected and Mrs. Mosby's occasionally idiosyncratic punctuation has been standardized for readability. Her paragraph breaks, such as they are, have been preserved. Her underlinings — which are frequent, emphatic, and rarely predictable in their targets — have been rendered in italics.

For context: The Cutlery Creek Estates subdivision was a planned residential community of forty-six single-family homes located approximately eleven miles southeast of central Dayton, in unincorporated Greene County. Developed in 1968 by Prestige Home Corporation (later Prestige-National, later defunct), the subdivision was governed from its inception by a homeowners association whose bylaws, adopted in 1969, included provisions for architectural review, common-area maintenance, and the enforcement of residential standards including but not limited to fence height, lawn maintenance, exterior paint color, and the permissibility of various ornamental features.² The HOA was formally dissolved by unanimous vote of its four surviving non-resident property holders on February 3, 1988.

On the evening of June 13, 1987, during the subdivision's annual block party, resident Dale Edward Spurlock (1934–1987) killed twenty-seven people over a period of approximately ninety minutes using a brush hook, a gasoline-powered hedge trimmer, and a Ball-Peen hammer.³ Spurlock was found deceased the following morning on the median strip of Cutlery Creek Drive by officers of the Greene County Sheriff's Department.⁴ Mrs. Mosby was the sole surviving resident present at the event.

Mrs. Mosby was contacted by the Greene County Historical Preservation Society in 1994, 1997, and 2003 regarding the possible donation of her manuscript to the county archive. She declined on each occasion. In her final response, dated November 11, 2003, she wrote: "I have received your letter and I appreciate the interest. I would prefer not to. I am no longer involved in any form of homeowner governance and I would like to continue not being involved in any form of homeowner governance. The bird feeders here are adequate."

We are grateful to Mrs. Mosby's niece, Sandra Mosby-Kline, for permission to publish.

— *Dr. Janet Hoch, Department of Local and Regional History, Wright State University*  
— *Kevin Brill, M.A., Greene County Historical Preservation Society*

April 2020

## The Account of Geraldine Mosby

I want to say first off that Barbara Ridley's agapanthus was not properly mulched and never had been, not in any of the years I lived across the street from her, and whatever else happened that night, this fact should be entered into the record because I tried to bring it up at the meeting in April and Hal Westbrook talked right over me the way he always did when he thought a woman was being *particular,* and then three months later a man with a brush hook came through the cul-de-sac like God's own weed-whacker and it turned out being particular was the only thing that saved my life, so perhaps Hal should have listened, though of course by the time he might have reconsidered the value of listening he was in the Ridley pool and not in a swimming capacity.

I am writing this in Tucson because there is nowhere else to write it. The condo has adequate light. The bird feeders attract a type of hummingbird that Sandra tells me is called Anna's hummingbird and one that I believe is a Rufous, though the Rufous may be a different bird visiting at odd hours. I have no one to consult on this because I no longer know my neighbors and I intend to continue not knowing my neighbors for the remainder of my natural life, however long that turns out to be, which given the family history and the fact that I am eating better out here — the grapefruit situation is *outrageously* good — may be considerable.

But I have to go back.

I want to set the context.

People will say Dale just snapped, and I want to say first that a man does not *snap.* A man does what a man has been practicing in miniature for decades, and Dale Spurlock had been practicing his particular form of measurement since at least 1970, which is when he first came to my fence with a tape measure and I learned something about him that I filed away in the part of my brain where I keep things I have noticed but cannot yet *use,* the way you keep a button in a drawer because you don't know which coat it fell off of but you know it fell off of *something.*

---

It was a Tuesday. I know it was a Tuesday because I had just gotten back from the Kroger on Dorothy Lane, which I only went to on Tuesdays because Wednesdays were when Connie Ferris went and Connie Ferris had a habit of standing in the cereal aisle and telling you about her son's orthodontic situation for a duration that could not be measured with any instrument known to modern science, and I say this as a woman who has just spent an evening crawling through a neighborhood with a man who took measurement *very seriously,* so I feel I am qualified to speak on the limits of measurement.

I was putting the groceries away. I had purchased a reasonable quantity of canned pears — the Dole ones in light syrup, not the Del Monte, because the Del Monte packed in heavy syrup and heavy syrup on a canned pear is an act of aggression against the pear — and I was stacking these in the pantry when I saw, through the kitchen window, Dale Spurlock standing at my side fence with a tape measure.

Now.

I should describe Dale Spurlock for posterity.

Dale was not a large man or a small man. He was the exact size you would expect of a man who would one day kill twenty-seven people at a block party with a brush hook, which is to say he was *medium.* He was of medium height and medium build and he wore pleated khaki shorts that were exactly the color of *institutional disappointment,* a sort of beige that gave up on itself halfway through the dyeing process. He had a short-sleeve button-down shirt — also beige, also defeated — and white tennis shoes of the kind that were sold at Sears and purchased by men who had never played tennis and did not intend to start. He had a pocket protector. He had a pocket protector in 1970 and he had a pocket protector in 1987 and I am certain that if the coroner's office checked, they would find that he died with a pocket protector, because Dale Spurlock did not enter any situation without preparation for the taking of notes.

He was standing at my fence — which was a four-foot painted cedar fence, regulation height, I want to be very clear about this, it was *four feet* — and he had the tape measure extended from the ground to the top rail, and he was looking at the tape measure the way a doctor looks at an X-ray. Not at the fence. Not at the yard. Not at me, standing in my own kitchen in my own house on a Tuesday with canned pears in my hands. At the *measurement.* The number on the tape was the only living thing in his field of vision.

I went outside. This was 1970. You went outside when a neighbor was at your fence. You did not call anyone. You went out and you said good morning and you asked if everything was all right and this was the social contract and this was what the brochure from Prestige Home Corporation had sold us on when Gerald and I bought the lot in 1968: *community.* Neighbors who looked out for each other. Neighbors who *noticed.*

I said, "Good morning, Dale."

He did not look up.

I said it again. "Good morning."

He wrote a number in a pocket notebook — a small spiral one, the kind reporters used on television shows — and then he pressed the button on the tape measure and it retracted. The sound it made was a snap and a hiss together. Like a small animal biting its own tail. I can hear it right now, sitting in Tucson with the hummingbirds, I can hear the exact pitch and duration of that tape measure retracting, which is a terrible thing to carry in your head for seventeen years but I have carried worse since June and I believe I will carry worse still before the Rufous and I are done.

Then he looked at me.

He had colorless eyes. I don't mean they were pale blue or light grey. I mean they were the color of *nothing.* They were the color of the space between channels on a television set. He looked at me and he did not greet me and he said — and this was the only thing he said, and this was the thing I put in the drawer with the button — he said: "Four feet and one-quarter inch."

Then he left.

I stood in my yard with the smell of cut grass and the sound of the Hendersons' sprinkler system going *tch-tch-tch-tch* and I thought: *That's a strange man.* And then I went back inside and I finished putting away the canned pears and I started the laundry and I did not think about Dale Spurlock again for several weeks, at which point the first HOA violation notice arrived in our mailbox, typed on a manual typewriter, citing Cutlery Creek Estates Bylaws Section 4, Subsection 2(a), regarding the maximum permissible fence height of forty-eight inches, accompanied by a handwritten note in the margin that read: "48.25 inches. See measurement. Attached."

Gerald laughed. Gerald was alive then and inclined to laugh at things that later turned out not to be funny, which was his primary quality as a husband and I loved him for it and I occasionally wanted to strangle him for it, which is I suppose the range available in a marriage. He said, "Gerry, the man measured your fence and you're a quarter inch over. That's just Dale. That's just *Dale being Dale.*"

That was the phrase people used. That was the phrase the whole neighborhood used for seventeen years. *Dale being Dale.* Dale measuring the Ohlsens' mailbox post and finding it two inches too close to the sidewalk. Dale photographing the Chens' Christmas lights and submitting a formal complaint that the icicle-style lights constituted a "non-approved hanging ornamental feature." Dale at every single HOA meeting with his spiral notebook and his pocket protector and his colorless eyes, raising his hand during the open comment period and reading, in a voice that was the audio equivalent of his beige shorts, a prepared statement about setback violations and drainage-grade interference and the ongoing, *unconscionable* non-compliance of multiple residents with the fence-height provisions of Section 4, Subsection 2(a).

Dale being Dale.

And every time he did it, and every time the board thanked him for his input and tabled the matter and moved on to the question of whether to resurface the tennis court, something in Dale's colorless eyes did not go away. It *accumulated.* It was there at the next meeting and the next and the next, and it was a quarter-inch thicker each time, like sediment, like something geological, and I saw it and I filed it and I told Gerald once, in 1978, "That man is going to do something one day," and Gerald said, "He's going to do something all right, he's going to bore us all to death," and then Gerald laughed the laugh of a man who was not, as it turned out, going to die of boredom.

Gerald died of a heart attack in 1983, in the driveway, carrying a bag of mulch. Cedar mulch. I had asked him to buy cypress but they were out. That is the kind of detail I cannot stop providing and I want to acknowledge that I am aware of it as a tendency and I do not intend to curb it because these details are the only things I have left and I will hold them with both hands until my hands stop working.

---

The night of June 13, 1987, I was at the block party, which was held annually in the cul-de-sac at the end of Cutlery Creek Drive. There was a potluck arrangement. Connie Ferris brought her corn casserole, which tasted the way her son's orthodontic stories felt: like something endured rather than enjoyed. The Hendersons brought bratwurst. Barbara Ridley brought a fruit salad with Cool Whip that she called "ambrosia" the way Napoleon called himself Emperor — with complete conviction and no supporting evidence.

I had brought deviled eggs, which were good, because I am good at deviled eggs, and I do not say this to boast but to *record,* because those were the last deviled eggs I made for a neighborhood function and I want it known they were well-received.

Dale was not at the block party. Dale was never at the block party. Dale had not attended a social function in Cutlery Creek Estates since 1979, when he came to the Hendersons' Fourth of July picnic and spent the entire event measuring the setback between the Hendersons' portable badminton net and the property line. This was the year Joan Henderson asked him to leave, and he left, and he did not come back to any gathering, and no one thought this was a problem because Dale not being at a party was the best thing that could happen to a party, which is a horrible thing to say about a person and is also true and I am finished pretending those two things cannot both be true at the same time.

At approximately 7:40 in the evening — and I know the time because I had just looked at my watch to calculate whether I could leave by 8:00 without being rude, which was a calculation I performed at every neighborhood event with the precision Dale brought to fence measurement — the first thing I heard was not a scream. It was the hedge trimmer.
