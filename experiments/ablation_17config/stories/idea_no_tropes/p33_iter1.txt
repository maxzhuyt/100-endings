# The Architecture of Evil

---

At fourteen hundred hours on the eleventh of April, 1989, the wind was coming from the northeast at eleven kilometers per hour, which meant the smell traveled southwest toward the ejido road before dissipating over open scrub, which meant the three agents standing upwind—Comandante Benitez, Agent Zamarripa, Agent Luis Gavaldón, who had driven the second Suburban and who would be dead of a heart attack in Reynosa before the decade was out—could work for several minutes at a stretch without turning away, while those of us downwind worked in intervals of forty seconds, sometimes a full minute if you'd eaten nothing since morning, which I had not. The soil temperature at five centimeters' depth was thirty-nine degrees Celsius. I know this because Zamarripa had a probe thermometer intended for engine diagnostics that he pressed into the ground the way a nurse takes a pulse, and I watched him do it, and I asked for the number, and he said *treinta y nueve*, and I wrote it in the margin of a fuel receipt because my notebook was in the Suburban and I was not going to walk the ninety meters back across the clearing to retrieve it, because I understood already that if I stepped away from what we were finding I might not return without external compulsion, the way a diver who surfaces too quickly understands that the next descent requires an act of will the body will organize itself against.

The shovels were not ours. They belonged to the ranch. They were hanging in the equipment shed beside the corral, four of them, wooden-handled, the blades filmed with calcium oxide residite, which is to say quicklime, which is to say someone had used them recently and for the same purpose we would now use them, and Benitez sent Gavaldón to get them because Gavaldón was the youngest and because Benitez was the kind of commander who preserved hierarchy even in the presence of annihilation, which I respected then and respect now, because hierarchy was the only architecture we had against the knowledge that was coming up through the ground.

The first shovel blade went in at the southern edge of the corral, where the informant Serafín Hernández García had indicated, his hand shaking badly enough that the direction of his pointing was imprecise by approximately two meters, which did not matter because the relevant zone was large. I remember the sound. Caliche soil resists a blade the way certain bureaucracies resist inquiry—with a mineral stubbornness that is not active opposition but simply the structural consequence of calcium concentrations deposited across geological time, calcium that has no opinion about what passes through it but which nonetheless requires force, and Zamarripa was the one who struck the surface first, a sound like a pick against fired clay, and then the second stroke broke through, and then the texture changed, the soil went dark, and the blade met something that compressed instead of fracturing, and Zamarripa stopped. He did not look at me. He looked at the shovel. He looked at the differential where the color of the earth transitioned from grayish white to a brown so dark it was almost arterial, and he said nothing, and I said nothing, and the eleven-kilometer wind rearranged nothing.

The color of the transition layer is not in any report. I am telling you because the reports were written later and by men who arrived after the relevant moments had already been absorbed by those of us who were present, and because color is a fact, and I am in the business of facts even now, even thirty years after my credentials expired and my authority became merely personal, which is to say the most dangerous kind of authority there is, the kind no one audits, the kind that feeds only on its own conviction, which I acknowledge, which I tell you so that you understand I am aware of what I am, a man talking in a room that may or may not contain another person, performing an act of retrieval so practiced that the question of whether it remains retrieval or has become instead an act of—but that is not useful. The color was the color of coffee ground with piloncillo, the color of certain clays in Veracruz after rain, the color of what soil becomes when it has digested iron and protein and the particular suite of volatile fatty acids that are released during the anaerobic decomposition of human tissue in a subtropical climate at an average depth of eighty centimeters, and if you have smelled it once you carry the smell thereafter not in your memory but in the organ of smell itself, in the cilia, in the receptor neurons, so that certain ordinary encounters—a butcher shop in August, a drainage ditch after flooding, the particular humid breath of a city bus crowded beyond capacity—will return you instantly and without your consent to a corral in Tamaulipas where the ground is opening and nothing that comes out of it will ever go back in, not into the ground and not into certain compartments of the mind that you had believed, with the naïve confidence of a man who has not yet been tested, were under your authority.

But I have started wrong. Or rather I have started where my mind starts, which is with the excavation, which is with the dead, when in fact the dead are the consequence, and if you want to understand the consequence you must first understand the instrument, and if you want to understand the instrument you must first understand the land on which the instrument was built, because the land was there before the dead and before the man who made them dead and before the border itself, which is a fiction that the land tolerates the way a body tolerates a scar—by incorporating it into its surface without recognizing its authority.

---

The Rancho Santa Elena sits twenty-two kilometers west of Matamoros, Tamaulipas, in a stretch of scrubland that cartographers have named and renamed without improving their understanding of it. On a road map it is empty space between the city and the river and the secondary highway that connects nothing to nothing by way of nothing. The ejido roads feeding the property are unpaved, graded once in the administration of López Portillo and not since. In the dry season they are passable. In the rainy season—May through October, though the Rio Grande sets its own calendar and has flooded as early as March and as late as November, in 1988 swelling over its eastern bank with such indifference that the Customs station at Los Indios found river silt on its floor and dead catfish in its parking lot—in the rainy season the roads become theoretical, and the ranch becomes what it has always fundamentally been: an island surrounded not by water but by difficulty.

The brush is mezquite and huizache and granjeno, all thorn-bearing, the mesquite reaching four meters in the bottomlands where the moisture table is accessible, forming a canopy so continuous that from the air in summer it appears as solid ground, a green false floor beneath which entire geographies of movement are invisible. I have walked that brush. I have walked it in daylight and I will tell you that at twenty meters I lost visual contact with the agent ahead of me and could navigate only by sound, and the sound was poor, because the mesquite absorbs it, the density of branch and thorn operating as a baffle so effective that a man screaming at the center of a hundred-meter thicket would register at its edge as an ambiguity, a sound you would attribute to an animal or to the wind or to the creak of mesquite wood itself, which in heat produces its own frictional noise, a low intermittent groaning that the ranchers call *la queja*, the complaint, as though the trees are protesting their own existence, which in a landscape this brutal seems reasonable.

You must understand the thermal environment. From May through September the daytime temperature at the ranch exceeds forty degrees Celsius with a regularity that has the force of natural law. The heat is not abstract. It is a physical medium. It buckles the sight-lines so that the horizon becomes liquid and the scrub-line vibrates and a man standing at the ranch gate looking south toward the river twelve kilometers away sees nothing real—he sees refraction, the air bending light around its own temperature, and the landscape beyond three hundred meters becomes a hallucination produced by physics, which means that visual surveillance of the property from any distance is not merely difficult but optically impossible for five months of the year. The Policía Judicial could have parked a kilometer away every day from May to September and seen nothing. Not through negligence but through thermodynamics.

The caliche layer. I must tell you about the caliche because it is the true floor of everything that happened. Beneath twelve to fifteen centimeters of topsoil—sandy loam mixed with decomposed organic matter, workable, unremarkable—there is a stratum of calcium carbite so concentrated it qualifies in geological terms as calcite, not calcium carbonate in the sense of limestone but a pedogenic accumulation, decades or centuries of calcium leaching downward through the soil profile and reconsolidating into a layer that is, in practical terms, a natural concrete. This caliche extends across the Tamaulipas coastal plain with the universality of an agreement nobody signed. It is what makes the land cheap. It is what makes agriculture futile. It is what makes graves difficult to dig and, once dug, difficult to detect, because the caliche seals itself—rain carries calcium back across the disturbed surface and within a single wet season the scar in the ground is healed, is sutured shut by the same mineral process that created the difficulty in the first place, so that a grave dug in October is invisible by the following July, and a grave treated with the quicklime that Constanzo insisted upon—and here I must note that the calcium oxide reacts with the moisture in decomposing tissue to produce heat, temperatures exceeding sixty degrees at the center of the reaction, which accelerates dissolution—a grave treated with quicklime in caliche country becomes, within two rainy seasons, indistinguishable from the earth itself.

This is not atmosphere. I am not setting a scene. I am explaining to you why the Rancho Santa Elena worked. I am telling you that the functional requirements of what Constanzo needed—acoustic isolation, visual isolation, thermal camouflage, self-healing burial medium, jurisdictional ambiguity produced by a river that rearranges its own banks annually so that a crime committed on the Mexican side today might sit on coordinates that the river will claim for the American side by spring—that these requirements were met not by any human engineering but by the landscape itself, offered freely, the way the land has always offered its services to those who need to make things disappear. The smugglers were using that corridor before Constanzo was born. The ranch had been a transit point for marijuana under the Hernández family since at least 1981, possibly earlier, and before that it had been a staging ground for cattle theft, and before that it had served purposes I cannot document but can infer, because the land does not change and neither do the uses to which men put it, and the chaparral that hid the marijuana bales and the stolen longhorns and the bodies of Constanzo's victims was the same chaparral, answering to the same physics, indifferent in precisely the same way.

The Rio Grande is twelve kilometers south of the ranch and I must speak of it directly because it is the structural fact that every other fact depends upon. It is not a border. I have said this to prosecutors in two countries and to journalists who nodded and did not understand and to my wife who understood and wished she didn't. It is not a border. A border requires fixity. A border requires that the line it draws remain where it was drawn. The Rio Grande draws a new line every year. In 1988, the spring floods shifted the main channel fourteen meters east of its 1987 position along a three-kilometer stretch precisely adjacent to the territory most frequently used for cross-river drug transport, which meant that photographs taken by DEA aerial surveillance in 1987 were useless for jurisdictional determination in 1988 and which meant that the Mexican and American authorities could not agree, during the critical early weeks of the investigation, on which country's soil certain evidence had been found on, a dispute I watched consume eleven days while the remaining members of Constanzo's group dispersed toward Brownsville, toward Mexico City, toward the impunity that bureaucratic confusion confers as surely as any bribe.

The land cooperated with no one and everyone. That is the point I am arriving at. That is the argument beneath every physical description I have given you. The land was not cursed—the very word drags us into Constanzo's thinking, into the supernatural architecture he erected on this same ground, and I will not enter that architecture from this door—the land was *efficient.* It had been efficient for smugglers and would be efficient for smugglers again the moment the excavation was completed and the cameras left and the international attention ebbed, which took approximately eleven months, after which the corridor resumed its operations with the same reliability as the mesquite and the caliche and the river, and if you drive the ejido road today you will still lose the agent ahead of you in the brush, and the sight-lines will still buckle in the heat, and the ground will still resist your shovel and will still seal shut whatever you put inside it, and I am telling you this because the architecture of what happened at that ranch was not designed by Adolfo Constanzo. It was designed by the Pleistocene. He simply moved in.

---

The first body was not the first body. I mean by this that we knew, from Serafín's statement given at the Matamoros PJF station the previous night under conditions I will not describe because they are not relevant to the physical evidence and because I was not present and because what agents do in the hours between an arrest and a confession is a territory I surrendered my authority over when I surrendered my credentials, though I will say that the statement was detailed and that its details correlated with subsequent findings at a rate that was either miraculous or the product of a very specific kind of interview technique—we knew before the first shovel stroke that the ground held at least twelve. Serafín said twelve. Constanzo's other followers, interrogated separately, gave numbers between twelve and fifteen. The final count would be fifteen, and then, when the subsequent excavation in May expanded the search grid, it would be revised upward to include remains of victims whose identities were never established and whose death dates could not be fixed and who entered the official record as fragments: a femur consistent with a male between fifteen and twenty-five, a mandible with a single gold-capped molar, a cluster of phalanges found in a context that suggested a separate burial or a disturbance by animals, coyotes being common in the chaparral and having their own relationship with the land's efficiencies.

But the first body we recovered—the first body that was a body, that had a face still capable of being matched to a photograph, that came up from the ground in a condition that permitted what the forensic report calls *positive visual identification*—was the body of Mark Kilroy.

He was twenty-one. He was a pre-medical student at the University of Texas at Austin. He was from Santa Fe, Texas, which is not in New Mexico but in Galveston County, a small town near the Gulf Coast where his family had lived for the duration of his life, and his parents were named James and Helen, and his uncle was a U.S. Customs agent, which is a fact that does not change the nature of the crime but which changed the velocity of the investigation so dramatically that I cannot omit it without lying by omission, which I will not do, though I will lie to you in other ways before this is finished and I ask you to determine for yourself what those ways are, because a man who tells you he is honest is asking to be trusted, and a man who tells you he will lie is asking to be *listened to,* and I would rather be listened to.

Mark Kilroy was last seen alive at approximately two in the morning on the fourteenth of March, 1989, on a street in the zona de tolerancia of Matamoros, separated from the three friends with whom he had crossed the bridge from Brownsville for spring break. Spring break. I have studied this American custom. The young cross the river to drink because the drinking age in Mexico is eighteen and the drinking age in Texas is twenty-one, and the crossing takes seven minutes on the international bridge and costs a quarter and delivers them into a district where the economy depends on their arrival the way the mesquite depends on the water table—not gratefully but structurally—and the streets at two in the morning on a spring break Thursday are crowded beyond surveillance, and a young man separated from his friends for even ninety seconds is a young man who has entered the same probability field as a calf separated from the herd, and the predators who operate in both systems understand this, and patience is not required, only presence.

Serafín Hernández García and David Serna Valdéz took him. This is established.
