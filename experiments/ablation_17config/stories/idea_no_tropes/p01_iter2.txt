The car was a 2019 Chevrolet Equinox in federal white with 114,000 miles and an air freshener shaped like a pine tree that had stopped smelling like anything in the previous administration. Linda Chavez was driving. She had been driving since Baton Rouge because Margaux Elling drove the way she did everything else — too fast, with an air of moral conviction, as if speed limits were just another system that had failed to protect people.

Linda was on her third reorganization of the clipboard. The forms were nested: 91A on top, then the hazard register template, then the structural assessment matrix, then the egress evaluation sheet, then three blanks for supplementary notes. She had pre-tabbed each section with colored flags. Green for structural. Yellow for electrical. Red for imminent danger. She liked the flags. They made the invisible visible. They said: *this is where you look.*

In the passenger seat, Margaux held the coroner's reports in her lap and read them the way a thoughtful person reads poetry — slowly, with her finger tracing the lines, pausing to reread. She had highlighted the decedents' names in yellow. Not the causes of death. The names. Raymond Thibeaux. Marie-Pearl Arceneaux. Damon Lyle Fontenot. The fourth name was redacted, but the age was listed: nine. Margaux had highlighted the age twice.

"You highlighted the age twice," Linda said.

"I know."

"That's not going to change it."

"I know that too."

They did not particularly like each other. Linda found Margaux's intensity exhausting, the way she carried other people's deaths around with her like luggage she refused to check. Margaux found Linda's precision sterile, a woman who had transmuted compassion into tab colors. They were both, in their own estimation, the only person in the regional office who took the work seriously. This was the source of the friction: they agreed on the premise and diverged on every conclusion.

Louisiana flattened around them. Live oaks trailed moss over the road like torn curtains. The GPS said twenty minutes. Linda checked her flags one more time.

The lot was gravel and weeds. A chain-link fence surrounded the perimeter in eight-foot panels, the kind you saw at construction sites and Little League diamonds and anywhere that the boundary between *here* and *there* needed to be cheap and obvious. The padlock on the gate had been cut — bolt cutters, clean bite — and the gate stood six inches ajar, as if someone had opened it and then not bothered with the pretense of closing it again.

Linda parked twenty feet back and killed the engine. She sat for a moment with both hands on the wheel, looking at the gap in the gate the way a copy editor looks at a missing period.

"Padlock's been cut," she said.

"Coroner's people, probably. Or the fire marshal." Margaux was already unbuckling. "Or kids."

"I'm noting it."

"Note it."

Linda noted it: *Entry gate padlock severed (pre-arrival). Chain-link perimeter intact. No visible signage re: condemnation status — noncompliant with Parish Order 2024-441, §3(b).*

They gathered their equipment. Linda had the clipboard, a measuring tape, a flashlight, a digital camera, and a bag of colored flags on wire stakes for marking hazards in situ. Margaux had a handheld voice recorder, a printout of the site diagram, the coroner's reports, and a pen she kept clicking. They both wore steel-toed boots, high-visibility vests, and white hardhats. They looked, walking toward the gate, like two women about to have a very ordinary day.

The carnival was still in the way that abandoned places are still — not peaceful but *held*, like a breath someone started and forgot to release. Tarps covered the game booths in blue and silver, mottled with leaf-rot. The Ferris wheel was visible over the fence line, its frame intact but two of its gondolas missing, the empty mounting brackets jutting out like broken fingers. The sky was the color of old dishwater. It was 10:47 a.m.

Margaux stopped just inside the gate and inhaled. "You smell that?"

"I smell rust."

"Under the rust. Machine grease and — " She inhaled again. "Cotton candy."

"There is no cotton candy. This site has been closed for three months."

"I said I smell it, Linda. I didn't say there *is* cotton candy." Margaux clicked her pen and walked ahead.

Linda stood at the gate for three more seconds, cataloging: *Gravel lot, approx. 2.3 acres. Surface condition — poor. Drainage — no visible grading. Weed infiltration consistent with 90+ day vacancy.* She stepped through and followed Margaux into the carnival, her clipboard squared against her ribs like a shield.

Linda stepped into the funhouse because the funhouse was next on the diagram, and Linda did things in the order they appeared on the diagram. That was the whole point. You had a plan. You followed the plan. The plan was a leash you kept on the universe to stop it from getting strange.

The foyer was dim — one overhead bulb still worked, somehow, casting a brownish light that made everything look like an old photograph. The walls were painted plywood: clown faces, cartoon ghosts, a fat grinning moon. Standard carnival schlock. Linda swept her flashlight across the floor and noted the surface material (plywood over joists, paint worn to bare grain) and the visible condition (fair, excluding grade issue) and was three steps in when the floor decided to have an opinion.

It tilted. Not dramatically — eight degrees, she would later estimate, though in the moment it felt like the whole building had leaned over to whisper in her ear. Her left foot went out, her right knee buckled, and her body did what bodies do when the ground breaks its contract: it panicked. Her arms went wide. The clipboard flew. She grabbed for the wall with her left hand and caught it — or she caught *a* wall — but it was closer than it should have been, as if the room had narrowed by a foot while she wasn't looking, and her palm slapped painted plywood where a second ago there had been open air.

Her hardhat popped off her head with the lazy inevitability of a champagne cork, bounced once on the tilted floor, rolled in a half-circle, and came to rest at her feet, upside down, like a beggar's cup.

"*Goddammit.*"

She said it the way she said everything: precisely, with full phonetic commitment. She straightened up. She swiped dust off her vest. She picked up the hardhat and the clipboard and examined both for damage with the same degree of concern (the clipboard was fine; the hardhat had a scuff; the clipboard mattered more). On the deficiency log, she wrote, pressing so hard the pen embossed the page beneath:

*Item 005 — CONFIRMED. Floor grade creates immediate slip/fall hazard. Recommend CLOSURE of funhouse pending full structural evaluation.*

She put her hardhat back on. It sat on her head like it was already thinking about leaving again.
