&nbsp;

The haar lay over the water like a compress over a closed eye.

Moira sorted the letters in their cage, the wire diamonds of the partition pressing faint pink lozenges into her fingertips as she leaned, as she always leaned, slightly forward, as though the mail might speak if she inclined her body toward it at the correct angle. The morning post was thin. It was always thin now. Three letters for the Mackenzie croft, one of them from North Africa and bearing a censor's tape across its throat. A seed catalogue addressed to a woman who had been dead since Candlemas. A rate notice. And then the envelope.

It had no return address. Only a frank stamp she had never seen — not the General Post Office crown, not a military frank she recognized, but something that looked almost ecclesiastical, almost heraldic, pressed in ink the colour of cold tea. She turned it over. Her name was typed across the front. Not her married name. Not the name on the post office register. Her baptismal name, the one her mother had given her in the Gaelic, the one that appeared on no official document she had ever signed.

She opened it the way she opened everything that came through the cage: completely, carefully, with the posture of someone whose occupation was to handle other people's information without being changed by it.

Outside, the haar pressed itself against the glass. Kelp and paraffin. The particular grey of the coast in the third summer of the war — not the grey of absence but the grey of presence withheld, the colour of a sky that knows what it contains and will not say. The herring boats were out beyond the point, or were not out beyond the point; in the haar, one could not be sure if the sounds of engines carried from the water or from memory. She had lived here eleven years. She had sorted every letter that passed through this village. She knew which boys had written home and which had stopped writing. She knew the silences by shape, the way a compositor knows the spaces between lines of type. She knew that some absences were merely distance, and others were the distance that has no return address.

The requisition told her almost nothing. Report to the coastal facility. Date and time. Bring clothing for one week. The purpose of the assignment was obscured beneath a stripe of black ink so thick it had warped the paper.

She closed the cage. She counted the remaining letters into their slots. She drew the shutter and latched it. She climbed the stairs to the flat above, where the kettle was still warm and the bed was still made with the corners her mother had taught her, the hospital-tight corners, because her mother had believed that discipline in small things was a negotiation with catastrophe — if the corners held, the centre might.

She packed a bag: one change of clothes, a washcloth, her comb, the lipstick she had not worn since Donald left for the merchant fleet, a paperback whose title she would not afterward remember. She stood at the door. The haar was thinning at its edges, and through its gauze she could see, or thought she could see, the dark shape of the island in the bay, though it was too far, surely, to see from here.

She reached for her coat—

The fishing lodge smelled of creosote and old rope and something beneath both — the mineral exhalation of stone that has been wet for longer than anyone has been alive to notice. It had been repurposed with the brutal efficiency of wartime requisition: bunks bolted to walls that still bore the shadow-marks of mounted fish, a deal table scored with knife-cuts from decades of filleting, a single coal stove whose chimney drew badly and wept rust-coloured tears down the plaster.

Moira arrived at seven in the morning and found one person already there.

He sat on the farthest bunk with the stillness of someone who had been still for a long time — not resting but *stationed*, the way a sentry is stationed, except that what he guarded was only the particular pocket of air around his own body. He was reading a New Testament, its cover bloated and water-stained, the gilt of its edges eaten to a dull copper by damp. He held it as one holds a tool whose purpose one has forgotten but whose weight in the hand still feels correct. No piety in it. Just habit. The way a man might continue shaving in a house where there is no one left to see his face.

He looked up and assessed her the way she assessed him — quickly, by surface, by what could be inventoried. She catalogued: young, younger than her, but with the premature creases of a man who has spent time being disapproved of. Narrow hands. A conscientious objector's pallor — not the pallor of illness but of exclusion, the particular grey that comes from standing always slightly outside the room everyone else is in.

"Moira," she said.

"Ewan." He returned to his text, and she felt — *he was relieved that she had not asked why he was here*, the relief of a man who has explained himself too many times to too many people who were not listening — and then the thought was gone, or was not hers, and she was setting her bag on the bunk nearest the window.

The twins arrived an hour later, together, in oilskins that smelled so powerfully of herring and diesel that they preceded the men into the room by several seconds, the smell entering first like an advance party, an announcement. They were not identical — Callum was perhaps an inch taller, and the scar that ran through his left eyebrow had no corresponding mark on his brother — but they moved with the synchronization of men who had spent their lives hauling the same nets, reading the same water, sleeping in the same forecastle with the same pitch and roll translating their bodies into the same dreams. Callum said nothing. He surveyed the room the way one surveys a new boat — for leaks, for structural faults, for the places where the water would get in. Dòmhnall said, "This'll be us, then," which was not a question, and Callum took the bunk beside his brother's and sat on it and began unknotting a length of rope he had brought with him for no evident reason other than that his hands required employment.

Fenn arrived last, in the late morning, when the haar had burned off and the light through the salt-rimed windows was the pale, depthless light of the northern coast, the light that makes every surface look like a witness giving testimony. She came in with mud on her boots — heavy clay, not Highland peat, a southern soil, the rich ochre of arable land — and an accent that none of them could place. Not English exactly. Not Scots. Something grafted, the accent of someone who has moved too often to belong to a single inflection. She carried a canvas bag and a notebook and she looked at each of them in turn with the frank, undeferential attention of a woman who has spent the last year working in fields alongside people who do not speak to her, and who has learned to gather what she needs from observation alone.

"You're the last," Moira said. It was the kind of thing a postmistress says — the sorting statement, the confirmation of a complete delivery.

"Am I," Fenn said. Not a question either.

They waited. The stove ticked and sighed. Through the window, the light moved over the water in long, slow tongues. Moira looked at the view because the view was there, and because looking at a new room full of strangers requires interruptions. The water was grey, then silver, then grey again. The far shore was a dark line. And there — between the shore and the horizon, sitting in the water like a stripped skull, low and bare and treeless — was the island.

She knew it, of course. Everyone on this coast knew it. Gruinard. The military had taken it the year before. The sheep had been cleared off. No one said what happened there. No one said because no one knew, or because everyone knew and the knowing was itself a kind of quarantine, a thing you kept inside the locked ward of your own discretion.

The door opened. A man in uniform entered. She could see his collar and his hands. His collar bore no insignia she recognized. His hands held a clipboard. Above the collar, his face was — she could see his face, she was looking at his face, but afterward, when she tried to reassemble it, there was nothing there. Not blankness. Not a void. Simply a region of her memory that declined to report.

"You are to remain in this ward for a period of observation," he said. His voice was the voice of someone reading from a document. Not reading *aloud* — reading *from*. As though the document were the source and the voice merely the conduit. "You will be examined daily. You will take a supervised walk in the yard once per day. You will not attempt to communicate with anyone outside this facility."

"Observation of what?" Ewan asked. He had closed his book around one finger to mark his place.

"Observation," the man said, and went out.

The door closed. They heard it lock — not the click of a latch but the heavy, tidal sound of a bolt sliding home, the sound of iron finding its groove in iron. It was a sound Moira knew. It was the sound her cage made at the post office when she drew the shutter down.

She turned back to the window. The island sat in the water, a mile off, immovable, treeless, low and dark. The light crawled over it and found nothing to catch on.

She watched it the way she watched everything — completely, carefully, with the posture of someone whose job was to observe without being changed.

The island did not watch back. That was what she told herself. That was the version she kept.
