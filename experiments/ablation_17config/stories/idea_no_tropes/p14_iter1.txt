# The Corner Booth

## I.

They arrive in the same order every Saturday, and I could set my watch by them if I wore a watch, which I don't, because a watch on a waiter's wrist clinks against the plates and a man who clinks against plates is a man who has surrendered his dignity for a timepiece. Diane is first, always, at ten to seven, because Diane believes that arriving early to a reservation communicates moral superiority over people who arrive on time. She wears her reading glasses on a chain around her neck even though I have never once seen her read anything, including the menu, which she doesn't need because she orders the Montepulciano d'Abruzzo without looking at the wine list and the insalata caprese without looking at anything at all. She sits on the left curve of the banquette, facing the door, because Diane must see everyone who enters a room. This is not a preference. This is a load-bearing feature of her personality.

Linda arrives second, usually forty-five seconds after Diane, because Linda parks in the same lot but walks slower and will not, under any circumstances, be seen rushing. Linda orders whatever wine is newest to the list, not because she is adventurous but because she believes novelty is a form of revenge. Against whom, I have never been entirely certain, though I have my theories. She takes the seat to Diane's left, one cushion-width of distance between them, close enough for conspiracy, far enough for deniability.

Then Patrice and Joan together, always together, because they carpool from the same neighborhood and have bonded over a shared conviction that their ex-husbands, who do not know each other, are essentially the same man. Patrice is the one who needs a different fork. Not a clean fork — I've brought her clean forks, I've brought her forks still warm from the dishwasher, I've brought her forks I personally polished with a napkin while she watched — a *different* fork, by which she means one whose tines are spaced in some configuration I have never been able to identify but which she recognizes on sight with the unerring instinct of a woman who has transferred an enormous amount of emotional energy onto flatware. Joan is the one who cries. Between the salad and the entrée, never during dessert. The tears arrive with the punctuality of a bus schedule, and they depart the same way, and by the time the tiramisu lands she is laughing about something terrible her ex-husband said to his lawyer, and the collateral emotional damage of that laugh registers three tables deep.

Carol arrives fifth, always apologizing, always breathless, always carrying a handbag so large it requires its own logistical strategy. She is the group's historian: she remembers the date each marriage ended, the specific grounds stated in each filing, the name of every attorney involved, and she recites these facts with the reverent precision of a woman who has confused documentation with healing. She sits in the middle of the banquette, which is to say the deepest part of the booth, which is to say the seat from which escape is most difficult, which is the kind of choice that tells you more about a person than their therapist will learn in a year.

Nina is last, seven minutes late every time, because Nina was seven minutes late to her own wedding and sees no reason to alter the fundamental rhythm of a life simply because the wedding didn't work out. Nina is the quietest. She orders the house red and a side of bread and sometimes doesn't eat the bread but breaks it into smaller and smaller pieces until her plate looks like a topographic map of a flour-based country. She takes whatever seat remains, which is always the right edge of the banquette, nearest the wall, and she folds herself into it with the practiced compactness of a woman who has spent years making herself fit into spaces that were never designed for her.

This is the corner booth at Dolce Far Niente, Table 12, Saturday evenings, seven o'clock, and I have been serving it since the arrangement began, which is to say for three years and approximately one hundred and forty Saturdays, minus holidays, minus the Saturday Joan had that procedure she will not name, minus the two Saturdays the restaurant closed for plumbing, minus the single Saturday in February when a nor'easter shut down the city and Diane called to ask if we were still open and I said no and she said, "Well, that's disappointing," as though a blizzard were a personal failing on the part of the restaurant's management.

I should tell you about the restaurant. Dolce Far Niente means "the sweetness of doing nothing," which is a name I find privately hilarious for an establishment in which I have never once done nothing. The food is — and I say this with the loyalty of a man who depends on these kitchens for his rent — adequate. The pasta is fine. The veal is usually fine. The risotto is fine in the way that a painting of a sunset is fine: it references something beautiful without achieving it. The tiramisu, however, is genuinely good, and I have never once called it merely good to a customer, because tiramisu is the only item on this menu that does not require me to lie, and I protect that small honesty the way other men protect their reputations.

The six of them — Diane, Linda, Patrice, Joan, Carol, Nina — discovered each other three years ago when Linda's sister, who is Carol's coworker, who is Nina's neighbor, who is Joan's cousin by marriage (a marriage that, unlike Joan's, survived), mentioned at a Fourth of July barbecue that she knew "another one," and then another, and then another, and by the time the summer was over, six women whose marriages had all ended within the same twelve-month period had been introduced by a chain of tenuous social connections and had immediately, with the inevitable gravity of a shared wound, formed a group. They tried meeting at each other's houses. They tried a bar. They tried Carol's church basement, which lasted one session because Joan said, and I quote from a story they have told me enough times that I can reconstruct it verbatim, "I'm not going to process my divorce under fluorescent lighting, Carol, I'm just not."

They found Dolce Far Niente the way most people find this restaurant: by accident, on a night when their first choice was full. The hostess seated them at the corner booth because it was available and because it seats six, and they came back the following Saturday, and the Saturday after that, and within a month the booth was theirs in the way that a pew becomes a family's — not by contract but by the accumulated weight of repetition until displacement would feel like sacrilege.

I know all of this because waiters know everything. This is not a boast. It is a structural fact about the profession, like the fact that load-bearing walls cannot be removed without consequence or the fact that the corner booth has a draft. We stand three feet from the most intimate conversations of strangers' lives, holding plates, and because we are holding plates we become invisible, and because we are invisible we hear things that therapists charge two hundred dollars an hour to hear, and we hear them for free, and we carry them home in our bodies like silt.

They have a name for their group, these six women. They call themselves the Saturday Saints, which is a joke, because none of them are saints and at least two of them have, in my hearing, described acts of marital sabotage that would disqualify them from canonization in any functioning diocese. But the name stuck, the way names do when they are funny enough at the moment of invention and no one can be bothered to replace them later.

I am Marco. I have worked at Dolce Far Niente for eleven years. I am their waiter. I set their table. I bring their wine. I clear their plates. I know them the way a priest knows his parishioners, which is to say: from a fixed distance, in a fixed role, with a fluency that could be mistaken for intimacy but is actually something else entirely, something closer to surveillance conducted with affection.

Every Saturday, the hostess walks them to the booth. She pulls the table out so they can slide into the banquette, pushes it back, sets the small "Reserved" placard to the side. She does this efficiently, the way one does a task that has been performed enough times to become muscular rather than conscious. And then she returns to her post, and I begin.

## II.

You can read a marriage from a water glass. I don't mean this metaphorically. I mean it literally, precisely, the way a radiologist reads a chest X-ray, which is to say: there is a specific body of evidence, visible to trained eyes, and it tells you things the patient does not know.

Watch which one pours. In a functional marriage — a real one, the kind that looks unremarkable from the outside because all functional things look unremarkable from the outside; you do not notice a bridge that holds — one person pours water for the other without being asked. Not always the same person. It alternates. It is unconscious. It is, if you want to be precise about it, a somatic contract: *I will attend to a need you have not expressed because I have internalized your needs as data my body processes automatically.* That is love, or at least it is the hydraulic infrastructure of love, the groundwater that makes the visible garden possible.

Now watch a marriage in trouble. No one pours. Both glasses sit at identical levels, because each person has poured for themselves, which means each person has, at the level of the body, decided: *I am a single unit. My thirst is my own problem.* Or — and this is worse, this is the variant that makes me want to look away — one person pours for both, and the other does not notice. The pourer is still operating under the terms of the old contract. The non-noticer has already left. They are sitting at the same table in different countries.

I have a whole taxonomy. I have eleven years of taxonomy. I could teach a course.

Couples who order for each other: functional, usually, though there are subtypes. The man who orders for the woman because he knows what she wants — fine. The man who orders for the woman because he has decided what she will want — leave him, madam, leave him before the appetizer. Couples who face the door together: early relationship, still performing unity for an audience. Couples where one faces the door and the other faces the wall: one wants to see who's coming, the other has decided that this table is the whole world. This is usually good. It means the wall-facer has chosen immersion over vigilance. Unless the wall-facer is simply bored, in which case the body language will tell you — a bored wall-facer keeps their shoulders parallel to the table, a contented one angles them slightly toward their partner, two or three degrees, no more, like a satellite dish making a micro-adjustment toward a signal.

The divorcées carry their old marriages in their hands.

Linda still pours wine for the empty space to her right. She doesn't know she does this. I have watched her tilt the bottle toward a glass that isn't there, catch herself, redirect to her own glass, and not register the error. The muscle memory of her marriage is still operating her arm. Somewhere in the fascia of her right shoulder there is still a husband.

Diane cuts her steak into pieces no larger than a centimeter squared. Her ex-husband had a medical condition — something esophageal, she mentioned it once, loudly, with the casual specificity of a woman who has decided that her ex-husband's medical history is public property — and for fifteen years she cut her own food small in solidarity, or in habit, or in that strange marital osmosis where one person's body begins to observe another person's constraints. The marriage ended. The cutting didn't.

Carol leaves four inches of space at the table's edge nearest her right hand: room for an elbow that no longer exists. She arranges her handbag, her phone, her rolled napkin in a perimeter that accommodates a phantom. If you didn't know what you were looking at, you'd think she was just a woman who liked space. I know what I'm looking at.

Patrice holds her fork in her left hand, continental style, because her ex-husband was English, or had lived in England, or had a fantasy about England, and she adopted the affectation during their marriage and never released it. She doesn't hold it naturally. There is a visible tension in her wrist every time she lifts a bite, as though the fork is a small protest she is staging with her hand.

Joan does not leave room. Joan does not adjust her silverware. Joan sits in the center of her space with the deliberate expansiveness of a woman who is occupying every square inch her marriage denied her. But she cries between the salad and the entrée, and the crying is not expansive, it is compressed, silent, three or four tears on the left side only, and she wipes them with the base of her palm, not a napkin, which means the tears are private even when they are visible, which means the marriage is still private, which means it is still alive in the place where private things live.

Nina breaks bread.

I see all of this. I see every couple in this restaurant every night with the same clarity, and I could tell you within two courses whether the couple at Table 8 will be back in six months or whether the man at Table 3 is going to say something tonight that his wife will cite in a filing. I see the woman at Table 5 who laughs too eagerly at her husband's jokes — that's fear. I see the couple at Table 14 who argue about the check with genuine hostility dressed as playfulness — they have six months, maybe eight. I see the old couple at Table 2 who eat in complete silence and hold hands between courses, and that silence is not emptiness, it is the silence of two people who have said everything and discovered that what remains after everything is said is not nothing but something better than language, something like rest.

I have been doing this for eleven years. Eleven years of standing three feet from love in all its stages, holding a plate, and I have never been wrong. I do not say this with pride. I say it the way a command might report a body count: with accuracy and no feeling I'm prepared to name.

On this particular Saturday — all six still present, this is still early, this is still the full liturgy — Patrice says to Joan, across the salad plates, "God, she always gives us the good booth," meaning the corner booth, meaning Table 12, and she says "she" with a tilt of her head toward the front of the restaurant.

I correct this internally. The corner booth is not a good booth. It has a draft from the HVAC vent that building management has been promising to repair for three years and will promise to repair for three more. The left side of the banquette has a spring that has begun its slow journey through the vinyl. The light is slightly too dim because one of the three pendant lamps above it has been set on a different dimmer circuit since a renovation in 2019, and no electrician has been called because the owner considers ambient inconsistency to be "atmosphere." Elena seats them here because it is in my section.

I refill Diane's water glass.

I have never asked Elena why she assigns them to my section. It is possible she assigns them to my section because I am good with groups. It is possible she assigns them to my section because my section includes the only six-top booth. These are reasonable explanations. They are the explanations I have provided to the part of my mind that, occasionally, typically at around two in the morning on nights I cannot sleep, asks the question.

The Montepulciano needs replacing. I go to the bar. I bring it back. I pour.
