# Miss Crane's Last Stand

## I.

The new ones always arrive on Mondays. I don't know if the district plans it that way or if there's some instinct in the species — like how certain birds will only build nests facing east — but I have now seen five teachers arrive at the Elk Fork K-8 Consolidated School and every single one has done it on a Monday, and every single one has come through the door carrying something they believed would save them.

Mr. Paulsen had a guitar. Mrs. Veck had a cardboard box of hand puppets and a copy of something called *The Growth Mindset Workbook* that she referenced so often it became the name of a card game the sixth graders invented, the rules of which I will not describe here. Mr. Faraday had nothing visible, but he had a philosophy, which he delivered on his first morning as a kind of inaugural address: "I'm not here to be your friend, and I'm not here to be your enemy. I'm here to be your *guide*." He lasted eleven days. Then there was Ms. Ridley, who arrived with an actual suitcase because she'd driven straight from the airport, and who cried on the fourth day but stayed five weeks, which was long enough that some of the younger kids learned her first name — Julie — and said it sometimes after she left, the way you keep reaching for a tooth that's been pulled.

Miss Adelaide Crane arrived on a Monday in early October with a laminated poster.

It was not a small poster. It was the large format, the kind you can see from the back of a classroom, printed in primary colors with a sans-serif font that radiated the cheerful authority of an airport safety card. RAISE YOUR HAND. RESPECT OTHERS' SPACE. NO FOOD DURING LESSONS. She affixed it to the wall above where the teacher's desk used to be — Lyle Pettit broke it during what historians now call the Furniture Period — using mounting putty she had brought from a teaching-supply store in Missoula, which is a four-hour drive from here on good roads and there are no good roads.

I watched her smooth the poster's edges with the flat of her palm, pressing each corner twice, and I thought about a nature documentary I'd seen at my cousin's house in Butte where a bird spent forty minutes arranging colored pebbles outside its nest to attract a mate that was never shown onscreen, and the narrator said, "The bowerbird continues its display, unaware that the female has already moved to an adjacent territory," and my cousin laughed, and I did not laugh, and I have thought about that bird more often than I can justify.

Miss Crane wore shoes with low heels — the kind that are appropriate for the linoleum hallways of a consolidated elementary school in a town with sidewalks, not the kind that are appropriate for the gumbo mud of Elk Fork in October, which has the consistency and moral character of wet cement. She would learn this. They all learned the mud. The mud was the first lesson Elk Fork taught and it was usually the kindest.

She scanned the room the way a person does when they're looking for something they were promised. I knew what she was looking for: rows. Desks in rows, facing front, oriented toward a teacher, implying an order of operations in which knowledge flows from a single adult source to multiple juvenile receivers, the basic hydrodynamic model of American education. She had been told there would be desks. There were desks. Twenty-three of them. But they had been rearranged into a loose parliamentary semicircle sometime in the fall of 2021 — during the interregnum between Mrs. Veck and Mr. Faraday — by the eighth graders of that era, who needed a format conducive to structured debate regarding gymnasium access rights, and the desks had not moved since, except for the four that had been requisitioned for other purposes I'll get to.

She looked at the semicircle. She looked at her poster. She looked at us — the fourteen kids who had shown up, which was about average, out of an enrollment of twenty-six, the rest being either seasonally absent, functionally truant, or, in the case of the Otterbein twins, on what their mother had described to the district as an "extended educational travel experience" that everyone knew meant they were living in their uncle's trailer in Roundup.

"Good morning," she said. "I'm Miss Crane."

Nobody said anything. This was not hostility. It was simply that we had learned, collectively, that reciprocal introductions implied a commitment to remember someone's name, and names were expensive to remember when the person attached to them was going to leave.

I should mention that the cafeteria — which is a generous word for the folding table and the steam trays in the back of the room near the utility closet — had recently switched canned-peach suppliers, and the new ones, from a company whose label featured a peach so aggressively orange it looked irradiated, had a mealy, dispiriting texture that I can only compare to eating a wet apology. The old peaches, from Pacific Gold, were firm and arrived in a syrup that was merely sweet. The new peaches, from a brand called Harvest Sun, arrived in a syrup that tasted sweet in the way that a person smiles when they are about to tell you something you don't want to hear. This is not irrelevant. This is, in fact, the kind of thing that matters in a place like Elk Fork, where the difference between a good day and a bad one is sometimes exactly the width of a peach.

Miss Crane smiled at us. It was a real smile. That was the problem.

## II.

What Miss Crane walked into — and what she could not have understood from the outside, because it is the kind of thing that can only be understood from inside, the way you can't know the layout of a cave until you're already deep enough that the entrance is just a memory of light — was a political system of considerable sophistication and almost no adult supervision.

I'll describe it plainly, because I believe in plainness, and because the alternative is to describe it the way Miss Crane eventually did in a phone call I was not supposed to overhear, which was: "It's *Lord of the Flies* but with snack cakes and a rudimentary tax code."

The eighth graders — which was me and Starla Pfeiffer and Martin Kye, three of us, technically a plurality — controlled the gymnasium. This was not a recent development. Gymnasium sovereignty had been established during the Paulsen administration through a series of negotiations I will not detail except to say that they involved the strategic withholding of janitorial cooperation, and that the gymnasium was the most valuable real estate in the building because it was the only space large enough for basketball, floor hockey, and the kinds of full-body negotiations that sometimes replaced verbal ones. Martin Kye was our military wing, though I say that loosely — Martin was six feet tall and had the calm, slow-moving authority of a large animal that has never been given a reason to hurry. Starla was our diplomat. I was — I don't know what I was. The person who kept track. The person who remembered things. The historian, maybe, of a country no one had asked me to chronicle.

The seventh graders — five of them, led by a kid named Colton Bray who had the charisma of a youth pastor and the strategic instincts of a parking-lot bookie — operated as a kind of affiliated but autonomous faction. They had nominal gymnasium access through a power-sharing agreement that Colton had brokered the previous spring, but their real base of power was informational: Colton knew things. Who was mad at whom. Which parents were threatening to pull their kids. Where Mr. Faraday had hidden the confiscated candy (ceiling tile, third from the window, south wall). Colton's intelligence network ran on the simple principle that younger kids will tell you anything if you let them sit in the good chair during movie time. The good chair was a rolling office chair with one broken wheel that we'd found in the supply closet, and it was, per capita, the most contested piece of furniture in the building.

The fifth and sixth graders — seven of them, when they all showed up, which they almost never did — ran a barter economy with a complexity that would have impressed a graduate student in anthropology and alarmed a graduate student in child psychology. The currency was snack-based: fruit snacks from the cafeteria supply were the standard unit, but the exchange rate fluctuated depending on variety and availability. Strawberry fruit snacks were stable. Tropical fruit snacks were volatile. The mixed berry, which nobody liked but which appeared with suspicious frequency, functioned as a kind of junk bond — useful for bulk transactions but worthless in retail. There was a futures market. I am not exaggerating. Tyler Meek, a sixth grader with the eyes of an actuary and the social skills of a fencepost, had invented a system by which you could trade future snack allocations for present goods, and the fifth graders enforced contracts through a mechanism that was technically just "telling everyone you're a cheater" but which functioned, in practice, with the same devastating efficiency as a credit rating.

The fourth graders were a faction of two: Angie Hebert and Angie Hebert's friend, who was a boy named Sam, but I'm going to call them both Angie because Sam did whatever Angie said with the unquestioning devotion of a vice president in a two-person government, which is what they were. Angie was nine years old and had the diplomatic bearing of a person four times her age. She had maintained a strict policy of non-alignment since the Veck administration, trading favors with all factions but committing to none, and her neutrality was so reliable that she had become the default arbitrator of minor disputes — a kind of Switzerland with braids and a puffy coat she wore indoors because the heating system in the schoolhouse was, as it had been for as long as I could remember, a matter of personal faith and occasional mechanical coincidence.

The younger kids — K through 3, ranging in age from five to eight, numbering about six on any given day — were, I'll be honest, a client state. They belonged to whichever faction had fed them most recently. Their loyalty shifted with the nutritional tides. They were not stupid. Harley Burch, the kindergartner, had a sophisticated understanding of leverage: she knew that her crying could summon adult attention from the aide who came twice a week, and she deployed it strategically, though never cynically — she was five, and her tears were real, even when they were tactical, the way rain is real even when it ruins your day.

This was the nation Miss Crane walked into. This was the context in which she stood at the front of the room — in front of her laminated poster, beside the ghost of the broken teacher's desk — and attempted a read-aloud.

She had chosen *Charlotte's Web*. It was a fine choice. It was the choice all of them made, or something like it — a book about kindness and death and the quiet nobility of small creatures, which, if you think about it, is exactly the kind of book an adult brings into a room when they want children to feel something without being told what to feel. She opened it. She read the first line. She read the second line.

Nobody stopped her. That would have required engagement, and engagement was a resource we allocated carefully, like heating fuel or goodwill. What happened instead was that the room continued. Martin tossed a balled-up piece of paper at Starla. The sixth graders conducted a quiet transaction involving three tropical fruit snacks and what appeared to be a mechanical pencil of unusual quality. Colton Bray leaned back in his chair and watched Miss Crane with the expression of a person timing something. The younger kids drifted. Angie Hebert read her own book — a chapter book about horses, which she read with the focus of a scholar and the speed that suggested she'd read it before, many times, and was not reading it for the plot but for the steadiness of returning to a known thing.

Miss Crane read for eleven minutes. I know because I was watching the clock, which was one of the old analog ones with a second hand that stuttered, and I watched it the way I imagine sailors watch the horizon — not because there was anything to see, but because looking at it was better than looking at what was actually happening, which was a woman reading a beautiful book to a room full of children who had learned that listening to adults was an investment with no guaranteed return.

She finished the first chapter. She closed the book. She looked at us.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we'll read chapter two."

She said it with a steadiness that I recognized. It was not the steadiness of confidence. It was the steadiness of a person standing on a surface they have just realized is not solid, and who has decided that if they simply do not look down, the physics might cooperate.

That night, or maybe the next morning — time moves differently in a story than it does in life, and I'm telling this in the order I remember it, which is not always the order it happened — I thought about the poster. RAISE YOUR HAND. RESPECT OTHERS' SPACE. NO FOOD DURING LESSONS. It was still on the wall. Nobody had torn it down. Nobody had drawn on it. Nobody had bothered. It hung there with the quiet irrelevance of a map to a country that no longer exists, and Miss Crane walked past it every morning as if it were still descriptive, as if the territory it referred to were the same territory she was standing in, and I did not know if this made her brave or something else for which I did not yet have a word.

## III.

The Spelling Bee Court was not in any lesson plan. I know this because I saw her lesson plans — she kept them in a green binder on the shelf where the encyclopedias used to be, and I am a person who reads whatever is in front of me, including binders, including ingredient lists, including the small print on the canned peach labels, which is how I know that Harvest Sun peaches are a product of China and packed in a facility that also processes tree nuts, which is a phrase that has always struck me as mildly threatening, like a polite warning from the universe.

Her lesson plans were organized by week, tabbed by subject, cross-referenced with state standards. They were beautiful the way a building is beautiful before anyone moves in. They assumed conditions that did not exist — consistent attendance, functional groupings by grade level, a shared understanding between teacher and student that the teacher was, in fact, in charge. By the end of her second week, the green binder had not been opened in three days. I noticed. I notice things like that. I notice things like that instead of other things.

What happened was the storage-closet dispute.

The storage closet was a narrow room between the main classroom and the gymnasium, and it contained, among other things, the floor-hockey sticks, a box of cones, four rubber balls of varying inflation, the holiday decorations that nobody had put up since Mrs. Veck left, and a sleeping bag that belonged to no one and everyone and had been in there so long it had become a kind of archaeological fact. Ownership of the closet had been contested since the Gymnasium Sovereignty Compact of 2021, which established eighth-grade control of the gym but was ambiguous — deliberately, I now believe — about the closet. Colton Bray's seventh graders claimed it fell within their informational jurisdiction, on the grounds that they had been the ones to discover and catalog its contents. The sixth graders claimed it by right of proximity, their desks being closest to the door. The dispute had been dormant for months — a frozen conflict, like one of those border disagreements between countries that everyone agrees not to talk about until someone builds a road.

Someone built a road. Specifically, Tyler Meek attempted to store a cache of fruit snacks in the closet as part of a speculative venture involving the upcoming parent-teacher conference, during which he anticipated a supply disruption. Colton's people intercepted the cache. Words were exchanged. A rubber ball was thrown, not at anyone, but in the general direction of a sentiment.

Miss Crane, standing in the middle of this, holding a chapter book she had not yet given up on, said: "Stop."

They stopped. Not because she had authority, but because she had volume, and volume was novel. The previous teachers had all gone quiet toward the end. Miss Crane was loud in a way that suggested she had not yet learned quietness, and this was interesting enough to pause for.

"If this is a dispute," she said, "we'll settle it properly."

She went to the shelf. She did not open the green binder. She opened the dictionary — the big one, the Webster's Third, which had been in the schoolhouse since before I was born and which had a cracked spine and a water stain on the cover that looked, depending on your mood, like either a map of Italy or a dog sitting down.

"I'll give each side a word," she said. "Spell it correctly, you get a point. Best of five. Winner gets the closet for the week."
