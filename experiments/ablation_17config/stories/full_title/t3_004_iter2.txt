# The Commissar's Confession

---

## I.

From the registry of the Tribunal Révolutionnaire, supplementary docket, 14 Thermidor, Year II:

*Citizen Inspector Étienne Maulnier, attached to the Committee of General Security by order of the Convention, supervised the transfer and lawful execution of seventeen enemies of the Republic on the 9th of Messidor, Year II, at the Place de la Révolution, acting under extraordinary warrant No. 1174 of the Committee of Public Safety. The condemned, convicted of conspiracy against the sovereignty of the nation, seditious correspondence, and participation in a federalist network operating through the sections of the Faubourg Saint-Marcel, were delivered without incident. Citizen Inspector Maulnier's conduct was noted as exemplary in its efficiency. No irregularities were recorded.*

*Citizen Maulnier was himself arrested on the 4th of Thermidor, Year II, on charges of moderatism, communication with suspected enemies of the people, and failure to disclose intelligence pertaining to ongoing conspiracies against the Republic. He was condemned on the 8th of Thermidor. No record of execution survives. It is possible the sentence was carried out on the 9th, the day preceding the fall of Robespierre, though the relevant folio of the executioner's ledger is missing or was destroyed in the confusion that followed. No further administrative trace of Citizen Maulnier has been found in the departmental or municipal records. His personal effects, if any were inventoried, do not appear in the Conciergerie's receipts for that period.*

*Note appended by archivist, Frimaire, Year IV: Among papers recovered from the former offices of the Committee of General Security, Section Three, a manuscript of some forty pages was found in a hand tentatively identified as Maulnier's, though no formal comparison of scripts has been undertaken. The document is personal in nature. It does not constitute an official report. It is preserved here as a curiosity. Its reliability as testimony is doubtful, the author being at the time of writing evidently under considerable mental strain, and the contents being largely incompatible with the record of his public conduct. What follows is a transcription.*

---

## II.

The light is the color of a dirty sheet and it is already warm, the warmth that will by noon taste of iron and the river and the particular sweetness that has been in the air since June which I have decided is not sweetness at all but the smell of the tanneries working overtime because even the tanners now work for the Republic, everything works for the Republic, the leather for boots for the armies of the Republic, and I am walking on the Rue de la Harpe and my boots are good boots, I requisitioned them from a man whose name I recorded in a ledger that no longer exists because I burned it, and the sound—

—there. The wheels.

A tumbrel turning onto the Quai from the direction of the Châtelet. The iron rims chewing at the cobblestones. Ungreased. They never grease them. There is no reason to grease them; the carts go one direction and they come back empty and they go one direction again and no one has filed a requisition for axle grease because who would file it, to what office, under what heading, and so the wheels shriek, a rhythmic shriek, not like a voice, not like anything human, more like the sound a gate makes when it has swollen in its frame and is forced. That sound. I have heard it four hundred times. I have heard it so many times it became part of the texture of the city, the way the bells used to be before they melted the bells, and this morning—

This morning the sound has—I am noticing the interval. The space between each shriek as the wheel completes its revolution. I am counting. Three seconds. Then the stones are smoother and the interval stretches to four. Then a rut, and two shrieks close together like a stammer. I am listening to this the way I once listened to the cadence of a man's breathing through a wall in the Rue Mouffetard to determine whether he was alone. I am reading the wheels the way I read the breathing: is there information here? What does the rhythm tell me? And the answer is nothing. The answer is that a cart is going where carts go and I am walking where I am going and the distance between us is—

A woman on the second floor of a house with blue shutters is closing them. It is not yet six. The shutters were open. She saw me. She is closing them. I see the movement of her arm, the left arm, pulling the right shutter inward, and then her body turns and I see the shape of her through the gap before the second shutter closes: she is not young, she is wearing something gray, she moves stiffly, the right hip perhaps, or perhaps she is simply moving with the care of someone who does not want to be heard. The shutter clicks. The latch. A small decisive sound.

Assessment: she recognized the uniform. Or she recognized me specifically. Or she recognized nothing and is closing her shutters because a draft, because the light, because she is going back to bed, because—

I cannot complete the assessment. The machinery is running but the gears are not catching. I observe the movement, I classify the movement, I reach for the inference and my hand closes on air. She is closing her shutters. A woman is closing her shutters in a city where there are reasons to close shutters and what this tells me is—

A dog near the gutter is eating something. I do not look at what it is eating.

I am afraid. I will say it in plain language because I have spent three years not saying things in plain language and this document, if it is anything, will be the one place where I say what is actual. I am afraid. Not of dying, or not primarily, though I am also afraid of dying. I am afraid of the next four hours. I am afraid of what I will do in the next four hours, which is the thing I have been assigned to do, which I have done before in other forms, which is to walk to the Luxembourg prison and collect seventeen people whose names are on a list in my coat and walk them through the streets of this city to the Place de la Révolution and watch them die. I have supervised transfers before. Not executions. Transfers. I have moved prisoners from one facility to another. I have signed documents. I have verified identities. But today the tribunal has specified that the supervising inspector will accompany the condemned to the place of execution and confirm the sentence is carried out, and the supervising inspector is me, and I said yes, or rather I did not say no, which in the Republic is the same as yes. Silence is ratification. I learned that in my first week.

The tumbrel has passed. The sound fades down the Quai, thinning, and now it is gone and the street is just a street and the sky is getting lighter and my boots are good boots and I am three hundred meters from the prison.

I know what justice actually means. Someone taught me.

I am going to the prison.

---

## III.

The courtyard of the Luxembourg. Bare earth, packed hard by feet. The fountain has not worked since March. A guardsman I recognize—Pelet, twenty-two, formerly a clerk in a notary's office in Auxerre, recruited into the National Guard in Ventôse and assigned here because he can read—Pelet opens the gate and nods. He does not salute. The Committee's inspectors do not wear military rank. We exist in an administrative crevice. We are civilian and we carry warrants and our authority derives from paper, from signatures, from the seals of committees that derive their authority from the Convention that derives its authority from the sovereign people who are at this moment largely asleep or largely afraid.

They are in the corridor. Seventeen. I count them because I must verify the count against the list. I count them again because I cannot believe the number even though I already knew the number. Seventeen people standing and sitting and one leaning against the wall in a stone corridor lit by two lanterns and a window that admits the early light at an angle that falls across the floor like a blade.

I take out the list.

I have read lists. I have compiled lists. I have copied lists from other lists and transmitted lists through channels that no longer exist to people who are dead or have repudiated every principle the lists were meant to serve. I know what a list is. A list is the Republic's most fundamental literary form. The Republic thinks in lists: lists of suspects, lists of émigrés, lists of maximum prices, lists of approved virtues, lists of approved words. A list is a sentence in which every noun has been convicted and the verb is always the same verb.

I read the first name.

"Citizen Jean-Baptiste Varenne."

He looks up. Printer. Forty-one years old. Operated the press on the Rue du Pot-de-Fer from which seventeen pamphlets in the Girondin style were produced between September and January. I know this because I commissioned seven of them. I wrote two. He did not know I wrote them. He thought they came from Louvet's circle. His hands are ink-stained even now. The ink is in the grain of his skin, permanent, the way certain truths are in certain people, so deep in the material that they cannot be washed out, only destroyed with the material itself. He looks at me. His face: composed. Unrecognizable as the face of the man who laughed in November when the press jammed and we—when the press jammed.

"Citizen André Sorel."

Does not look up immediately. Then does. A teacher. Mathematics, at a school in the Faubourg Saint-Marcel that no longer exists. He taught the children of artisans. He was recruited by Catherine—

I will come to Catherine.

Sorel looks at me and his expression is—

It is—

I have spent three years reading faces. I have read faces across café tables and through windows and in mirrors positioned to show me a face without the face knowing it was being shown. I have calibrated my reading of faces against subsequent actions to test accuracy: this man's expression means he will inform if pressed, that man's expression means he is lying but not about the thing I think he is lying about. I have a taxonomy of expressions. I have subcategories. I have made correct assessments under conditions that would—

Sorel's face. I cannot read it. He is looking at me and what I see is either recognition or absence of recognition and I cannot determine which because both would involve exactly the same configuration of features: the eyes level, the mouth closed, the brow neither raised nor drawn. It is the face of a man who is either concealing everything or experiencing nothing and these are structurally identical from the outside and I have no additional data points because the room is a stone corridor and the context is a death list and every face here—

"Citizen Philippe Dumont."

"Citizen Marguerite Tessier."

"Citizen Louis Bonhomme."

Each name. Each face. Bonhomme—I did not know him well, he was peripheral, a courier, but I knew the route he ran and the route he ran passed through the house on the Rue des Noyers where the Committee of Six used to meet and the Committee of Six has been defunct since Germinal when three of its members were arrested and two fled and one—

"Citizen Hélène Marest."

She was the one who hid the type when the Sections searched Varenne's shop. She told the guards it was her dead husband's dental equipment. They believed her because she was fifty and respectable and because the Revolution, for all its grand rhetoric about the equality of suspicion, still hesitates for a quarter-second before suspecting a woman of a certain age and respectability, and in that quarter-second Hélène Marest saved eleven pounds of movable type and five unfinished pamphlets and the names of everyone who had contributed to them, which is to say she saved what I am now reading aloud as a death list.

"Citizen—"

I stop.

The seventh name.

I knew the seventh name would be on the list. I have known since yesterday. I verified it with the clerk at the tribunal before I verified it with the list in my pocket and I verified it with the list in my pocket before I verified it with the clerk and I do not remember which verification came first because the knowledge existed before either verification the way certain kinds of dread exist before their objects, free-floating, already structured, already complete, merely awaiting a surface on which to land.

The seventh name.

I say it.

"Citizen Catherine Vauclair."

She does not look up.

This is the thing. This is the thing I will spend whatever time remains to me attempting to describe and I already know I will fail because language is the Republic's instrument and I am trying to use the Republic's instrument against the Republic and this is like trying to pry open a lock with the very bolt that secures it.

She does not look up. Around her, faces turn to me, or turn away from me, or hold still. But Catherine's face remains angled slightly downward and to the left, as if she is examining something on the floor, or as if she is listening to something I cannot hear, or as if she has made a decision about what to do with her eyes in this moment and the decision is: not him.

She knows.

She knows what I was. She knows what I did. She knows the names I passed and the routes I mapped and the information I transmitted and the people I tried, in my cramped and vicious and insufficient way, to protect. She knows I was the Republic's man who was not the Republic's man. She knows I served the Committee and served the network and told myself these two services were compatible and believed it, for three years I believed it. She knows this.

And she will not look at me.

I have been accused. I have been denounced in section meetings by men who suspected less than a tenth of what is true. I have sat through interrogations in which my questioners circled the borders of what I had done without quite mapping the interior. I have felt the particular terror of the almost-discovered, the man whose cover is thinning, whose fabrications are beginning to contradict one another. None of it—none of it was—

She will not look at me and her refusal is not fear and it is not contempt and it is not strategy. There is no tactical content in her averted gaze. I could read tactics. I could read contempt. I could process an accusation, mount a defense, calculate the implications. Her refusal to look at me is not an act within any system I know how to operate in. It is—

I read the remaining names. I read them correctly. I do not stumble. My voice is level and my pronunciation is clear because I am a professional and the machinery is running and the gears are catching on nothing but they are catching and the sound my voice makes is the sound of a competent man performing a clerical function and in the entire corridor there is not a single person who could hear my voice and conclude that anything is wrong.

I finish the list. Seventeen names. Verified against seventeen faces. The count is correct. I sign the transfer order. The ink is still drying on my signature as the guards begin to move the condemned toward the courtyard.

Catherine walks past me. She does not look up.

---

## IV.

I am going to tell what she taught me. I am going to tell it inside the thing that is happening, because the thing that is happening will not pause while I recover the past, and the past will not stay in its place while the present processes these seventeen bodies through the gate and into the street and toward—

The street. We are in the street
