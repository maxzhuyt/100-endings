# The Hateful Immortals

### A Documentary Account of the Longest Marriage in Recorded History

*Assembled and narrated by Dr. Amelia Thornton, Department of Anomalous Historical Studies, Columbia University. Originally produced for the Public Broadcasting Service, 2024. Transcript adapted for publication with additional footnotes and archival materials.*

---

## I. The Vow That Means What It Says

The daguerreotype is small enough to hold in one hand.

It was purchased at an estate sale in Marblehead, Massachusetts, in 1987, by a dealer who catalogued it as *Unidentified couple, Boston, circa 1847, sixth plate, fair condition.* For fifteen years it sat in a cardboard box in a climate-controlled storage unit in Somerville alongside approximately nine hundred other orphaned faces from the antebellum period. In 2002, a graduate student named Claire Nakamura, working on a dissertation about Chinese-American portraiture in the daguerreian era, recognized the man in the image from a shipping manifest she had cross-referenced that morning. She pulled the plate from its box with cotton gloves. She tilted it to catch the light—daguerreotypes must be held at precisely the right angle, or the image inverts into a ghostly negative of itself, a fact that will feel like metaphor before this account is finished—and she saw them.

They are young. That is the first thing. They are young in a way that stops your breath, because you know, as they did not yet know, that they will look exactly like this in 2024. He is tall, thin-faced, sharp-boned, with the kind of stillness that reads as composure in a photograph and might, in person, read as something more unsettling—the absolute physical calm of a man who has already survived things that should have killed him and has begun, at some subterranean level, to suspect that nothing will. She is not beautiful in the way the word was deployed in 1847. She is *present*. Even in a medium that required subjects to hold motionless for fifteen to thirty seconds, she appears to be vibrating with attention, as if she is memorizing the camera, the studio, the smell of the mercury vapor, the exact pressure of his hand on her arm—which, of course, she was. She was memorizing all of it. She is still, right now, at this moment, remembering the click and the flash and the faintly chemical smell of the plates, and his hand, and the way the photographer said *steady now,* and the sound of a horse outside on Washington Street, and her own heartbeat, and his.

Their names, on their wedding day: Margaret Alice Greenbriar and James Wei Chen.

Their names since then: too many to catalog here, though Appendix C of my full monograph attempts it. The partial list includes Margaret and James Aldworth (London, 1871–1889), Marguerite and Jacques Chenier (Paris, 1893–1912), Maggie and Jim Clarke (Chicago, 1920–1941), Mei-Ling and James Chang (Taipei, 1965–1978), and—in what I believe was an act of either exhaustion or comedy—Margaret and James Smith (Tucson, 2003–2019). They have been, as of this writing, married for one hundred and seventy-seven years. They have never divorced. They have never, despite several determined attempts, managed to stay separated for longer than eleven years. They are, by any reasonable metric, the longest-married couple in the recorded history of the human species.

They also cannot stand each other.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only tool I have, and because the Chens—I will use their original surname throughout—have been the subject of so much sentimental distortion since their existence became a matter of public record in 2019 that I feel a professional obligation to correct the narrative. They are not a love story. They are not a hate story. They are a *marriage*, which is to say they are both of those things simultaneously, at full volume, all the time, with no prospect of resolution, and they have been performing this particular duet for nearly two centuries while the rest of us have had the mercy of growing old and forgetting and dying. The Chens do not have that mercy. James Wei Chen is, as far as any medical or scientific authority has been able to determine, physically indestructible. Margaret Greenbriar Chen possesses what the clinical literature now calls "absolute eidetic perception"—total, permanent, sensory-complete recall of every moment she has ever experienced, with no degradation, no compression, and no off switch.

Till death do you part, the priest said to them in a small parish church on Hanover Street in Boston on October 11, 1847.

They said they did.

Here is what we know about the courtship, drawn from the seventeen surviving letters held by the Beinecke Rare Book & Manuscript Library at Yale, donated anonymously in 2020 from a source I have confirmed, through handwriting analysis, to be Margaret Chen herself.[^1]

[^1]: The donation was accompanied by a typed note on Holiday Inn stationery that read: *He'll say I'm being dramatic. I am being dramatic. He is the reason I am dramatic. The letters are real. — M.*

The letters are real. They are also—and I say this as a scholar who has spent four years living inside this archive—almost unbearable to read, because they are so *naked*. They have the quality of love letters written by people who have not yet learned to be embarrassed by sincerity, which is to say they have the quality of love letters written by people who are very young and do not yet know that the thing they are building will outlast mountains.

I will not quote them at length. The Beinecke volume is available to qualified researchers. But three passages are essential to any understanding of what follows.

The first is from James to Margaret, dated July 1847, three months before the wedding:

> *You told me last night about the thing you can do. I know you expect me to be frightened or to think you strange or perhaps even wicked. I find that I am none of these things. I find only that I am jealous—jealous of your eyes, which have seen me, and which will keep what they have seen forever, when I would give a great deal to be certain that any moment with you might last. You asked me if it troubled me that you would remember everything. Margaret, I have walked through a building as it came down on top of me and stood up without a mark. The world treats me as if I am not entirely here. You are the first person who makes me feel that I am solid. Remember everything. I want to be remembered by someone who will never have to wonder if the memory is true.*

The second is from Margaret to James, undated but placed by internal evidence in August 1847:

> *You ask what I remember most clearly from Tuesday. You expect me to say the sunset or the walk or the remarkably poor chowder. I remember those too, and I will remember them when the sun that set that evening has burned itself to nothing and the harbor has gone dry. But what I remember most clearly is this: you said, "You don't have to perform for me." You said it while I was in the middle of reciting the entire front page of the Transcript from memory because I thought it would impress you, and you touched my hand and said, "You don't have to perform for me, Margaret, I am already here," and it was the first time in my life that I understood that someone might want the part of me that is not extraordinary. I will not tell you it was the moment I fell in love with you, because I suspect I fell in love with you somewhat earlier, possibly when you mispronounced "Beethoven" with such confidence that I did not have the heart to correct you (I will correct you eventually; I correct everyone eventually; this is among my less attractive qualities and you should be informed of it before you are legally bound). But it was the moment I understood that being loved by you might be *restful*, and rest is something I have never known, because my mind does not stop, James. It does not stop. And you made it feel, for a moment, like something other than a punishment.*

The third passage—

I need to pause here and tell you what I have not yet told you, which is that I have met them. I have interviewed them. I have sat in a rented conference room in a Holiday Inn in Flagstaff, Arizona, with two people who look roughly thirty years old and who have been married since James K. Polk was president, and I have watched them argue about whether the chowder in the letter was "remarkably poor" or merely "mediocre," and I have watched him say "it was fine chowder, Margaret, you are rewriting history," and I have watched her say "I am *incapable* of rewriting history, that is literally the point of me, and it was *poor*, James, the fish was stringy and the broth was thin, and you said so yourself at the time, August 14, 1847, approximately 7:40 in the evening, you said 'this chowder is a crime against the ocean,' your *exact words*—" and I have watched him put his face in his hands and I have watched her not stop.

They are *exactly* like this. They have been exactly like this, I believe, since approximately 1851.

But they were not always like this. The letters prove it. And the third passage—the one I hesitated over, the one I keep returning to in my own notes like a tongue to a broken tooth—is the one that proves it most completely, because it is the one where Margaret, for the only time in the surviving correspondence, sounds *afraid*.

It is from Margaret to James, dated October 9, 1847—two days before the wedding:

> *I must tell you something I have not told you, and it is this: I am afraid that my remembering will be the thing that ruins us. Not today. Today I remember only that you are good and that you see me and that your hand is warm and that you mispronounce "Beethoven." But I am twenty years old, James, and I have already accumulated twenty years of every unkindness, every slight, every disappointment that any person has ever inflicted upon me, and I carry them all at the same fidelity as the first moment I received them, and I cannot put them down. They do not get lighter. Imagine twenty years of that. Now imagine forever. I am going to marry you because I love you with a completeness that frightens me more than my memory does. But I need you to understand that you are marrying a woman who will remember every wonderful thing you ever do and every terrible thing in equal and permanent measure, and I cannot promise you which catalog will grow faster, and I am sorry. I am sorry. I am so sorry, and I love you, and I will see you Thursday.*

He married her on Thursday.

The parish record at St. Stephen's—since demolished, rebuilt, demolished again; I found it on microfilm at the Archdiocese of Boston—lists the officiant as Father Thomas Grealish. The witnesses were a Miss Honoria Vane and a Mr. David Chen, James's father, who would die of tuberculosis eight months later and about whom James has, in every interview I have conducted, declined to speak. The ceremony was Catholic, brief, and—according to a later account Margaret gave to a journalist in 1987 under the name Mei-Ling Chang, in what she told me she considered "a moment of weakness brought on by too much Riesling and a reporter with a kind face"—unremarkable in every way except one.

"The priest said 'till death do you part,'" she told the reporter, "and James squeezed my hand. He squeezed my hand and he smiled at me, and I remember—I *remember*, I mean really remember, I mean I can see his face right now as clearly as I see yours—he smiled as if death were a certainty and not a question. And I smiled back. And we said 'I do.' And I heard a pigeon on the windowsill shift its weight from one foot to the other, and I heard James's father cough into his handkerchief, and I heard the candle nearest the altar gutter in a draft, and I filed it all away the way I file everything away, which is to say permanently and without my consent, and at the time I thought: *I will remember this as the happiest moment of my life.*"

She paused.

"I was right," she said. "I do."

## II. The First Wound That Will Never Heal

There is a journal entry dated March 7, 1852.

The journal itself is unremarkable—a clothbound octavo notebook purchased, Margaret later told me, from a stationer on Court Street whose name she still remembers (a Mr. Aldridge; he had a limp and a ginger cat named Nelson and he routinely overcharged for ink). She began keeping journals in 1849, a practice she maintained, with occasional interruptions, for the next 175 years. The journals now fill forty-two archival boxes at the Beinecke. I have read them all. The entry for March 7, 1852, is the one that matters.

I will reproduce it in full:

> *He said it at approximately ten minutes past eight o'clock in the evening. We had been arguing about the household accounts. The specifics: I had purchased linen at a cost he considered extravagant. He did not say "extravagant." He said "reckless," and then he said "unnecessary," and then—because we had by that point been shouting for perhaps ten minutes, which is long enough for an ordinary argument and nowhere near long enough for one of ours—he said the thing I am writing down now, not because I need to write it down to remember it (I could not forget it if I excised the relevant portion of my brain, and I have, in low moments, considered this) but because I want him to see it in written form so that he cannot later pretend it was less than what it was.*
>
> *He said: "For God's sake, Margaret, must you keep *score*? Must everything be a *ledger* with you? I am married to an accountant of grievances and I am bankrupt and I am *tired.*"*
>
> *He meant it to wound, and it did wound, and then he did what he always does when he has been cruel: he softened. His face changed. I watched it change. I can see it changing now. The anger drained out and was replaced by something worse, which was the look of a man who has just heard himself say a true thing in the most unkind possible way and would like very much to un-say it.*
>
> *He said: "Maggie. I'm sorry. That was—I didn't mean—"*
>
> *And I said: "You did mean it."*
>
> *And he said: "I meant some of it. Not the way it came out."*
>
> *And I said: "The way it came out is the way I have it. The way it came out is the way I will always have it."*
>
> *He went quiet. He sat down in the chair by the window—the Windsor chair, the one with the nick in the left armrest from when he set his pen down too hard last November—and he looked at me, and I believe it was the first time he truly understood what he had married. Not the marvel of it. The *problem* of it. He has known what I can do since before the wedding. He has thought of it, I believe, as a kind of parlor trick—extraordinary, perhaps unsettling, but ultimately exterior to the marriage, the way his indestructibility is exterior. He walked through a building collapse. I walked through all of human experience without the capacity to leave any of it behind. He had thought these were parallel oddities. Matching disabilities. Tonight he understood that my ability is not parallel to the marriage. It is *inside* the marriage. It is the medium in which the marriage *occurs*.*
>
> *He sat in the chair. A long time. The candle guttered.*
>
> *Then he said: "What do I do, then? What am I supposed to do, if you'll never forget anything I say?"*
>
> *And I said: "Be more careful."*
>
> *And he said: "No one can be careful enough for *forever*, Margaret."*
>
