# The Collectors

## I.

Margo Kessler-Park had a system.

The east hallway was for textiles. Not clothing — clothing lived in the second bedroom, organized by decade and then by color, winter on the left wall, summer on the right, a canyon between them exactly twenty-two inches wide because that was the width of the laundry basket and Margo was, above all things, practical. The east hallway was for textiles in the broader sense: bolts of fabric she'd rescued from estate sales, curtain panels still in their packaging, a tower of fleece blankets from the winter Costco had run that promotion and she'd gone back nine times because you never knew when someone might need a blanket. You truly never did.

The kitchen was navigable. She wanted that stated for the record. The kitchen was navigable if you understood the logic of it, which was: appliances currently in rotation on the counters, appliances in seasonal reserve on the floor beneath the counters, and appliances held against future need on top of the refrigerator, in front of the refrigerator, and in the space between the refrigerator and the wall that most people didn't realize existed but which, once you measured it, could hold a stunning amount. There was a path from the sink to the stove to the coffee maker. The path was a consistent fourteen inches. Margo moved through it with the ease of a dancer, hip-checking the bread machine, pivoting past the second stand mixer with the grace of someone who had, through years of practice, memorized the topography of her own abundance.

The living room was beautiful. People didn't expect that. They expected squalor when they heard the word — not that anyone used the word, because Margo did not have a *problem*; she had a rich material life and a realistic assessment of how quickly the supply chain could collapse, and if that sounded paranoid, well, she'd been right about toilet paper, hadn't she? She had been *specifically, provably right* about toilet paper, and she would go to her grave on that hill. But the living room: she'd maintained it as a showpiece. Two linen sofas visible and unobstructed, a marble-topped coffee table she kept clear except for a single orchid she replaced weekly, tall windows with functioning curtains. When people came over — which was rarely, which was on purpose — they sat in the living room and they thought, *What a lovely home,* and they never asked to see the rest, because Margo managed the conversation the way she managed the hallways: with paths that led exactly where she wanted and nowhere else.

It was a Saturday in April, and Margo was integrating a new acquisition. A box of sixty-four glass canning jars — wide-mouth, zinc lids, the old kind they didn't make anymore — sourced from a woman in Gresham who was liquidating her dead mother's garage and didn't understand what she had. Margo had paid forty dollars. She could have paid four hundred. The ethical thing would have been to tell the woman, but ethics operated on a spectrum when it came to preservation, and these jars *needed someone who would understand them.*

She found space in the basement, behind the filing cabinets (tax records, every year since 1994, because seven years was what the IRS required but seven years was a suggestion made by people who had never been audited) and in front of the shelving unit that held the camping equipment she'd bought when she thought she might start hiking. She arranged the jars in rows, labels facing out, counted them twice, then stood there in the cool basement light and felt the thing she'd been chasing all morning: a brief, warm pulse of *enough.* Completeness. As though one more gap in the architecture of potential future need had been sealed.

It lasted about eleven seconds.

Then the pulse faded, as it always did, and underneath it was the other thing — the low hum she'd carried so long it had become indistinguishable from her own heartbeat. Not anxiety, exactly. A vigilance. The sense that something essential was missing, that she had not yet acquired the one object that would make the hum stop, that she needed to keep looking.

Margo climbed the basement stairs, closed the door behind her, and locked it. She kept the basement locked. She kept the second bedroom locked. She kept the east hallway accessible only from the back of the house, through a door she'd told her husband led to storage, which in a sense it did. The locks were small, brass, unremarkable. She'd installed them herself. Her husband, David, was not a curious man, which was one of the reasons she'd married him.

She washed her hands, put on a podcast, and began preparing lunch. From the living room, her home looked like a photograph in a magazine. From the living room, you would never know.

Elliot Tanaka did not need the ChapStick.

He owned ChapStick. He owned, at a conservative estimate, forty or fifty ChapSticks in various stages of use distributed across coat pockets, desk drawers, glove compartments, and the ceramic bowl on his entryway table that his ex-girlfriend had called "the lip balm graveyard" in a tone that suggested she believed she was making a proportionate observation and not, in fact, identifying the one thing about Elliot that he could not explain and did not find amusing.

He didn't need it. The CVS was three blocks from his office. He'd stopped in for a legitimate reason — saline spray, allergy season, his sinuses were a war zone — and he'd paid for the saline spray like a person, with a credit card, at the self-checkout, receipt in the bag, everything ordinary. The ChapStick was in his left jacket pocket. It had been there since aisle six. He could not precisely reconstruct the moment it had traveled from the shelf to his hand to his pocket, because that was the trick of it, the thing that made Elliot Tanaka different from a shoplifter: shoplifters *decided.* Shoplifters had a plan, a method, maybe a manifesto about the ethical bankruptcy of late capitalism. Elliot just — had things. Things migrated into his possession through some gravitational flaw in his character, a curvature in his personal space-time that bent small objects toward his pockets with the inevitability of light toward a black hole.

He was a patent attorney. He made two hundred and thirty thousand dollars a year. He had once pocketed a ninety-cent pack of gum from the same store where he'd just spent three hundred dollars on cold medicine and vitamins and the expensive face wash that his dermatologist recommended. He had once stolen a single, loose battery from an electronics store while carrying a bag containing the eight-hundred-dollar television he'd just purchased. The television had been paid for. The battery had not. The battery — a single double-A, value approximately forty cents — had been in his pocket before he'd finished the thought that he should probably buy batteries.

He sat in his car in the CVS parking lot and took the ChapStick out of his pocket and looked at it. Cherry. He didn't even like cherry. He preferred the original, the black label, or the green one that tasted like spearmint and felt like someone had iced his lips. Cherry was for children.

The feeling arrived on schedule: a simultaneous spike of satisfaction and self-loathing so intertwined they were physiologically indistinguishable, a single sensation that lived in his chest like a swallowed coal, hot and sick and his. He could go back in and pay for it. He could go back in, find a cashier, and say, *I forgot this was in my pocket.* He had done that before, and the cashier had smiled and said, *No worries,* and the coal in his chest had not gone out because the problem was never the object. The problem was the hand. The problem was whatever silent engine drove the hand, and paying after the fact did not turn off the engine; it just gave it a better alibi.

He dropped the ChapStick in the center console and started the car. He would throw it away later. He always said that. The center console was full.

Jade Amory was an exceptional cook and an even better host.

The lamb was a six-hour braise — pomegranate molasses, sumac, toasted pine nuts, a yogurt sauce she'd spiked with preserved lemon. She'd done a mezze spread for the table: three kinds of hummus, blistered shishitos, labneh with chili oil, the good bread from the bakery on Division that you had to order forty-eight hours in advance and pick up before seven a.m. like it was a controlled substance. She'd done cocktails. She'd done a playlist. She'd done the thing where she arranged the living room so the conversation would flow in one direction and the food would be always within reach but never the center of attention, because Jade understood — had made a career of understanding, as a food writer and sometime restaurant consultant — that the best meals were the ones where people forgot they were eating.

There were eight guests. Two couples, a few solo friends, her editor, her editor's new boyfriend, who was very young and very earnest and asked Jade how she'd *found her voice,* which she answered with a warmth that cost her nothing because the answer was practiced and the warmth was professional. Jade was funny. Jade was generous. Jade kept the wine flowing and deflected every compliment about the food toward the farmers who'd grown it and the grandmothers whose recipes she'd adapted and the sheer dumb luck of finding good pomegranates in April. She ate with everyone else. She ate visibly, appreciatively, with obvious pleasure. She had seconds of the lamb. She pressed dessert on people. She was the last to stop eating.

Everyone left by eleven, full of admiration and Côtes du Rhône. Jade stood at the door and watched the last car pull away and then she closed the door and locked it and stood in her hallway and felt the silence of her house close around her like water.

The kitchen was destroyed in the way kitchens are after a good party — stacked plates, wine-ringed counters, the enormous braising pot she'd need to soak overnight. The leftovers were on the island. Enough lamb for four more meals. The entire untouched second loaf of bread. A full container of labneh. Half a sheet cake she'd made because her editor was turning forty-one and Jade couldn't resist a reason to bake, which was a funny way to put it, *couldn't resist,* as though the decision to make a three-layer tahini cake at midnight the night before a dinner party were a whimsical creative choice and not the forward edge of a pattern she'd been running since she was thirteen.

Jade ate standing up. She ate the way you'd pour water into a hole in the ground — not fast, not slow, with a steadiness that had nothing to do with hunger and everything to do with a need she'd never successfully named but which had something to do with filling, with volume, with the temporary abolition of the empty space inside her that no amount of cooking for other people could reach. She ate lamb with her hands. She tore bread. She went through the labneh with a spoon, then without a spoon. She ate cake directly from the pan, not a slice, not a piece, just a fork moving through the soft crumb with mechanical regularity while her mind went somewhere else, somewhere quiet and far away, a white room where she didn't have to be charming or generous or the kind of woman who hosted dinner parties for eight on a school night.

She stopped when the pain started. Not emotional pain — she was an expert at not feeling that during, only after — but physical pain, the dull distension below her ribs that was her body's crude alarm system saying *this is too much, you have to stop,* and then, as it always did, the alarm pivoted from *stop eating* to *undo it.*

The bathroom was at the end of the hall. She'd renovated it the year she bought the house: good tile, a rainfall showerhead, a toilet she'd selected partly for its design and partly because it flushed with a force that could erase evidence. She ran the faucet. She'd been running faucets for twenty-two years. The sound was a kind of permission.

Afterward, she brushed her teeth twice, gargled with mouthwash, splashed cold water on her face, and looked in the mirror. Her eyes were bloodshot from the capillary pressure. She'd explain that away as wine if anyone were here, but no one was here. No one was ever here for this part. She cleaned the kitchen, packaged the remaining leftovers neatly, threw the packages in the outside bin so she would not repeat the process in the morning, and went to bed in her beautiful house with her face smelling like peppermint and her throat raw.

She set her alarm for six a.m. She had a column due. The column was about the joy of feeding people.

Nate Russo had a feeling about the Bucks.

Nate Russo always had a feeling. The feeling was a bodily conviction, dense and physical, that lived just below his sternum and told him: *this one is different.* This was not the other times. The other times had been emotional, sloppy, based on loyalty or narrative or the desire to see a particular team win. This time was pure signal. He'd been watching the line move all morning. He'd seen the injury report before the books adjusted. He had information — not inside information, nothing illegal, just the product of an attentiveness so total and so constant that it amounted to a kind of vision. He could see the shape of the game before it happened.

He put three thousand on the Bucks minus four and a half.

Nate ran a marketing consultancy. He was good at it. He was good at most things that involved reading rooms, seeing patterns, and making decisions with charismatic certainty, which was why his clients trusted him and his employees admired him and his wife, Carla, had married him even though her mother had said, famously, at the rehearsal dinner, *He's the kind of man who's either going to make you very rich or very nervous,* and everyone had laughed because it was a joke, and Carla had laughed because she loved him, and Nate had laughed because he thought the old woman was wrong. She wasn't wrong. She was imprecise. He had made Carla both.

The three thousand was from the account Carla didn't know about. Not the joint checking, not the savings, not the brokerage account they'd opened together when their first kid was born. The other account. The one that functioned as an organ in Nate's body — necessary, hidden, pumped by a heart he couldn't stop. The account rose and fell with the consistency of breathing: in when he won, out when he lost, in, out, in, out, the balance oscillating around a center of gravity that — if he were forced to be honest, which he was not, because no one was asking — trended irreversibly downward in a way that would, at some point, become a problem he could not solve with another win.

But not today. Today he had a feeling.

He placed the bet on his phone in his parked car in the lot behind his office, his thumb moving across the screen with the practiced efficiency of a surgeon or a pianist or anyone else who has performed one motion so many times that the body no longer consults the mind. Then he deleted the betting app, as he always did after placing a bet, and re-downloaded it fourteen minutes later to check the line, as he always did after deleting it, and then deleted it again.
