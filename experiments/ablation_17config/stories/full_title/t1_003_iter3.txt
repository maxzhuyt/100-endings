# The Collectors

## I.

Margot kept a beautiful home. Everyone said so. The living room had been photographed for a regional shelter magazine two years ago — the velvet sectional in ochre, the Noguchi lamp, the single vast canvas of poured resin the color of fog. What the magazine did not photograph was the second bedroom. When friends asked about it, Margot called it "the office." When her boyfriend asked about it, Margot said she was reorganizing. When her boyfriend asked again, four months later, Margot said it was a storage situation that had gotten out of hand. When he tried the door and found it locked, she told him she needed him to trust her, and he said he did, and they made love with a quality of tenderness that Margot recognized, even in the moment, as mutual cowardice.

Behind the locked door: 340 square feet packed to the ceiling. Seven sewing machines, none of which Margot could operate. Eleven boxes of National Geographic, 1974–1985. A child's bicycle still wrapped in the plastic it had shipped in. Forty-six mason jars, empty, the lids sorted by rust level into Ziploc bags that were themselves sorted into a rolling suitcase that could no longer roll. A taxidermied owl she'd bought at an estate sale on the day her mother was diagnosed, which she understood, intellectually, was a connection worth examining, and which she'd placed on top of a box labeled IMPORTANT — LOOK THROUGH SOON, a box she had labeled three years ago and never opened because opening it would mean deciding what to keep and what to release, and releasing things was—

The point is, Margot kept a beautiful home. The part of it she showed to people was immaculate. She had systems. She had a label maker she actually used. The magazine had called her style "restrained maximalism," which Margot had found so perfectly wrong that she'd laughed until her ribs ached, alone, standing in her kitchen, holding the glossy page, stunned by the distance between what she presented and what she contained.

She was good at distance. She could measure it exactly: the distance between who she appeared to be and who she was measured roughly 340 square feet, and it was growing.

---

Dean took the Montblanc pen from the conference table at 2:47 p.m., between the handshake and the elevator. No one saw. No one ever saw. That was the thing about Dean—he had the kind of face that cameras forgave, the kind of presence that made security guards relax. At thirty-four he ran a consulting firm that specialized in supply chain optimization for midsize retailers, a job so numbingly legitimate that he sometimes marveled at it, this life he'd built out of spreadsheets and client dinners, this whole gleaming architecture of respectability, and the fact that he could not stop stealing.

Not money. Never money. Dean was scrupulous about money. He tipped thirty percent, filed his taxes early, once drove back to a gas station to return a dollar in change. The things he took were small and purposeless: pens, matchbooks, a coaster from a hotel bar, a woman's hair clip from a restaurant table after she'd left with her husband. He kept nothing. That was the part his one terrible attempt at therapy had snagged on — the therapist wanted to talk about acquisition, about filling a void, about material attachment, and Dean had tried to explain that he threw everything away, that he didn't want the things, that the act of taking was the thing itself, the cool pulse of not-getting-caught, the way the world briefly reorganized around his secret and he stood at the center of it, unseen and total. The therapist had said, "So it's about control," and Dean had said, "No, it's about the moment right before you know if you'll get away with it," and the therapist had written something down and Dean had never gone back.

The Montblanc was warm from someone's hand. He held it in his pocket through the elevator ride, through the lobby, through seventeen blocks of walking. At the corner of Ninth and Broadway he dropped it into a storm drain with the efficiency of a man discarding a receipt.

Then he went home and ran six miles on the treadmill, which was what he did when the pulse faded too quickly, and he thought about absolutely nothing in a way that required enormous effort, and he was fine.

Nadia's dinner parties were legendary. This was not an exaggeration — her friends used the word *legendary* and meant it. The centerpieces were seasonal and deranged. The wine was always a half-step nicer than anyone expected. She made her own pasta, her own bread, her own stock from bones she bought from the butcher on Clement Street, where they knew her by name and saved her the marrow bones because she asked with such warmth, such genuine interest in the provenance of animal knuckles, that they couldn't help themselves. Nadia had that effect on people. She made you want to give her things.

Tonight it was eight guests, a cassoulet, three bottles of Côtes du Rhône, and a pear tart with a crust so architecturally flawless that someone actually said, "I don't want to eat this, I want to frame it," and Nadia laughed and cut the first slice and placed it in front of her friend Cara and watched her take a bite and felt, for a moment, the specific pleasure of feeding someone you loved, which was genuine, which was real, which was not the problem.

The problem was the moment between the main course and the dessert, when Nadia excused herself—"Be right back, keep talking, who needs more wine?"—and walked down the hall to the bathroom and locked the door and knelt and did what she did, quickly, efficiently, with a practice that had reduced the act to something almost mechanical, almost administrative, a task on a checklist: *clear the table, serve dessert, empty yourself.*

She flushed. She rinsed her mouth. She checked her eyes in the mirror for burst capillaries—there were none tonight, a mercy—and reapplied the lip color that she'd chosen specifically because it didn't transfer, and she thought about the word *legendary* and the word *empty* and how they rhymed if you were drunk enough, which she was not, because Nadia never drank at her own parties. Control was the menu. Control was the shopping list, the mise en place, the seating chart that put no ex next to a current, no recent divorcée across from a happy couple. The dinner party was a system, and the system worked, and she was part of the system, and the system required regular maintenance, and she went back to the table and smiled and served the tart and ate a slice herself—because not eating would be suspicious, because the performance required participation—and she tasted nothing.

"This might be your best one," Cara said.

"Next month I'm doing a whole lamb," Nadia said.

"You're insane."

"I know," Nadia said, and she meant it more than Cara did.

Eli answered every email within four minutes. He had measured this once, during a week when he was tracking his own efficiency as part of a "personal optimization audit" he'd designed for himself—not for work, not for any external purpose, just to confirm what he already suspected, which was that he was coping well. He had a morning routine that involved cold water, breathwork, journaling, and a smoothie containing nine ingredients that he could list in order of antioxidant density. He did not drink. He did not smoke. He ran but not compulsively—three times a week, varied routes, always with a podcast about something improving: behavioral economics, sleep architecture, the neuroscience of habit formation. He had read every book. He had implemented every framework. He was, by any reasonable measure, the most together person in any room he entered, and this was so consistent, so thoroughly maintained, that it had become a kind of signature, a brand, the thing people said about him at parties: *Eli's got it figured out.*

What people did not say, because people did not notice, was that Eli had not slept more than four consecutive hours in seven years. That his journaling practice was in fact a nightly log of every interaction he'd had that day, reviewed for errors, for moments of insufficient control, for instances where he'd said the wrong thing or failed to anticipate someone's response. That his apartment contained no personal photographs, not by aesthetic choice but because he'd removed them all during a three-day episode he did not have a name for, in which he'd determined that every image of his past was a vulnerability, a potential vector for feeling, and that the optimal life was one in which nothing surprised you because you had already accounted for everything.

He was a project manager for a biotech firm. His performance reviews used the word *indispensable*. His direct reports described him as "calm in a crisis," which was true in the way that a person who has run every crisis simulation in advance is calm — not through equanimity but through *rehearsal*. He did not manage crises. He pre-empted them. He lived in the predicted space between the present and every possible future, and he was vigilant there, because the alternative was to be surprised, and the last time he had been truly surprised he was fourteen years old and the world had shown him exactly what it did to people who were not paying attention.

Eli did not think of himself as having a compulsion. He thought of himself as having *standards*.

So when the letter arrived, he opened it within four minutes.

---

The letters were handwritten. That itself was a violence — who handwrites anything anymore — and the handwriting was careful, patient, almost affectionate, as though the author had taken pleasure in the forming of each letter, which they probably had.

The text was identical in all four envelopes:

*I know what you buried at Camp Alder in the summer of 1999. I know where it is. I know what you did before you buried it, and I know what each of you has become since. I've been watching the four of you grow around the shape of it, like trees around a fence post. Isn't it remarkable, what people can build on top of a hole in the ground?*

*You will come to Camp Alder on October 11th. You will arrive by 9 p.m. You will come alone, and you will not contact the authorities, for the same reason you did not contact the authorities in 1999: because what you did is yours, and you know it.*

*I have something each of you lost there. I'd like to return it.*

*I'll leave the lights on.*

There was no signature. There was no return address. The postmark was local — four different locals, one for each of them, which meant whoever sent this knew where they all lived, which meant—

But we'll get to that. For now, the letters sat in four different hands in four different cities, and four people who had not spoken in nineteen years felt the same thing at the same moment, a sensation like a hairline fracture running up through the center of a tooth, invisible until you bit down, and then: everything.
