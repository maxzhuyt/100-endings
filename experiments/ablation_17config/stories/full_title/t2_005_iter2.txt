# Form 27-B: Application for Continued Existence

---

## I.

The form was pink. Not the assertive pink of a thing that has chosen to be pink but the weary, institutional pink of a thing that has been copied so many times the redness has bled out of it, leaving only a suggestion of color — the bureaucratic memory of pigment. It was designated SCE-1, Species Continuity Extension (Standard), and it had been printed, in a run of eleven thousand, on the colony ship *Adelaide* sixteen years, eight months, and fourteen days ago, shortly after the Auditors reduced Earth to its constituent minerals and filed the planet under "Processed — No Further Action Required."

The form asked fourteen questions. The questions were specific. Question 4 asked for the species' "Primary Modality of Consciousness (check one)," and offered eleven options, nine of which described states of awareness that no human had ever experienced or could experience without ceasing to be, in any meaningful sense, human. Question 11 asked for a "Designated Continuity Rationale" and provided a space exactly four centimeters tall in which to justify, in the Auditors' preferred syntax, why a given species merited the continued privilege of metabolizing. The humans aboard the *Adelaide* had been answering these fourteen questions with great seriousness, in rotating committees of three, every ninety-one days, for seventeen years.

They had been answering the wrong questions.

Not the wrong answers — though several of those answers were also wrong, notably Question 7, where the committee had consistently misidentified humanity's "Reproductive Authorization Tier" because the question used a glyph that resembled, but was not, the Standard Galactic character for "biological" and in fact denoted a concept closer to "self-perpetuating aesthetic tradition." No: the problem was not the answers. The problem was that Species Continuity Extension (Standard) Form SCE-1 had been retired by the Auditors approximately eleven thousand years before the evolution of *Homo sapiens*, replaced first by SCE-1(a), then by a completely restructured document called a Provisional Existence Waiver (Form PEW-6), then by a system so thoroughly revised that the concept of "species continuity" had been folded into a broader category the Auditors referred to, in their own filing conventions, as "Ongoingness Assessment." The current form — the form humanity should have been filing — was OA-7741-C, which bore no resemblance whatsoever to SCE-1, asked no questions about consciousness or reproduction, and was, for reasons that would later become both relevant and heartbreaking, turquoise.

What the *Adelaide* had been submitting, every ninety-one days, with meticulous earnestness, to the Auditors' automated intake system was the bureaucratic equivalent of returning a library book to a mailbox. The system had been accepting the forms. The system accepted everything. Acceptance was not approval. Acceptance was not even acknowledgment. Acceptance was a function of the intake portal's design, which had been built to receive *any* physical submission — up to and including, as the *Adelaide*'s crew would later learn, a flat stone with lichen on its underside, which a silicon-based civilization in the Crab Nebula had once submitted in desperation and which the system had cataloged, without comment, as "Miscellaneous Correspondence (Mineral)."

Seventeen years. Sixty-eight submissions. Eleven thousand pink forms printed, of which seven hundred and forty-eight had been filled out, signed by the captain, countersigned by the elected oversight committee, and fed, with something that could only be called ceremony, into the submission portal the Auditors had installed in what had been the ship's forward cargo bay. Each submission was accompanied by a moment of silence. Several crew members had wept at early filings. A child — born on the ship, now eight, the only child who had been born on the ship and survived past infancy in the recycled air and the low-spectrum light — had once drawn a picture of the form and taped it to the submission portal's housing, the way a child might tape a drawing to a refrigerator. The picture was still there.

The discovery of the error was made by Yun Jae-won, a former systems librarian from Daejeon, who had been assigned to the filing committee not because of any relevant expertise but because the committee's membership was determined by a rotating lottery that Captain Óscar Vásquez had instituted in Year Three to distribute what he called "the burden of existential responsibility" and what most crew members called "form duty." Yun had been conducting a routine cross-reference — the kind of cross-reference a systems librarian conducts not because anyone asks but because systems librarians are, by nature, people who believe that the relationship between a document and its index is a moral relationship — when she found the discrepancy in the Auditors' publicly accessible filing taxonomy, which was available, if one knew where to look, in the submission portal's maintenance subroutine, in a file labeled with an eleven-digit alphanumeric string and the Auditors' equivalent of "README."

She brought the finding to the captain at 0600 hours on a Tuesday, because it was a Tuesday, and on Tuesdays Captain Vásquez held open office hours in the repurposed crew lounge that served as the ship's administrative center, which still smelled faintly of the synthetic coffee substitute that had run out in Year Nine. She presented her findings in a six-page memorandum. She had included charts.

Captain Vásquez read the memorandum twice. He set it on the desk. He picked it up and read it again. Then he said, in a voice that the omniscient record must describe as controlled in the way that a dam is controlled — which is to say, the control *is* the evidence of the pressure:

"This isn't right."

And then, louder, standing, the memorandum crumpling in his fist:

"This *can't* be right."

But it was right. It was, in the way of bureaucratic errors, *obviously* right — the kind of error that, once seen, reveals itself to have been visible all along, like a misspelling on a sign you pass every day. The filing taxonomy was unambiguous. The form numbers did not require interpretation. SCE-1 was not OA-7741-C. It had never been OA-7741-C. The distance between them was not a matter of nuance or translation or cultural difference; it was the distance between a document and a non-document, between a filing and a piece of paper that happened to have words on it.

Within six hours, the captain had convened an emergency session of the full Advisory Council, which was the name given to the twelve-person body that governed the *Adelaide* in all matters not directly related to navigation or life support, though its actual function, in the four years since its formation, had been primarily to argue about resource allocation and, on one memorable occasion, to spend eleven consecutive hours debating whether the ship's single functioning entertainment terminal should default to a screensaver of Earth or a blank screen, a debate that had fractured along surprisingly deep ideological lines and that several council members still referenced, in moments of tension, as evidence of their opponents' fundamental unseriousness.

The council argued.

They argued about whether Yun's analysis was correct. (It was.) They argued about whether the filing taxonomy might have changed in the seventeen years since the Auditors' last direct communication. (It had not; the timestamp on the README file predated Earth's existence by a factor of six.) They argued about who had selected SCE-1 in the first place. This last question produced a silence that lasted forty-five seconds — a significant silence, on a ship where silence meant recycled air and the hum of systems keeping three thousand and eleven people alive in the space between stars — before Dr. Priya Chandrasekaran, the ship's chief medical officer and a woman whose voice carried the quiet authority of someone accustomed to delivering news that reorganized other people's realities, said:

"We selected it because it was the only form in the submission portal's default queue. We assumed the default queue contained the correct form. We did not check because we did not know there was anything to check."

"We assumed," Captain Vásquez repeated.

"Yes."

"For seventeen years."

"Yes."

In Chair 7, at the far end of the council table — which was not a table but an arrangement of cargo containers with a sheet of repurposed hull plating balanced across them — a woman who had not been elected to the Advisory Council, and who was present only because she had been passing through the corridor when the emergency session was announced and had entered because she recognized the quality of the voices, the particular pitch of institutional crisis, the way she might recognize the smell of a specific cleaning product, sat with her hands folded in her lap, and nodded.

She nodded the way a person who has spent decades at a counter nods when a customer, ashen and shaking, realizes that the form they filled out in blue ink needed to be filled out in black ink, and that this means they must start over, and that starting over means returning to the back of a line that extends, at this hour, out the door and into a parking lot where the August asphalt sends up shimmers that make the line itself appear to waver, as if reality is uncertain about how many people are actually waiting.

Her name was Ndayeli Uwimana. She was fifty-one years old. Before the Auditors had arrived, before the sky over Kigali had filled with structures that looked, to Ndayeli, less like spacecraft than like filing cabinets turned inside out, she had worked for nine years at the equivalent of a motor vehicle registration office in a satellite bureau outside the capital. She had processed an average of one hundred and twelve transactions per day. She had been screamed at in four languages. She had learned — not from training, because the training was a single afternoon of someone reading a manual aloud in a room without air conditioning, but from the accumulation of days, from the ten thousand small negotiations between a system's demands and a person's confusion — how to read a form the way a doctor reads a body: where the errors would cluster, where the language would mislead, where the structure itself would betray the assumptions of the person who had designed it, assumptions that were never visible to the person filling it out.

Ndayeli had not spoken at the meeting. She would not speak at the meeting. She was not, technically, supposed to be at the meeting. But when the council voted — nine to three, with one abstaining on procedural grounds — to file a corrected submission using the appropriate form, she allowed herself to close her eyes for a moment, and behind her eyelids she saw the counter in Kigali, and the line, and the parking lot, and the August heat, and she thought: *Here we go.*

## II.

The corrected submission was filed on a Wednesday, in a small ceremony that Captain Vásquez had insisted upon and that no one else had wanted. He stood in the forward cargo bay, in front of the submission portal — which resembled, and may in fact have been, a mail slot scaled up to the size of a door, set into a wall that was not a wall of the ship but a wall of something else, something that had installed itself in the cargo bay the way a government agency installs a service counter in a rented storefront — and he read a brief statement about humanity's resilience, and half the audience wept, and the other half stared at their shoes, and Ndayeli, who was present this time by invitation because Yun Jae-won had, with the systems librarian's instinct for correct attribution, told the council that Ndayeli had been the one to explain the difference between a filing taxonomy and a filing queue, stood near the back and watched the captain feed the turquoise form into the slot with both hands, as if releasing a bird.

The form was turquoise. It had been printed hastily, on the *Adelaide*'s last functioning fabricator, using a color profile approximated from the Auditors' taxonomy description. Whether the specific shade of turquoise mattered was a question no one had been able to answer. Ndayeli had argued that it mattered. She had argued this with the quiet certainty of a woman who had once rejected a vehicle registration renewal because it had been printed on cream-colored stock instead of white, not because she was cruel but because the system did not process cream. Cream was not a category. The system had categories, and the categories were the system, and the space between cream and white was the space in which a person's file could vanish as completely as if it had never been filed at all.

The turquoise form entered the slot. The slot accepted it. For a moment — perhaps three seconds, perhaps four — nothing happened, and the cargo bay was filled with the particular quality of held breath that accompanies any interaction with an automated system that may or may not be functioning.

Then the ship moved.

It did not move in the way a ship moves — not in the way the *Adelaide* had been moving for seventeen years, which was the slow, determined movement of a vehicle carrying everything that was left, crossing a distance that its passengers understood intellectually but felt only as the unchanging hum of the drive and the slow degradation of systems designed to last for thirty years and now, in Year Seventeen, beginning to express their mortality through flickering lights and seals that wept small beads of condensation in the corridors where the temperature regulation had begun to falter.

The ship did not move. Space moved. Or rather: the relationship between the ship and space was amended by an administrative action, the way a file is transferred from one drawer to another without the file itself being opened or read or considered in any way. The *Adelaide* was re-assigned.

In the cargo bay, the lights went out and were replaced by other lights: flat, white, and sourceless, the light of a room designed not to be looked at but to be occupied during the interval between arrival and processing. The hum of the ship's drive was replaced by a different hum — lower, patient, the hum of a system that had been running so long it had become indistinguishable from silence, the same way the refrigerator hum in a government office becomes inaudible to the people who work there and audible only to those who have just walked in from outside, from the world where sounds have sources and silence means the absence of machinery.

Captain Vásquez gripped the podium. Several crew members fell. A child screamed and was hushed. Through the cargo bay's single narrow viewport — which now looked out not on the familiar void of interstellar space but on something else, something that should not have had a floor or a ceiling but did — Ndayeli saw the chairs.

They were plastic. They were bolted to a floor that extended in every visible direction with the serene indifference of a surface that was not designed to end. They were orange, the specific orange of injection-molded polypropylene that has been standardized not for beauty or comfort but for the purpose of being wipeable, and they were arranged in rows with the spacing of objects that have been placed not by a person but by a specification, and the specification has been copied so many times that no one remembers the original intent, only the measurement: forty-four centimeters between seat centers, measured from the midpoint of the backrest.

There were other things in the chairs. Some of them might have been sitting. Others occupied the chairs the way fog occupies a valley — by being there, without appearing to have arrived. In the nearest row, Ndayeli could see what looked like a delegation of translucent tubes, each approximately one meter tall, pulsing with a slow internal luminescence that reminded her of nothing so much as the lava lamps that her college roommate had collected, though the tubes were clearly alive, or at least clearly agential, because they were filling out forms, and they were filling out forms using cilia that extended from their upper surfaces and manipulated instruments that were too small and too numerous to see clearly, and they were doing this with the focused, slightly defeated posture of beings who had been filling out forms for a very long time and expected to be filling out forms for a very long time to come.

Beyond the tubes, in a section of chairs that had been modified — or that had always existed in this configuration — to accommodate a body of indeterminate shape and tremendous volume, something that might have been a single organism or might have been a committee sat very still and radiated a smell that the *Adelaide*'s external sensors would later identify as a complex phospholipid chain but that several crew members, independently and without consultation, described as "waiting room."
