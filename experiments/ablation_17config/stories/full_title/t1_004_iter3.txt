# The Divided Caretaker

## I.

The mop hit tile in a rhythm that had worn itself smooth over years, the way a river rounds a stone. Dip and wring, pull and arc, the gray water drawing its half-moon across the floor of the east corridor. His hands knew the resistance of the wringer before the rest of him caught up—the satisfying moment when the mop head compressed to a damp wedge and the water ran back brown into the basin. Wrist, forearm, twist. The fibers released. He set the mop down and began again.

The fluorescent tube above Room 14 had developed a tic three shifts ago—a barely perceptible flutter that threw the hallway into an almost subliminal stutter of shadow. He could feel it in his peripheral vision while he worked, the way you feel a draft before you identify the open window. He added it to his internal list. But not now. Now the floor.

Eleven-fifteen. The night shift had settled into its particular silence, the one that was not really silence at all but a layered composition: the building's ventilation, a low mechanical exhalation through the ceiling vents; the arrhythmic chorus of heart monitors from the rooms with open doors, each one pinging at its own idiosyncratic interval; the creak of the hospice's skeleton as the temperature dropped outside and the old framing contracted by fractions of a degree. He moved through these sounds the way a fish moves through current—not hearing them so much as being borne along by them, alert to any variation.

Room 11: Mrs. Oleander's breathing had shifted since last Thursday from the long, tidal rhythm that meant deep sleep to something shallower, a half-respiration that kept catching, like fabric snagging on a nail. He paused outside her door. The mop handle rested against his collarbone. He stood there for four seconds, maybe five, listening the way a mechanic listens to an engine—not for what was there but for what was changing. Then he moved on.

The night nurse came around the corner from the supply room, her sneakers making their familiar compression-squeak against the tile he'd just mopped. Janet, Monday through Thursday. She carried a medication tray balanced on her forearm, the little paper cups arranged in their grid. She saw him and gave a nod—chin down, chin up, a motion so economical it barely qualified as communication. He returned it. The same motion. Their eyes met for half a second, the way two people's eyes meet who have long since exhausted the need to perform mutual acknowledgment but do it anyway, out of a residual decency.

She passed. He watched her sneakers leave damp impressions on his clean floor, each one a ghost footprint that would evaporate in minutes. He did not mind. He dipped the mop.

Room 9 had been empty since Thursday when Gareth Mosley had been taken out under a sheet at 4:47 a.m. The janitor had cleaned that room afterward—stripped the bed, wiped the rails with the disinfectant that smelled like synthetic lemon laid over something chemical and final, mopped the floor in the particular way he mopped rooms after a death, which was no different from the way he mopped any other room except that he was more thorough in the corners. The room still held the cleaned-out quality of a space that had recently contained a person and now contained only the idea of the next one. He mopped past it without pausing.

His route was a circuit, invariant. East corridor, south wing, the common room where the television murmured to no one at this hour, west corridor past the nurses' station, north wing where the sicker patients were clustered near the oxygen supply, then back. Forty-two minutes if nothing interrupted. He had never timed it deliberately; the knowledge had accumulated in his legs the way a path accumulates in grass.

At the station, he emptied the basin into the utility sink. The water swirled gray-brown, the universal color of dissolved human presence—skin cells, tracked-in dirt, the invisible leavings of illness. He ran hot water from the tap, added a capful of floor solution from the jug, watched the basin fill. The cap had a crack along its rim that he'd been meaning to report, except that reporting required a form and the form required writing and his right hand, which had once written with the small, tidy print of a man who'd kept household ledgers and helped his daughter with her school essays, now produced a script that looked like a seismograph recording a distant tremor. He had tried, early on. The pen in his grip, the letters shaking apart on the page like a bridge in wind. He had stopped trying to write reports. The cap continued to crack. He poured carefully to compensate.

The utility closet was in the north wing, just past Room 3. He returned his supplies there at the end of each shift—basin on the lower shelf, mop in the spring clamp on the wall, spray bottles arranged by color code (blue for surfaces, green for glass, red for biohazard, though the red one was almost never used and had a film of dust on its trigger). He aligned the mop with the clamp and pressed it in. The spring engaged with a soft click. He stood in the closet for a moment, his hand still on the mop handle, feeling the day's residue in his shoulders. The closet smelled like every utility closet in every institution he had ever known: pine concentrate, damp cotton, the mineral tang of old plumbing.

His shift ended at seven. Until then, the circuit. He filled the basin again and started back toward the east corridor.

Room 14's fluorescent tube flickered. Mrs. Oleander's breathing caught and released, caught and released. The building breathed around him, its old lungs working, and he mopped the floor in long arcs, and the floor received the mop, and neither of them spoke.

## II.

It was already there when he arrived Tuesday.

He came through the back entrance at eleven, the one near the loading dock where the linen service made its deliveries, and hung his coat on the third hook in the break room, the hook that had been his since before anyone currently on staff could remember assigning it. He changed into his soft-soled shoes. He filled his basin. He began.

He first registered it at the nurses' station as a change in the geometry of the space. Where there had been an open stretch of counter between the second computer terminal and the wall-mounted hand sanitizer, there was now something standing. Not leaning, not placed—standing, the way a person stands when waiting to be addressed. It was roughly the height and width of a slender woman. Its surface was a matte polymer the color of putty, featureless except for the faint seams at the neck, wrists, and waist where its modular components joined. Its face was a simplified human arrangement—two recessed optical sensors, a slight protrusion suggesting a nose, a horizontal line where a mouth would be that did not open. It wore scrubs. Someone had dressed it in scrubs—the same pale blue as the nursing staff, as though this concession to uniform might absorb it into the existing order of the place.

He mopped around it. His arc adjusted by eighteen inches to the left, giving the station's new geometry its required clearance. The mop moved. The thing stood. Janet came around the corner with a chart and spoke to it, or near it—"Vitals in six are flagged, you're on that wing"—in a voice so flatly procedural it could have been directed at a coatrack. The thing turned and walked toward the south wing with a gait that was almost human but carried no weight shift, no asymmetry, no trace of a body compensating for its own imperfections. It moved the way a diagram of walking would move if it could step off the page.

Over the next hour, he heard the rest in pieces.

A woman from administration—he recognized her heels on the tile, the particular authority of a stride that does not adjust for wet floors—stood at the station speaking to someone on a phone pressed between her shoulder and ear. "No, the donated unit. The one from St. Raphael's when they upgraded. It's fine, the tech said it's within acceptable parameters. No, wiped. Memory-wiped. Clean install. Listen, the board approved it in March, this isn't—yes. Yes. For nights only. It's a pilot."

He wrung the mop. The word *pilot* hung in the air like the antiseptic smell—institutional, purposeful, indifferent to the people it would settle on.

Later, near Room 9, which was still empty and which the thing had been assigned to monitor for the next admission, he found a laminated quick-start sheet on the floor beneath the door-mounted chart holder. It must have slipped from a folder. He picked it up the way he picked up anything on the floor—automatically, because the floor was his jurisdiction. The sheet was dense with text in a small sans-serif font, and his eyes moved over it the way they moved over any printed material in the building, with the skimming attention of a person who has learned that most institutional text does not concern him. But certain phrases snagged:

*Caregiver-class Android, Model TH-7 (Refurbished)*
*Emotional Calibration Subroutines: Version 3.1.2 (NOTE: partial corruption detected during transfer; non-critical; override not recommended)*
*Decommissioned from St. Raphael Medical Center, 08/2043*
*Donated under liability waiver (see attached Form 9-B)*

He placed the sheet on the counter by the chart holder, aligning it with the edge. His hand hesitated for a moment over the phrase *partial corruption detected*, not because he understood its technical meaning but because the word *corruption* lived in him with a different weight than it lived in a spec sheet—it was a word from sermons, from the news, from the smell of a thing going wrong in a dark room. Then his hand withdrew, and he returned to the mop.

By the end of the week, the thing had been absorbed into the hospice's ecology the way all new additions were absorbed: not through acceptance but through the accumulation of small accommodations that, taken together, constituted a fact. The nursing staff spoke to it in the clipped imperative they used with equipment. The orderly on the day shift called it "the unit." Someone had taped a handwritten sign to the supply closet door: *TH-7 charging station — DO NOT unplug.* The sign was written in the same hand that wrote all the supply closet signs, the same blue marker, the same block capitals. The thing had been filed.

He adjusted his circuit. The eighteen-inch deviation at the nurses' station became permanent, encoded in his legs alongside all the other adjustments he had made over the years—the wide berth around the loose tile near Room 2, the slight rightward drift in the north wing where the baseboard heater protruded an extra inch, the pause outside Mrs. Oleander's door. His route was a palimpsest of accommodations, and this was one more.

On Friday, at 2:00 a.m., he was refilling his basin when the thing passed him in the corridor. Its footsteps made a sound like fingers tapping softly on a desk—a precise, evenly spaced percussion that produced no echo. It carried a folded blanket. It did not look at him, or avoid looking at him, or adjust its path. It passed through his space the way light passes through glass: without registering the medium.

He watched it go. Then he turned off the tap, because the basin was full, and the water was running over.

## III.

He first noticed on a Wednesday, or rather, his hands first noticed—they slowed on the mop as he passed Room 6, where the thing was adjusting Eleanor Voss's blanket.

Eleanor Voss was seventy-eight and had the kind of cancer that proceeded like a slow siege, taking territory so gradually that each day's loss seemed negligible until you compared the current map to the one from a month ago. She slept fitfully, her body temperature oscillating as her systems lost their ability to regulate. At 1:40 a.m., from the hallway, the janitor saw the android standing at her bedside. Eleanor was asleep. Her blanket had slipped below her shoulders but not off them—a displacement of perhaps four inches, not enough to trigger the call button, not enough to wake her, barely enough to register as discomfort. The android reached down and drew the blanket up. Not briskly. Not with the efficient tug of a nurse redistributing linens during rounds. It drew the blanket up the way a current moves a leaf into an eddy—slowly, with such calibrated gentleness that Eleanor's breathing didn't skip. The fabric settled over her shoulders without a crease.

He was mopping Room 7 when his hands slowed. He realized he had been watching from the corner of his vision, tracking the motion through the open doors the way he tracked the fluorescent flicker—peripherally, but with full attention.

Coincidence. Good programming. He dipped the mop.

Three nights later. Arthur Pell, Room 4, a man whose throat cancer had reduced his voice to a whisper and whose pride had reduced his use of the call button to nearly zero. At 12:55 a.m., the janitor was working the north corridor when he saw the android enter Room 4 carrying a cup of water. He knew it was water by the way light caught the surface through the translucent cup—a small wobble of refracted fluorescence. The android set the cup on the bedside table, angling it precisely to the right side where Arthur's one functional hand could reach without overextension. Then it left. Eleven seconds later—the janitor counted, without deciding to count—Arthur Pell's hand emerged from the blanket and reached for the table, fingers searching. They found the cup. He drank.

The janitor stood in the corridor with the mop handle against his sternum, watching the sequence complete itself. His eyes tracked from the room where Arthur was drinking to the android's retreating form as it continued down the hall toward its next task. It had known. Not responded—there had been no call, no sound, no signal he could identify. It had *known*, the way a body knows to swallow, the way a hand knows to close around a falling object before the mind registers the fall.

He began, without deciding to, adjusting his timing. His circuit had always been forty-two minutes. Now he found himself stretching certain legs and compressing others—lingering an extra minute in the north wing, shortening his time in the common room—so that he passed through the patient corridors during the hours when the android was most active. He did not think of this as surveillance. It was not a plan. It was the same instinct that made him pause outside Mrs. Oleander's door—an attunement to a change in the environment that required closer reading.

The reading grew more granular.

A Tuesday. Helen Park, Room 12, unconscious for forty-six hours, her body motionless except for the mechanical rise and fall driven by the oxygen assist. The android entered at 3:10 a.m. It moved to the left side of the bed—not the right, where the IV stand and monitoring equipment clustered, but the left, the side visitors were asked to use, the human side. It placed its hands beneath Helen Park's left hip and shoulder and, with a motion so controlled it looked almost tidal, rotated her body fifteen degrees onto her right side. It then repositioned her left arm, extending it slightly, and placed a folded towel beneath her knee to maintain the angle. The entire procedure took ninety seconds. When it was done, the android stood for a moment—three seconds, four—with its hands at its sides, its optical sensors directed at the place where Helen Park's spine met the mattress. Then it left.

The janitor had stopped mopping. His hands rested on the handle, both of them, the left steady and the right with its permanent fine tremor, and he stared at the bed through the open door. He knew what a pressure sore looked like. He had cleaned around enough of them—the raw, weeping craters that opened in the skin of people who could not move and were not moved enough. He had mopped the floors of rooms where the sweet-sick smell of necrotic tissue overpowered his cleaning solutions. He knew the physics of it: sustained pressure, reduced blood flow, tissue death.
