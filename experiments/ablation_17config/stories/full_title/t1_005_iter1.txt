# The Court of Investigation

## I.

The fluorescent lights in the gymnasium at the Arthur Reeves Recreation Center had been on since 4:00 a.m., though no one had turned them on at 4:00 a.m. The timer switch on the east wall was set to that hour by a man who believed that a room should be ready before anyone needed it, that readiness was a form of love, and that love, if it was worth anything, had to arrive early and stay late. That man was Marcus Welty, and at 5:47 a.m. on a Wednesday in March, he was dead on the hardwood between the three-point line and the equipment cage, and the lights he had programmed shone down on his body with the quality of patience that only fluorescent tubes possess — a flat, lidless, unapologetic illumination that did not know how to look away.

His left arm was extended toward the cage door, which was open. The padlock lay on the floor three feet from his hand, intact, unlocked with its own key. Inside the cage, eight sport wheelchairs sat in their folded-spider configurations on the storage rack, frames interlocking at precise angles because Marcus had insisted on precise angles, had spent hours teaching the players to fold and rack their chairs in the correct sequence, had said, more times than any of them could count, that the chairs were not equipment but partners, and you do not throw your partner in a pile. The chairs were undisturbed. Nothing in the cage appeared to be missing. The only visible disorder was Marcus himself — face-down, the back of his gray Reeves Rec Center polo darkened with a stain that the overhead lights rendered not red but a deep industrial brown, as though the blood had arrived already old.

A custodian named Terrance Muñoz found the body at 5:47 when he came to mop the court before the early-bird seniors' walking group. He called 911 from the hallway because he could not stay in the room with the stillness. The police arrived at 6:02. The coroner's van at 6:41. The phone calls began at 7:15.

---

In a bedroom in the Arbor Glen apartments on the south side, Raquel Morse was already awake. She had been awake since 5:00, sitting in her TiLite rigid-frame chair at the small desk below the window, reviewing a hand-drawn play diagram the way a chess player reviews a middle game — not the moves themselves but the spaces between the moves, the lanes that would open if the defense shifted a quarter-second late. The Reeves Rolling Thunder had a championship game in seventy-two hours, and Raquel had been unable to sleep for reasons she told herself were entirely about the game. Her phone buzzed on the desk. She read the name — Coach Harlan — and her hand stopped, fingers two inches from the screen, suspended in the blue-white glow. She did not pick up the phone for three full rings. When she did, she listened without speaking for eleven seconds. Then she said, "I understand." She set the phone face-down on the desk. Her eyes went to the play diagram, then past it, through it, to a place the diagram could not map. Her jaw was working, very slightly, as though she were chewing the inside of her cheek. She reached for the phone again and opened her contacts and scrolled not to a teammate's name but to a number saved without a name, just six digits and an asterisk. She stared at it. Then she locked the phone and placed it in her lap and wheeled to the bathroom and turned the faucet on and watched the water run.

She did not look like a person who had just learned that someone she loved was dead. She looked like a person doing arithmetic.

---

In a house on Delancey Street, four blocks from the rec center, Deshawn Kato was eating cereal in the kitchen with the television on and the volume off, because the volume was always off. He was seventeen, six-foot-one in his chair, broad through the shoulders in a way that made opposing centers reassess their positioning, and he had been deaf in his left ear since birth and profoundly deaf in his right since the age of nine, when the nerve damage his doctors had called "probably stable" turned out to be neither. He read the television — a morning news anchor whose lip movements he parsed with the fluency of long practice — while spooning Frosted Mini-Wheats into his mouth with metronomic regularity. His phone, set to vibrate, buzzed against the table. He glanced at the caller ID, set down the spoon, and answered.

He watched the closed-caption text scroll across the phone's screen — the relay service transcribing Coach Harlan's voice in its usual three-second delay.

MARCUS. FOUND THIS MORNING. HE'S GONE. DESHAWN. I'M SO SORRY.

Deshawn read the words twice. Then his eyes closed. Not a slow, grief-stricken closing — not the wince of shock, the scrunch of disbelief. A single, controlled descent of both eyelids, as though someone had pressed a switch. The expression beneath the closed eyes was not pain. It was confirmation. It was the face of a person who had opened an envelope already knowing the letter inside, and finding exactly the words he expected, and understanding that expectation did not make them easier.

He opened his eyes. He looked at the television anchor, who was now discussing weather. He set the phone on the table and placed both palms flat on either side of it and breathed in through his nose and held the breath for a count of five and released it.

His mother, drying her hands on a towel in the doorway, signed: *What's wrong?*

*Nothing yet,* he signed back, which was a lie so precise it was almost the truth.

---

In her mother's car, idling in the drop-off lane at Jefferson Alternative High School, Simone Quesada-Harris heard the news and cried. She cried immediately, fully, with the unselfconscious openness of a person whose grief reflexes had not yet been trained to perform or suppress. Tears ran from under her glasses and fell onto the phone she held to her right ear and onto the hand that held the phone and onto the armrest of the passenger seat. Her mother, Adaeze Harris, a patient-intake administrator at County General, glanced over and said, "Baby?" and Simone shook her head and held up one finger — *wait* — and said into the phone, "Thank you for telling me, Coach," in a voice that was thick and cracked and entirely genuine.

She ended the call. She told her mother what had happened. Adaeze pressed her hand to her mouth. She had met Marcus dozens of times — at games, at fundraisers, at the adaptive sports banquet where he'd worn the same gray polo he wore everywhere, where he'd spent the entire evening making sure every player's chair was positioned with a clear sightline to the stage.

But Simone's grief, as real as it was, contained a room she did not open. Behind the tears and the shaking hands and the ugly, honest sobs was a sealed compartment in which a different reaction was taking place — not about Marcus's death but about what Marcus had known before he died and who else might know it now and whether the records Marcus had accessed at County General, the ones Simone's mother had unknowingly facilitated by leaving her login credentials on a sticky note on the home computer, would now surface in a police investigation and land on her mother's desk in the form of a termination letter. Simone was sixteen years old and she was grieving a man she loved and she was, in the same body, in the same minute, calculating the blast radius of his death.

She let none of this show. She did not need to. The tears covered everything.

---

And in a studio apartment above a laundromat on Fitch Avenue, Jay Baskin was already dressed.

This was the detail that, if anyone had been watching, would have made no sense. It was 7:16 in the morning. Jay's alarm, according to the phone on the milk-crate nightstand, was set for 7:45. School did not start until 8:30. Jay was a creature of economy — fourteen years old, small even for fourteen, a bench player who conserved energy the way a desert animal conserves water, never moving before the moment required movement. And yet at 7:16, Jay was not in bed. Jay was fully dressed — jeans, sneakers positioned on the footrest, a hoodie zipped to the sternum — sitting at the apartment's single window, looking down at the street, as though waiting for something.

The phone vibrated. Jay picked it up on the first ring. Listened. Said, "Okay." Not "Oh no," not "What happened," not "Are you sure." Just: "Okay." A word that closed a door rather than opening one.

Jay set the phone down and continued looking out the window. After a moment, one hand rose to the hoodie's zipper and pulled it up the remaining two inches to the collar, a small gesture of self-enclosure, the body sealing itself before the world could reach in. The expression on Jay's face was not grief, not shock, not the calculated composure of Raquel or the braced confirmation of Deshawn. It was something harder to name. Readiness, perhaps. Or the aftermath of readiness — the look of a person who has been waiting for a thing to begin and now feels, with something adjacent to relief, the beginning arrive.

Jay was already dressed. Jay had answered on the first ring. Jay had said "Okay" to the news that a man was dead, and the word had carried no surprise.

Five different rooms. Five different faces shown to no one.

The lights in the gymnasium continued to shine on the space where Marcus Welty had been, and now was not, and the sport wheelchairs sat in their careful arrangements in the cage and waited, as they always waited, for someone to unfold them into the shape of use.

---

## II.

By noon, the gymnasium was sealed behind yellow tape that Terrance Muñoz would later have to scrub residue from, and the Reeves Rolling Thunder had been summoned to the rec center's multipurpose room for a meeting that was not called a meeting but a "check-in," as though the act of renaming a thing could soften its edges.

They gathered in a loose half-circle — eleven players, Coach Dale Harlan, the assistant coach, and a grief counselor from the school district whose name no one would remember by evening. The room smelled of instant coffee and floor wax. The windows faced the parking lot, where two police cruisers still sat, though their purpose had shifted in the last hour from investigation to announcement.

Coach Harlan stood at the front of the room. He was a tall man, forty-four, with the particular leanness of a former distance runner who had never fully accepted that his running days were over. His face, in the fluorescent light, was the color of parchment. He gripped the back of a folding chair with both hands and told the team that the police had made an arrest.

The name was Don Kennick.

A murmur moved through the half-circle — not surprise, exactly, but the sound of a narrative clicking into place. Everyone on the team knew the name. Don Kennick had been the head coach of Eastlake Academy's adaptive sports program until eighteen months ago, when a dispute over tournament seeding escalated into a public, profanity-laced confrontation with Marcus at a regional qualifier. Kennick had accused Marcus of manipulating equipment inspections to disadvantage Eastlake's players. Marcus, who had never in his life raised his voice at a tournament, had responded with a single sentence so quietly devastating that the gymnasium had gone silent: "Don, you should coach your players instead of blaming a man who measures tire pressure." Kennick had been fired three weeks later — not for the confrontation itself, but because the confrontation drew attention to a pattern of intimidation that Eastlake's athletic director could no longer overlook.

Coach Harlan told the team that Kennick had confessed. That the police believed the motive was revenge — a grudge nursed for months, a man who blamed Marcus for the end of his career. That the case was, in the word the detective had used, "resolved."

"The championship is Saturday," Coach Harlan said. He paused, and the pause had the weight of a man choosing between two scripts and selecting the one that sounded more like care. "Nobody's asking you to play if you're not ready. But Marcus would have wanted—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "We'll practice tomorrow. Show up if you can."

The room exhaled. The sound was collective, almost tidal — eleven bodies releasing a tension that had been held since 7:15 that morning. Simone pressed her palm flat against her sternum as though physically pushing the breath out. Deshawn nodded once, slowly, the nod of a person filing information rather than agreeing with it. Two of the younger players hugged. The grief counselor distributed cards with a phone number.

Coach Harlan's hands, on the back of the folding chair, did not relax. But nobody was watching Coach Harlan's hands.

---

The parking lot emptied slowly. Parents arrived. Players loaded into cars and accessible vans. Simone left with her mother, who had come straight from County General, still in her badge lanyard. Deshawn's father pulled up in a Honda Odyssey with the ramp already deployed. Jay was picked up by a woman in a county social services vehicle who might have been a foster parent or a caseworker; it was hard to tell from a distance, and Jay did not introduce her.

Raquel stayed.

She told her father, who had dropped her off, that she needed a few minutes alone, and he understood, or thought he understood, and drove to the Wendy's on the corner to wait. Raquel wheeled herself to a spot at the far edge of the parking lot, near the dumpster enclosure, where the asphalt was cracked and patchy and the wheelchair required more effort per push. She set her brakes. She looked at the rec center's side entrance — the one Marcus always used, the one closest to the equipment cage's exterior door — and she sat there for a long time.

Then she reached into the pocket on the side of her chair's backrest — a small mesh pouch meant for a water bottle — and pulled out a pocket-sized spiral notebook with a green cover. The cover was worn soft. The spiral binding was crimped in two places. On the inside front cover, in the careful handwriting of a twelve-year-old, someone had written: *RAQUEL MORSE — JUNIOR DETECTIVE CAMP — CABIN 4 — SUMMER SESSION 2.*

She had been carrying this notebook for three years. She had never told anyone on the team about junior detective camp, because junior detective camp was for kids who watched too much television, kids who believed in logic and evidence and the solvability of problems, and Raquel had spent the last three years building an identity as a person who operated on instinct and nerve and the fast-twitch authority of a point guard who sees the floor before it moves. The notebook was a relic of a self she had not quite outgrown and was not quite ready to bury.

She opened it to a blank page. She wrote:

*MARCUS — timeline*

And below that:

*Kennick arrested 11:40 a.m.*
*Confessed.*
*Motive: grudge, public humiliation, career ended.*

She stared at what she'd written. Then, in smaller letters below, she added:

*Problem 1: Kennick was fired 18 months ago. Tournament confrontation was 6 months ago. If revenge, why now?*
*Problem 2: Marcus was found near the equipment cage. Kennick hasn't had access to this facility since his firing
