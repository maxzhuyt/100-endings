# The Maintenance of Silence

## I.

The mop described its arc across the linoleum and the linoleum gave back a shine that lasted four minutes before the next pair of shoes came through. He knew this the way he knew most things now — not because he had measured it, but because his body had absorbed the interval and held it somewhere below thought, in the place where rhythms live.

The bucket rolled on its casters with a particular squeak at the left rear wheel. He had packed the axle with petroleum jelly in October and it had quieted for eleven days before returning, chastened, to its original complaint. He no longer tried to fix it. The squeak had become part of the night's architecture, as fixed as the ventilator sighing in 14-B or the soft digital chime — three ascending tones, then silence, then three again — of the IV pump in the room at the end of the east corridor where Mrs. Denny lay in her eighth week of dying.

Two-fourteen in the morning. He knew this without looking at a clock because he had just passed the juncture of the east and north corridors, and at two-fourteen he was always at the juncture of the east and north corridors. His route was not assigned. No one had drawn it on a map or typed it into a schedule. Over the years — how many now, he was not sure, the stroke had dissolved certain borders between befores and afters — the route had simply grooved itself into his legs the way water grooves limestone: imperceptibly, irreversibly, until the groove becomes the only possible path.

East corridor first, starting from the supply closet where the Pine-Sol lived in gallon jugs on the bottom shelf and the urinal cakes sat in their cellophane like small pucks of compressed sadness. Then north, past the nurses' station where Denise or Camille or the new one whose name he hadn't learned sat in the blue glow of their screens. Then west, the short arm, only four rooms, the ones with the windows that faced the parking lot and caught the orange wash of the sodium lights. Then south, back to the supply closet, and the route began again. Three circuits per shift. By the third, the first stretch had dried and was ready for the next night's first pass. The building breathed in this rhythm, and he breathed inside it.

The north corridor. Denise was on tonight. He could tell before he turned the corner because Denise kept the radio on, very low, tuned to the station that played songs from the seventies, and the faintest thread of a Carpenters song reached him as he wrung the mop — both hands, the twist that seated the strands in the wringer press, the satisfying weight of water leaving the gray ropes of cotton and falling back into the bucket. His left hand was weaker than it had been. The fingers curled when he wasn't watching them, drawing inward like a fern at dusk, and sometimes he would look down and find his hand had made a fist around nothing. He would open it deliberately, finger by finger, as if releasing a small animal, and set it back to work.

Denise did not look up as he passed. This was a kindness and he received it as one. In his first months back — after the hospital, after the rehabilitation center where a woman with patient eyes had tried to teach his mouth to form the shapes of words it had forgotten — the staff had watched him with the particular attention people give to the recently damaged, as if he might shatter further at any moment. He had hated it. Not the concern but the visibility. He had spent his working life — however many years it was now — in the profession of invisibility, and to be suddenly *seen*, to be the object of glances and lowered voices, had been worse than the silence in his throat. Gradually, they had stopped watching. He had become what he had always been: the man who pushed the mop, the man who emptied the sharps containers and replaced the trash bags and ran the floor buffer on Tuesday and Friday mornings after the day shift arrived. The man who was always there and never, exactly, present. He had accepted this gratefully. It was a kind of freedom, being furniture.

Room 9, Mr. Cahill. The door stood open four inches — always four inches, because Mr. Cahill's son had asked the nurses to leave it cracked so his father wouldn't feel closed in, and four inches was the distance at which the door caught on the small ridge in the threshold tile, the ridge where the grout had humped upward and that he had reported to maintenance in writing — a note, carefully printed with his right hand, left on the facilities manager's desk — three times without result. Through the gap: the sound of Mr. Cahill's breathing. A wet, effortful sound, the air pulling through fluid like water through a clogged drain. The pulse oximeter's steady tone, a metronomic beep at seventy-two beats per minute. The oxygen concentrator's low hum in the bass register beneath the beep. He stood at the door and listened. This was not in his duties. He did it anyway, had always done it, would have been unable to explain why even if he could still speak. He listened the way a shepherd might count a flock — not from concern for any single animal but from a need to know that the total number remained what it should be.

Mr. Cahill breathed. The machines confirmed it. He moved on.

The west corridor at two-thirty-one. Room 17, Mrs. Alvarez: even breathing, no machines, she was one of the ones who had refused all of it, and the staff honored this with what he sensed was a mixture of respect and discomfort. Room 18, empty since last Tuesday when the man whose name he had never learned — a quiet man, former electrician, the family had left a hard hat on the windowsill and then forgotten to take it when they collected the other things — had died during the day shift while he was home in his apartment not-sleeping. Room 19, Mr. Kim: the BiPAP machine's rhythmic push-pull, a sound like a slow mechanical bellows, and beneath it the almost inaudible whisper of the man's actual breathing, following the machine's lead the way a tired dancer follows a partner. Room 20, Mrs. Okafor: television on, volume barely audible, the blue flicker visible through the half-open door. She did not sleep. In six weeks he had never found her sleeping. She lay propped on pillows and watched the television with eyes that seemed to be focused on something several feet behind the screen. Once, early on, she had seen him in the doorway and spoken to him — something gentle, something he understood as greeting though the particular words hadn't stayed — and he had raised his hand in a wave that he immediately felt was inadequate, a child's gesture from a man in his fifties, and had moved on with heat in his face.

South corridor. Supply closet. He emptied the bucket into the utility sink, watching the gray water spiral. He refilled it, added Pine-Sol — the smell was so deeply embedded in his experience of nighttime that he sometimes caught a trace of it at noon in his apartment and felt, for a dislocated instant, that he was at work. He wrung the mop. He began again.

At three-fifteen, he entered the break room to refill his thermos. Coffee, black, from the pot Denise kept going all night. The room was small and bright, the fluorescent tube above the table buzzing at a frequency that had been bothering him since September, a high thin whine that no one else seemed to hear, or perhaps they heard it and had simply accepted it the way people accept most of the sounds that constitute their lives. The bulletin board by the microwave was layered with the sediment of institutional time: a flyer for a blood drive in March, a sign-up sheet for holiday shifts, a photocopy of a cartoon about Mondays that had been there so long its edges had curled inward.

There was a new sheet of paper pinned at the center, printed on the facility's letterhead. He stood with his thermos in one hand and read it the way he read everything now — slowly, with a care that was not comprehension difficulty but a kind of reverence for the written word that had deepened since he'd lost the spoken one. The memo was addressed to ALL STAFF and signed by the facility director. It announced the arrival of an *Assistive Autonomous Caretaker Unit* as part of a pilot program. The unit would perform nighttime patient monitoring and comfort tasks. Staff were to treat the unit as they would any new team member and to direct questions or concerns to the day-shift supervisor. The unit would begin its orientation on Monday.

He read the word *autonomous* and his eyes stayed on it for a long time. The fluorescent tube buzzed. The coffee in his thermos grew incrementally cooler. He did not know what the word meant in this context — what kind of machine could be trusted alone with people who were dying — but he felt its weight the way he felt changes in barometric pressure: not as information but as a shift in the body, a subtle tightening behind the sternum.

He finished his coffee route, returned to the hallway, and completed his second circuit. The building settled around him like an animal lowering itself to rest. Ventilators sighed. IV pumps chimed their patient trinities of tone. Somewhere on the east corridor, a woman coughed once, then was quiet. He mopped the south corridor for the third and final time, watching the linoleum accept its temporary shine, and felt, as he always did in the last hour before dawn, that the building and he had reached an understanding so deep it did not require language, had never required language, was in fact purer for the absence of it.

Then the day shift came and the lights brightened and voices filled the corridors and he put his supplies away and signed out with his shaking hand and went home to his apartment where he sat by the window and watched the street below and waited to come back.

## II.

On Monday he arrived to find it already there.

He came through the service entrance at ten-forty, twenty minutes before his shift, as he always did — the extra time belonging to the ritual of preparation, the changing into his work shirt in the supply closet, the assembly of his cart, the filling of the bucket, the small adjustments that tuned his body to the building's nighttime key. He pushed through the double doors to the east corridor and saw it standing at the far end of the hall, motionless outside the door to Room 11, and he stopped.

It was roughly human in height and proportion but the proportions were approximate in a way that opened a small trapdoor in the stomach. The shoulders slightly too even. The stance recalling standing without fully being standing — the weight distributed with an evenness that no human skeleton, with its asymmetries and compensations, could achieve. It wore a uniform that matched the nursing staff's scrubs, pale blue, and this seemed to him the most unsettling detail: someone had decided it should look as though it belonged.

He stood at his end of the corridor — seventy feet of freshly buffed linoleum between them — and watched. The android did not move. It faced the door of Room 11, where Mr. Cahill lay with his wet breathing and his four-inch gap, and it seemed to be *listening*, though he could not have said what feature of its posture communicated this. It had no visible ears. Its head, slightly tilted, reminded him of something — a dog attending to a sound beyond human frequency — and this comparison troubled him in a way he could not locate.

He moved. The bucket's caster squeaked. The android did not turn, did not register his approach. He pushed past it — giving it the berth he might give a piece of medical equipment left in the corridor, something to be navigated around — and he smelled nothing. No machine oil, no ozone, no synthetic polymer scent. It occupied space the way a hole occupies a wall: through an absence that drew attention precisely by offering nothing to attend to.

He cleaned the east corridor. The android remained at Room 11's door for seventeen minutes — he knew because he glanced at the hall clock each time he turned at the end of his stretch — and then it moved. He did not see it begin to move. He looked up from his mop and it was in a different place, as if the intervening motion had been edited out. It was now inside Room 12, visible through the open door, standing at the bedside of Mrs. Renner. The room's overhead light was off. The android stood in the glow of the monitoring equipment — green and amber numerals floating in the dark — and its hands were doing something near the pillow. He watched from the corridor. The hands adjusted the pillow's angle by what appeared to be less than an inch, a micro-calibration, and then the hands withdrew and hung at the android's sides with that same unnatural equilibrium, and seven seconds later Mrs. Renner stirred, shifted her head on the newly adjusted pillow, and settled deeper into sleep.

He replayed the sequence. The pillow had been adjusted *before* the patient moved.

He did not think this in words. He registered it as wrongness — a glitch in the expected order, like a door swinging open before the hand touches it. His mop paused mid-stroke. Water pooled against the baseboard.

The next night. Two-oh-seven. He was on his first circuit, north corridor, when he saw the android emerge from Room 6 carrying a small plastic basin. It crossed the corridor to the supply alcove, emptied the basin in the sink, rinsed it, and returned to Room 6. He continued his mopping and four minutes later heard, from behind the door the android had entered, the sound of Mrs. Cole vomiting. Denise appeared from the nurses' station, moving quickly, and went into the room. She came out looking mildly puzzled. "Basin was already right there," she said to no one in particular, perhaps to the empty corridor, perhaps to him. He had been close enough to hear. She shook her head with the expression of someone deciding a small mystery is not worth pursuing and returned to her station.

The basin had been placed before the need. Not during. Before.

He began watching with an attentiveness that reorganized his cleaning route into something it had never been: a surveillance pattern. He still mopped, still emptied wastebaskets and replaced trash bags and wiped down handrails with disinfectant. But within the frame of these tasks, he built a secondary architecture of observation. He learned the android's circuit: it moved counterclockwise, beginning on the east corridor and ending on the south, the mirror image of his own route, so that they passed each other twice per cycle like gears turning in opposite directions. He learned its pace: slower than his own, with longer stations at each room, the pauses outside doorways extending sometimes to twenty minutes before the android entered or moved on. He learned that it hummed — a low, toneless vibration, not a melody but a frequency, as if something inside it was resonating at a pitch just above silence.

And he learned that it acted, always, in advance of need.

A patient's blanket was straightened moments before restless legs kicked it askew. A water cup was refilled and repositioned seconds before a hand groped for it in the dark. One night the android stopped in the corridor between rooms and stood perfectly still for three full minutes — he timed it by the clock above the fire extinguisher — and then walked directly to Room 9, where Mr. Cahill's oxygen saturation was about to drop to a level that would trigger the monitor's alarm. The android adjusted the oxygen flow. The alarm never sounded. Mr. Cahill breathed on.

The janitor stood at the intersection of the east and north corridors with his mop handle against his breastbone like a staff. If he could have spoken, he might have framed a question: *How does it know.* But he could not speak, and the question existed not as language but as a sensation — a door opening onto a room he had not known was part of the house, a room with no light switch and no windows, and from inside it, air moving.

He finished his third circuit that night and found reasons not to leave. He re-mopped the west corridor. He cleaned the break room sink, which did not need cleaning. He reorganized the supply closet shelves by height. He was still in the building at five-forty when the day shift began arriving, and Camille, passing him in the corridor, gave him a look that was not quite concern and not quite suspicion but something between the two.

He put away his mop. He signed out. His hand, writing the time in the logbook, shook worse than usual.
