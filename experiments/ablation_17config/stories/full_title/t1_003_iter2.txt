# The Collectors

## I.

The new toaster had to go between the 1987 Olympus Trip and the shoebox of foreign coins, obviously, because it was the same brushed chrome as the camera body and the same width as the shoebox and it completed a diagonal of silver-toned objects that ran from the doorframe to the blocked-off window like a vein of ore in rock. Meg could see it. She could always see it. The taxonomy was invisible to anyone else—her mother, the one time she'd forced her way in, had stood in the entrance and said *oh, Meg* in a voice like a door closing—but to Meg the living room was as legible as a library, every object catalogued by color, material, weight, era, and the private fourth axis she had never been able to name to a therapist but which she thought of as *emotional specific gravity*. The toaster was heavy. Not in pounds. In the other way.

She turned it in her hands. The chrome caught a bar of light that had fought its way through the gap between a stack of National Geographics (1974–1981, excluding November '77, which was filed in the bathroom) and a broken humidifier she'd been meaning to fix for three years. The light made my reflection stretch across the toaster's curved side, and she looked warped and enormous and almost beautiful, and she set it down in its place and felt the click.

Not an audible click. A click in the architecture.

She exhaled. Her shoulders dropped. The five- to seven-second window opened—the window her therapist called "the parasympathetic release" and Meg called "the only time I feel like a person"—and in that window the doorbell rang.

No one rang her doorbell.

On the front step: a cream envelope, hand-addressed in block capitals. No return address. No postmark. Hand-delivered.

Inside the envelope: a single photograph and a single sheet of paper.

The photograph showed four children standing at a lakeshore, holding shovels. She recognized the lake before she recognized the children. Lake Paugus. The pebbly shore with the red canoe rack. The tree line that looked like a jaw.

The sheet of paper said:

*Retrieve what you buried or watch your secrets destroy your adult lives. Camp Abenaki. Friday, 11 PM. The gate will be open. Come alone. Tell no one.*

*You have twenty years of silence to protect. So do the others.*

*You know which others.*

Meg sat down on the only open square foot of floor in the hallway, which she kept clear for exactly this purpose—sitting when the world suddenly required sitting—and stared at the four children in the photograph. She had not thought the word *Abenaki* in perhaps six years. She had not said it aloud in twelve. She had organized her entire adult life, her home, her beautiful and monstrous home, around the principle that if you found the right place for everything, nothing would ever come loose.

She looked at the photograph again. The four children, grinning. The shovels. The lake behind them flat as a plate.

Something had come loose.

The ChapStick was watermelon-flavored, which Dave didn't even like. That wasn't the point. The point was the moment—the three-quarter second between the shelf and the pocket, the slight flex of the middle and ring fingers closing around the tube, the seamless integration into the natural swing of his arm as he moved down the aisle toward dental care. A person standing six inches away would not have seen it. A camera would have to be reviewed frame by frame. Dave had been doing this for twenty years and had been caught exactly once, when he was nineteen, by a Walgreens security guard in Nashua who was himself a former pickpocket and who had recognized the technique with the wounded admiration of a retired athlete watching tape of his own sport.

Dave did not need the ChapStick. Dave did not need the batteries (AA, eight-pack, lifted from Home Depot on Tuesday), the carabiner clip (REI, Saturday), the birthday candles (Trader Joe's, impulse, poor form, beneath him but the release had been unusually intense). Dave had a salary. Dave was a project manager at a midsized logistics firm. Dave had a 401(k) and a dental plan and a girlfriend named Priya who thought his apartment was "minimalist" and who had no idea that the storage unit in Somerville, paid in cash, contained $40,000 worth of stolen objects organized in the same system as a stockroom because Dave's hands needed to take things the way other people's lungs needed to take air.

He dropped the ChapStick into his jacket pocket and rounded the corner to checkout, buying a bottle of ibuprofen he actually needed, paying with a card, making eye contact with the cashier, whose name tag said TERRENCE. "Have a good one, Terrence," Dave said, and Terrence said, "You too, man," and Dave walked out into a Tuesday evening that smelled like warm asphalt, and the relief was already fading by the time he reached his car, which was where he found the envelope.

On the windshield. Under the wiper.

He read the letter standing in the CVS parking lot with a stolen ChapStick in his pocket and a photograph in his hands and a feeling in his chest like a lock turning the wrong way.

Carrie ran the faucet first. Always the faucet first. The sound covered everything and also marked the boundary—on this side of the running water was the world where she was Dr. Caroline Voss, DPT, clinic director, the person who helped other people recover the function of their bodies, and on the other side was the world where she knelt on a bathmat and undid herself with two fingers and a mechanical efficiency that she had, over twenty years, refined to the point that the whole process—binge, pause, purge, rinse, brush, mouthwash, reapply lipstick—took eleven minutes. She had timed it. She had optimized it. She was, in her own ghastly way, an expert.

She did not enjoy any of it. That was what the therapists never understood when she'd seen them, before she stopped seeing them. They kept asking about the *pleasure* of the food, the *satisfaction* of the purge. There was no pleasure. There was a pressure that built in her chest over the course of hours or days, like a tire being inflated past its rating, and there was a sequence of actions that released the pressure, and there was afterward, which was eleven minutes of feeling like a cleaned-out room. Neutral. Blank. Habitable.

She flushed. She washed her hands. She brushed her teeth with a travel brush she kept in her purse. She reapplied lipstick—a specific shade of muted rose that she had once, in a moment of uncharacteristic self-awareness, realized she had chosen because it looked exactly the same before and after.

She opened the bathroom door and walked back into the restaurant where she was meeting her sister for dinner, and her phone buzzed, and it was not her sister but a text from an unknown number:

*Check your car. Driver's seat. The door was not locked.*

She almost didn't go. But she went.

The envelope was on the seat. Inside: the photograph, the letter. She held the photograph up to the dome light and looked at the four children and the shovels and the lake and felt the pressure begin to rebuild in her chest immediately, not the slow inflation of a normal day but a sudden violent compression, as if the tire had been connected to an air compressor, and she put the photograph in her purse and gripped the steering wheel and breathed and breathed and breathed.
