## PRIVATE AUDIO DIARY — LINDA BECKWITH, CSR-1187
## TAPE 01 / SIDE A
## SEPTEMBER 14, 1987 / 11:47 PM

---

This is Linda Beckwith. Certificate number 1187, State of Texas, certified shorthand reporter since 1971. Sixteen years, for those keeping count, and I am keeping count because no one else will.

Tomorrow morning I begin transcription duties on *State of Texas v. Eleanor Flood*, Case No. 87-CR-0234, Sutton County District Court, the Honorable Warren R. Ketchum presiding. Three counts of capital murder, one count of desecration of a corpse, one count of injury to a child, and — here is where it gets colorful — one count of engaging in organized criminal activity, which is how they're packaging what the Sonora paper has been calling, and I'm quoting from the August 19th edition, "ritualistic devil worship conducted upon the bodies of innocents."

Innocents. The children, they mean. Three foster children, ages four, six, and nine, placed in Eleanor Flood's care through the Texas Department of Human Services between March 1985 and January 1987. All three found dead in the home on February 2nd of this year. The medical examiner's report lists the cause of death for each as blunt force trauma, which is perfectly prosaic, but nobody in Sutton County is interested in prosaic, because the responding deputies also found, and I'm working from the evidence inventory here: seventeen candles arranged in a geometric pattern, several jars containing animal remains, books in what the inventory describes as "foreign and/or unknown languages," and symbols drawn on the walls and floor in a substance initially believed to be blood, later determined to be a commercial livestock paint available at the Sonora feed store for $4.50 a can.

Livestock paint.

I mention this because it is representative. Every element of this case that appears, at first contact, to be sinister has a second reading that is merely sad, or stupid, or both. The "foreign language" books turned out to include a Latin missal and two secondhand copies of Aleister Crowley that you could buy at any head shop in San Antonio. The "geometric pattern" of the candles matches no known occult configuration and is consistent with someone who lost power during the January ice storm and lit what she had. I have not yet reviewed the forensic evidence regarding the children's deaths, and I will reserve judgment, as I always do, until I have read every word entered into the record. But I have seen this before. McMartin. Jordan, Minnesota. Kern County. The machine gets going and it doesn't particularly care what it catches.

I requested this assignment. I'll note that for my own records. Marjorie Keene, who runs the reporter pool for the 51st District, called me on September 3rd and asked if I would take it. There had been some difficulty staffing. The original stenographer, a woman named Dorris Welch, was pulled off two weeks into pretrial motions. The second — I didn't catch the name and Marjorie didn't offer it again — lasted eight days. Marjorie described it as "personal reasons" in both cases, which is the administrative phrase for anything from a drinking problem to a death in the family. I didn't press. I said I would take it and I meant it before she finished the sentence.

I collect these cases. That is not exactly the right word. I *seek them out*. I have transcribed nine cases involving allegations of ritual abuse, occult practice, or satanic conspiracy since 1983, across four Texas jurisdictions. I have a filing system. I have cross-referenced the exposed hoaxes and the exposed prosecutorial misconduct and the exposed junk forensics, and the exposed everything — because they do get exposed, eventually, after the convictions, after the sentences, after the lives have been efficiently destroyed by the collaborative hallucination of adults who should know better.

None of this is morbid. I want to be clear about that on the tape. I am not one of those women who reads Ann Rule on the sofa with a glass of wine and a fascination she mistakes for empathy. I am a court reporter. My accuracy rate, verified by the Texas Court Reporters Association annual audit, has not dropped below 99.2% in eleven years. I am the record. I don't editorialize. I don't react. I sit and I type and the words pass through me and come out on paper exactly as they were spoken, and when the paper goes into the file and the file goes into the county clerk's office, what happened in that room is preserved — not interpreted, not dramatized, *preserved* — with a fidelity that no journalist, no author, no filmmaker will ever match.

Tomorrow I will sit six feet from a woman the State of Texas intends to execute, and I will write down every word anyone says about her, and I will do it perfectly, and I will not have an opinion until I have the complete record in front of me, at which point my opinion will be private and irrelevant, because the record is the thing. The record is the only thing.

That's all for tonight. Beckwith, CSR-1187. End of entry.

THE COURT: The State may proceed with its opening.

MR. HARGRAVE: Thank you, Your Honor.

Ladies and gentlemen of the jury, over the coming weeks you will hear testimony that will disturb you. I want to acknowledge that at the outset, because I believe you are owed honesty, and honesty compels me to tell you that what happened in that house on Poplar Street was not an accident, was not a tragedy in the way we usually mean that word, but was the deliberate, premeditated, ritually motivated destruction of three children who had no one in this world to protect them except the woman who killed them.

[Defendant motionless. Hands folded in lap. No visible response.]

I want to tell you their names. I want you to carry their names with you through every hour of this trial, because their names are the reason we are here.

Cody Allen Watts. Age nine.

Bethany Suzanne Hale. Age six.

Marcus James Delacroix. Age four.

[Defendant motionless. Eyes directed at defense table surface.]

These three children were placed by the State of Texas into the foster care of the defendant, Eleanor Anne Flood, over a period of approximately twenty-two months. During that time, the defendant maintained a household that the evidence will show was permeated — *permeated* — with the symbols and apparatus of occult ritual. You will see photographs. You will see the jars. You will see the writing on the walls. You will hear from experts in the field of ritual crime who will explain to you what these symbols mean, what they are used for, and what they require.

MR. DARBY: Objection, Your Honor. This is opening statement, not testimony. Counsel is characterizing evidence not yet admitted.

THE COURT: Sustained in part. Mr. Hargrave, confine yourself to what you expect the evidence to show.

MR. HARGRAVE: Yes, Your Honor. Thank you.

What the evidence will show, ladies and gentlemen, is this: on the night of February 1st, 1987, the defendant killed Cody Allen Watts, Bethany Suzanne Hale, and Marcus James Delacroix in the course of an organized ritual conducted in the basement of her home. The medical examiner will testify to the nature and extent of the injuries. The forensic evidence will establish the defendant's direct physical involvement. And the testimony of neighbors, social workers, and law enforcement will paint a picture of a woman who hid her practices behind a veneer of normalcy while, inside that house, she —

MR. DARBY: Objection. Inflammatory.

THE COURT: Overruled. Counsel may continue.

MR. HARGRAVE: — while, inside that house, she conducted acts that defy the ordinary language of criminal law and enter the territory of something we may not have a statute for, but which we must confront nonetheless. The State will prove its case beyond a reasonable doubt. That is all I ask you to hold in your minds. Proof. Not fear. Not rumor. Proof.

Thank you.

## TAPE 01 / SIDE B
## SEPTEMBER 15, 1987 / 9:38 PM

---

Beckwith. End of day one.

Courtroom observations. The jury is majority female, which will not help the defendant. Rural women in these cases tend to align with the prosecution early and hold. Two of the male jurors are ranchers. One fell asleep during the forensic pathologist's credentials voir dire this afternoon. Judge Ketchum runs a tight room — sustained the defense's objection on characterization, which suggests he's not entirely in Hargrave's pocket, though Hargrave is going to be a problem. He's good. He has the cadence. Small-town DA with a revival preacher's instinct for when to drop his voice. The jury watched his mouth when he said the children's names. That's bad for Eleanor Flood.

Darby, the defense attorney, is court-appointed and doing his best, which is not enough. He objects on procedure and loses on momentum. He'll get steamrolled in cross.

The defendant herself.

I have been doing this long enough to have a catalog. I have seen defendants who rage. Defendants who weep. Defendants who perform. Defendants who have been so thoroughly medicated by their counsel that they sit like dolls and blink at the fluorescent lights. Eleanor Flood is none of these. She sat with her hands folded and looked at the surface of the table in front of her as though she were reading something written there. Not dissociated. Not medicated. *Focused*, in a way I could not immediately explain and that I am noting here only because it was unusual and I note unusual things. That is my job.

She made her statement. It was poorly advised — Darby's face confirmed that — but she was coherent, articulate, and, to my ear, genuine, though my ear is not the instrument of record and I have been wrong before about genuineness.

The vocalization at the midpoint of her statement. I'm irritated with myself about this. I have a system for rendering nonstandard speech — phonetic brackets, IPA notation when necessary, cross-reference to the audio log when my system fails. My system failed. The utterance lasted approximately three seconds. I was watching her mouth. The sounds she produced did not correspond to any phonemic pattern I could parse in real time. It was not a stammer. It was not glossolalia in the standard sense — I have transcribed Pentecostal custody hearings; I know what glossolalia looks like on the page. This was — structured. Shaped. As though she began to say something in a language she knew and then lost the thread halfway through, except that isn't right either, because there was no moment of transition. She was speaking English and then she was not, and then she was again, and the "not" portion resisted my notation.

I have marked it as inaudible with a reference to the audio log. This is the correct professional response. I will review the audio tomorrow and make a second attempt at transcription. If I cannot render it, it stays as [inaudible], which is not a failure. An [inaudible] tag is an honest acknowledgment of the instrument's limitations, and I am the instrument, and every instrument has a threshold.

I will note, because I note everything: nobody in the courtroom reacted to the vocalization. Not the judge, not the jury, not Hargrave. Either they didn't hear it, which is unlikely given the room's acoustics, or they processed it as noise — a woman losing her composure under pressure, making sounds that don't signify. The court reporter is the only person in the room whose professional obligation is to attend to every sound a speaker makes, which means I am the only person who registered that there was something there to attend to.

This is not unusual. This is what I do. I hear things other people don't hear because I am paid to hear them.

That's all. End of entry.
