# The HOA Board Will Now Come to Order

---

## I.

**NOTICE OF VIOLATION**

**Meadow Creek Estates Homeowners' Association**
*Serving Our Community Since 1987*

**Date of Issuance:** March 14, 2024
**Property Address:** 412 Bridle Path Lane, Unit N/A (Single-Family Residential)
**Homeowner(s) of Record:** Meredith Y. Chen & David R. Chen

**Violation Type:** Unauthorized Structural Modification — Exterior
**Bylaw Reference:** §4.7.2(c): *"No structural addition, alteration, or modification to the exterior of any dwelling, including but not limited to porches, decks, ramps, awnings, trellises, or permanent fixtures, shall be initiated, constructed, or maintained without prior written approval from the Architectural Review Committee (ARC). All requests must be submitted on Form ARC-11(a), accompanied by scaled drawings and a materials list, no fewer than forty-five (45) days before the next scheduled quarterly meeting of the ARC."*

**Nature of Violation:** Construction of an unpermitted wooden access ramp affixed to the front entrance of the above-referenced property. Ramp measures approximately 14 feet in length and 3.5 feet in width, constructed of pressure-treated lumber (unfinished), with non-regulation handrails. No Form ARC-11(a) on file. No prior approval granted.

**Remediation Required:** Remove unauthorized structure OR submit retroactive application to ARC for consideration at next quarterly meeting (June 12, 2024). A fine of **$50.00 per day** will accrue beginning March 21, 2024 (seven days from date of issuance) until the violation is resolved.

**Questions?** Contact the HOA Management Office at your convenience. We're here to help!

*Doug Whitmore, President*
*Meadow Creek Estates HOA Board of Directors*

The notice was laminated.

That was the thing Meredith kept returning to, standing on the ramp in question with the notice in her hands and the March wind cutting through her cardigan. Someone — Doug himself, almost certainly, because he did not delegate — had fed this piece of paper into a lamination machine. Had waited for the machine to warm up, because they always took a minute. Had aligned the document carefully inside the plastic sleeve, because a crooked lamination would have been unprofessional. Had listened to the mechanical purr as the rollers pressed the heated film against the paper, sealing it, preserving it, making it weatherproof.

He had done all of this because it might rain before she found it, and he wanted the text to remain legible.

She held the notice at its edges. The lamination was good — no bubbles, no wrinkles, the seal flush to the margins. He had used the quality sleeves, the five-mil ones. She knew this because David had owned a laminator for years, kept it in his home office for preserving important documents, and she had bought the five-mil sleeves in bulk from an office supply catalog. She recognized the thickness.

Behind her, through the screen door, David was in his hospital bed in what had been the living room. The bed faced the television but also the front door, because he liked to see who came and went, liked the parallelogram of daylight that moved across the hardwood floor through the afternoon, liked to watch for the mail carrier, who sometimes waved. He may have been asleep. He may have been watching her read the notice. His wakefulness had become ambiguous in recent weeks — a state she had learned not to test, because testing it meant asking, and asking meant he had to speak, and speaking exhausted him in a way that was difficult to witness, the words forming and reforming in his mouth like something he was trying to pass without quite enough strength.

She folded the notice along its creases. The lamination resisted — it wanted to stay flat, to remain an open declaration — but she pressed firmly with her thumbnail and made the fold sharp and exact, the way she had folded actuarial tables for forty-one years at Midwestern Mutual: briskly, at the correct axis, with a crease you could cut yourself on. She folded it again, and again, until it was a small hard rectangle that fit in her palm, and she put it in the pocket of her cardigan, and she went inside.

"What was that?" David said. Or didn't say. He was asleep. Or he wasn't.

"Nothing," she said. "HOA business."

The ramp had been built in eleven days by Meredith's nephew, Kevin, who did handyman work and had come from Dayton on short notice and had slept in the guest room and had not charged her for labor, only materials. The pressure-treated lumber had cost $387.42. The handrails were not regulation, whatever regulation meant. They were smooth and sturdy and bolted properly and David could grip them with his left hand, which was still strong, while she guided the wheelchair with both of hers. The ramp had an appropriate slope — she had looked it up; she had always looked things up — and it did not extend into the sidewalk right-of-way, and it was not, by any metric available to her, a structure that needed to pass through a committee that met four times a year.

But it had not been approved. And so it would cost her fifty dollars every day until it was approved, or until she tore it down, or until David died and she no longer needed it.

She ran the math without trying to: $50 times 90 days until the June ARC meeting equaled $4,500. She would not remove the ramp to stop the bleeding. So $4,500, assuming the ARC approved it in June — which it might not, given that it might request modifications, or table the matter, or require a survey, or any of a dozen procedural detours she could already envision with the pattern-recognition instincts of someone who had spent four decades modeling the consequences of fine print.

She sat in the kitchen and unfolded the notice and read it again in the light above the table. She read it a third time. The language was careful. The citations were correct. She checked them against the bylaws booklet she kept in a drawer — everyone had received a copy when they moved in, twelve years ago, and she had actually read hers — and found that Doug had not made an error. §4.7.2(c) said exactly what Doug said it said.

On the counter, in a bread bag with a twist tie, sat a half-loaf of banana bread. Doug had dropped it off two weeks ago, when word got around about David's decline. "From the Whitmores," the note had said, in handwriting she recognized from twelve years of HOA correspondence. "Thinking of you both."

She looked at the banana bread for a long time. Then she opened the drawer again and pulled out the entire bylaws booklet — forty-seven pages, plastic comb-bound — and began to read from the beginning.

## II.

Later, people would tell Meredith that they had always known, that Doug Whitmore had always been this way, that his fifteen-year reign over the Meadow Creek Estates Homeowners' Association had been a long accumulation of petty tyrannies that everyone recognized but no one had the energy or the standing or the temperament to fight. They told her this the way people always tell you things after the fact: with a kind of rueful generosity, as though the information had been freely available all along and she simply hadn't asked.

She hadn't asked. She was asking now.

The Kowalski basketball hoop was the story everyone started with, because it was the funniest, and people preferred to start with the funny ones. Jason Kowalski had installed a portable basketball hoop at the foot of his driveway in 2016 so his two boys could shoot after school. The base was filled with sand, not water (he'd read that sand was more stable), and the hoop sat approximately forty-one inches from the street curb. The bylaw specified a minimum setback of forty-four inches from any public right-of-way. Doug had measured. Doug had brought a tape measure — a Stanley FATMAX, twenty-five-foot, which Meredith would later learn he kept in his car at all times — and had stood at the curb on a Tuesday afternoon and extended the yellow blade to the base of the hoop and had found the discrepancy: three inches. He had photographed the measurement. He had issued the notice the following day.

Jason Kowalski moved the hoop three inches. That was the end of that. It was funny. A man with a tape measure, standing at a curb, quantifying the distance between childhood recreation and regulatory compliance. People told the story with a headshake and a half-smile: *That's just Doug.*

The Gupta garden flag was funnier still. Ananya Gupta had purchased a decorative flag from a craft fair — hand-sewn, depicting a stylized sun over a field of wildflowers, the words "Welcome Friends" embroidered along the bottom. She hung it from a small L-bracket affixed to her porch column. Within ten days, she received a notice citing §6.1.4: *"Commercial signage, advertising materials, or promotional displays visible from any common area or public right-of-way are prohibited."* Doug's interpretation: the embroidered text constituted "promotional display" because it communicated a message to passersby. Ananya had laughed. Then she received the second notice, with the fine schedule. She removed the flag. When she told the story at block parties, she performed Doug's earnest, slightly nasal explanation — "I understand it's decorative, Ananya, I really do, but the language of the bylaws doesn't distinguish between commercial and personal messaging, and if we make an exception here, we open the door" — and everyone laughed. It was an excellent impression. It was always the highlight of the evening.

Then there was the Henderson fence. Mark Henderson had replaced three rotting pickets on his backyard fence with lumber from Home Depot that was, as it turned out, a quarter-inch wider than the original pickets. Doug did not notice this for seventeen months, which meant either that he had not been conducting backyard inspections with the regularity he maintained on the front-facing sides of properties, or that a quarter-inch is genuinely difficult to detect, or both. When he finally noticed — and later no one could determine what had drawn his attention; Doug claimed a "routine walkthrough," Mark claimed Doug had been peering over the fence with reading glasses on — an eleven-week correspondence ensued. Mark offered to replace the three pickets with dimensionally correct lumber. Doug said the patch would not match the patina of the aged surrounding wood and that the entire fence section should be replaced, per §4.3.1(a), which required "uniform appearance and materials" in boundary structures. Mark, who had just undergone a hip replacement and whose wife was eight months pregnant with their third child, hired a fencing company and wrote a check for $1,400 and did not speak to Doug again for two years. Doug dropped off banana bread the week the baby was born.

The banana bread was always there. Meredith heard about it from every neighbor she talked to, always mentioned in the same offhand way, always adjacent to the enforcement action but never directly acknowledged as part of it. Doug made it himself. He used his mother's recipe. He wrapped each loaf in aluminum foil and tied it with kitchen twine and attached a small card — handwritten, always — that said something warm and neighborly. "Welcome to Meadow Creek!" for new residents. "Hope you're feeling better!" for the ill. "Just because!" for no occasion at all, which may have been the most unsettling variant, because it implied that Doug Whitmore was thinking of you, had identified your address, had mixed the batter and greased the pan and preheated the oven to 350 degrees and waited the fifty-five minutes and tested it with a toothpick and cooled it on a rack and wrapped it in foil and tied it with twine and written a card and driven to your house and left it on your porch, all *just because*. The banana bread was good. This was universally conceded. It was moist and rich and had the right amount of brown sugar and just a hint of cinnamon, and people ate it, and thanked him. Of course they thanked him. He was a neighbor. He had brought bread.

Meredith catalogued these stories in a legal pad she kept by the phone in the kitchen. She wrote in the precise, tightly spaced handwriting of someone trained to fit maximal data into minimal space. Each entry received a date, a name, a bylaw citation, and an outcome. The outcomes were uniform: **complied** or **departed**. No third category. No one had ever attended a board meeting to contest a fine. No one had filed a formal appeal. No one had run for the board. The meetings themselves were, by all accounts, attended by Doug, by the two other board members (Sandra Ouellet, treasurer, who had held the position for nine years without apparent enthusiasm, and no one could remember the name of the third, which told Meredith everything she needed to know about the third), and by no one else.

She filled three pages before she reached the case of Tom Petersen.

Tom Petersen was seventy-four years old in 2019 when he repainted his shutters. He had lived on Bridle Path Lane for thirty-one years — longer than anyone in Meadow Creek except the Ogdens, who were original buyers and who kept to themselves in a way that had hardened, over three decades, into near-total invisibility. Tom's shutters had been Approved Sage (#7742) since 2006, when the paint palette was last updated. In the spring of 2019, he had gone to the hardware store and asked the kid at the paint counter for "that sage green from the HOA list." The kid looked it up. The kid mixed it. Tom painted the shutters over a weekend with a two-inch brush and a steady hand and the unhurried satisfaction of a man completing a task he understood.

The shade the kid mixed was Approved Fern (#7744).

Doug noticed within a week. He arrived on Tom's porch with his Stanley FATMAX (unnecessary, but part of his kit), a printed copy of the exterior paint palette, and a physical swatch of #7742. He held the swatch against Tom's shutters and pointed to the discrepancy. The difference between Approved Sage and Approved Fern was, to most human eyes, essentially invisible — a faintly cooler undertone in the Fern, a whisper of blue where the Sage ran warmer, a distinction meaningful only to paint manufacturers and people who held swatches at arm's length against shutters and squinted, which Doug was now doing.

Tom offered to repaint.

Doug said he would need to submit a paint approval request, because the original approval on file was for Sage and any change — even a correction — required ARC review.

Tom pointed out that this was not a change but a mistake, and that he wanted to go back to Sage, which was already approved.

Doug noted that the *current* shutters were Fern, which was not approved for Tom's address, and that any modification from the current unapproved state would still constitute a new alteration requiring ARC review, per §4.7.2(c). He was sorry. He truly was. He said "I'm sorry" three times during the conversation, and each time it sounded approximately like a man who was sorry.

He left a loaf of banana bread on the porch rail.

Tom submitted the request. The ARC met in June and noted that his form was missing a materials specification — he had listed "exterior latex" without a brand name. The application was tabled until September. In September, the committee requested a second coat specification and drying time guarantees, because a previous homeowner (unnamed) had experienced peeling after using a single-coat application. Tom resubmitted. The December meeting was canceled due to lack of quorum. The March 2020 meeting was canceled due to the pandemic. By July 2020, Tom had accrued $6,150 in fines for maintaining a non-approved exterior paint color.
