# Form 27-B: Application for Continued Existence

## I.

The error was discovered on a Tuesday—or what the *Corridor* designated as Tuesday, which was really just the arbitrary label given to the third rotation of the ship's artificial light cycle, which itself had been calibrated seventeen years ago to the memory of Tuesdays on a planet that no longer existed. So: a Tuesday. The most appropriate day for the end of everything to reveal itself, because if catastrophe has any sense of poetry at all, it arrives not on a Friday, when you might at least have the weekend to absorb it, but on the drabbest, most featureless day of the week, the day that exists only because calendars require a bridge between Monday's ambition and Wednesday's resignation.

Yuki Hayashi found it.

She was, it should be noted, not a scientist, not a leader, not a navigator of the interstellar vessel that carried the last 3,012 human beings through the bewildering dark. She was a records clerk. Had been a records clerk on Earth—at a firm in Osaka that manufactured industrial adhesives—and was a records clerk now, because the selection committee that had assembled the *Corridor*'s manifest in those frantic last months had recognized, correctly, that a civilization of 3,000 would still need someone to file things. Perhaps more than ever. When everything else is gone—the cities, the forests, the billions of dead, the vast and irreplaceable libraries of a species' accumulated memory—what remains is the paperwork. The paperwork is always the last to die.

Yuki's job, her specific and not-at-all-glamorous contribution to the survival of the human race, was to maintain the ship's copies of every form, petition, and application that humanity had submitted to the Auditors since the Processing. There were, by the morning of this particular Tuesday, 11,342 such documents. She had organized them by date, by category, by Auditor reference number, and—in a filing system of her own devising that she was privately quite proud of—by the emotional state of the person who had completed them, which she could generally determine from the pressure of the pen strokes. (Terrified people pressed hard. Exhausted people drifted right. Angry people left marks you could feel with your fingertips, like Braille written in fury.)

What Yuki found, cross-referencing the most recent quarterly submission against the Auditor Procedural Index—a document of 900,000 pages that she had been reading, in small daily increments, for the past four years—was this: every form humanity had filed since the Processing had been submitted under Jurisdictional Category 7-Gamma, which governed "Provisional Continuance of Recognized Civilizational Entities." Humanity was not a Recognized Civilizational Entity. Humanity had never been a Recognized Civilizational Entity. Humanity was classified, in the Auditors' system, as a "Pre-Archival Residual Population (Unincorporated)," which fell under Jurisdictional Category 12-Omega, a category that used entirely different forms, different filing schedules, different paper sizes, and—critically—a different definition of the word "existence."

Every form, filed with enormous care, with translations and retranslations, with seventeen years of accumulated institutional knowledge about Auditor preferences for margin width and ink color, had been going, essentially, nowhere. Not rejected. Not denied. Simply *unrouted*. Sitting in some dimensional pocket of the Auditors' system like letters addressed to a house that had never been built on a street that had never been named in a city that existed only in the confident handwriting of the sender.

Yuki sat at her desk for eleven minutes after confirming the error. She confirmed it again. She confirmed it a third time, because she was the kind of person who confirmed things a third time, and then she placed her pen parallel to the edge of her desk, aligning it with the precision of someone who understood that if the small things were in order, the large things might at least be *ashamed* into following suit.

She stood up. She walked to the cafeteria. She got breakfast.

It was, as previously noted, a Tuesday. The *Corridor*'s cafeteria served reconstituted eggs on Tuesdays, and had been doing so for seventeen years, and would presumably continue doing so until the eggs ran out or the species did, whichever came first. Yuki ate her eggs. Around her, 3,011 other human beings went about the business of being alive: eating, arguing, teaching children, repairing air ducts, composing music on instruments built from reclaimed materials, falling in and out of love with the desperate, compressed intensity of people who understood, on some level they did not often visit, that they were the last of everything.

Nobody knew yet. The eggs were fine. The lights hummed at the frequency that meant Tuesday.

Yuki finished her breakfast, returned her tray, and walked to the Administrative Suite, which was a closet-sized room on Deck 4 that smelled permanently of recycled air and the particular despair of government work conducted in insufficient square footage. She sat down. She pulled up the relevant files. She began making a list of every form that would need to be refiled, which was all of them, and every form that would need to be filed for the first time, which was an additional and staggering number that she could not yet calculate because the Category 12-Omega index alone ran to 40,000 pages and she had not previously had reason to read it.

She did not cry. She did not swear. She did not go to the captain or the council or broadcast the discovery over the ship's intercom. She made her list. The list was the thing she could do, and so she did it.

Three days later she brought the list to the weekly administrative meeting, which was attended by eleven people, nine of whom were actively doing other work on their tablets while pretending to listen. She presented her findings in the manner she presented all findings: clearly, precisely, without embellishment, in a tone that expected nothing from her audience and was rarely disappointed.

The room was quiet for four seconds.

"So we're—" began Henrik Olafsson, the head of Resource Allocation, who had the gift of starting sentences he could not finish.

"Unfiled," said Yuki. "Bureaucratically, we are unfiled. We have been unfiled since the Processing."

"But we've been submitting forms for seventeen—"

"Yes."

"And the Auditors never—"

"They would not," Yuki said. "Category 7-Gamma forms submitted by a Category 12-Omega entity are not rejected. They simply do not register. It would be like—" She paused, reaching for the comparison. "It would be like submitting a tax return to a veterinary clinic. The veterinary clinic does not refuse your tax return. Your tax return does not *exist* in the conceptual universe of the veterinary clinic."

Another silence. Someone's tablet made a small, embarrassing sound.

"I have prepared a preliminary list of the correct forms," Yuki said. "There are approximately nine hundred. Several require information we may not possess. A number of them reference sub-forms that reference further sub-forms in a branching structure I have not yet fully mapped. I believe the initial filing, if completed correctly, would take one person approximately eleven years."

"We might not have eleven years," said Henrik.

"No," said Yuki. "We might not."

She passed out copies of her list. For a long time, the only sound in the room was the whisper of pages turning, which is the sound of human beings confronting the infinite through the only technology they have ever truly mastered: paper, subdivided.

## II.

Ruth Yee heard about the forms crisis the way she heard about most things on the *Corridor*: from Benny Tran, who delivered hydroponic lettuce to her section on Deck 6 and who had never, in seventeen years of acquaintance, successfully conveyed a piece of information without first delivering a twelve-minute preamble about his knees.

"—and so they're saying the whole thing, every form, Ruth, every single one, it's like we've been mailing love letters to the void, and my knees, Ruth, I'm telling you, the cold on this deck—"

"Benny. Stop. Which forms?"

"All of them! The Auditor forms. Yuki Hayashi figured it out. Wrong category, wrong jurisdiction, wrong everything. Seventeen years of—"

But Ruth had already stopped listening, because her body had done something her mind had not yet authorized: it had gone still. Not calm. *Still*. The kind of stillness that descends on a person when they hear a sound from very far away that they recognize, that they have been trained for, that their muscles and nerves understand before their consciousness catches up.

Ruth Yee was fifty-seven years old. She had black hair that she cut herself, badly, every six weeks. She had hands that were unremarkable except for a callus on her right middle finger from thirty-one years of holding a pen at an angle that every ergonomic specialist she'd ever met had called "incorrect" and that she had continued to use because it was *hers* and it worked. Before the Processing, before the end of everything, before her name had appeared on the *Corridor*'s manifest under the bewildering designation "Administrative Specialist (General)," Ruth Yee had worked at the California Department of Motor Vehicles. Specifically: the Glendale branch. More specifically: Counter 7.

Counter 7 was the counter where they sent you when every other counter had failed you. Counter 7 was where you went when your paperwork was not merely incomplete but *conceptually flawed*—when you had brought the wrong documents, or the right documents in the wrong format, or the right documents in the right format but from the wrong jurisdiction, or no documents at all and a heartfelt conviction that the universe owed you a driver's license because you were a good person. Ruth had worked Counter 7 for nineteen years. She had seen every permutation of bureaucratic failure the human spirit could produce. She had explained, with what she considered saintly patience, to a man holding a fishing license and a Costco membership card that neither of these was, in fact, a birth certificate. She had guided a ninety-year-old woman through a license renewal that required seven separate forms, two notarized affidavits, and a vision test that the woman passed only because Ruth had quietly adjusted the lighting. She had, on her last day on Earth—though she hadn't known it was her last day on Earth, because who knows such things in advance?—helped a teenager get her learner's permit, and the teenager had cried, and Ruth had said, "It's just a form," and the teenager had said, "I know, but it means I'm *real* now," and Ruth had thought about that sentence for seventeen years in space and had never stopped thinking about it.

*I know, but it means I'm real now.*

Forms were not obstacles. Ruth had known this since her first week at Counter 7. Forms were a language. They were the way that institutions spoke, and institutions—however maddening, however sclerotic, however staffed by people who were just trying to get through the day and go home—were the structures that stood between a person and the void. You could hate the DMV. Everyone did; it was practically a constitutional right. But the DMV was the place where the state looked at you and said, *Yes, you exist. We have a record of you. You are in the system.* And to be in the system, whatever system, was to be *acknowledged*, which was as close to being *real* as any piece of paper could make you.

Ruth found Yuki Hayashi in the Administrative Suite on Deck 4 at 2300 hours. Yuki was still at her desk. The Category 12-Omega index was open on her screen, and she was reading it with the focused despair of a person who has just discovered that the mountain she must climb is not made of rock but of additional, smaller mountains, each of which is made of paperwork.

"I can help," said Ruth.

Yuki looked up. She knew Ruth the way everyone on a ship of 3,000 people knew everyone: vaguely, contextually, as a face in the cafeteria, a name on a deck manifest. "You're—Resource Recovery? Deck 6?"

"I sort recycling," Ruth said. "I used to work for the California Department of Motor Vehicles. Nineteen years. Counter 7."

Yuki stared at her.

"I know forms," Ruth said. "I know how they work. I know what they want. Give me the index."

She did not wait for authorization. There was no authorization process for this, because nobody had anticipated this, because the crisis existed in a gap between every existing protocol the way water finds the space between stones. Ruth sat down in the only other chair—a chair that had been salvaged from a school on Earth and still had a wad of seventeen-year-old gum on its underside, a small, revolting monument to human persistence—and she began to read.

She read for six hours. When she stopped, her eyes were burning and her neck had seized into a shape that would require two days and a heating pad to undo, and she understood the scope of the problem.

"It's not nine hundred forms," she said.

"No?"

"It's nine hundred forms if everything goes right on the first submission, which it won't. The Category 12-Omega framework has a recursive amendment structure. Every rejected filing generates a secondary form, the R-14, which must be filed before a corrected version of the original form can be resubmitted, and the R-14 itself has a forty-percent historical rejection rate—I'm extrapolating from the procedural notes—which generates *its own* secondary form, the R-14-Sub-7, and—"

"How many forms?" Yuki said.

Ruth looked at her list, which she had been compiling in the neat, slanted handwriting of a woman who had filled out forms for a living and who believed, with the fervor of the deeply competent, that legibility was a moral virtue.

"Optimistically? Four thousand. And that's assuming we can get a correct filing on Form 27-B within the first three attempts."

"What's Form 27-B?"

"Application for Continued Existence," Ruth said. "It's the big one. Everything else is scaffolding. You can't file for Provisional Species Status, or Resource Allocation, or Territorial Non-Encroachment, or anything
