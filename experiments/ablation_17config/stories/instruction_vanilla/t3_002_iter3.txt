# The Last Watch of the Mara Celeste

I want to tell you about the tea.

Not the storm, not the dying — everyone asks about the dying. The newspapers got that part close enough, the parts they could guess at. What they couldn't guess was the tea. How I brought it to her in the same chipped mug every four hours, the one with the faded painting of a heron on it that had been her grandfather's, and how she'd wrap both hands around it even when the galley was ninety degrees and the sweat ran down her jaw like she'd been rained on. She held that mug the way other women hold rosaries. Two hands, always. Thumbs overlapping.

The last cup I brought her went cold on the binnacle. She never touched it. That was Tuesday. I remember because on Tuesdays we played rummy.

---

My name is Dolores Ferreira. I cooked aboard the *Mara Celeste* for eleven years. Before that I cooked at a hotel in Ponta Delgada that smelled of mildew and lemon soap, and before that I cooked for my mother's boarding house, and before all of it I was a girl who burned rice and wept about it. Captain Wen hired me in Horta when her last cook jumped ship over a disagreement about overtime pay and the fact that he'd seen a rat the size of a terrier in the dry stores. She asked me two questions. Could I make bread at sea. Could I keep my mouth shut about things that weren't my business.

Yes and yes, I told her, and both were true, and I have kept that second promise for six years now, which is how long it has been since the *Mara Celeste* went down, and I am breaking it tonight only because Wen is dead and the bank is dead — the bank failed last April, did you know that? — and the insurance men are scattered to their separate miseries, and there is no one left to hurt with the telling.

Also I am old. Also I have been drinking. You'll forgive me.

---

The *Mara Celeste* was a coastal freighter, 200 feet, steel-hulled, built in 1954 at a yard in Setúbal by a man named Dao-Ming Wen, who was Captain Wen's grandfather. He had come to Portugal from Fujian province by a route I never fully understood — something about the war, something about a woman in Macau — and he built the ship with money he'd saved over thirty years of working other men's vessels. His name was riveted to a plate above the engine room door. DAO-MING WEN, OWNER AND MASTER. Wen — our Wen, the captain — used to touch that plate every morning when she made her rounds. Just her fingertips. A brushing. Like checking the forehead of a sleeping child for fever.

The ship was old. I won't pretend otherwise. The ship was old and the boiler was older and the hull had been patched so many times that Machado, our engineer, once said she was less a ship than a theory of a ship held together by stubbornness and red lead. But she ran. She made her routes. Funchal to Bermuda, Bermuda to the Azores, the Azores to anywhere that would take a small freighter with reasonable rates and a captain who didn't ask too many questions about cargo manifests. Legal work, mind you — I don't mean to suggest otherwise. But Wen was not a woman who needed to know what was in every crate. She needed to know the weight, the balance, the port of delivery. The rest was philosophy.

---

The letters started in March.

I know because Wen left one on the mess table and I read it while clearing dishes, which was a violation of my second promise, but the thing was lying open, face-up, like a wound. From the Banco do Atlântico. I didn't understand all of it — my Portuguese is better than my financial literacy — but I understood the numbers, and I understood the word *execução*, and I understood that the mortgage on the *Mara Celeste* was four payments in arrears, and that the bank had initiated proceedings.

I put the letter back exactly as I'd found it. When Wen came in for dinner, I made bacalhau à brás, which was her favorite, and I put extra olives in it, which was my favorite, and we ate together in silence, which was our favorite.

She didn't mention the letter. I didn't mention the letter. This was the language we spoke.

By June, the letters were coming weekly. By August, Wen had stopped opening them. I'd find them stuffed into the navigation desk drawer, envelopes unbroken, accumulating like sediment. She began making mistakes — small ones, nothing dangerous, but unlike her. Forgetting to log position. Leaving her coffee in the wheelhouse overnight so I'd find it in the morning growing a skin. Once I caught her standing at the stern rail at three in the morning, looking at the wake, and when she saw me she said, "Dolores, what's the Portuguese word for the feeling you get when you owe someone something you can never pay back?"

"Dívida," I said. Debt.

"No," she said. "The feeling. Not the fact."

I thought about it. "Vergonha," I said. Shame.

She shook her head and went to bed.

I've thought about that conversation many times since. I think the word she wanted was *saudade*, but not the kind they put on postcards. The kind that is a homesickness for a home that is being taken from you while you're still standing in it.

---

The repo men. Let me tell you about the repo men.

Wen got the formal notice in September. The bank had sold the debt to an outfit in Hamilton — two brothers named Garrett who specialized in maritime asset recovery, which is a polite way of saying they took people's ships. The notice informed Captain Wen that she was to deliver the *Mara Celeste* to the port of Hamilton, Bermuda, by October 15th, where the vessel would be seized, inventoried, and sold at auction.

I was there when she read it. This one she opened. She sat at the mess table with her reading glasses on — she was vain about the glasses, only wore them when she thought no one was watching, but she'd given up on vanity by then — and she read the letter twice, and then she folded it very carefully into a square and put it in her shirt pocket and said, "Dolores, I think I'd like eggs for breakfast tomorrow. Fried, not scrambled."

"You always have fried," I said.

"Then there's no reason to change," she said.

We were in Funchal at the time, taking on cargo — crates of wine, machine parts for a factory in Bermuda. The scheduled route would take us exactly where the Garrett brothers wanted us. Wen filed her departure papers, cleared port authority, and we left on the morning tide, October 9th. Seven days to Bermuda. Six days to the deadline.

On the second day out, she turned the ship south.

Machado noticed first. He came to me in the galley, smelling of diesel and confusion, and said, "Dolores, we're headed into the Sargasso."

"I don't navigate," I said. "I cook."

"The Sargasso," he repeated, as if I hadn't heard. "There's a system building. Tropical depression. Possibly a typhoon."

"Would you like some soup?" I said.

He looked at me for a long time. "She's going to lose the ship," he said.

"She's already lost the ship," I said. "The question is who she loses it to."

---

The crew was seven, not counting Wen. Myself. Machado. Two deckhands, Beto and Faria. A bosun named Guimarães who had sailed with Wen's grandfather and was so old his tattoos had blurred into blue suggestions. A first mate named Souza who was twenty-six and terrified of everything but did his work well. And a boy — I can't remember his name now, God forgive me, but he was seventeen, from São Miguel, and he'd signed on as an ordinary seaman because his father told him the sea would make him a man.

On the third day out, when the barometer started its long, sickening slide, Wen called them all to the mess and told them the truth. Not all of it — she didn't mention the bank, the letters, the Garrett brothers. She said the ship had been ordered to Bermuda for seizure. She said she intended to ride out the coming storm in open water rather than make port. She said anyone who wanted off could take the lifeboat and make for the shipping lanes, where they'd be picked up within hours.

"And you, Captain?" Souza asked. His voice only cracked a little.

"I'm staying with my ship," she said.

"That's suicide," Machado said.

"It's seamanship," she said, and I could hear her grandfather in her voice, though I'd never met the man. Some ghosts don't need to be present to speak.

---

They all left except Guimarães and me.

Guimarães stayed because he'd promised the old man Wen he'd look after the ship. I stayed because — well. I'll get to that. The rest took the port lifeboat, Souza nearly in tears, Machado furious, Beto and Faria and the boy from São Miguel whose name I cannot remember and for which I will have to answer someday. They launched in the grey afternoon swell and motored northeast and I watched them until the sea took them behind a wave and gave them back and took them again and then didn't give them back. They were picked up by a container ship eleven hours later. All safe. That part, at least, was mercy.

Which left three of us on a two-hundred-foot freighter heading into the mouth of Typhoon Lena with a hold full of port wine and machine parts, a boiler that Machado had called "optimistic," and a captain who hadn't smiled in six months.

---

I should tell you about the card game.

Every morning, without exception, since my second week aboard the *Mara Celeste*, Wen and I played rummy. Two hands, sometimes three. She kept score on a napkin. She cheated — nothing dramatic, just a tendency to shuffle in a way that favored her, which I noticed in my first year and never mentioned because it seemed to bring her a private satisfaction, the idea that she was getting away with something. Over eleven years, she was ahead by some 1,200 points, of which I estimate 400 were legitimate.

On the morning of October 12th — the first day of the typhoon — we played. The mess was already at a fifteen-degree list and I'd lashed the table legs to the floor bolts with cargo straps. My tea sloshed. Her tea sloshed. The cards kept sliding, and I had to hold my hand against my chest like a woman hiding a love letter. She dealt, cheated, won. The ship groaned.

"Gin," she said.

"That's not how you win in rummy," I said.

"It is on my ship," she said.

On the morning of October 13th, we played again. The list was twenty degrees. I'd stopped cooking — the galley stove had broken free of its gimbal mounts during the night and I'd found it wedged against the port bulkhead like a drunk leaning on a wall. I'd managed tea, nothing more. The cards were damp. Everything was damp. The ship spoke in a language of metal, a vocabulary of stress and torque and the particular sob that steel makes when it is being asked to do more than steel was meant to do.

She dealt. I picked up my hand. Three queens, a run of hearts.

"You didn't cheat," I said.

She looked at me, and for a moment something moved behind her eyes — surprise, maybe, or amusement, or the acknowledgment that eleven years of intimacy in a small space had made us transparent to each other in ways that neither of us had agreed to.

"I forgot," she said.

We played. She won anyway. The hull screamed.

On the morning of October 14th — but I'll get to that.

---

Typhoon Lena was not the worst storm in the Atlantic that year. The newspapers would later call it "moderate to severe," which is the kind of language used by people who experience weather through windows. At sea, in a vessel that was forty years past her prime, Typhoon Lena was the hand of something ancient and indifferent pressing down on us with the slow, inexorable patience of geology.

The first day was wind. I don't mean wind the way you know it — I mean the air itself became hostile, became a thing with weight and intention. The rigging sang, then screamed, then made sounds I'd never heard rigging make, sounds like animals in great distress. The waves were not waves but landscapes — dark, moving hills with valleys between them that the *Mara Celeste* slid into and climbed out of with the terrible slowness of a creature that knows it is being hunted.

Wen stayed at the wheel. She didn't lash herself — not yet. She stood, both hands on the spokes, her feet planted, her body absorbing the pitch and roll with a fluency that was almost unconscious. I brought tea. She drank it. I brought more tea. She drank that too.

"You should sleep," I told her.

"I'll sleep in Bermuda," she said, and then caught herself, because there would be no Bermuda, and the word hung in the air between us like a bell that's been struck and cannot be unrung.

Guimarães worked the pumps. The old man was seventy-three and had forearms like hawsers and he worked the manual pump in the engine room for six hours without stopping, and when I brought him water he drank it without pausing his rhythm, and when I brought him food he ate it with one hand while the other kept pumping, and when I said, "Guimarães, you'll kill yourself," he said, "Dona Dolores, I've been dying since 1962, and the sea has been too slow about it."

The second day was water.

The waves broke over the bow, over the gunwales, over everything. The forward hatch gave way at noon — I heard it go, a sound like a giant clearing his throat — and the sea began to fill the hold. The port wine, the machine parts. All of it. Somewhere below us, crates were being shattered by the surge, and for an hour the bilge water ran red, and I thought it was blood before I realized it was the wine, and then I thought: what a waste, and then I thought: what a stupid thought to have while dying.

Guimarães pumped.

The pumps failed at four in the afternoon. Not the manual one — Guimarães was still on that, God bless his furious old heart — but the electric pumps, which had been wheezing for hours, gave a final asthmatic cough and went silent. The water rose faster after that. You could feel it in the ship's movement, a new heaviness, a reluctance in the way she climbed each wave, as if she were considering whether to bother.

That was when Wen lashed herself to the wheel stand.

Not the mast — I said mast earlier, I think, or maybe I'll say it later; memory is not a straight line, it is a current that circles back on itself. She lashed herself to the wheel stand with a length of cargo rope, the good hemp, triple-knotted, and she did it with the calm efficiency of someone tying their shoes. I watched from the wheelhouse door. The rain was horizontal. Her hair was plastered to her skull and her shirt was plastered to her body and she looked like something carved from wet stone.

"Captain," I said.

She didn't turn. She was watching the sea ahead, reading it the way her grandfather must have taught her to read it, looking for the pattern in the chaos, the moment between waves when the ship could breathe.

"Captain, the pumps have failed."

"I heard."

"We're taking water faster than Guimarães can move it."

"I know."

"What do we do?"

She was quiet for a moment. A wave struck the port side and the ship heeled so far I grabbed the door frame with both hands and my feet left the deck. When we righted, she said, "Dolores, have you ever read the deed to this ship?"

"No."
