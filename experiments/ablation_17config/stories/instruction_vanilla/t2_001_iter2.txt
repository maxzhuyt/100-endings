# The Reykjavik Frequency Heist

## Part One: The Antenna

### 2019

The microphone is a Neumann U 87, which is ridiculous. They have brought a studio-grade condenser microphone into my kitchen in Seltjarnarnes to record the ramblings of an eighty-one-year-old man who hasn't been relevant since Nixon was sweating through his shirts. The young woman operating it—Katrín, she said, Katrín Helgadóttir, from RÚV—has taped the cable to my linoleum with blue painter's tape so I won't trip. This is thoughtful but also insulting.

"Whenever you're ready, Magnús."

I am not ready. I have not been ready for forty-eight years. But the others are dead now—Elísabet in 2003, Gunnar in 2011, poor Tomás only last winter—and Katrín has found the filing request at the National Archives, the one that came back stamped *LOCATION UNKNOWN*, and she is the kind of young woman who does not let stamped files stay stamped.

"You should understand," I say, "that I am going to tell you something true that sounds like a lie. And then I am going to tell you something that sounds true but is much stranger than any lie I could construct. I am an engineer. I don't have the imagination for good lies."

Katrín smiles. She thinks this is charming, the old man's preamble.

"Start with how you met Elísabet," she says.

I start with the antenna.

---

### 1971 — March

The antenna was wrong.

I knew this the way I knew most things that year: by listening. I was thirty-three, employed by the Icelandic Post and Telephone Administration, and my entire professional existence consisted of making sure signals went where they were supposed to go and didn't go where they weren't. I maintained relay equipment. I calibrated transmitters. I was, in Elísabet's later formulation, "a man whose highest ambition was that nothing interesting would ever happen to a radio wave."

She wasn't entirely wrong.

The anomaly appeared on March 7th, a Sunday. I was at the Eiðar relay station in the northeast, performing routine checks on the longwave transmitter array, when I logged a persistent signal at 14.7 kilohertz. This was not a frequency anyone should have been using. It sat below the Omega navigation band and above the range typically occupied by natural VLF emissions. It was strong—absurdly strong for something that wasn't in our broadcast registry—and it appeared to be coming from the interior, somewhere in the Mývatn-Krafla volcanic region.

I filed a report. The report went to my supervisor, Björn Sigurðsson, who filed it in a cabinet that I suspect also contained sandwich wrappers and yesterday's *Morgunblaðið*. I filed a second report. This one went to the same cabinet. I filed a third, and this time Björn told me to stop being a nuisance and go recalibrate the Vestmannaeyjar relay, which didn't need recalibrating.

So I did what any responsible engineer would do when confronted with bureaucratic indifference to a genuine electromagnetic anomaly. I called the University of Iceland's physics department and asked if anyone there was curious about a mystery signal from a volcano.

Elísabet Jónsdóttir called me back within six hours.

---

### 2019

"What was she like?" Katrín asks. She has done her research. She has a photograph, which she shows me: Elísabet at a conference in Copenhagen, 1968, dark hair pulled back severely, wearing a man's suit jacket over a turtleneck, cigarette in hand, caught mid-sentence with an expression that could be fury or delight. In Elísabet's case, these were often the same expression.

"She was the most unpleasant person I have ever admired," I say.

"That's a strange compliment."

"It is not a compliment. It is a topographical description. She was a landscape you had to navigate. Complimenting her would be like complimenting a glacier—it doesn't care, and you'll lose a toe regardless."

Katrín laughs. I don't.

"She had been denied tenure twice," I continue. "The physics department found her 'temperamentally unsuitable.' This was 1971's way of saying she was a woman who was smarter than her committee and wouldn't pretend otherwise. She was studying geomagnetic field variations at Krafla. She had her own monitoring equipment up there—purchased, I later learned, with money she embezzled from a National Science Foundation grant intended for seismological research in Vestfirðir. When I described the signal, she went quiet for exactly four seconds. This was the longest silence I ever observed from Elísabet Jónsdóttir."

"What did she say?"

"She said, 'I've been listening to that signal for nine days and it is not geological.' Then she said, 'Come to Akureyri. Bring a spectrum analyzer. Don't tell Björn.'"

---

### 1971 — March

I told Björn I was taking personal leave to visit my mother in Akureyri. This was a lie. My mother lived in Kópavogur and we hadn't spoken in two years.

I drove north with a Hewlett-Packard 8553B spectrum analyzer borrowed—stolen, technically, but temporarily—from the Eiðar station, wrapped in blankets in the back seat of my Saab 96 like a mechanical infant. The roads were terrible. They are always terrible in March. I arrived in Akureyri with cracked lips and a numb right foot and found Elísabet's address, which turned out to be a converted fish processing building on the harbor that smelled, appropriately, of death and the sea.

She opened the door before I knocked. She was shorter than I'd imagined—perhaps 160 centimeters—and she was wearing rubber fishing waders over corduroys.

"You're late," she said.

"I was never given a time."

"If you'd been the right kind of person, you would have driven faster and arrived at the right time. Come in. Don't touch anything."

The interior of the building had been transformed. Where fish had once been gutted and packed into salt, Elísabet had assembled what I can only describe as a shrine to electromagnetic obsession. Oscilloscopes lined one wall—at least four of them, models I didn't recognize, possibly East German. A chart recorder trailed paper across the concrete floor like a mechanical tongue. Cables ran everywhere, connected to equipment that ranged from military-grade to homemade, and at the center of it all stood a reel-to-reel tape machine—a Studer A80, which in 1971 cost more than my car, my apartment, and my annual salary combined.

"Where did you get a Studer?"

"I am persuasive," Elísabet said.

I later learned she had seduced and then blackmailed a Danish recording studio owner. But that was Elísabet.

"Listen," she said, and pressed play.

---

What came out of the Studer's monitors was not what I expected. I had assumed, based on the frequency characteristics, that we were dealing with some kind of VLF natural emission—a whistler, perhaps, or a chorus generated by the magnetosphere. These have a musical quality. They sound like the earth singing to itself.

This did not sound like singing.

It sounded like a voice.

Distorted, fragmented, buried under layers of noise—but unmistakably a human voice, speaking Icelandic.

"—verðið á olíu hefur hækkað um—"

I looked at Elísabet.

"The price of oil has risen by," she translated unnecessarily. "It repeats. Not exactly—the content shifts. But it's clearly a news broadcast of some kind. My first assumption was that we were picking up a reflected signal, perhaps from Rás 1 or Rás 2 bouncing off the ionosphere in some unusual skip pattern."

"That would explain it," I said, relieved.

"That would explain it if the content matched any broadcast that has ever been made."

"What do you mean?"

Elísabet pulled a notebook from a stack on her workbench. It was filled with her handwriting—cramped, furious, annotated in three colors of ink.

"I have been transcribing fragments for nine days. On March 3rd, at approximately 03:00, I captured a clear thirty-second window. A male voice—not any newsreader I recognize—discussing the eruption of a volcano on Heimaey."

"There is no volcano erupting on Heimaey."

"No," said Elísabet. "There is not." She fixed me with a look that contained no warmth but enormous intensity, like staring into a heating element. "Not yet."

---

### 2019

I pause here because Katrín is giving me the look I expected—the look anyone gives when you say the word they're afraid you're going to say.

"I'm not saying precognition," I tell her. "I'm not saying time travel. I'm not saying anything. I'm telling you what we heard. The signal contained news broadcasts that did not correspond to any broadcast ever made in Iceland up to that date. Some of the content was unintelligible. Some was fragmentary. And some was very, very clear and described events that had not yet occurred."

"And the Heimaey eruption—"

"January 23, 1973. Eldfell. It happened almost two years after we recorded that fragment. Every detail matched. The evacuation, the lava threatening the harbor. Everything."

Katrín is quiet for a moment. She is recalculating what kind of story this is.

"Why haven't you told anyone before now?"

"Because we destroyed the tapes. And because telling this story without the tapes makes me sound like a man who needs his grandchildren to manage his finances. But the others are dead, and the filing request you found—that means someone in the government still knows. Which means the story exists whether I tell it or not. I would prefer my version."

"Why now?"

"Because I'm eighty-one and my version has an expiration date."

---

### 1971 — April

Elísabet wanted more. Of course Elísabet wanted more. A thirty-second window of discernible audio was, to her, a personal insult from the universe—a glimpse through a keyhole when she wanted to kick down the door.

The problem was technical. The signal at 14.7 kHz was powerful but erratic. It would emerge from the background noise for minutes at a time, then submerge again for hours. The content was layered under severe multipath distortion, as though it had bounced off a hundred surfaces before reaching us. And our equipment, cobbled together from academic leftovers and Elísabet's questionable procurement methods, was inadequate for the kind of precision capture we needed.

"We need a proper receiving array," Elísabet announced one evening, standing over a map of the Krafla region she had pinned to the wall with fishing hooks. "Multiple antennas in a phased configuration. Wide baseline. And we need them close to the source."

"The source is inside a volcanic system."

"Near the source."

"How near?"

"Seven kilometers."

I stared at her. "You want to build a phased antenna array in the highlands, in April, seven kilometers from an active geothermal area."

"I want to build it in March, but you drove too slowly."

This was when Elísabet introduced me to the rest of the team—a word I use loosely, as it implies a level of organization and mutual respect that never existed among us.

There was Gunnar Einarsson, a fisherman from Dalvík who had spent twenty years maintaining and repairing the radio equipment on North Atlantic trawlers and who understood more about practical RF engineering than anyone with a university degree. He was sixty, barrel-chested, missing two fingers on his left hand from a winch accident, and he never spoke when a grunt would do. Elísabet had recruited him by walking into his boat repair shop, setting a bottle of Brennivín on his workbench, and explaining, in technical terms he later admitted he only half understood, that she needed someone who could make electronics work in conditions that would kill electronics.

"She talks like a storm," Gunnar told me during our first meeting, which took place in a bar in Akureyri called Sjallinn. "You can't argue with a storm. You just lash everything down and wait."

Then there was Tomás Pétursson, a graduate student of Elísabet's—her only graduate student, the department having quietly discouraged others from working with her. Tomás was twenty-four, thin as a fencepost, and had the permanent expression of a man who has just been told something that contradicts everything he previously believed. This was because Elísabet told him such things approximately every forty minutes. He was brilliant with mathematics. He could not change a tire.

And finally, improbably, there was Ólafur Briem, who was not a scientist or an engineer or a fisherman but a poet. Ólafur was Elísabet's ex-husband—or one of them; I never established the definitive count—and he had attached himself to the project in a capacity that was never clearly defined. He drove the second car. He made coffee. He wrote, in a small leather notebook, observations that he later published as a poetry collection called *Tíðindi frá Morgundegi* (*News from Tomorrow*), which sold eleven copies and was described by one reviewer as "either visionary or evidence of a psychotic break."

I liked Ólafur. He was the only person in the group more confused than I was.

---

The equipment question plagued us for two weeks. To build the receiving array Elísabet envisioned, we needed wire—kilometers of it—and insulators, and at least one signal amplifier capable of operating at VLF ranges without introducing excessive noise. We needed all of this in a country that, in 1971, did not have an electronics surplus store on every corner.

Gunnar solved it.

"Trawler antennas," he said one morning, producing a hand-drawn schematic on the back of a shipping manifest. "The deep-sea fleet uses trailing wire antennas for long-range communication. Hundreds of meters of insulated copper. I know where six boats are laid up for refit in Siglufjörður. The captains owe me favors."

"We can't just take antenna wire from fishing boats," I said.

"Why not?"

"Because they need it. For fishing."

Gunnar gave me a look of profound pity. "Magnús. The boats are in dry dock. The fish are in the ocean. The wire is doing nothing."

"It's still—"

"Elísabet," Gunnar said, turning to her. "Tell your engineer to stop being an engineer and start being a criminal."

"Magnús," said Elísabet, without looking up from her calculations, "stop being an engineer and start being a criminal."

This became a recurring theme.

---

### 2019

"You stole the wire," Katrín says.

"We *borrowed* the wire," I say, and then: "No. We stole it. Gunnar drove to Siglufjörður with Tomás and they stripped eight hundred meters of trailing antenna wire from three trawlers in a single night. They also took two signal preamplifiers, a marine-band receiver, and, for reasons Gunnar never explained, a fire extinguisher."

"Wasn't anyone concerned about this? Legally?"

I consider how to explain 1971 in the northeast of Iceland to a woman born, probably, around 1990. "Katrín, in Siglufjörður in 1971, the legal system consisted of one sheriff who was also the postmaster and whose primary law enforcement activity was confiscating unlicensed dogs. We were not operating in a high-security environment."

"But later—"

"Later, yes. Later, people became very concerned. But in April of 1971, we were five idiots in a fish factory, planning to haul stolen equipment into the highlands in May when the roads opened, and the only person who thought we were doing anything significant was Elísabet."

"Did you believe her? About the signal?"

This is the question I've spent nearly five decades trying to answer honestly.

"I believed the signal was real," I say. "I had measured it. It was there. I believed it contained voice transmissions in Icelandic. I had heard them. What I did not believe—what I *could not* believe, as an engineer, as a rational person—was Elísabet's hypothesis about what the signal was."
