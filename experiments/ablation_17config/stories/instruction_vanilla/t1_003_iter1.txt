# The Collectors

## Part One: What We Keep

The letter arrived at Margot Huang's apartment in Cleveland on a Tuesday in October, which meant it took the postal carrier eleven minutes of navigating the hallway canyon—newspapers stacked waist-high on both sides, plastic bins of unmatched Tupperware lids forming a secondary wall, a complete set of 1994 World Book encyclopedias acting as a retaining structure—just to reach the kitchen table where Margot ate her meals standing up, because three of the four chairs held categorized collections of hotel shampoo bottles and the fourth supported seventeen years of unfiled tax documents.

She found the envelope pressed under her door, plain white, no return address, her name typed in a font that looked like it had been chosen to be anonymous and achieved something closer to sinister. She opened it with a butter knife she kept in the hallway for exactly this purpose, because you never know when you'll need a butter knife and the kitchen was forty-seven steps away through a passage that required turning sideways.

*Dear Margot,*

*You buried something at Camp Abenaki in the summer of 2004. I watched you do it. I have proof. You have until 6:00 AM on October 19th to come retrieve it, or I'll send everything I know to the people you've spent twenty years hiding from.*

*Arrive at sundown. Tell no one. The gate will be open.*

*You know what's at stake.*

Margot read the letter twice, set it on the kitchen table, and then—because she could not help herself, because the compulsion was a tide that had long since overwhelmed the levees of her rational mind—she opened the junk drawer, removed a gallon-sized Ziploc bag, placed the letter inside, sealed it, labeled it with today's date using a Sharpie she kept on a lanyard around her neck, and filed it in the plastic bin she'd designated for "Correspondence: Threatening/Ambiguous."

It was the only item in the bin. She'd bought the bin six years ago, just in case.

---

Leo Grady received his letter in Portland, Oregon, at the front desk of the boutique hotel where he worked as assistant manager—a job that required him to wear a name tag and sport coat, both of which he'd stolen. The name tag from a conference in Boise. The sport coat from a Nordstrom Rack, slipped under his own coat during a lunch break two Decembers ago, the security tag removed with a neodymium magnet he kept in his glove compartment for exactly such occasions.

He was not poor. That was the thing people never understood.

Leo earned $58,000 a year, owned a perfectly functional wardrobe purchased with legal tender, and could afford anything in the hotel gift shop that called to him with its small, pocketable voice. The stealing wasn't about acquisition. It was about the three-second window between the act and the exit—that breathless temporal crack in which the entire world narrowed to a single point of risk, and every ambient noise became a judgment, and his heart worked so hard it felt like proof of living. Three seconds of being completely, annihilatingly present.

Then it was over, and he was holding a travel-sized hand lotion or a coworker's pen or a room key to a suite he'd never enter, and the shame came in like weather.

He read the letter in the break room, and his hand—independent of his mind, moving with the autonomy of a thing that had long ago seceded from his executive function—slipped the pepper shaker from the table into his pocket.

---

Darcy Okafor-Bell opened her letter at her kitchen island in Scottsdale, Arizona, where she was in the middle of what her therapist called an "episode" and what Darcy called a Tuesday. The counter held the evidence: four bags of Trader Joe's chocolate-covered pretzels (empty), a family-sized container of hummus (empty), an entire rotisserie chicken reduced to a gleaming skeleton, and a sleeve of Ritz crackers that had simply ceased to exist.

She was working on a two-liter of ginger ale when she noticed the envelope among the circulars and bills.

She read it. She read it again. She set it down carefully, as though it might detonate, and then she walked to the bathroom and threw up, which she'd been planning to do anyway, but which now felt less like compulsion and more like commentary.

When she'd rinsed her mouth and washed her face and stared at her reflection long enough to confirm she was still a person—forty pounds heavier than the girl in the photographs from that summer, though her husband told her she was beautiful every morning with a sincerity that made her want to scream—she called the only phone number she'd kept from that era of her life.

"Jin," she said. "Did you get one too?"

---

Jun-seo "Jin" Park received his letter in Atlantic City, New Jersey, which was either an extremely fitting or extremely unfitting place for a compulsive gambler to reside, depending on your theory of proximity. Jin's theory was inoculation—that living inside the beast would teach him to stop feeding it. This had proven about as effective as treating alcoholism by sleeping in a distillery.

He was $140,000 in debt, a figure he knew with the precision of someone who calculated odds and probabilities the way other people breathed. His marriage had ended. His CPA license had been suspended. He now worked as a bookkeeper for a chain of dry cleaners, a job chosen specifically for its soul-crushing tedium, which was supposed to function as a form of aversion therapy against the electric thrill of risk.

It had not functioned.

When Darcy called, he was sitting in his studio apartment, staring at the letter and calculating the probability that this was a bluff.

"I got it," he said.

"So it's real."

"The letter is real. What it's threatening might not be."

"Jin, he says he has *proof*."

"Proof is a strong word. Evidence is a spectrum." He paused. "Have you called the others?"

"I don't have their numbers. I haven't spoken to Margot since 2011. Leo since—God, since the thing in Portland."

"There was a thing in Portland?"

"I tried to visit him. He stole my earrings while I was in the bathroom. Not because he wanted them. Because—"

"Because he's Leo."

Silence opened between them, vast and familiar, the specific silence of people who shared a vocabulary they'd spent two decades trying to unlearn.

"Are you going?" Jin asked.

"We have to."

Jin stared at the letter. His mind, against his explicit instructions, began calculating the odds. The risk-to-reward ratio. The expected value of compliance versus defiance. The numbers lit up behind his eyes like a slot machine settling into alignment.

"Yeah," he said. "We have to."

## Part Two: The Property

Camp Abenaki had been abandoned for sixteen years, which was twelve years longer than the statute of limitations for what they'd done and exactly as long as it took for the Pocono Mountains to begin reclaiming human structures with the patient, vegetative aggression of an ecosystem that held grudges. The main lodge had a tree growing through its porch. The cabins along the lake had achieved a collective lean, like drunks supporting each other at a bar. The dock reached into the water and stopped, its far end having surrendered to rot sometime during the Obama administration.

Margot arrived first, at 5:47 PM, thirteen minutes before sundown, because she had always been early and because the alternative was to sit in her apartment calculating whether she should bring supplies and, if so, how many, and whether seventeen protein bars constituted reasonable preparedness or the opening movement of a hoarding symphony.

She'd brought thirty-one.

She stood at the gate—open, as promised—and stared at the property and felt the years collapse like a telescope being pushed shut. The smell was the same. Pine sap and lake water and something underneath, some mineral exhalation from the earth itself that she'd never identified but had never forgotten.

She'd also brought, in the trunk of her car: a first-aid kit, a second first-aid kit in case the first was insufficient, a change of clothes, a second change of clothes in case the first got wet, a headlamp, a backup headlamp, four varieties of battery, a tarp, fifty feet of paracord, a Swiss Army knife, and a gallon Ziploc bag containing the letter, now triple-bagged for preservation.

She had not brought the courage to throw any of it away. She never could. Discarding an object—any object, from a cracked mug to a bread twist tie—produced in Margot a grief response so immediate and physiological that her therapist had once hooked her up to a biofeedback monitor during a session and watched her heart rate spike to 160 BPM when asked to put a used tissue in the wastebasket. The tissue had been her own. She'd used it thirty seconds earlier. It didn't matter. Releasing it was a small death, and Margot had been dying in increments since the summer of 2004, and she was so tired of dying that she'd simply stopped.

She kept everything now. Everything stayed.

A Honda Civic pulled up behind her Subaru at 5:53 PM. Leo got out. He was taller than she remembered, or maybe she was shorter, or maybe twenty years had simply rearranged the spatial grammar of their bodies until they no longer parsed each other correctly. He wore a sport coat she was eighty percent sure had been stolen, and he had the look of a man who'd been handsome in his twenties and had commuted that handsomeness into something more weathered and specific in his forties—the face of someone who'd been in rooms he shouldn't have been in.

"Margot," he said.

"Leo."

They stood four feet apart. The distance felt political.

"You look—" he started.

"Don't."

"I was going to say you look like you've been preparing for this for twenty years."

She glanced back at her open trunk. "Only ten. The first ten I was in denial."

Something moved across his face that might have been a smile. Then a silver Range Rover crunched up the gravel drive—Darcy, because of course Darcy drove a Range Rover; Darcy had married a periodontist in Scottsdale and lived in a house with a pool and a succulent garden and a bathroom floor she knew more intimately than anyone should know a bathroom floor—and behind the Range Rover, a Kia that had seen several wars, piloted by Jin, who parked at an angle that suggested either casualness or a tremor in his hands.

Four doors. Four people. Four versions of the children who'd stood in this exact spot the night everything went wrong.

Darcy looked at Margot. Margot looked at Leo. Leo looked at Jin. Jin looked at all of them at once, because Jin had always been the one who watched, who calculated, who saw the whole board.

"So," Jin said. "Who wants to talk about it?"

Nobody wanted to talk about it.

"Great," said Darcy. "Let's go find what we buried so I can get back to my life."

"Which part of your life?" Leo asked, not unkindly.

"The part where I don't have to look at any of you."

She walked through the gate. The others followed, because they always had, because Darcy had been the brave one once, before bravery got rerouted through her esophagus and became something else entirely.

The main lodge was unlocked. This was the first wrong thing.

The second wrong thing was the lights.

Camp Abenaki had been disconnected from the grid in 2011—Margot had researched this; Margot had researched everything about this property on a quarterly basis for two decades, saving every article and county record in a labeled file box in her bedroom closet. But the lodge was lit, not with the overhead fluorescents she remembered from childhood but with something warmer and more deliberate, a theatrical amber that made the interior look like a stage set.

Which, they would soon discover, was exactly what it was.

The great room of the lodge had been transformed. Where there had once been folding tables and a Ping-Pong setup and a wall of cubbies for the campers' mail, there was now a kind of foyer—and beyond it, clearly visible through a series of doorways that had been cut into what used to be the back wall, a corridor stretching into the darkness of what must have been additional structures built or annexed onto the original building.

A television sat on a table in the center of the foyer, its screen blue and waiting.

"Nope," said Darcy.

"Hold on," said Jin.

"This is a horror movie. This is literally the first act of a horror movie. We should leave."

"We can't leave. He has—"

"I know what he has, Jin."

"Then you know we can't leave."
