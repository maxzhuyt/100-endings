# The Hateful Immortals
## A Documentary History of the Greenbriar-Chen Marriage (1847–2024)

### By Dr. Amelia Thornton, PhD
#### Harvard University Press, 2024

---

## FOREWORD

I first encountered Margaret Greenbriar and James Chen the way most people do: through the viral TikTok video. The one where they're arguing in a Portland farmers market about whether James did or did not promise to learn French before they moved to Paris. Margaret is insisting, with a frightening specificity that I initially attributed to hyperbole, that the promise was made on "Tuesday, the fourteenth of March, 1882, at approximately ten minutes past four in the afternoon, while you were eating an underripe pear on the settee and getting juice on the antimacassar, which, by the way, you never apologized for either."

James, for his part, is holding a bag of organic kale and staring into the middle distance with the haunted, thousand-yard gaze of a man who has, quite literally, heard it all before.

The video has 43 million views. The comments are equally divided between "divorce him queen" and "this man is my spirit animal." Neither camp understands the full scope of what they are witnessing.

I am a historian. I hold an endowed chair at Harvard. I have spent my career studying what we now call "the Emergent"—those individuals who, since at least the dawn of recorded history, have manifested extraordinary abilities at the onset of puberty. My previous books have examined the Emergent's role in the French Revolution, the likely Emergent identity of several Roman emperors, and the troubling history of government surveillance programs targeting superpowered individuals during the Cold War.

None of that work prepared me for Margaret and James.

What follows is the product of three years of research, fourteen months of interviews, access to Margaret's personal journals (she has kept one every day since 1855, though she insists this is "purely for James's benefit, since he doesn't believe me when I tell him what he said, even though I am constitutionally incapable of misremembering"), and James's somewhat less organized collection of memorabilia, which he keeps in a series of storage units across four countries.

It is a love story. It is also, depending on your perspective, a horror story. Most of all, it is a story about the single most terrifying sentence in the English language:

*Till death do us part.*

—Dr. Amelia Thornton
Cambridge, Massachusetts
January 2024

## PART ONE: THE MEETING (1847–1852)

### Chapter 1: Boston

Margaret Greenbriar was born on September 3, 1833, in Boston, Massachusetts, the fourth daughter of Reverend Elias Greenbriar, a Congregationalist minister of moderate reputation and immoderate opinions, and his wife Prudence, née Alcott, who was a distant cousin of the Concord Alcotts but, as Margaret would later note, "not so distant that my mother ever let anyone forget it."

James Chen was born on June 17, 1831, in the same city, the son of Chen Wei-Lin, a merchant who had emigrated from Guangzhou in 1822 and built a modest but successful trade business on the waterfront, and Mary Sullivan Chen, an Irish immigrant whose marriage to Wei-Lin had scandalized approximately everyone available to be scandalized, which in 1829 Boston was a considerable number of people.

"My parents' marriage was the great outrage of their social circle," James told me during one of our interviews, leaning back in a chair in the cluttered living room of their current Portland apartment. "A Chinese man and an Irish woman. People crossed the street to avoid them. My mother said God had a sense of humor. My father said God had nothing to do with it, that he'd simply married the only woman in Boston more stubborn than he was."

He paused, and glanced toward the kitchen, where Margaret was aggressively doing something to a piece of salmon.

"Apparently stubbornness is what I find attractive," he said. "This is a design flaw."

From the kitchen: "I heard that."

"I know you did. You hear everything. You *remember* everything. That is the central problem."

"The *central* problem," Margaret called out, "is that you called my mother a pestilent harridan."

"That was in *1897*."

"It was 1898. February. The ninth. A Wednesday. It was snowing. You were wearing the brown waistcoat I mended for you, which, given what you were about to say about my mother, I wish I had burned instead."

James looked at me. He had the expression of a man who has fallen into a pit of his own digging and, upon hitting the bottom, discovered that the pit is infinitely deep.

"You see what I'm dealing with," he said.

---

The details of Margaret's and James's early lives are, thanks to Margaret's ability, preserved with an almost painful fidelity.

Margaret's Emergence occurred in the spring of 1846, when she was twelve. She described the onset to me with characteristic precision:

"It was April 7th. A Thursday. I was in my father's study, and he was quizzing me on scripture. Deuteronomy. He asked me to recite Chapter 8, and I opened my mouth, and the entire chapter came out—perfectly—but so did the memory of learning it. Not just the words, Dr. Thornton. The room. The light. The dust motes in the air above my father's desk, which was walnut, with a scratch on the left corner where my sister Harriet had dropped a candlestick on the night of December 2nd, 1841. The smell of the ink. The scratchiness of my collar.

"And then I realized I could remember everything. Not just scripture. Everything. Every conversation I had ever had. Every face I had ever seen. Every slight, every kindness, every boring sermon my father had ever inflicted upon his congregation—and Dr. Thornton, my father was a man who could make the Book of Revelation feel bureaucratic."

She paused, stirring her tea with a small silver spoon that, she informed me, she had purchased in London in 1923 from a shop on Piccadilly that no longer exists.

"People think perfect memory is a gift," she said. "People are idiots."

James's Emergence came a year earlier, in 1845, though he didn't fully understand it until somewhat later. He had always been durable—childhood falls that should have broken bones didn't, fevers that ravaged other children left him untouched. But the defining moment came on an August afternoon when he was fourteen and fell from the mast of one of his father's cargo ships directly onto the dock, a distance of approximately forty feet.

"I landed on my back," James said. "On solid wood. Several longshoremen saw it happen. One of them fainted. Another started praying. I just... got up. I had a headache for about ten minutes. That was it."

He shrugged.

"I didn't fully understand it then. I thought I was just lucky. It took a few more incidents—a bar fight in 1849 where a man hit me in the head with a bottle and the bottle broke, a factory accident in 1850 where a beam fell on me and I walked away—before I started to realize that 'lucky' wasn't quite the word."

"The word," Margaret added, appearing in the doorway with a plate of excessively well-prepared salmon, "is 'unkillable.' He is literally unkillable. I have sometimes found this very frustrating."

"She's joking," James said.

Margaret set the plate down in front of him. The silence that followed was not entirely reassuring.

---

They met on October 3, 1847, at a lecture on phrenology at the Boston Athenaeum. The lecture was, by Margaret's account, "preposterously wrong about everything, but well-attended, because Bostonians in 1847 would attend the public reading of a grocery list if it were held in a sufficiently prestigious room."

James was there because his mother had heard that a young woman of good family would be attending and had not-so-subtly suggested that James might benefit from what she called "social improvement" and what James called "mother's endless campaign to marry me off to someone who would remind her less of herself."

Margaret remembers the following about the first time she saw James Chen:

"He was standing near the back of the hall, wearing a dark coat that didn't fit him particularly well—the sleeves were slightly too long, approximately three-quarters of an inch—and he was trying very hard to look interested in what Dr. Felton was saying about the relative sizes of cranial compartments, which, of course, was all nonsense, but James didn't know that yet. He had dark eyes and he was frowning in a way that I found either very thoughtful or very confused. They looked the same on him. They still do."

James's recollection, filtered through 177 years of merely human memory, is somewhat less detailed:

"She was pretty. She looked angry about phrenology. I found that interesting."

---

What followed was a courtship that was, by the standards of the era, wildly improper.

Margaret and James began meeting at lectures, then at bookshops, then on walks along the waterfront, then at a small tea house on Tremont Street that Margaret's family would never have approved of and that James's family would have found hilariously pretentious. They talked about science, philosophy, the growing abolitionist movement—Margaret's father was vocally opposed to slavery, which was one of the few subjects on which Margaret later conceded he was "not entirely wrong"—and, increasingly, about themselves.

"He told me about the fall from the mast," Margaret said. "About the bottle, and the beam. He was the first person who had ever spoken to me honestly about being... different. About being Emergent, though we did not use that word then. We didn't have a word for it. We were simply *strange*. And I was so desperately tired of being strange alone."

"She told me about her memory," James said. "About remembering everything. And I said, 'That must be extraordinary,' and she looked at me with this expression—this very Margaret expression—and said, 'It is like being buried alive in your own past.' And I thought: oh. I am in love with this person. This is going to be a problem."

"Why a problem?" I asked.

"Because I was a half-Chinese, half-Irish son of a merchant, and she was the daughter of a minister who would have described my parents' marriage as 'an affront to the natural order.' Because it was 1847 and Boston was Boston. Because—" He stopped. "Because I already suspected that I couldn't die. And I didn't know what that meant yet, but I knew it meant something terrible. You don't get to be unkillable and also lucky. The universe doesn't work that way."

Margaret, who had been reading in the other room and was—as always—listening, called out: "That is the most intelligent thing you've said in a decade. I want that noted for the record."

"Noted," I said.

---

The Reverend Elias Greenbriar's reaction to his daughter's courtship with James Chen has been preserved, with unfortunate precision, in Margaret's memory.

"He said—and this is verbatim, Dr. Thornton, because everything I say is verbatim—'Margaret, I would sooner see you married to the Devil himself than to the mongrel son of a Celestial and a papist washerwoman.' To which I replied, 'Father, Mrs. Chen is not a washerwoman; she is a bookkeeper.' To which he said, 'Do not be impertinent.' To which I said, 'I am not being impertinent; I am being accurate, which is more than I can say for your last four sermons on Leviticus.'"

She smiled at the memory—one of the few times during our interviews that Margaret smiled at anything prior to approximately 1920.

"I was not an easy daughter," she said. "But then, he was not an easy father."

The Reverend Greenbriar forbade the match. Margaret ignored him. On June 14, 1851, Margaret Greenbriar and James Chen were married in a small civil ceremony in Cambridge, witnessed by James's parents, Margaret's sister Harriet (the only family member who attended), and a justice of the peace named Arthur Hodges, who was, Margaret noted, "visibly uncomfortable with the entire proceeding but willing to overlook his discomfort for the fee."

"The vows were standard," Margaret said. "Love, honor, cherish. Till death do us part." She paused. "I remember every word. I always will. That's the thing about a vow when you have my ability. You can never pretend you didn't mean it."

## PART TWO: THE FIRST CENTURY (1852–1952)

### Chapter 2: The Discovery

The first fifty years of any marriage are the hardest—or so James once told a therapist in 1987, causing the therapist to put down her pen and stare at him for an uncharacteristically long time.

For Margaret and James, the particular challenges of their union became apparent almost immediately. The first major argument in their marriage occurred on March 15, 1852—Margaret can tell you that it was a Monday, that there was a light rain, and that James was wearing his second-best shirt because his best shirt was drying on the line in the kitchen of their small apartment on Beacon Hill.

The argument was about whether James had promised to write to Margaret's sister Harriet to thank her for the wedding gift she had sent (a set of embroidered handkerchiefs). James maintained that he had written the letter. Margaret maintained that he had not, because she remembered every single thing James had done in the intervening nine months, and at no point had any of those things included sitting down, picking up a pen, and writing to Harriet Greenbriar.

"This was the moment," James told me, "when I first understood what I had married into."

"You had married into common courtesy," Margaret said. "Harriet embroidered those handkerchiefs herself. They had our initials. She spent weeks on them. She was the only member of my family who acknowledged our marriage, and you couldn't be bothered to—"

"It was 1852!"

"The year is not a defense."

"I meant it was *172 years ago*."

"And yet here we are."

This argument—which Margaret refers to as "the Harriet handkerchief incident" and James refers to as "oh God, not this again"—has, by Margaret's careful accounting, been revisited in some form approximately 2,340 times across seventeen decades. It holds the record for their most frequently recurring argument, surpassing "James's refusal to learn French properly" (approximately 1,800 iterations) and "the 1898 harridan comment" (approximately 1,650 iterations, though Margaret notes that this one "carries more emotional weight per instance").

---

The unique hell of their situation did not become fully apparent until the 1870s. They had been married for twenty years. Margaret was, by all outward appearances, still a woman of about thirty-eight. James still looked thirty-five or so. This might not have been remarkable on its own—many people age slowly—but their neighbors on Beacon Hill had begun to notice.

"Mrs. Patterson, who lived in the apartment below ours, told her husband that I must be using 'Oriental creams,'" Margaret recalled. "She meant this as an insult to James. But she also meant it as a genuine attempt to explain why I looked the same as I had in 1858. People needed explanations. We were running out of them."

In 1874, they made their first relocation—a pattern that would define the next century and a half of their lives. They moved to New York, introducing themselves as the children of the Boston Chens, a convenient fiction that Margaret found "morally distasteful but practically necessary."

"This was when we realized," James said, "that we were going to have to do this forever. Move. Start over. Pretend. Every fifteen, twenty years—pack up, go somewhere new, become someone else. Because the alternative was people figuring out what we were, and in the 1870s, that would have meant... well. Nothing good."

It was not an idle fear. The treatment of Emergent individuals in the nineteenth century ranged from suspicion to outright violence.
