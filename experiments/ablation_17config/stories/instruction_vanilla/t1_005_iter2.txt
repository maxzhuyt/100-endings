# THE LAST FAST BREAK

## I. The Whistle

The yellow tape went up at 6:17 a.m., and by 6:43 the Eastview Thunderbolts had lost their equipment manager, their practice facility, and any remaining belief that the world made sense.

Ricky Molina was fifty-one years old. He had a beard he never trimmed evenly, a left knee that predicted rain better than any forecast, and a talent for remembering exactly how each player liked their chair adjusted—down to the quarter-inch of camber, the precise tire pressure, the tilt that meant the difference between a good shooting day and a bad one. He called every player "champ" regardless of whether they'd scored thirty points or ridden the bench for the entire fourth quarter. He had been found by the morning janitorial crew at the foot of the pull-out bleachers in Eastview Community Center's gymnasium B, his skull fractured, his body cooling on the hardwood he'd swept a thousand times.

By 9:15 a.m., police had arrested Dale Brennan, former head coach of the Westridge Raptors, who had been terminated eighteen months prior after Ricky Molina filed a formal complaint documenting Brennan's verbal abuse of a fourteen-year-old power forward who'd made the mistake of missing a free throw in regionals. Brennan had made threats. Brennan had been seen in the parking lot. Brennan, when brought in for questioning, had smiled his thin, hateful smile and said, "About time somebody stopped that man from ruining people's lives."

The detectives looked at each other and thought: *Easy.*

They were wrong about that. But they wouldn't know it for seventy-one more hours.

---

Maya Chen heard the news the way everyone hears the worst news of their life—through a screen, at the worst possible moment, when she had no defenses prepared.

She was in bed, her racing chair folded against the wall, her competition chair in the back of her mother's minivan where it always stayed during game weeks, and she was doing what she did every morning before her eyes fully opened: reviewing plays on her phone, running the Thunderbolts' offensive sets through her mind like music, the pick-and-roll with Terrence, the skip pass to Aaliyah in the corner, the patience required to cycle the ball until something broke open. The championship was seventy-two hours away. The Cedarville Cougars were beatable but not easily, and Maya had been dissecting their zone defense the way she'd once dissected mysteries at Camp Hawthorne, the junior detective program she'd attended for three consecutive summers, back when crime was still a game and the victims were made of paper.

Then the text from Aaliyah: *Ricky's dead. They found him in the gym. I can't stop shaking.*

Maya sat up in bed and felt the room rearrange itself around a new and terrible axis.

What she did next would later seem cold. She would know that, and she would do it anyway.

She texted back: *How?*

She did not text: *Oh my God.* She did not text: *Are you okay.* She did not cry, not yet, not because she didn't love Ricky Molina—she did, they all did—but because the part of her brain that had spent three summers learning to observe before reacting had already seized the wheel from the part that grieved. That grief would come. It would come hard and not leave for a long time. But not yet. Not yet.

*How* was the question that mattered.

And here is what Maya Chen knew and did not want anyone else to know:

Four days before his death, Ricky Molina had pulled her aside after practice, his face wearing an expression she'd never seen on it—pained, reluctant, as if the words in his mouth tasted of something foul. He'd said, "Champ, I found something. Your classification paperwork. The 2.5 you were assigned at evaluation—it doesn't match the assessment notes. Someone changed it. You should've been classified at 4.0."

In wheelchair basketball, every player on the court receives a functional classification from 1.0 to 4.5, reflecting the degree of physical function available for playing. The total classification points of the five players on the court cannot exceed 14.0 at any time. A player classified at 2.5 had significantly more restricted function than one at 4.0. Maya's incomplete spinal cord injury at T10 left her with substantial trunk control, strong hip flexion, and enough core stability that her assigned 2.5 had always felt—if she was being honest with herself in those 3 a.m. moments when honesty becomes unavoidable—generous.

At 2.5, Maya could be on the court alongside Terrence, who was classified at 4.0 due to a below-knee amputation that left him with nearly full physical function. At 4.0, she could not. The team's entire lineup mathematics would collapse. Every game they'd played that season, every win in their undefeated record, would be retroactively forfeit.

"Who changed it?" Maya had asked, and her voice hadn't trembled, because she'd already understood that this was the kind of question that didn't lead anywhere good.

"I don't know yet," Ricky had said. "But I'm going to find out, and then I'm going to make it right. I have to, champ. You understand that."

She had understood. And she had hated understanding.

Now Ricky was dead, and Maya Chen sat in her bed with her phone going dark in her hand and thought two things simultaneously. The first: *I need to find out who did this.* The second, smaller and uglier: *If nobody else finds out about the classification, the championship still happens.*

She was already ashamed of the second thought. She would carry that shame into every moment of what came next, and it would both sharpen and compromise everything she did.

## II. The Assembly

The Eastview Community Center had two gymnasiums, a weight room, a multipurpose space, and a parking lot that collected rain in a low corner every spring. The team assembled in the multipurpose space at noon because gym B was a crime scene and gym A was being used for a seniors' tai chi class. There were eight of them: five starters, three bench players, and no coach, because Coach Vaughn had called each of them individually that morning and said, in a voice none of them recognized, "Take the day. Take two. I need to—I'll be in touch."

Terrence Williams arrived first, as he always did, his broad hands resting on his push rims while he stared at the wall-mounted whiteboard where Ricky used to write inspirational quotes before every game. The last one was still there, in Ricky's blocky handwriting: *THE ONLY EASY DAY WAS YESTERDAY.* Terrence read it and felt something tighten behind his sternum that he knew would not loosen for a long time.

Terrence was six-foot-two in his chair, a former high school running back who'd lost his left leg below the knee to a motorcycle accident at sixteen and found wheelchair basketball the way some people find religion—desperately, and with total conversion. He was classified at 4.0, the highest-functioning player on the Thunderbolts' roster, and he played center with a ferocity that made opposing teams plan around him the way armies plan around geography.

He was also, among all of them, the one who had been physically closest to the last moments of Ricky Molina's life, and he had told no one.

On Tuesday evening—two nights before the body was found—Terrence had been in the community center's lobby waiting for his ride home after a solo shooting session. Through the glass doors of the administrative office, he had seen Ricky on the phone. Terrence had been deaf in his left ear since birth and profoundly hearing-impaired in his right since the accident, and he navigated the world through a combination of hearing aids, lip-reading, and a stubbornness that refused to let sound dictate his limitations. His hearing aids had been out—he always took them out after practice, when the world became too much—and so he'd watched Ricky's lips move in silence.

What Ricky's lips had said: *I know what you did, and I know how long you've been doing it. The classifications, the paperwork—all of it. You can come forward yourself or I go to the federation on Friday. That's it. That's the choice.*

And then, after a pause while the other person spoke: *I don't care about the championship. I care about these kids.*

And then: *No. I will not let you threaten me, Arthur.*

Arthur. Coach Arthur Vaughn.

Terrence had backed his chair away from the glass doors quietly, slowly, the way he did when he drove the lane and then pulled up, baiting the defense into overcommitting. He'd gone outside and waited in the cold and not said a word to anyone about what he'd seen, because Coach Vaughn had saved his life. Not metaphorically. After the accident, when Terrence was seventeen and studying the precise weight-to-lethality ratios of various over-the-counter medications, Coach Vaughn had appeared in his hospital room with a basketball and a wheelchair and a refusal to leave. Coach Vaughn had driven him to practices and to therapy and to the precipice of believing that the world still had a place for him.

You don't turn in the man who kept you alive. You don't. Even when you know what you know.

Terrence sat in the multipurpose room and stared at the whiteboard and felt the knowledge pressing against his teeth like a scream he couldn't afford to release.

Aaliyah Brooks arrived at 12:10, her eyes red, her phone clutched in her hand with the intensity of someone holding a grenade. She positioned her chair next to Terrence—they always sat next to each other, had since the first day of tryouts two years ago—and said nothing, because some silences are more honest than words.

Aaliyah was the shooting guard, classified at 3.0, a seventeen-year-old with spina bifida and a three-point shot so pure it made a sound like a hymn when it went through the net. She was quiet by nature but devastating in motion, the kind of player who disappeared on the court until the exact moment she materialized in the corner with her hands up, alone and lethal.

Her mother, Denise Brooks, was an administrative supervisor at St. Joseph's Medical Center. This fact had never been relevant to basketball or to anything else, until twelve minutes ago, when Aaliyah had called her mother and asked, very carefully, if there was any way to see whether Ricky Molina had been treated for anything recently. Any injuries, any complaints.

"Baby, you know I can't do that," Denise had said.

"Mom. Someone killed him."

"They arrested someone."

"Mom. *Please.*"

Denise Brooks loved her daughter with the particular fierceness of a woman who had fought insurance companies and school boards and the vague, insidious cruelty of a world not built for her child, and she had sighed the sigh of a woman about to cross a line.

"I'll see what I can see. Don't you dare tell anyone."

Aaliyah hadn't told anyone. But she had a thing she'd already found on her own—not in hospital records, which she hadn't seen yet, but in Ricky's social media, which she'd spent the morning scrolling through with the obsessive focus she usually reserved for film study.
