# The HOA Board Will Now Come to Order

## Part One: The Ramp

The ramp cost Meredith Chen four thousand two hundred dollars and took her nephew, who was not a contractor, three weekends to build. It was not beautiful. It listed slightly to the left, as if the wood itself were exhausted, and the handrails were the wrong shade of brown—"Rustic Walnut" instead of the community-approved "Heritage Chestnut"—because the Heritage Chestnut had been back-ordered until March and Richard did not have until March.

Richard Chen had ALS. He had been diagnosed fourteen months ago, and in those fourteen months he had lost the use of his legs, most of his right hand, and his ability to pronounce words that began with the letter "S," which he found darkly hilarious since his neurologist's name was Dr. Samantha Sutherland-Shah. He communicated now through a text-to-speech device that rendered everything in a flat Midwestern baritone that sounded nothing like him. The real Richard had a reedy tenor and laughed at his own jokes before he finished telling them, which Meredith had found annoying for the first twenty years of their marriage and essential for the subsequent twenty-two.

The letter from the Whispering Oaks Homeowners Association arrived on a Tuesday. Meredith recognized the envelope—that particular shade of bureaucratic cream, the logo with its swirling oak tree that looked, she had always thought, vaguely like a tornado eating a piece of broccoli. She assumed it was the quarterly newsletter, which was always eight pages of Doug Whitmore's editorializing about community spirit, drought-resistant landscaping, and the ongoing war against basketball hoops visible from the street.

It was not the newsletter.

*Dear Homeowner,*

*This letter is to inform you that a compliance review conducted on February 14th identified the following violation(s) at your property located at 1847 Whispering Oaks Lane:*

*VIOLATION #1: Unauthorized structural modification (wheelchair ramp, front entrance) installed without prior Architectural Review Committee approval, per CC&R Section 14.3(b).*

*VIOLATION #2: Non-compliant exterior paint/stain color on unauthorized structure, per CC&R Section 8.7(a) — "Heritage Chestnut" required; apparent color does not match approved palette.*

*You are hereby assessed a fine of $50.00 per day, beginning February 15th, until such time as the violation is corrected or a retroactive approval is obtained through the proper application process. Please note that the Architectural Review Committee meets quarterly, and the next scheduled review is May 12th.*

*We value you as a member of our community and appreciate your cooperation in maintaining the standards that make Whispering Oaks a wonderful place to live.*

*Warm regards,*
*Doug Whitmore*
*President, Whispering Oaks Homeowners Association*

Meredith read the letter twice. Then she sat down at the kitchen table across from Richard, who was having his morning coffee through a straw, and read it aloud to him.

Richard's device took a moment to process his input. Then the flat Midwestern voice said: "Fifty dollars a day until May. That is four thousand two hundred and fifty dollars for the crime of not dying on the front steps."

"I'm going to call him," Meredith said.

"You should burn his house down," Richard's device said, and there was a wheezing sound from Richard's chest that Meredith recognized as laughter.

She called Doug Whitmore that afternoon. She had known Doug for twelve years—he'd been HOA president for all of them, running unopposed each cycle with the serene confidence of a man who believed his calling in life was to regulate the distance between mailboxes and property lines. Doug was sixty-one, paunchy, bald in a way he'd stopped being self-conscious about, and broadly considered to be a nice man. He volunteered at the food bank. He organized the block party. He brought homemade banana bread to every HOA meeting, and it was genuinely excellent banana bread—moist, with just the right amount of walnut.

"Meredith!" His voice was warm, concerned, avuncular. "How's Richard doing? Janet and I have been thinking about you two."

"We got the letter, Doug."

A pause. She could hear him recalibrating—shifting from neighbor mode to president mode, a transition she imagined as something almost physical, like watching a friendly golden retriever suddenly remember it was supposed to be guarding the yard.

"Right, right. The ramp. Meredith, I want you to know, I understand the situation. I really do. Janet's mother was in a wheelchair for the last three years of her life, so this is close to our hearts. But we have a process, and we have to follow it. If we make exceptions for one homeowner—and believe me, I *want* to make an exception—then the whole system falls apart. You understand."

"Doug, my husband is dying."

"I know. And I'm so sorry. Truly. But the application process exists for a reason. Now, what I'd recommend is—"

"The next review is in May. You're asking me to either remove my dying husband's wheelchair ramp or pay fifty dollars a day for three months."

"I'm not asking anything, Meredith. The bylaws are asking. I'm just the messenger." He paused. "Tell you what—I'll personally walk your application through the committee. I'm sure we can get it approved at the May meeting, and then we can discuss whether the board might consider a partial fine abatement, given the circumstances. I can't promise anything, but I'll advocate for you. You know I will."

"Can you waive the fines until May?"

"I wish I could. But the CC&Rs are clear. Section 14.3(b) is pretty black and white on this. My hands are tied."

After she hung up, Meredith sat very still for a long time. She was sixty-three years old. She had spent forty-one years as an actuary for Pacific Mutual, which meant she had spent forty-one years staring into the mathematics of catastrophe—calculating the precise dollar value of a house fire, a flood, a man's death at forty versus sixty versus eighty. She understood systems. She understood that systems were built by people, operated by people, and could be dismantled by people. She understood that a man who describes himself as "just the messenger" is never just the messenger.

She opened her laptop. She navigated to the Whispering Oaks HOA website, which was hosted on a platform that appeared to have been cutting-edge in 2006, and she downloaded the CC&Rs, the bylaws, the standing rules, the minutes from every meeting for the last eight years, and the annual financial statements.

Then she began to read.

## Part Two: The Team

Meredith's book club met every other Thursday at rotating houses. There were seven members: Meredith; Dolores Huang, a retired immigration attorney with a voice like a disappointed foghorn; Patrice Washington, who had been an ER nurse for thirty years and had the unflappable calm of someone who had seen a man arrive with a claw hammer embedded in his skull and walk out three days later asking for the hospital Wi-Fi password; Barbara Kowalski, who was seventy-four and had the energy of a spite-powered nuclear reactor; Linda Okafor, who was the only member who actually read all the books; Connie Marsh, who was soft-spoken and made extraordinary lemon bars and was, beneath the lemon bars, the most strategically vicious person Meredith had ever met; and Faye Delgado, who was new and still slightly terrified of the rest of them.

They had been working through Hilary Mantel's *Wolf Hall* trilogy for seven months. They were still on the first book. This was because their "discussions" inevitably devolved into wide-ranging arguments about Tudor politics, the nature of power, and whether Thomas Cromwell was a visionary pragmatist or history's most competent sociopath, which Dolores insisted was a distinction without a difference.

At the next meeting—held at Barbara's house, which smelled of lavender and righteous indignation—Meredith told them about the letter.

The room went quiet. Not the polite quiet of sympathy, but the pressurized quiet of seven women doing rapid internal calculations about what kind of response this situation warranted.

Barbara spoke first. "I always knew Doug Whitmore was a son of a bitch."

"You told me last month his banana bread was 'transcendent,'" Linda said.

"A man can make transcendent banana bread and still be a son of a bitch. The two are not mutually exclusive. If anything, it makes it worse. He's hiding behind the banana bread."

"What are you going to do?" Connie asked, and the way she asked it—quiet, level, her hands folded on the table as if she were a general awaiting a field report—made it clear she was not asking whether Meredith *planned* to do something. She was asking for the specifics.

Meredith laid it out. She had spent three days with the bylaws—all forty-seven pages, including the eleven amendments ratified over the last two decades. She'd read them the way she used to read actuarial tables: not for the obvious information, but for the gaps, the inconsistencies, the places where the numbers didn't quite add up.

"Doug has been president for twelve years," she said. "In that time, the HOA has collected approximately $2.3 million in dues. The annual budget is published—landscaping, maintenance, insurance, the reserve fund. But I've been comparing the published budgets to the actual financial statements, and there are discrepancies."

"How big?" Dolores asked. Dolores, as a former attorney, perked up at the word "discrepancies" the way a retriever perks up at the word "walk."

"Over fourteen years—and I've gone back further than his presidency, using records from the county—approximately $147,000 is unaccounted for. Most of it from grounds maintenance and landscaping line items. The biggest single category is the mulch fund."

"The *mulch fund?*" Patrice said.

"Whispering Oaks spends—according to the budget—approximately $18,000 a year on mulch and ground cover for the common areas. I drove every common area last weekend with a measuring wheel. Based on square footage, material costs, and labor estimates from three different landscaping companies, actual mulch expenditure should be approximately $7,000 to $9,000 a year."

"He's been skimming off the mulch," Barbara said, with the grim satisfaction of a woman whose suspicions about banana bread had been vindicated.

"I can't prove that yet. What I can prove is that the numbers don't match, and that as president and *de facto* treasurer—since the actual treasurer, Ed Magnusson, has been a rubber stamp for over a decade—Doug has had sole discretionary authority over grounds maintenance contracts. He's been hiring his brother-in-law's company, Green Valley Landscapes, for all common area work since 2011."

Connie leaned back. "So we have financial malfeasance and a conflict of interest. What's the play?"

"The annual meeting is April 19th. Under the bylaws, any homeowner can introduce new business. A motion to audit the financial records requires a simple majority of the quorum. A motion to remove a board member requires a two-thirds vote of homeowners present, provided certain conditions are met under Section 22.4—specifically, evidence of 'fiduciary misconduct or breach of duty.' If we can get enough people to the meeting, present the evidence, and navigate the procedural requirements perfectly—and I mean *perfectly*, because Doug knows those bylaws as well as anyone—we can remove him."

"And the fines?" Linda asked.

"A new board could retroactively rescind the fines as part of a policy review. It's within their authority under Section 16.2."

Dolores was already pulling out a legal pad. "You'll need someone who knows parliamentary procedure cold. Doug will try to rule things out of order, call for adjournment, challenge the quorum—every trick in the book."

"I know," Meredith said. "I've already thought about that."

This is when she told them about Gerald Frisch.

Gerald Frisch was sixty-seven years old and lived in Unit 23-B of the Whispering Oaks townhome section. He was tall, rail-thin, owned fourteen different editions of *Robert's Rules of Order* (including a signed first-edition reprint that he kept in a humidity-controlled cabinet), and had once been ejected from a city council meeting for correctly but aggressively informing the mayor that his method of handling a subsidiary motion was "procedurally illiterate." He had competed in parliamentary procedure tournaments—yes, they existed—through the National Association of Parliamentarians, reaching the semi-finals in the 2019 national competition in Boise before being eliminated on a technicality involving the precedence of a motion to reconsider versus a motion to rescind, a ruling Gerald still considered, with the quiet intensity of an unhealed wound, to have been incorrect.

Gerald was also, in the parlance of the neighborhood, "a lot." He had filed more HOA complaints than any other resident, most of them against Doug Whitmore for procedural violations that no one else noticed or cared about—failure to distribute meeting minutes within the required fourteen-day window, improper use of voice votes when a counted vote was mandated by the standing rules, that sort of thing. Doug treated Gerald with the patient condescension of a kindergarten teacher dealing with a child who kept insisting that the class hamster deserved a formal election. The rest of the neighborhood mostly avoided him.

Meredith had never avoided him. She had always found him oddly endearing—a man whose entire emotional life had been channeled into the procedural architecture of democratic governance. She suspected, though she would never say so, that Gerald's obsession with rules was a way of imposing order on a life that had not provided much. He'd never married. His career—something in municipal water management—had been undistinguished. His apartment, the one time she'd visited to drop off a casserole during a bout of pneumonia, was immaculate and sparse, the walls decorated almost exclusively with framed parliamentary procedure flowcharts.

She rang his doorbell at 10 a.m. on a Saturday.

"Meredith," he said, surprised. He was wearing a cardigan and corduroy pants, which she suspected was his permanent outfit. "Is everything—is Richard—"

"Richard's the same. Gerald, I need your help."

She told him about the ramp. She told him about the fines. She told him about the financial discrepancies.

Gerald listened without interrupting, which, for Gerald, was an act of extraordinary self-control. When she finished, he removed his glasses, cleaned them with a cloth he kept in his cardigan pocket, replaced them, and said:

"Section 14.3(b) does require prior approval for structural modifications. However, the ADA compliance provisions in the federal Fair Housing Act would arguably preempt the CC&R in cases involving disability accommodation, which means the fine itself may be legally unenforceable. But that's a legal argument, and legal arguments take time. Months. Years, if he fights it."

"I don't have years. Richard doesn't have years."

Gerald nodded slowly. "You want to take him down at the annual meeting."

"I want to take him down at the annual meeting."

Something happened to Gerald's face that Meredith had never seen before. It was as if someone had turned on a light in a room that had been dark for decades. His eyes sharpened. His shoulders straightened. He looked, she thought, like a man who had been training his entire life for a fight that had finally arrived.

"You'll need a quorum," he said. "The bylaws require twenty percent of homeowners in good standing. That's—" He closed his eyes, calculating. "Whispering Oaks has 312 units. Twenty percent is 63 homeowners. Annual meetings usually get thirty, maybe thirty-five."

"I know."

"You'll need to nearly double attendance. Doug will challenge the quorum if he senses trouble. He'll challenge credentials. He'll try to adjourn before new business. Section 11.2 gives the chair the right to set the agenda order, and he'll bury new business at the end and then call for adjournment before you get there."

"I know."

"Under Robert's Rules, a motion to adjourn is not debatable. If he calls for adjournment and gets a second, it goes to an immediate vote. You need to be prepared to defeat that motion, which means you need bodies. Loyal bodies."
