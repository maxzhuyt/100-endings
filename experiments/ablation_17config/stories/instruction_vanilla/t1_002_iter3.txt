# THE SUTTON COUNTY TRANSCRIPTS

## Being the Compiled Personal Records, Annotated Documents, and Audio Diaries of Marion Graves, Court Stenographer, 53rd Judicial District Court, Sutton County, Texas

### Assembled Posthumously by the Sutton County Clerk's Office, 1989

---

## EDITOR'S NOTE

The following materials were found in the home of Marion Louise Graves on June 14, 1989, eighteen months to the day after the conclusion of the trial documented herein. Ms. Graves had not reported to work in eleven days. Her home was found unlocked. Her cat had been fed recently—the food in its dish was still moist—but no evidence of Ms. Graves herself was recovered, save for these documents, seven cassette tapes, and a pair of reading glasses on the kitchen table, one lens cracked cleanly in half.

The Sutton County Clerk's Office presents these materials without editorial judgment as to their contents.

—R. Halloway, County Clerk

## DOCUMENT 1

### Official Transcript (Excerpt)

**IN THE 53RD JUDICIAL DISTRICT COURT**
**SUTTON COUNTY, TEXAS**

**THE STATE OF TEXAS v. ELEANOR RUTH VANCE**

**Cause No. 87-0413-CR**

**VOLUME I — JURY SELECTION**
**September 8, 1987**

**Court Reporter: Marion L. Graves, CSR #4511**

---

THE COURT: We are on the record. This is Cause Number 87-0413-CR, the State of Texas versus Eleanor Ruth Vance. Ms. Vance is present in the courtroom with her attorney, Mr. David Farber. The State is represented by Assistant District Attorney Carolyn Pruitt. We are here this morning for the purpose of jury selection. Is the State ready?

MS. PRUITT: The State is ready, Your Honor.

THE COURT: Is the defense ready?

MR. FARBER: We are, Your Honor.

THE COURT: Very well. I'll ask the bailiff to bring in the first panel.

*(Panel of forty-two prospective jurors enters the courtroom.)*

THE COURT: Good morning, ladies and gentlemen. You have been called here today as prospective jurors in a criminal case. This case involves serious charges, and I want to be forthright with you about the nature of those charges so that we can determine whether any of you has a conflict that would prevent you from serving. The defendant, Eleanor Ruth Vance, has been charged with three counts of capital murder in connection with the deaths of Michael Whitfield, age nine; Danielle Whitfield, age seven; and Thomas Reyes, age four. These children were in the defendant's care as a licensed foster parent through the Department of Human Services. The State's theory of the case involves allegations of ritualistic abuse. I want to ask each of you—

THE DEFENDANT: They're wrong.

THE COURT: Ms. Vance, you will have your opportunity—

THE DEFENDANT: Not about me. About what's happening *now*. You brought forty-two in here. You should count again. There are forty-three.

*(Disturbance in the courtroom.)*

THE COURT: Bailiff, please—

THE DEFENDANT: Count them. *Count them right now.*

MR. FARBER: Eleanor, please—

THE COURT: Mr. Farber, control your client.

THE DEFENDANT: *(speaking rapidly, voice rising)* Vas eelam caret soh, vas eelam caret soh, nomen kee scree-bet nomen kee—

THE COURT: Remove the jury panel. We are in recess.

*(Jury panel is removed from the courtroom. Proceedings recessed at 9:47 a.m.)*

---

### Stenographer's Annotation

*[Handwritten in red ink along the left margin of the above page:]*

I counted. There were forty-two. I'm certain there were forty-two. But when Eleanor said to count, I looked up from my machine and I scanned the rows, and I lost my count three times. Every time I tried to count from left to right, my eyes slipped at the same point—fourth row, third seat from the center aisle. Like my gaze was sliding over something. Not someone. Something that occupied the *space* of a someone.

I've been doing this twenty-three years. I do not usually annotate my transcripts. But I want to note, for my own record: the bailiff counted afterward, quietly, while the panel was filing out. I watched his lips. He counted forty-three, then frowned, and counted again. The second time he got forty-two. He did not count a third time.

The sounds Eleanor made. My machine captured phonemes—I logged them as best I could. They're not words. Not in English.

*[A second annotation, added later in blue ink:]*

Played back my steno tape at home tonight. The phonemes are cleaner than I thought. She wasn't screaming—she was *reciting*.

## DOCUMENT 2

### Audio Diary of Marion Graves — Tape 1, Side A

**Recorded: September 8, 1987. Approximately 11:45 p.m.**

*(Sound of ice clinking in a glass. A television is on in the background—the local news, volume low. A chair creaking.)*

Okay. All right. I'm recording this because... I don't know why I'm recording this. Because Donna at the office said I should keep a diary, and I told her I'm no good at writing, and she said well then talk into one of your little tapes, Marion, and so here I am talking into one of my little tapes.

*(Pause.)*

Today was the first day of the Vance trial. Eleanor Vance. She's—let me describe her. She's smaller than I expected. All the news coverage, you'd think she was some kind of monster. She's five-two, maybe five-three. Thin. Mousy brown hair, the kind of hair that doesn't hold a curl. She was wearing a blue dress that was too big for her—the kind of dress a lawyer picks out. She looked like a schoolteacher. She looked tired.

Three dead children. Michael, Danielle, Thomas. I have the crime scene photographs in the case file and I will not look at them. The clerk told me—no. I won't repeat what the clerk told me.

What I want to talk about is what happened during jury selection. Eleanor's outburst. She said there were forty-three people in the panel. There were forty-two on the list. But I—

*(Long pause. Ice clinking.)*

In twenty-three years of court reporting, I have developed certain habits. I am precise. I am not imaginative. My ex-husband said I was the least imaginative woman he'd ever met, and I took that as a compliment. I am the kind of person who counts things. I count the tiles on the courtroom ceiling when testimony gets slow. There are two hundred and sixteen. I count the buttons on the judge's robe. Seven. I count the people who walk in.

Forty-two were called. Forty-two names on the list. But when they sat down and I scanned the room, I could not make the number come out right. My count kept catching—like a zipper catching on fabric. Fourth row. Third seat from the aisle. Every time.

It's nothing. The light in that courtroom is bad. They've been meaning to replace those fluorescents since I started.

*(Pause.)*

The sounds she made—the not-words. I have them in my steno notes. I'm going to transcribe them phonetically for my own reference. This is probably ridiculous. This is almost certainly ridiculous.

*(Sound of papers shuffling.)*

She said: "Vas eelam caret soh." Repeated twice. Then: "Nomen kee scree-bet, nomen kee—" and then she was cut off by Judge Alvarez.

I took Latin in high school. Mrs. Finney's class, 1953, Odessa High. I remember almost none of it and what I remember is probably wrong. But "nomen" means name. That much I know. "Caret" is... to lack? To be without? I could be wrong. I'm going to the library tomorrow on my lunch break.

*(She laughs softly.)*

My God, Marion, listen to yourself. The woman killed three children. She's clearly disturbed. You're not a code-breaker, you're a stenographer. Go to bed.

*(Click.)*

## DOCUMENT 3

### Official Transcript (Excerpt)

**VOLUME III — STATE'S OPENING STATEMENT**
**September 14, 1987**

---

MS. PRUITT: —and the evidence will show, ladies and gentlemen, that in the spring of 1986, Eleanor Vance underwent a dramatic transformation. She had been, by all accounts, a devoted social worker. Fifteen years of commendable service with the Department of Human Services. Colleagues will tell you she was the one you wanted on a case. She was careful. She was thorough. She loved those kids.

But sometime around March of 1986, Eleanor Vance began attending meetings at a property on Route 12, outside of Sonora. We know this because her own dayplanner, State's Exhibit 4, documents her visits. She wrote them down. Twenty-three visits between March and November.

What was at this property? You will hear testimony from Sergeant Randall Deckard of the Sutton County Sheriff's Office, who executed a search warrant on November 3rd of 1986. He will describe what he found there. I want to prepare you for that testimony. Some of what he will describe will be deeply disturbing.

The evidence will show that Eleanor Vance became involved in a group—and I will use the word the evidence supports: a *cult*—that practiced ritualistic activities on this property. The evidence will show that three children in her foster care—Michael Whitfield, age nine; Danielle Whitfield, his sister, age seven; and Thomas Reyes, age four—were brought to this property. And the evidence will show that these three children were killed, deliberately and with premeditation, as part of a ritual that—

THE DEFENDANT: *(from the defense table)* She knows. *(indicating the court reporter)* She's writing it all down and she doesn't know she knows yet, but she will. Ask her to play back the tape. Ask her what she heard.

MR. FARBER: Eleanor—

THE DEFENDANT: *(calmly, in a conversational tone)* Marion. Marion, it's all right. You don't have to be afraid right now. You have time. You have time until the verdict.

THE COURT: Mr. Farber, I will not warn you again.

MR. FARBER: Your Honor, I sincerely apologize—

THE COURT: The jury will disregard the defendant's statements. Ms. Vance, there will be no further disruptions in my courtroom or I will have you bound and gagged for the remainder of these proceedings. Am I understood?

THE DEFENDANT: Yes, sir.

THE COURT: Ms. Pruitt, you may continue.

---

### Stenographer's Annotation

*[Handwritten in red ink, pressing hard enough to score the paper:]*

She said my name. She looked directly at me and she said my name.

I have never met Eleanor Vance. I was assigned this case on August 26 by standard rotation. My name is on the docket sheet, which is a public document, so she could have seen it. Her lawyer could have told her. This is not supernatural. This is a disturbed woman in a courtroom.

She said: "You have time until the verdict."

*[Below, in blue ink, written at a different time—the handwriting slightly less controlled:]*

I went to the library. I looked up the phonemes from her first outburst. I had to call Mrs. Finney—she's eighty-one years old and still sharp as a tack, living in a care home in Midland. She was happy to hear from me. I spelled out what Eleanor had said. I heard Mrs. Finney's breathing change.

"Vas eelam caret soh" → *Vas illam caret suo* → "The vessel lacks its own"
"Nomen kee scree-bet" → *Nomen qui scribet* → "The name of the one who writes"

The one who writes.

What am I doing.

## DOCUMENT 4

### Audio Diary of Marion Graves — Tape 1, Side B

**Recorded: September 14, 1987. Approximately 8:30 p.m.**

*(No background noise. Very quiet. When Marion speaks, her voice is measured, the way a person speaks when they are trying very hard to sound normal.)*

The defendant addressed me in court today. By name. During the State's opening statement. She pointed at me and said—well, it's in the transcript. I'll attach it to this tape.

I want to be clear about what I'm doing and why.

I am not a conspiracy theorist. I voted for Reagan. I go to First Methodist on Sundays. I have a cat named Biscuit and a subscription to Reader's Digest. I am not the kind of person this happens to.

But.

*(Very long pause.)*

Last night I stayed up late with the steno tape from the first day. The outburst. I ran it through the playback at half speed, which I sometimes do to check accuracy, and I noticed something. Eleanor's vocalizations—the Latin, or pseudo-Latin—she spoke them very quickly, almost blurring the syllables together. At full speed, it sounds like hysteria. At half speed, you can hear something else. There's a rhythm to it. A cadence. Like verse.

And then—I don't know why I did this—I rewound the tape and held the playback head while rewinding. Playing it backward. It's something my daughter showed me once, years ago, because of that silliness with the rock and roll records. Backward masking. She played me a Led Zeppelin record backward and said, Mom, listen, can you hear the devil? And I said, Jackie, I hear noise, now please do your homework.

But I did it with Eleanor's outburst. I played it backward.

And forward, what she said was: *Vas illam caret suo, nomen qui scribet.*

Backward, what I heard was—

*(She stops. Silence for nearly twenty seconds.)*

Backward, what I heard, very clearly, in English, was: "Marion. You write the words that bind. Marion. You are the vessel that lacks."

*(Her voice doesn't change. It stays perfectly even, the way a person in shock sounds even.)*

Now. I am aware of how that sounds. I am aware that the human brain is a pattern-matching machine and that we hear what we expect to hear. I know about pareidolia—seeing faces in clouds, in toast, in water stains. The audio equivalent. I know that if you play any speech backward and someone tells you what you're supposed to hear, you'll hear it. I know all of this.

But here is what I also know: nobody told me what to hear. No one primed me. I sat alone in my kitchen at midnight with Biscuit on my lap and I played a tape backward and I heard my own name, unprompted, in a dead woman's voice—

*(She catches herself.)*

Not dead. Eleanor is not dead. She is alive and she is sitting in my courtroom and she is on trial for her life.

Why did I say dead?

*(Click.)*
