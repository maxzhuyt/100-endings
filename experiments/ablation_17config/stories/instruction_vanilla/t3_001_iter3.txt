# The Infinite Playoff

The first thing you need to understand about scorekeeping is that it is an act of faith.

Every diamond-shaped cell in the book, every little notation—6-3 for a groundout, short to first, or the backwards K for a called third strike—is someone's testimony that a thing happened exactly this way. One person, usually volunteer, usually half-drunk by the seventh, inscribing the official account. And from that account, everything else descends: the box scores, the averages, the season records, the archived history of what a body did on a field under lights on a given night in a given minor league town where the outfield wall is a plywood advertisement for a tire store.

I had served that faith for eleven years.

My name is Gerald Mauthe, and when the Tri-City Scrappers hired me to build the case against the Mahoning Valley Bolts, I was living in an efficiency apartment above a frozen custard stand in Niles, Ohio, surrounded by bankers' boxes full of old scorebooks—some mine, some borrowed, some stolen. The custard stand had closed for the season in September, but the smell of vanilla and freon persisted through the floorboards long into December, into the winter meetings, into the cold dark hours when I sat with a lamp and a magnifying loupe and studied the handwriting of men I'd known for years, looking for lies in the margins.

The allegation was specific: during the 2019 New York–Penn League playoff series, the Bolts had shaved runs in Games 2, 3, and 5—strategic underperformance in exchange for money from a gambling syndicate operating out of Youngstown. The Scrappers' front office, such as it was—a former high school principal named Diane Croft and her nephew who handled "operations," which meant he pressure-washed the bleachers—had retained me at four hundred dollars a week to gather statistical evidence of the scheme. They wanted anomalies. They wanted proof that the Bolts' plate discipline in critical moments was measurably inconsistent with their season performance, that the pattern of failure was not random but designed.

I was qualified. I was cheap. I was available.

This last quality should have been a warning to them.

---

The first anomaly I found was in Game 3.

According to the official scorer's report, filed with the league and digitized in their archival system, the Bolts' second baseman, Jesús Delgado, came to the plate in the bottom of the sixth with runners on first and third, one out, and the Scrappers leading 4-2. He grounded into a 4-6-3 double play, ending the inning and stranding the runner on third. It was the kind of outcome that, if you were looking for evidence of tanking, sang like a tuning fork—a double play ball from a player who'd hit .289 on the season with a ground ball rate of 38%, well below the league average. Right situation, right result, suspicious discipline collapse.

But I also had a physical scorebook from that game—one I'd purchased from a collector in Erie who'd gotten it at an estate sale. Some old timer's careful pencil notations. And in this book, Delgado's at-bat in the bottom of the sixth was recorded differently: he'd lined a single to right, scoring the runner from third, advancing the man on first to second. 4-3 game. No double play. The inning continued with the next batter, Tomás Reyes, who flew out to center to end the frame.

Two records of the same plate appearance. One showed evidence of point-shaving. The other showed a man doing his job.

I assumed a simple error—the physical scorebook was wrong, kept by an amateur, maybe a fan who'd misremembered or been distracted. This happens. Scorekeeping is faith, and faith is practiced by imperfect congregants.

But when I went to cross-reference the rest of Game 3, the problems multiplied.

In the official electronic record, the Scrappers' pitcher threw 97 pitches. In the physical book, he threw 104. The discrepancy wasn't in one inning—it was distributed across the entire game, as if the two accounts described the same event in mostly the same way but with a persistent wobble, a parallax between two vantage points that couldn't quite agree on what had happened. The box score totals in the physical book didn't match either the official box score or the internal math of the physical book's own inning-by-inning account.

And the physical book had my handwriting in the margins.

Not a lot. Just a few pencil annotations, lightly made, in the crabbed tiny script I use when I'm working through calculations. Expected batting averages. Leverage indices. Notes in my own hand on a scorebook I had supposedly never seen before I bought it from a collector three weeks ago.

I sat with that for a long time.

The custard smell rose through the floor, sweet and chemical and persistent, and I held the book under my lamp and studied my own handwriting as though it belonged to a stranger. Which, I decided, it might. My handwriting is not uncommon—a particular type of statistician's hand, trained in the same notation systems, arriving at the same shorthand. I was being paranoid. I was projecting significance onto coincidence.

This is what I told myself.

---

By the second week, I had assembled a matrix of all five playoff games cross-referenced across every source I could locate: the league's official electronic records, three physical scorebooks from three different keepers, newspaper box scores from the *Tribune Chronicle* and the *Vindicator*, and a partial video recording of Game 5 that someone had uploaded to YouTube and that had since been deleted but cached in an archival service I paid nine dollars to access.

The matrix did not resolve. It worsened.

In Game 2, the Bolts' cleanup hitter, Marcus Slade, was recorded in the official scorer's report as going 0-for-4 with three strikeouts. In one physical scorebook, he went 1-for-4 with a solo home run in the eighth. In the newspaper box score, he went 0-for-3—one fewer plate appearance, which meant either an at-bat had been added or subtracted somewhere. In the cached video, which covered only the fourth through seventh innings, Slade appeared twice: once striking out on a pitch that was clearly outside, and once not appearing at all in the batting order where he should have, replaced by a pinch hitter named Cosgrove who didn't exist on the Bolts' roster.

I checked the roster. No Cosgrove. I checked three times.

Then I checked my own notes—the working documents I'd been assembling, the spreadsheets and handwritten tallies—and found a reference, in my handwriting, dated two days earlier, to "Cosgrove, DH, Game 2, 0-1, BB." As if I'd already encountered him. As if I'd already incorporated a nonexistent player into my analysis without question.

I stared at the note.

I had no memory of writing it.

---

I should tell you about the gambling because it is either the cause of everything or the explanation I later invented to give the chaos a center of gravity, a human motive around which the impossible could organize itself into something like a story.

I had been betting on minor league games since 2016. Not heavily at first—the lines were soft, the markets thin, and I knew the league's statistical architecture better than possibly anyone alive. I knew which relievers' velocity dropped on two days' rest. I knew which umpires expanded the zone in cold weather. I knew, or believed I knew, the small structural truths that made prediction marginally better than guessing.

For two seasons, I was profitable. Not dramatically—enough to supplement the meager consulting fees I collected from teams whose front offices were high school principals and pressure-washing nephews. But in 2018, my models failed, as models do, in a specific and catastrophic way: three consecutive playoff series in which the outcomes deviated sharply from every projection, in which the teams I'd identified as structurally superior lost in patterns my frameworks couldn't explain. I chased the losses. The losses compounded. I borrowed from a man in Youngstown whose interest rates were expressed not in percentages but in implications.

By the start of the 2019 season, I owed this man—his name was Petrovic, though I'm no longer certain of that—enough that my continued physical comfort was a subject of ongoing negotiation. I made payments. I missed payments. I sold my car. I moved above the custard stand, where the rent was cash and the landlord didn't ask questions because the landlord was a corporation in Delaware that had purchased the building as part of a tax write-off and had no local representative.

And during this period, I was also the official scorer for the Tri-City Scrappers.

Do you see.

The man hired to investigate the falsification of game records was the same man who, during the season in question, had been keeping those records. And I had not mentioned this to Diane Croft or her nephew. I had not mentioned this to anyone. And until I sat with my matrix of contradictions at one in the morning with vanilla freon seeping through the floor, I had not fully mentioned it to myself.

---

The third week was when the architecture of the problem became genuinely recursive, and I understood—or began to understand, or began to construct the understanding I would later present as discovery—that the investigation was not hovering above the corruption but was another layer of it.

I found, in the league's electronic archive, a record of a protest filed by the Scrappers' manager after Game 4 of the 2019 playoff series. The protest alleged that the Bolts' starting pitcher had used a foreign substance, that the umpiring crew had been notified and had declined to inspect. Standard bureaucratic grievance. But attached to the protest was a statistical appendix—spin rate analysis, batted-ball exit velocity data, comparison charts—and the appendix was authored by me. It bore my name. Gerald R. Mauthe, Independent Statistical Consultant.

I had no memory of writing it. But the work was competent, thorough, exactly what I would have produced. The data sources were cited. The methodology was sound. And buried in the footnotes was a reference to a dataset I'd compiled titled "Adjusted Outcome Probabilities, NYPL Playoff Series, 2019 (Corrected)."

That word. *Corrected.*

I searched my files for this dataset. I found it on an external hard drive I kept in a shoebox under my bed, in a folder I didn't recognize, dated November 2019—six weeks after the playoff series had ended, long after the results were official.

The dataset contained play-by-play logs for all five games of the series. But they weren't the official logs and they weren't the alternative logs from the physical scorebooks. They were a third version. A *corrected* version. And the corrections were systematic: wherever the official record showed a Bolts player failing in a high-leverage situation—a strikeout with runners in scoring position, a double play with the game on the line—the corrected version showed a different outcome. A single. A walk. A sacrifice fly. The corrected version told the story of a team that had performed, in critical moments, exactly as their season statistics would predict. No tanking. No shaving. No conspiracy.

And wherever the corrections appeared, the math was impeccable. The expected outcomes aligned perfectly with regression models. The distribution was smooth, natural, free of the suspicious clustering that marks manipulation.

Which meant either: the corrected dataset was the true record, and the official record had been falsified to create the appearance of point-shaving. Or: the corrected dataset was itself a fabrication, designed to look like truth, to provide the Bolts with a defense if the allegations ever materialized.

And I had made it. Or I believed I had made it. Or I was constructing, now, in January, a narrative in which I had made it, because the alternative—that someone else had placed this file on my hard drive, using my methodology, in my notation, with my name—was either more frightening or less useful, depending on which version of myself I consulted.

---

Here is the thing about falsifying data, which I can tell you from either experience or imagination, and I'm no longer confident of the distinction:

It is not a single act. It is a practice, like prayer. You begin with a correction so small it barely registers—a pitch count rounded, a batted ball reclassified from line drive to fly ball, a timing change that shifts a play from one frame of the inning to another. You tell yourself you're cleaning noise, improving signal, doing what any responsible analyst does when the raw data contains obvious errors. And sometime later—weeks, months, years—you discover that the original error was yours, that the raw data was fine, that what you "corrected" was reality, and what you installed in its place was a small, precise lie that made the numbers more beautiful than the truth.

And then you have a choice: you can undo the lie, which means admitting you made it, which means tracing backward through every analysis that relied on the corrupted data to identify the full scope of the contamination. Or you can leave it, and build on it, and accept that your entire analytical framework is now a house constructed on a foundation that contains, somewhere, a crack you put there yourself.

I don't know when I chose the second option. I don't know if I ever made a conscious choice. What I know is that when I sat down in the third week of January to map every discrepancy in my matrix—every point where the records diverged, every at-bat that happened in one version and didn't happen in another—the map I produced looked less like a conspiracy and more like a territory I recognized. The logic of the contradictions was not the logic of a gambling syndicate covering its tracks. It was the logic of a mind that had lost count of its own revisions.

I knew this because the handwriting in the margins of the contested scorebooks was mine. I knew this because the "corrected" dataset on my hard drive used a regression model I'd built in 2017 and never shared with anyone. I knew this because the ghost player, Cosgrove, DH, 0-1, BB—when I searched my oldest files, the ones from before the gambling, before the debts, before the playoff series—I found him there too. Cosgrove. A name I'd used in a sample dataset in 2015, a placeholder when I was teaching myself to build simulation models. A fictional player I'd invented to test my systems. And somehow he had migrated from the test environment into the live records, a ghost in the machine, a residue of my own imagination lodged in the official history of a baseball game.

Or so I reconstructed. Or so I invented. The distinction had collapsed.

---

Diane Croft called me on a Tuesday. She said the league had convened a tribunal. She said I would be called to present my findings. She said to bring everything.

I sat on the edge of my bed after the call and looked at the bankers' boxes and the hard drives and the notebooks and understood that I could not present what I'd found without presenting myself as the subject of the investigation. The crime and the investigation had become topologically identical—a Klein bottle of fraud in which the inside was the outside, in which the man searching for the manipulator was the manipulator, or had been, or had dreamed he was, or was dreaming now that he had been.

I considered running. I considered burning the boxes. I considered calling Petrovic—or the man I remembered as Petrovic—and asking what he knew, whether the debts were real, whether the payments I remembered making were things that had actually happened or another layer of the fiction. But I didn't trust my memory of his phone number. I didn't trust my memory of anything.

What I did—and this is the part I'm certain of, or as certain as I can be, because there were witnesses—was spend four days organizing my evidence into a presentation. I laid it out chronologically: the initial findings, the cross-reference matrix, the escalating contradictions, the discovery of the corrected dataset, the ghost player, the handwriting analysis. I did not editorialize. I did not draw conclusions. I organized the evidence the way a good statistician organizes data—transparently, reproducibly, with every assumption visible and every source cited. I let the structure speak.

The structure, of course, spoke about me. But I'd convinced myself that this was acceptable, even noble—that the honest presentation of evidence, even self-incriminating evidence, was the highest act of the statistician's faith. That the scorebook does not lie, even when the scorekeeper does. That the numbers, properly arranged, would tell a truth my mind could no longer access directly.

This is what I believed on the morning I drove a borrowed car to the league office in Brooklyn, carrying three bankers' boxes and an external hard drive and a physical scorebook with my handwriting in the margins.

---
