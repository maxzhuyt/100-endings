# Form 27-B: Application for Continued Existence

## Part One: Pending Review

The universe, it must be said, is not indifferent to suffering. Indifference would require a certain philosophical commitment that the universe has never quite gotten around to filing the paperwork for. The universe is, more accurately, *backlogged*.

This is important context for what happened to Earth.

---

Seventeen years, two months, and eleven days after the end of human civilization—an event that had been formally categorized as "Involuntary Archival, ref. Case No. 9,841,227,003-B, see attached"—the colony ship *Delilah* coasted through a region of space that smelled faintly of old carpet and photocopier toner.

Three thousand and six souls remained aboard. Three thousand, if you counted only the ones who'd been born on Earth and could remember the taste of rain. Six, if you counted the children who knew Earth only as a bedtime story that ended badly, and who had no strong opinions about rain one way or another.

Captain Petra Okafor-Nilsen stood on the bridge, which was not really a bridge but rather the repurposed executive lounge of what had once been the cruise liner *MS Celestial Rhapsody*, hastily retrofitted with navigation systems in the seventy-two hours between the Auditors' initial notice and the end of the world. The champagne dispensers still worked. The star charts were duct-taped to what had been the cocktail menu board.

"Status report," she said, because someone had to.

"Same as yesterday, Captain." Lieutenant Commander Dakarai Mensah didn't look up from his console, which was three tablets wired together and mounted on a drinks trolley. "We are alive. The Auditors have not returned. We are approximately four years from Kepler-442b, assuming the sanctuary planet actually exists and is not, as I suspect, a clerical hallucination."

"It exists. The signal was clear."

"The signal," Mensah said with the careful diplomacy of a man who had been having this argument for a decade, "was a series of tonal pulses that *could* be interpreted as navigational coordinates, or could equally be interpreted as the cosmic equivalent of hold music."

Captain Okafor-Nilsen did not dignify this with a response. She had commanded a crew of eleven hundred when the *Celestial Rhapsody* was a pleasure vessel. She had commanded three thousand refugees when it became a lifeboat. She had buried—well, spaced—forty-seven people in the years since, read words over each of them from a water-stained King James Bible she'd found in a stateroom nightstand, and she had done all of this without ever once accepting the fundamental premise of their situation.

The premise was this: humanity had been processed.

Not invaded. Not conquered. Not destroyed in any way that suggested intent or malice or even, frankly, interest. Earth had been *processed*, in the same way a form is processed, in the same way an application moves from one desk to another until it reaches the desk of someone who stamps it with a stamp that means the application has been *resolved*, which is a word that contains within it both "solved" and something far more final.

The Auditors had arrived without warning on a Tuesday in March—a fact that bothered Captain Okafor-Nilsen perhaps more than it should have, because she felt that the annihilation of a species deserved at minimum a Thursday, a day with some gravitas—and they had filled the sky like a migraine made architectural. They were structures. That was the only honest word. Not ships, not beings, not weapons. *Structures*. Regularized geometries that tessellated against the upper atmosphere, silent and absolute, like someone had cracked open the sky and revealed that behind it was an office building that went on forever.

The "processing" took eleven days.

It was not violent. This was, in many ways, the worst part. Violence could be fought, or mourned, or at least *understood* as a thing one entity did to another. What the Auditors did was more like—well. Imagine that your house is a line item on a spreadsheet, and someone selects the row and presses delete. Your house doesn't burn down. Your house doesn't collapse. Your house simply stops being included in the calculation of what exists. The formula updates. The totals rebalance. The house becomes a gap in the data where a house used to be.

Cities went first. Then ecosystems. Then oceans. It was not destruction; it was *de-listing*.

The *Celestial Rhapsody* had been three days into a Mediterranean cruise when the processing began. By the time it ended, the Mediterranean was a concept the universe no longer supported, and the ship—by some combination of luck, engine power, and the fact that the Auditors apparently did not consider international waters to be within their jurisdictional scope—had made it to low orbit with every soul it could carry.

Eleven days. Seven billion people, give or take, de-listed. The Auditors departed without a word, leaving behind only a single document, transmitted in every human language simultaneously and also in sixteen languages that had been dead for centuries, as if the Auditors wanted to be thorough, or perhaps simply couldn't be bothered to check which ones were current.

The document was Form 27-B: Notification of Archival.

It was nine hundred pages long.

---

In the seventeen years since, a small and quietly desperate group of survivors had dedicated themselves to reading Form 27-B in its entirety. This group called themselves the Compliance Office, which was a name that suggested order and purpose and was therefore deeply misleading.

The Compliance Office consisted of eleven people who worked in a former spa treatment room on Deck 7, surrounded by whiteboards covered in diagrams that looked like conspiracy theories designed by someone with a graduate degree in organizational psychology. Which, in fact, two of them were.

But the heart of the Compliance Office—its director, its driving force, its unlikely and self-deprecating prophet—was a woman named Gloria Chen, who, before the end of the world, had worked for twenty-three years at the California Department of Motor Vehicles, Branch Office #507, Bakersfield.

Gloria was fifty-nine years old. She had gray-streaked black hair that she wore in a bun held together by a stylus she kept forgetting was there. She had the particular patience of someone who had once explained to a man, calmly and without raising her voice, that his boat trailer did in fact require a separate registration even if the boat itself was also a vehicle, and that no, sir, the fact that it floated did not make it exempt from terrestrial documentation requirements, and that if he would please take a number and have a seat she would be happy to help him when it was his turn *again*.

Gloria Chen was, in short, the only person aboard the *Delilah* who had read Form 27-B and felt not despair but recognition.

"It's a form," she had told Captain Okafor-Nilsen three years into the voyage, when the Captain had summoned her to the bridge to explain why she'd requisitioned an entire storage bay for what she called her "reference archive." "It's just a form. I've seen a thousand like it. Ten thousand. It cross-references other forms. It cites regulatory codes. It has a header and a footer and a revision date and—look here, on page 447—a box that says 'For Office Use Only.' It's not alien. It's not incomprehensible. It's *bureaucracy*."

"Bureaucracy," the Captain had repeated flatly.

"The most advanced organizing principle in the universe, apparently."

"Gloria. They deleted our planet."

"They *archived* it. Read the language. Section 12, paragraph 4: 'Upon completion of the assessment period, the evaluated civilization and associated biospheric assets shall be archived in accordance with Standard Procedure 1167-C.' Archived. Not destroyed. Archived."

"Is there a difference?"

"Honestly? I don't know. But I know there's an appeals process."

She had pointed to page 612, subsection (q)(iii), a passage rendered in font so small it seemed to be trying to hide from its own implications:

*Any entity subject to archival may submit Form 27-B/R (Application for Reclassification and/or Continued Existence) within one standard cosmic fiscal cycle of the date of initial processing. Applications must be submitted in triplicate to the relevant Jurisdictional Office. Incomplete applications will not be reviewed. The Auditing Authority assumes no liability for delays, errors, or existential discontinuities arising from the application process.*

"There's an appeals process," Gloria had said again, and her eyes had been bright with something that was not quite hope and not quite madness but occupied the fertile territory between them. "There's always an appeals process."

---

This was the word that changed everything: *wrong*.

For fourteen years, the Compliance Office had been drafting Form 27-B/R. They had parsed every page of the original notification. They had constructed a lexicon of Auditor terminology from context clues. They had debated, bitterly and at length, whether "standard cosmic fiscal cycle" meant a year, a century, or something keyed to the rotational period of the galactic core. They had argued about formatting. They had argued about font. Gloria Chen had, on one memorable occasion, thrown a stapler at Dr. Henrik Vasquez because he insisted on using Oxford commas in a document addressed to entities that might not even possess a concept of the comma, or Oxford.

And then, on the morning of their seventeenth year in space, while reviewing their forty-third draft, Gloria discovered they had been filling out the wrong form.

The revelation came to her in the way that all important revelations come: not as a thunderbolt, but as a slow, sick feeling in the stomach, like biting into fruit and finding it hollow.

"Oh," she said softly, sitting at her desk in the spa room while around her the whiteboards loomed with their fourteen years of meticulous, useless work. "Oh, *no*."

Dr. Vasquez looked up from his terminal. "What?"

"Page 203. The jurisdictional table. I've been reading column B. It should be column D."

"What's the difference?"

"Column B is for civilizations that achieved interstellar travel through indigenous technological development. Column D is for civilizations that achieved interstellar travel through—" she squinted, translating the Auditor terminology in her head, "—'incidental displacement resulting from archival proceedings.' We didn't fly to the stars, Henrik. We *fled* to the stars. During the archival. That changes our classification. That changes everything. We're not Category 7. We're Category 12."

"And Category 12 uses—"

"Form 39-J. Not 27-B/R. Form 39-J." She put her head in her hands. "We've been writing the wrong letter to the wrong office for fourteen years."

There was a silence in the spa room that had the quality of a held breath, or a building about to collapse, or a very long line at a government agency when the only clerk on duty puts up the "Window Closed" sign.

"Well," said Dr. Vasquez, in the measured tone of a man who was determined not to scream, "what does Form 39-J require?"

Gloria flipped to the appendix. She read. She read some more. She turned pages with increasing speed, her lips moving silently, and Dr. Vasquez watched the color leave her face in stages, like a sunset running in reverse.

"It requires," she said, "a notarized certification of existential legitimacy from a recognized Jurisdictional Office."

"We don't have one of those."

"No."

"Where do we get one?"

"From a Jurisdictional Office."

"Where is the nearest Jurisdictional Office?"

Gloria turned to the star chart duct-taped to the wall—a copy of the one on the bridge, annotated in her own hand with references to spatial coordinates mentioned in Form 27-B—and traced a line with her finger to a point that was, inconveniently, in the opposite direction from the sanctuary planet.

"There," she said. "Maybe. If I'm reading the coordinate system right. Which is a significant 'if,' because the Auditors appear to use a base-thirteen numbering system, and I got a C-minus in high school algebra."

"How far?"

"Two years. Give or take."

"Two years in the *wrong direction*."

Gloria took the stylus out of her hair, realized it was a stylus, put it back in, and rubbed her eyes.

"I need to talk to the Captain," she said.

## Part Two: Please Take a Number

Captain Petra Okafor-Nilsen did not believe in the Auditors.

This requires some clarification. She believed they existed. She had watched them fill the sky. She had watched them un-make the world she'd been born in, the world where she'd learned to sail and kissed her first girlfriend and eaten her mother's groundnut soup and stood at her father's grave and felt the particular grief that comes from realizing your parents are just people, fragile and finite and yours only temporarily. She had watched all of that be reduced to a footnote in someone else's filing system, and she believed it had happened.

What she did not believe—what she *refused* to believe, with a stubbornness that was either her greatest strength or a form of very slow suicide—was the *premise*. She did not believe that the universe was a place where such things could be done by *accident*, by *paperwork*, by the dispassionate machinery of bureaucratic processing. She believed in enemies. She believed in purpose. She believed, because she had to, that what had happened to Earth was an act of will, and that acts of will could be defied.

"You want me to turn the ship around," she said.

Gloria stood in the bridge, hands clasped in front of her like a petitioner, which she was. "I want you to consider an adjustment to our heading."

"A two-year adjustment. In the opposite direction from our destination."

"Yes."

"To visit an office."

"A Jurisdictional Office. Yes."

"Gloria." The Captain leaned forward in her chair—a captain's chair that had been a barstool before the retrofit and still bore a brass footrail that she rested her boots on. "We have been on a heading for Kepler-442b for seventeen years. We have rationed food, recycled water, buried our dead, and *kept going* because there is a planet out there that might—*might*—be a place where human beings can live again. And you are asking me to add four years to that journey so that we can fill out a form."

"The correct form."

"I don't care about the correct form."

"Captain, with respect—"

"I care about land under our feet. I care about sky over our heads. I care about a place where children can grow up without recycled air and artificial gravity and the knowledge that they are the last of everything."

"And what happens when we get there and the Auditors come again? Because they will. According to Form 27-B, archived civilizations that re-establish without proper reclassification are subject to—" Gloria consulted her notes, "—'secondary processing with prejudice.' I don't know what that means, Captain, but the word 'prejudice' is not encouraging."

Captain Okafor-Nilsen was quiet for a long moment. Through the bridge windows—which were, in fact, the glass walls of what had been the ship's observation lounge and still bore a frosted decal of the words *Starlight Bar & Grill*—the stars moved with the imperceptible patience of things that have never had to justify their continued existence to anyone.

"How sure are you?" the Captain asked.

"About the form? Very."

"About the office being where you think it is?"

"Less very."

"How much less?"

Gloria considered lying. She had spent twenty-three years at the DMV, and the DMV was an institution that ran on small, necessary lies—*the wait time is approximately thirty minutes*, *your application is being processed*, *we apologize for the inconvenience*—lies that were not quite lies because they contained the ghost of the intention to be true.

"Maybe sixty percent," she said.

"Sixty percent."
