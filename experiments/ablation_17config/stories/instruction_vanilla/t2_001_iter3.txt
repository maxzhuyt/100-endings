# The Reykjavik Frequency Heist

## Part One: The Admission

**Reykjavík, November 2019**

The documentary crew arrived at his house on Skólavörðustígur at nine in the morning, which Magnús Þorsteinsson considered an obscenity. He was eighty-one years old. He had earned the right to wake at noon.

"You can set up in the kitchen," he told the young woman with the sound equipment. "But not the living room. The living room is for living."

They had been trying to interview him for two years. First the journalist from RÚV, then the podcasters from Copenhagen, then a graduate student at the University of Iceland who had somehow stumbled across the declassified NATO monitoring logs from Keflavík and noticed the eighteen-hour gap on September 14, 1971. The gap itself wasn't unusual. Equipment failed. Tapes degraded. The North Atlantic did what it pleased to signals and the instruments that chased them. What was unusual was the subsequent blackout order, signed not by the base commander but by someone in the Icelandic Ministry of Justice whose name had been redacted with such violence that the paper was torn.

Magnús had refused them all. He had refused politely, in the Icelandic manner, which meant he had said perhaps and then never answered the telephone again.

But the young director—her name was Katrín, and she had her grandmother's jaw, and her grandmother had been Björg Sigurðardóttir, who had driven the van—Katrín had come to his door personally in August and said, "Elísabet is dead."

He had known this already. He had known it for three weeks, since the obituary in *Morgunblaðið*. Dr. Elísabet Jónsdóttir, 1934–2019. Theoretical physicist. Pioneer of geomagnetic signal analysis. Formerly of the University of Iceland, the Niels Bohr Institute, and briefly—a period the obituary navigated with the delicacy of a trawler in a gale—a consulting advisor to France's *Commissariat à l'énergie atomique*. She had died in a care facility in Akureyri. Her mind had gone two years before her body, which Magnús found cosmically unjust because her mind had been the most dangerous instrument he had ever encountered, far more dangerous than anything they'd built or stolen or aimed at that fissure in the earth, and it deserved a more dignified end than simply flickering out like a thermionic valve losing its vacuum.

"I know Elísabet is dead," he had told Katrín.

"Then there's no one left to protect."

He had looked at her for a long time. She was wrong, of course. There were approximately three hundred and fifty thousand people left to protect, if you counted only Iceland. If you counted the rest, the number was considerably larger. But Elísabet was dead, and he was eighty-one, and the story was beginning to surface in fragments—the NATO logs, the memoir of a Soviet naval officer stationed in Murmansk that had been published in Saint Petersburg in 2016 and contained two curious paragraphs about an Icelandic fishing vessel that had appeared in coordinates where no fishing vessel should have been—and fragments were dangerous. Fragments invited speculation. Speculation invited reconstruction. Reconstruction, if done by the wrong people with the wrong intentions, might lead someone to attempt it again.

Better to tell it whole. Better to tell it true. Better to let the world hear what the signal said and understand why five people in a freezing radio shack on the northeast coast of Iceland had chosen to burn it.

"Fine," he had said. "November. I'll need time to prepare."

Now it was November. The kitchen smelled of coffee and electrical tape, the latter from the crew's equipment. Magnús sat at his table with his hands around a mug that read RÍKISÚTVARPIÐ—a relic from his thirty-two years at the national broadcasting service—and looked into the camera with the expression of a man who had been keeping a secret for forty-eight years and was not entirely sure that releasing it wouldn't kill him.

"Where do I begin," he said. It was not a question.

"Wherever feels right," Katrín said from behind the camera.

"Nothing about this ever felt right." He drank his coffee. "I'll begin with the sound."

## Part Two: The Sound

**Krafla, Northeast Iceland, June 1971**

The sound was not a sound.

Magnús would spend the rest of his life trying to explain this to people, and he would fail every time, because language was designed for things that existed in the ordinary world, and this did not. When Elísabet first played him the recording on the Tandberg reel-to-reel in her office at the university, what came through the monitor speakers was a kind of tonal stuttering—a rhythmic pulse that rose and fell across a frequency range that shouldn't have contained anything at all.

"This is noise," Magnús said.

"This is not noise."

"Elísabet. I have been a radio engineer for fourteen years. I have listened to noise from every source capable of producing it. Solar noise. Ionospheric noise. Precipitation static. Intermodulation products from poorly shielded Soviet transmitters in Murmansk. Whale song picked up by submarine detection arrays. This—" he gestured at the speakers, at the stuttering, churning, almost-but-not-quite-rhythmic signal, "—is noise."

"And if I told you it was coming from inside the Krafla caldera?"

"I would say the Krafla caldera is a volcanic system and volcanic systems produce electromagnetic emissions. This is well documented. Piezoelectric effects in stressed rock. Charge separation in eruption plumes. You could write a paper about the specific characteristics, and it would be moderately interesting, and I would read it over lunch."

Elísabet leaned forward. She was thirty-seven years old, narrow-shouldered, with cropped dark hair and the kind of angular face that looked perpetually unimpressed. She wore a wool sweater with a hole in the left elbow and glasses that she was always pushing up her nose with her index finger, and when she leaned forward like this, it meant she was about to say something that would rearrange the furniture in your skull.

"I have been monitoring this signal for nine months," she said. "It is not random. It is not piezoelectric. It does not correlate with seismic activity, geomagnetic indices, solar flux, or any other parameter I have tested. It broadcasts on 14.7 kilohertz, which is a frequency used by naval communication systems, specifically the kind of very low frequency transmitters that NATO and the Soviet Union use to communicate with submarines. But it is not coming from a transmitter. It is coming from a fissure in the rock approximately four hundred meters below the surface of the caldera."

"Elísabet—"

"I've mapped the modulation pattern. It is not noise. It is amplitude-modulated. AM. Like a radio broadcast."

"You're saying the volcano is broadcasting a radio programme."

"I am saying something inside the volcanic system is producing a structured signal in a format consistent with amplitude modulation."

"That's the same thing."

"It is not the same thing. One is a conclusion. The other is an observation. I am making an observation. You are the one leaping to conclusions, which is ironic, because you have spent the last five minutes telling me how cautious and experienced you are."

Magnús closed his eyes. He had known Elísabet Jónsdóttir for eleven years, since she had arrived at the university as a postdoctoral researcher with a dissertation on magnetotelluric field theory and an immediate, instinctive talent for making everyone around her feel like they were moving too slowly. She was brilliant. She was also, in his private and uncharitable opinion, the most exhausting person in Iceland, and in a country of two hundred thousand people who all knew each other and had winter-long opinions about everything, this was a meaningful distinction.

"What do you want from me?" he asked.

"I want you to help me demodulate it."

"Demodulate the volcano broadcast."

"Yes."

"And what do you expect to hear?"

Elísabet pushed her glasses up her nose. "I think it's speech," she said.

---

**Reykjavík, November 2019**

"She was right," Magnús told the camera. "It was speech. That was the first thing you need to understand. When I finally got the demodulation working—and it took three weeks, because the carrier frequency was drifting in ways that no carrier frequency should drift, as though whatever was producing it didn't fully understand the physics of radio transmission, or understood them differently than we did—when I got it working, what came through was a human voice. Speaking Icelandic."

He paused. The camera watched him.

"It was reading the news."

## Part Three: The News

**Reykjavík, July 1971**

The voice was female. Middle-aged. Clear diction, the kind cultivated by professional broadcasters. It read in the steady, measured cadence of someone accustomed to delivering information on a schedule, and the signal's drift meant that sections would phase in and out of intelligibility, as though the station—*the station*, Magnús thought, and nearly laughed at himself for using the word—were at the edge of reception range and slowly rotating.

They listened to it in Elísabet's office, the door locked, the windows closed despite the July warmth. The Tandberg turned. The speaker crackled. And the voice said:

*—confirmed that the eruption, which began shortly after two o'clock this morning, has produced a lava flow moving northeast from the Leirhnjúkur fissure toward—*

Then static. Then nothing. Then, forty seconds later, fading back in:

*—Minister Jóhannesson has urged residents of—*

Gone again.

Magnús rewound the tape. Played it again. Rewound. Played. He was listening to the technical characteristics—the frequency response, the dynamic range, the noise floor. These told a story independent of the content. The frequency response was narrow, consistent with AM broadcast. The noise floor was bizarre, shot through with harmonic distortions that pulsed at intervals of roughly 6.3 seconds, as though the signal were being filtered through something that resonated at that period. And underneath it all, a low, continuous rumble that he recognized from years of working with seismic monitoring equipment as the sound of the earth itself, of magma moving through rock.

"There is no eruption at Krafla," Magnús said.

"No."

"There has not been an eruption at Krafla since 1729."

"Correct."

"The newsreader mentioned a minister named Jóhannesson. There is no minister named Jóhannesson in the current government."

"Also correct."

"Elísabet. What is this?"

She rewound the tape one more time and pressed play. This time she let it run past the section they had been repeating, into a passage that Magnús had not yet heard. The voice phased in, mid-sentence:

*—seventy-five, the third consecutive year of significant volcanic activity in the Krafla system, has prompted renewed debate about the viability of the Krafla geothermal power station, which was only partially operational when the eruption series, now being called the Krafla Fires, began in December of nineteen seventy—*

She pressed stop.

The room was very quiet.

"Nineteen seventy-five," Magnús said.

"Yes."

"This broadcast is referencing events in nineteen seventy-five. It is currently nineteen seventy-one."

"Yes."

"You're going to tell me this is a broadcast from the future."

"I am going to tell you that this signal contains references to events that have not yet occurred. Whether it is *from* the future—whether it has traveled backward through time—or whether it is being produced by something that has access to information about events that have not yet occurred—these are different hypotheses, and I do not currently have enough data to distinguish between them."

"How much data would you need?"

"More than I can collect from this office with a surplus Tandberg and a borrowed antenna." She pushed her glasses up. "Which is why I need a team."

**Reykjavík, November 2019**

"You have to understand how this felt," Magnús said. He was holding his coffee mug but not drinking from it. "In 1971, the world was a particular kind of place. Iceland was a particular kind of place. We were a NATO country that didn't want to be a NATO country. We had the Americans at Keflavík and the Soviets watching from trawlers off the coast and the British stealing our fish. We had a population smaller than a decent-sized English town, and we took ourselves enormously seriously about everything, and underneath all of that—underneath the politics and the fishing and the insufferable weather—there was the land. The land was alive. Everyone knew this. We built our roads around elf-stones. We named our children after volcanoes we hoped wouldn't wake up. And here was Elísabet telling me the land wasn't just alive. It was *speaking*. And it was speaking about things that hadn't happened yet."

He looked directly into the camera.

"My first instinct was to walk away. My second instinct was to call a psychiatrist, either for her or for myself. My third instinct—" He stopped. "You have to understand Elísabet. She didn't believe in magic. She didn't believe in ghosts or elves or the hidden people. She was the most rigorously materialist person I have ever known. She once told me that if God existed, He would have published a paper, because any entity capable of creating the universe would understand the importance of peer review. When she told me the signal was real, she meant it was *physically* real. A phenomenon. Something that could be measured and recorded and, if we could get the right equipment into the right place, captured."

He paused again. The crew waited.

"My third instinct was that she was right. And that frightened me more than anything else."

## Part Four: The Team

**Reykjavík, August 1971**

There were, in the end, five of them. This was not by design. Elísabet had originally envisioned three, but the technical requirements expanded as she began to plan the capture in earnest, and each new requirement brought a new person into the circle, and each new person brought their own problems, and by mid-August the operation had acquired the organizational complexity and interpersonal toxicity of a small fishing cooperative.

Magnús was the engineer. His role was to design and build the receiving equipment: a portable VLF receiver sensitive enough to isolate the 14.7 kHz signal from the background electromagnetic noise of northeasten Iceland, which was considerable, because the auroral electrojet turned the ionosphere above them into a crackling curtain of interference every time the sun so much as hiccuped. He needed filters, amplifiers, a recording system, and—critically—a demodulation chain that could track the signal's maddening frequency drift in real time, because the signal did not stay put. It wandered. It breathed. It was, in the most literal sense, alive, and keeping a lock on it was like trying to tune a radio in a car that was driving over a road that was being built as you drove.

Elísabet was the theorist, the strategist, and the problem that all other problems orbited. She had spent nine months characterizing the signal's behavior—its daily patterns, its relationship to geomagnetic conditions, its intensity variations—and she had identified a window. On the night of September 14, 1971, three factors would converge: a predicted geomagnetic calm, a specific phase of the lunar tidal cycle that she believed affected subsurface hydrothermal pressures, and the autumnal equinox's approach, which—for reasons she could describe mathematically but not intuitively—seemed to correlate with the signal's peak intensity. The window would last approximately twelve hours.
