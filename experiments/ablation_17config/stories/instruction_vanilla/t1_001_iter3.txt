# The Thirty-Seven Letters of Monk Hideo

## As Compiled by Yamamoto Kenji, Eighth Dan, Tenshin-ryū

### With Editorial Annotations

---

## Compiler's Preface

I am dying in the customary fashion—slowly, and with the growing suspicion that I have misunderstood everything.

What follows are thirty-seven letters received over a period of forty-one years from the man known in competitive circles as Kuroda Hideo, later Brother Hideo of Entsū-ji monastery, and known to me as the single fixed point around which my life described its long and useless orbit. I have arranged them in chronological order. Where the postmark was illegible, I have estimated the date by the quality of the ink, the texture of the paper, and the particular character of the anguish.

I should state certain facts plainly, as the letters themselves never do.

On the fourteenth of November, 1981, at the Nippon Budōkan in Tokyo, the All-Japan Kendo Federation Championship concluded its final match between Kuroda Hideo, then thirty-one years old and undefeated across sixty-seven consecutive bouts spanning four years, and myself, Yamamoto Kenji, then thirty-four, a three-time finalist who had never claimed the title. Kuroda held the center. I held the outside. The referee raised his flags. Fourteen hundred spectators drew a single breath, and Kuroda Hideo lowered his shinai, stepped backward off the court line, and forfeited.

He bowed to me. He bowed to the judges. He removed his men and kote on the arena floor—a thing no one does; you do not undress in the place where you have fought—and he walked through the competitors' tunnel and into forty years of silence.

I won the All-Japan Championship that year. And the year after. And the year after that, until my knees failed and my shoulders became unreliable and I retired into teaching and the slow amber of reputation. I have been called the greatest kendoka of the postwar generation. This was never true. Kuroda Hideo was the greatest kendoka of the postwar generation, and perhaps of any generation, and this collection is my attempt to explain why I say so, and why the saying of it has cost me forty years of sleep.

His first letter arrived nine months after the final. I did not respond immediately. When I did respond, my words were—I believe—inadequate. I continued to respond inadequately for four decades. My own letters are not included here. Hideo did not keep them, or if he did, the monastery has not released them. This is appropriate. My letters were never the point. What is presented here is one half of a conversation that was itself only half of a deeper exchange, conducted in the space between what was written and what was meant.

I have added annotations where I felt context was necessary. I have tried to keep them brief. I have failed in this as I have failed in most things.

I publish this on the occasion of my own death, which the doctors have set, with the breezy confidence of men who will outlive me, somewhere within the next four months. I publish it without Hideo's explicit permission, which he has never granted, and without his explicit refusal, which he has never given. I publish it as the last act of a match that began forty-one years ago and that neither of us has found the grace to end.

The reader will draw conclusions. I ask only that you hold them loosely. Nothing in what follows is certain. That is, in fact, the entire point.

*Yamamoto Kenji*
*Kamakura, March 2023*

## Letter the First

*Postmarked August 1982. Written on paper of moderate quality, common to temple supply. The calligraphy is exceptional—fluid, unhurried, each stroke completed. This was the hand of a man who had not yet begun to doubt his own penmanship.*

---

Yamamoto-san,

I offer this letter with no expectation of reply and no right to one.

I am at Entsū-ji. I arrived eight months ago. The abbot is a man named Ryōgen. He is seventy-one and moves as though his joints have been individually negotiated with. He assigned me to firewood. I split logs from dawn until the mid-morning sutra. This is, I suspect, his commentary on my character.

I wish to say something about November. I find I cannot. I will try again in the next letter, if there is one. Please accept my congratulations on the championship. You fought beautifully in the semifinal. Takeda had no answer for your kote-nuki-men. I watched from the stands before our match and thought: *he has solved the problem of transition. He moves as though the interval between intention and execution has been surgically removed.*

I say this not to flatter. You know I am not capable of flattery. I say it because it is important to me that you understand I saw you clearly. I saw what you were. I saw what you had become.

That is all I can manage. The bell rings for evening practice. We do not practice kendo here—we practice the art of sitting until the legs achieve a state of philosophical crisis. Ryōgen says enlightenment begins in the knees. He may be having a joke at my expense.

I remain,
Hideo

---

**Compiler's note:** *I received this letter on a Tuesday. I remember this because I read it seven times before dinner and could not eat. The phrase "I saw you clearly" kept sounding in my head like a bell struck wrong. It was compliment or confession—I could not tell. I still cannot tell. I wrote back something about the weather.*

---

## Letter the Second

*Postmarked January 1983. The paper is the same. The ink is darker—he has begun grinding his own.*

---

Yamamoto-san,

Thank you for your reply. The weather in Kamakura sounds appropriate to the season.

You asked about my daily life. I will describe it, as it requires no courage.

I wake at half past three. I sit zazen until five. Chanting until six. Cleaning until seven. Breakfast—rice gruel, pickled radish, silence. Then labor. I have graduated from firewood to the garden, which Ryōgen says is a promotion but which I suspect is merely a relocation of suffering. The temple garden at Entsū-ji is composed of forty-seven stones, raked gravel, and one juniper that has been sculpted over two centuries into a shape suggesting—depending on the observer—a crane in flight, a wave cresting, or a monk's accumulated frustration.

I rake the gravel each morning. The patterns must be precise. When they are not, Ryōgen appears—he has a supernatural capacity for appearing—and says nothing, which is worse than anything he might say.

I have been thinking about the concept of *suki*—the opening, the gap. In kendo we are taught to perceive suki in the opponent and exploit it. In Zen we are taught that suki is the space through which enlightenment enters. These cannot both be true. Or perhaps they are the same truth spoken in two vocabularies and I am simply too stupid to translate.

I wanted to write about November. Instead I have written about gardening. Please forgive me. The stone I am circling is too large to approach directly.

Hideo

---

**Compiler's note:** *Suki. He wrote the word and I put the letter down and stood at my window for a very long time. In competitive kendo, the identification of suki—the moment when an opponent's concentration fractures, when a gap appears in their physical or mental guard—is the foundation of all victory. He was telling me something. Or he was telling himself something and I was merely the surface against which the telling echoed.*

---

## Letter the Fourth

*Postmarked November 1983. I omit the third letter, which concerns itself entirely with a detailed and surprisingly beautiful description of autumn persimmons. I do not believe the persimmons are metaphorical, though with Hideo, certainty was always premature.*

---

Yamamoto-san,

Two years now since the Budōkan.

I have begun teaching the young monks a simplified form of bokken practice—not kendo, precisely, but the basics of stance, grip, breath. Ryōgen approved it. He said: "The body is also a sutra." This is either deep wisdom or an old man's excuse to watch young monks hit each other with sticks. I respect him too much to determine which.

Teaching has returned me to first principles. I find myself examining what I thought I knew and discovering it is riddled with assumptions I cannot justify. For example: we teach that chudan-no-kamae is the foundation of all engagement. The sword held at center, point directed at the opponent's throat. From this, all attacks and defenses proceed. But why the throat? The throat is not the most efficient target. It is the most *honest* target—it announces itself. It says: here I am, and here is where I intend to strike. Chudan is less a tactical stance than a moral one. It is the posture of a man who has agreed to fight without deception.

But what happens, Yamamoto-san, when a man standing in chudan perceives something he should not? When the stance that was meant to be a contract of honesty instead becomes a window into another man's—

I have crossed out the next three lines. You may imagine I wrote something about the garden.

The persimmon tree has lost its leaves. The fruit hangs like unresolved questions.

I apologize. That was precisely the kind of metaphor I accused myself of avoiding.

Hideo

---

**Compiler's note:** *The crossed-out lines are real—three horizontal strokes of ink, methodical, deliberate. He did not scribble them out in haste. He eliminated them with the same precision he brought to everything. I held the paper to a lamp. The ink was too heavy to read through. He knew it would be. A man who spent his life seeking suki in others would know exactly how to seal his own.*

---

## Letter the Seventh

*Postmarked June 1985. Letters five and six detail his progress with Zen practice and a recurring disagreement with a younger monk named Tetsuo, who apparently snored with such commitment that even meditation could not encompass it. The letters are warm, even funny. I omit them not because they are unimportant but because their warmth makes what follows more difficult to bear.*

---

Yamamoto-san,

I watched your exhibition match at the Osaka Invitational on a small television in the monastery kitchen. Ryōgen permitted this. I believe he understood that some attachments must be loosened gradually, like a knot tied too tight, or they will break what they bind.

You were magnificent. Your footwork has changed—you slide less, anchor more. You have made the court smaller, which forces your opponents into your rhythm. This is intelligent. This is the adaptation of a man who knows his speed will decline and has therefore decided to make speed irrelevant. I admire it.

I must also tell you what I saw, because not telling you has become a weight I carry in my chest like a second heart, beating counter to the first.

When you move from chudan to your men strike—the overhead cut—there is a moment. I do not know if you are aware of it. Others are certainly not—I have watched recordings of your matches and no commentator has ever mentioned it. It occurs in the transition, the breath between intention and commitment. Forgive me. I have begun this confession eleven times across four letters and I cannot complete it. The words gather like water behind a dam. I know the shape of what I must say. I know the sound it will make when it falls. And I stop because beyond the dam are two rivers, and both lead to places I cannot allow either of us to go.

Ryōgen found me in the garden this morning standing completely still with the rake in my hands. He asked how long I had been standing there. I did not know. He said: "The gravel does not improve by being stared at." Then, more gently: "Neither do the things we cannot say."

I think he knows. Not the specifics—he was never a martial artist—but the shape of it. The architecture of a silence that has become load-bearing.

I will try again.

Hideo

---

**Compiler's note:** *Two-tenths of a second. He wrote "two-tenths of" and then the sentence collapses into that familiar cliff-edge. I know now what he was describing. I have spent thirty-eight years since this letter examining my own men strike in the mirror, in video, in the private theater of sleepless nights, and I believe I know the flaw he saw. I will not name it here, for the same reason he could not name it in his letters: to name it would be to resolve a tension that is, perhaps, the only real thing either of us ever created together. The reader should know this: the flaw he saw was not merely tactical. If exploited correctly—with proper timing, from the correct angle, at full competitive force—the counter-strike would land at the junction of the men-gane and the tsuki-dare, where the protective equipment admits the narrowest gap. A shinai striking this point at speed would—but here I find myself at his dam, looking down at his two rivers, and I understand him with a completeness that makes me want to put down my pen and stand in my own garden, holding my own rake, staring at my own gravel.*

*The two rivers, as I understand them: he could have struck me there and won the championship—his sixty-eighth consecutive victory, the completion of a career that would have been without parallel. But the strike, aimed at that gap, carried a probability of serious injury. Not certainty. Probability. Kendo is practiced with armor and bamboo swords specifically to prevent such things. But a shinai striking the throat at that precise angle, at the speed of which Hideo was capable—the probability was not zero. It was not even small.*

*Or he could have told me. He could have said: Yamamoto, your men strike exposes your throat for two-tenths of a second. Correct it. And I would have corrected it, and we would have fought fairly, and he would almost certainly have won anyway because he was the better kenshi and we both knew it and everyone knew it. But to tell me would have been to say: I could have destroyed you, and I chose not to, and here is the charity of my restraint. And to a man of Hideo's moral seriousness, this was as unacceptable as the strike itself. He could not be the man who wounded me, and he could not be the man who patronized me, and so he became the man who disappeared.*

*I have never asked him if this is correct. He has never confirmed it. We have spent forty years in the space between question and answer, and I am beginning to think this space is holy.*

## Letter the Tenth

*Postmarked April 1988. I omit letters eight and nine, in which Hideo describes his advancement in Zen practice, his growing fondness for calligraphy as a meditation, and a brief, startling passage about a fox that appeared in the temple garden and regarded him, he writes, "with the expression of a judge who has rendered a verdict but will not announce it." The tenth letter marks a shift. The letters before it were written by a man approaching a precipice. The tenth is written by a man who has decided to live on the precipice permanently.*

---
