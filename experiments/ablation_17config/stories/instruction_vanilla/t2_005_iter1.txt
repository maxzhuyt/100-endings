# Form 27-B: Application for Continued Existence

## Part One: Processing

The universe, it must be said, is not indifferent to suffering. Indifference would require a certain philosophical commitment, a deliberate turning-away that implies awareness of what one is turning away from. The universe is something worse than indifferent. The universe is *administered*.

This fact had been discovered seventeen years, two months, and six days ago—though "discovered" is perhaps too generous a word for the way humanity learned about the Auditors. One does not "discover" an avalanche. One is simply in the wrong valley at the wrong time, looking up.

They arrived on a Tuesday. This detail, meaningless in any cosmic sense, mattered very much to Gloria Alvarez-Whitfield, who had been working the late shift at the Department of Motor Vehicles in Bakersfield, California, when every screen in the building simultaneously displayed Form 1-A: Notice of Civilizational Audit. She remembered the Tuesday-ness of it because Tuesdays were her long days, and she had been in the middle of explaining to a man named Gerald Fong that his registration renewal required a secondary proof of residence, and Gerald Fong had been explaining in return, quite loudly, that he had lived in the same house for thirty-one years, and then both of them had stopped talking because the floor had begun to hum.

The Auditors did not look like anything. This was their primary visual characteristic. When humanity's telescopes and satellites turned toward the objects entering the solar system, the images came back as redaction—not blackness, not emptiness, but the specific visual quality of something that has been deliberately obscured, like a censored document the size of Jupiter. There were fourteen of them, arranged in a pattern that, it was later determined, corresponded to an organizational flowchart.

They did not attack Earth. "Attack" would be the wrong word in much the way that "attack" would be the wrong word for what happens to a tax return flagged for review. They *processed* it. Continents were not destroyed but *archived*. Oceans were not boiled but *filed*. Seven billion people were not killed so much as they were *resolved*, which meant roughly the same thing but came with different paperwork.

Three thousand and forty-one people survived. Not because of bunkers or heroism or the intervention of some benevolent god who had been paying attention, but because of a clerical error. The colony ship *USCSS Contingency*—built during the optimistic decade when humanity believed interstellar travel represented their next great achievement rather than, as it turned out, a triggering condition for audit—had been docked at the L2 Lagrange point for its final systems check. Its quantum-fold drive had been spooling up for a test firing. And its unique energy signature had, at the critical moment, been miscategorized by the Auditors' automated detection systems as a non-sapient navigational buoy.

It was the kind of error that, in any bureaucracy, human or otherwise, would eventually be caught during a routine reconciliation. But routine reconciliations, as Gloria Alvarez-Whitfield would later learn with a mix of horror and professional recognition, were scheduled on a cycle of approximately nine hundred thousand years.

Captain Aurelia Vasquez had made the only decision available to her. She fired the quantum-fold drive before the test sequence was complete, aimed the *Contingency* in the direction that was most emphatically *away*, and began what she privately called humanity's "unscheduled departure." Publicly, to the 3,041 souls in her care—a number that fluctuated over the years through births, deaths, one marriage-related fistfight that went too far, and an incident with an airlock that was officially ruled accidental—she called it "the mission."

The mission had a destination. Before the processing of Earth had been completed, a burst of data had been received from one of the deep-space monitoring arrays—one last gasp of human infrastructure before it was archived. The data contained coordinates and, more importantly, a word: *Sanctuary*. The word had been extracted, through heroic feats of decryption, from the edge of an Auditor transmission, and it appeared to reference a location—a planet, or something like a planet—that existed outside the Auditors' jurisdiction.

Seventeen years the *Contingency* had been traveling toward that word. Seventeen years of recycled air and hydroponics and a water reclamation system that worked beautifully but gave everything a faint taste of yesterday. Seventeen years of a community that had calcified into something between a family and a very small, very tense nation-state. They had formed committees. They had held elections. They had established a school for the children born in transit, who had never seen a sky, and a counseling service for the adults who could not stop seeing one every time they closed their eyes.

And seventeen years in, at a point in deep space equidistant from nothing and nothing else, they found the first Waiting Room.

---

It should be noted, for the sake of the record—and the Auditors were extremely concerned with the record—that the Waiting Room was not a room in the conventional sense. It occupied a region of space that had been zoned, in the Auditors' terminology, as a "processing nexus," which meant that the normal laws of physics had been filled out in triplicate and submitted for temporary suspension. The *Contingency*'s sensors interpreted it as a sphere approximately three kilometers in diameter that contained, impossibly, an interior volume roughly the size of Belgium. This interior volume was filled with chairs.

Not comfortable chairs. These were the chairs of waiting rooms across every civilization that had ever risen to prominence and then been processed: molded, unyielding, designed by some universal intelligence that understood ergonomics and had decided against them. They were arranged in rows that curved away into a foggy middle distance, and they were approximately sixty percent occupied.

This last detail was the one that made Captain Vasquez put her fist through her desk monitor, which she regretted immediately, because replacement monitors were allocated from a inventory that had been dwindling for seventeen years.

"There are *others*," said Lieutenant Commander Okafor-Singh, who served as the ship's science officer and whose primary qualification for that role was that she had been a graduate student in astrophysics and was therefore comfortable with not understanding things. "Ma'am, the signatures—the biosignatures in that structure. There are at least forty distinct species."

"Alive," said Vasquez. It was not a question. It was an attempt to make something true by stating it with sufficient authority, a technique she had relied on for seventeen years with mixed results.

"Alive and... waiting, it looks like. Some of these thermal patterns suggest they've been in there for—" Okafor-Singh paused, rechecked her readings, and then said, in the tone of someone who would like to file a formal complaint with mathematics, "—centuries."

"Waiting for *what*?"

"I think," said Okafor-Singh, "they're waiting for their number to be called."

---

Gloria Alvarez-Whitfield was fifty-three years old, though she looked sixty and felt ninety, which was the standard conversion rate for anyone who had spent seventeen years as the last of the human race's bureaucratic infrastructure. Before the Auditors, she had processed driver's license renewals, vehicle registrations, and the occasional request for a personalized plate (denied, usually, because the applicants consistently underestimated Gloria's ability to spot disguised profanity). After the Auditors, she had become the *Contingency*'s head of administrative services, a title she had given herself on the second day of the voyage when she realized that no one else was going to organize the survivor manifest and that if she didn't do it, people were going to start forgetting who was alive.

She was good at her job in the way that certain people are good at their jobs: not with passion or joy, but with a religious devotion to procedure that verged on the existential. Gloria believed, in the deepest chambers of her heart, that the world was a place of entropy and chaos, and that the only force holding back the void was the correct form, filled out correctly, submitted to the correct office, by the correct deadline. This belief had been, before the Auditors, a personality quirk that made her simultaneously indispensable and difficult to be around at parties. After the Auditors, it was something closer to a survival strategy.

She had been the one to decode the first Auditor communications. Not because she understood the mathematics—she did not—but because she understood the *structure*. When the *Contingency*'s communications array began receiving transmissions from the Auditors' automated systems, the linguists and mathematicians had stared at the data in bewilderment. Gloria had looked at it for fifteen minutes and said, "This is a form letter."

She was right. It was a form letter. Specifically, it was a Post-Action Summary of Civilizational Processing, addressed to "the responsible administrative entity of [SPECIES DESIGNATION: HUMAN (SOL III)]," informing said entity that Earth had been assessed, found to be in violation of Provision 7,749,812.4(c) of the Interstellar Expansion Regulatory Framework, and accordingly archived. The letter invited the responsible administrative entity to submit a Form 27-B: Application for Continued Existence, within one standard processing cycle, if they wished to contest the determination.

One standard processing cycle, Gloria had calculated after three weeks of cross-referencing the Auditors' temporal notation system with what humanity knew about physics, was approximately forty-two days.

That had been sixteen years and eleven months ago.

"So we missed the deadline," Captain Vasquez had said, in the flat voice of a woman who had already lost a planet and was not going to be daunted by a calendar.

"By roughly sixteen years."

"Is there a late filing option?"

Gloria had, at that moment, experienced something she could only describe as professional kinship with whoever—or whatever—had designed the Auditors' systems. Because of course there was a late filing option. There was *always* a late filing option. The late filing option was Form 27-B-1: Petition for Extension of Filing Deadline for Form 27-B, which required, as attachments, a Form 27-B-1(a): Demonstration of Extenuating Circumstances, a Form 27-B-1(b): Inventory of Surviving Assets, and, grimly, a Form 27-B-1(c): Certificate of Partial or Total Civilizational Loss, which had to be notarized by a recognized Auditor processing agent.

"And where," Vasquez had asked, "do we find a recognized processing agent?"

Gloria had looked again at the data. "I think," she said slowly, "they have offices."

---

The Waiting Room was, in fact, an office. Or, more precisely, it was the antechamber to an office, in the same way that purgatory is the antechamber to a decision. The *Contingency* docked—or was docked; the ship seemed to be drawn inward by a process that felt less like tractor-beam physics and more like taking a number—at one of several thousand berths along the Waiting Room's exterior. Each berth was sized differently. Some were barely large enough for a shuttle. One, seven berths down from the *Contingency*, appeared to accommodate a vessel the size of a small moon.

Captain Vasquez assembled a landing party, a term she used with full awareness of its absurdity given that they were entering what appeared to be a cosmic DMV. The party consisted of herself, Gloria, a security detail of four people who were armed with weapons that would almost certainly be useless against bureaucratic entities that could archive planets, and—because he had been standing near the airlock and no one had told him to leave—Dr. Tomás Reyes, the ship's only psychiatrist, who felt that someone should be present to monitor the psychological impact of whatever they were about to experience.

The airlock opened onto a corridor that smelled like nothing. Not like the absence of smell, but like *nothing*—a positive, assertive nothingness that filled the nostrils and informed them that this space had been deliberately de-scented, that smell was not a feature of this environment and any expectations to the contrary should be filed on the appropriate form. The corridor was beige. It was the most aggressive beige Gloria had ever encountered, a beige that had moved beyond color and become a philosophy.

They followed the corridor to the main waiting area, and there they found the others.

Forty-three species, as it turned out. Forty-three civilizations, or the remnants thereof, all of which had achieved interstellar travel, all of which had been subsequently processed, and all of which had managed, through some combination of luck, error, or sheer bewildered tenacity, to survive in sufficient numbers to make their way to this place and take a seat.

Some were recognizable as living things. Bipedal forms, tentacled forms, a shimmering column of what appeared to be sentient light that occupied an entire row by itself and pulsed in a pattern that Dr. Reyes immediately recognized as anxiety. Others defied easy categorization. In one corner, a delegation of beings that looked like ambulatory coral formations sat in perfect stillness, their surface textures shifting in slow, contemplative patterns. Nearby, a group of small, dark-furred creatures—imagine a badger designed by a mathematician—were engaged in a heated discussion conducted entirely in ultrasonic squeaks, passing documents back and forth with a fervor that Gloria found deeply, painfully relatable.

One of the badger-mathematicians noticed the humans and waddled over with the particular gait of someone who has been waiting for a very long time and has developed a philosophical relationship with that waiting.

"New?" it said, through a translation device clamped to its chest that made it sound like a British grandmother.

"We—yes," said Captain Vasquez, who had imagined first contact many times and had never pictured it happening in a waiting room. "We're human. From—from Earth. Sol system."

"Ah. Processed?"

"Yes."

"Survivors?"

"Three thousand. Roughly."

The creature made a sound that the translator rendered as a sympathetic tsk. "Not bad, not bad. We were down to twelve. Twelve! Can you imagine filing a Form 27-B with a population of twelve? The demographic projections alone—" It caught itself with visible effort. "Apologies. I'm Coordinator Voss. That's not my name, of course, that's just what the translator picked. My name is—" It emitted a sound like a pipe organ being fed through a paper shredder. "—but Voss will do. How long since your processing?"

"Seventeen years," said Gloria.

Voss's dark eyes widened. "Seventeen years and you're only *now* filing? Oh dear. Oh, that's going to be a problem. Have you submitted your 27-B?"

"We've been... traveling."

"Without filing? My dear creature, the deadline—"

"We know about the deadline," said Gloria, in the patient, immovable tone that had guided ten thousand Bakersfield residents through the requirements for a Real ID. "We're here to file a late petition."

"A 27-B-1? After seventeen years?" Voss's fur rippled in a way that seemed to indicate either distress or amusement or both. "Well. Well well well. You'll want Window 7,461. Down the main hall, past the Atemporal Refreshment Zone—don't eat anything there, it's technically from the future and causes ontological indigestion—left at the Reclassified Species Memorial, and then... just keep walking. You'll know you've gone too far if you start experiencing déjà vu, because that means you've wandered into the Temporal Filing Annex and you'll have submitted your form before you've filled it out, which causes no end of problems."

Gloria listened to these directions, nodded once, and committed them to memory with the same precision she had once applied to the location of every form, sub-form, and interdepartmental memo in the Bakersfield DMV.
