# The Hateful Immortals
## A Documentary History of the Greenbriar-Chen Marriage, 1847–2024

**by Dr. Amelia Thornton**
*Farnsworth Chair of Parahuman Studies, Columbia University*

*Winner of the National Book Award for Nonfiction*
*Winner of the Bancroft Prize in American History*

---

## A Note on Method

This volume represents fourteen years of research into what I have come to regard, with neither irony nor exaggeration, as the most significant marriage in recorded history. Not the happiest—the Greenbriars themselves would be the first to disabuse you of that notion—but certainly the longest continuously documented union between two living persons, and arguably the richest case study we possess of what parahuman abilities do to the most ordinary of human institutions.

I have had the extraordinary privilege of conducting over three hundred hours of interviews with both Margaret Greenbriar and James Chen, sometimes together (which I do not recommend), sometimes apart (which each of them prefers, and which each of them accuses the other of preferring first). I have reviewed their personal correspondence from 1847 to the present, a collection spanning over eleven thousand letters, notes, and—from approximately 1997 onward—increasingly hostile text messages. I have examined court filings from nine countries. I have consulted parish records, passenger manifests, property deeds, and one remarkably ill-advised joint Christmas letter from 1974 that circulated among their acquaintances in Melbourne and which several recipients independently described to me as "the most upsetting thing I have ever read."

Where I quote Margaret Greenbriar's recollections of past events, I do so with the understanding—verified by extensive scholarship on eidetic parahumans—that her recall is essentially perfect. She does not paraphrase. She does not embellish. When she tells you what James said to her on the evening of March 14, 1888, she is reporting his exact words, his exact tone, and, if pressed, the exact way he was standing and what the weather was doing outside. James has never once disputed the accuracy of her quotations. He disputes, instead, their *relevance*, which is a different problem entirely and, I would argue, the central problem of their marriage.

A word on terminology. Throughout this volume I refer to the subjects as "Margaret" and "James" rather than by the shifting constellation of surnames they have employed across seventeen decades. They have been, at various points, the Chens, the Greenbriars, the Delacroix family, the Porters, the Navarros, the Blackwoods, and—during a particularly hostile stretch in 1930s Shanghai—two unrelated individuals who happened, by sheer coincidence, to live in the same apartment. Margaret currently uses Greenbriar. James currently uses Chen. They have not agreed on a shared surname since 1979, and I did not consider it my place to force the issue.

## Part One: A Courtship in the Age of Miracles (1847–1853)

### Chapter 1: Boston

The first parahumans were documented in 1802, when a schoolteacher in Hertfordshire named Anne Calloway woke on her thirteenth birthday and discovered she could see through walls. By the time Margaret Greenbriar was born in 1831—the third daughter of a middling textile merchant in Boston's South End—the phenomenon was sufficiently understood that parents of means had begun watching their children's adolescence with a mixture of dread and anticipation. Roughly one in every four hundred humans developed a parahuman ability at puberty. The abilities followed no discernible pattern. They could not be predicted, bred for, or replicated. A child might gain the power to ignite fires with a thought, or she might gain the power to always know the precise temperature of any liquid within arm's reach. The abilities ranged from the godlike to the bathetic, and no amount of good breeding or moral instruction seemed to influence which end of the spectrum one's child might land on.

Margaret's ability announced itself in the autumn of 1844, when she was thirteen. She was reading a novel—she would later identify it as a pirated American edition of *Martin Chuzzlewit*—and realized, with a growing sense of unease, that she could recall every word of every page she had already read. Not merely the gist. Every word. Every comma. She closed the book and found she could reproduce the text of any page at will. She then discovered the same was true of everything else: every conversation she had ever had, every face she had ever seen, every scent and texture and passing cloud.

"I didn't understand it as a curse at first," Margaret told me in our initial interview, in the parlor of the Portland bookshop she would soon be forced to abandon. She has a direct, unsparing way of speaking. She is a small woman—she was small in 1844, and has been continuously small ever since—with dark hair that she currently wears shorter than James likes, a fact she mentioned to me three times without my asking. "I thought, how marvelous. I shall never forget anything. I was thirteen. I hadn't yet accumulated anything worth forgetting."

James Chen's early life is less well documented, a fact that bothers Margaret considerably and which she attributes to James's lifelong habit of being "cavalier about things that matter." He was born in 1829 in San Francisco to a Cantonese merchant family—his father, Chen Wei-Lin, had emigrated in 1820 and built a modest trading business. James's parahuman ability manifested when he was fourteen, in 1843, though the full scope of it did not become apparent until somewhat later. He fell from a warehouse roof and was, by all accounts, completely unharmed.

"I thought I'd gotten lucky," James told me. He is a tall man, somewhat rangy, with a face that Margaret once described, in a letter to her sister dated June 1851, as "the most handsome I have ever seen, God help me," and which she now describes as "still unfortunately handsome, which is part of the problem." He smiles often. Margaret has identified this as one of his most intolerable qualities. "It took a few more accidents before I realized something was different. I got kicked by a horse. Nothing. Cut myself fairly badly working on a ship. Watched it heal in about ten seconds. Then there was the incident with the boiler, which I won't go into, but after that I was fairly certain."

James's ability, as it is now understood, is comprehensive biological immortality paired with complete resistance to physical damage. He does not age. He cannot be cut, burned, drowned, poisoned, crushed, or harmed in any discernible way. He has been shot fourteen times across the course of his life—mostly by accident, twice by Margaret—and reports that the experience is "briefly annoying."

("I didn't *shoot* him," Margaret clarified, when I raised this point. "I discharged a firearm during an argument and the bullet *happened* to strike him, which is a meaningful distinction. Both times. And the first time, in Dodge City, was arguably his fault for standing where he was standing, which was in our kitchen, eating the last of my preserves without asking, which is what the argument was about in the first place."

"It was about significantly more than preserves," James said, from the other room, where he had been pretending not to listen.

"I am providing Dr. Thornton with the proximate cause."

"The proximate cause was that you were angry about something I'd said in 1869."

"I was alive in 1869! Things that happened to me while I was alive are allowed to be relevant! Amelia, please note that he thinks my memories have an expiration date, like *milk*.")

They met in Boston in 1847. Margaret was sixteen; James was eighteen. The circumstances of their meeting are, unusually for this marriage, one of the few points on which they completely agree. James had traveled east on business for his father's firm. He was attending a public lecture on the topic of parahuman abilities—the parlor-lecture circuit was booming in this period—and Margaret was in the audience with her elder sister, Catherine. James was seated one row behind them. During a pause in the lecture, Margaret dropped a glove. James retrieved it.

"He said, 'I believe this is yours,'" Margaret recounted. Even now—and I must emphasize that I conducted this interview in 2019, one hundred and seventy-two years after the event—her voice changed slightly when she said this. "He had a very particular way of looking at you. As if you were the only important thing in the room. He still does it, actually. It's infuriating."

"She looked at me," James said, simply, "and I thought, *oh, I'm in trouble.*"

Their courtship unfolded over the following months, complicated by distance—James returned to San Francisco, and they corresponded by letter—and by the social realities of the era. Interracial marriages were not illegal in Massachusetts in 1847, but they were far from commonplace, and the Greenbriar family's reaction ranged from Catherine's quiet support to their father's frank disapproval. Margaret's mother, Edith Greenbriar—who figures prominently in later chapters of this history, albeit posthumously—made her opposition known in terms that Margaret, with her perfect memory, would quote back to James during arguments for the next several decades.

("Her mother was a pestilent harridan," James said to me, flatly, in 2021, preempting a line of questioning I had not yet begun. "I stand by it. I have stood by it since 1898, when I first said it, and I will stand by it when the sun burns out."

"My mother had been dead for twelve years when he said that," Margaret observed. "Twelve years. And he said it during a dinner party. In front of the Hendersons."

"The Hendersons agreed with me."

"The Hendersons were *drunk*, James.")

But in 1847, the opposition of parents and society proved no match for what every piece of surviving evidence suggests was a genuine, almost reckless love. James made three trips to Boston between the autumn of 1847 and the spring of 1848. He wrote Margaret forty-one letters in this period, of which I have been able to locate and examine thirty-seven. They are—and I say this as a scholar who has spent a career studying documents and who is not given to sentimentality—remarkable. James Chen at eighteen was a man writing with the peculiar fearlessness of someone who perhaps already sensed, on some level, that he had all the time in the world. His letter of February 3, 1848, includes the passage:

> *I have given considerable thought to the question you posed in your last, namely whether I think our natures—yours to remember, mine to endure—make us particularly suited or particularly ill-suited for a life together. I confess I don't know. But I think most people enter marriage without knowing, and they have the luxury of forgetting the question. We will not. You will remember that I could not answer you, and I will survive whatever consequences follow from my silence. Perhaps this is as good a foundation as any. We are at least starting with open eyes.*

Margaret, for her part, did not need to write down her feelings to preserve them, but she did so anyway—a habit she would later describe as "the only truly romantic period of my penmanship." Her letter of February 19, 1848, in response:

> *You are wrong that most people enter marriage without knowing. Most people enter marriage with hope, which is a form of knowing that has not yet been tested. I remember everything, James. Every kind word and every slight. Every moment of joy and every tedium. I will not have the mercy of nostalgia. I will not be able to sand down our worst days into something bearable. You must understand what you are asking of yourself. You are asking to be married to a woman who will hold you to every promise, because she will remember every promise exactly as it was made. Most men would find this intolerable. I need you to tell me that you will not.*

He wrote back on March 7:

> *I will not find it intolerable. And if I do, you may remind me that I said so.*

"The idiot," Margaret said, when I read this passage back to her. She was quiet for a moment. "The dear, sweet idiot."

### Chapter 2: The Wedding and the Early Years

They were married on September 12, 1849, in a small ceremony at the home of Margaret's sister Catherine. Margaret's parents did not attend. James's father sent a letter of congratulation and a bolt of Chinese silk that Margaret still owns and keeps in a cedar chest in whatever home they currently occupy. It is, she told me, "one of the few objects I have never considered throwing at him."

The first years of the marriage were, by all accounts, happy. They settled in Boston, where James established himself as an import broker. Margaret, freed from the necessity of ever taking notes, developed a reputation as an extraordinarily effective bookkeeper, and managed the accounts for James's business with a precision that bordered on the supernatural—which, of course, it was. They were discrete about their abilities. The social position of parahumans in the 1850s was precarious; the Parahuman Registration Act would not pass until 1881, and in the meantime, those with abilities generally found it prudent to keep quiet.

The trouble with keep quiet, of course, was biological. By 1855, James was twenty-six years old and looked it. By 1865, he was thirty-six and still looked twenty-six. Margaret, whose ability was cognitive rather than physical, was aging normally—a point that would not become a source of friction for several more decades, but which she was already tracking with the meticulous precision that characterized her approach to all data.

"I began keeping a log," she told me. "A physical log, I mean—I didn't need one for my own reference, obviously, but I wanted documentation. Every year, on his birthday, I would note his appearance relative to mine. In 1860, we looked roughly the same age. By 1870, I looked older than him. By 1880, noticeably so. He never mentioned it. He never once mentioned it. He is both chivalrous and a coward in roughly equal measure, and in this instance I was unable to determine which quality was responsible for his silence."

"I didn't mention it because it didn't matter to me," James said.

"Everything matters to me, James. That is literally my defining characteristic."

Their first serious argument—the first one Margaret classifies as a *real* fight, as opposed to the ordinary bickering of cohabitation—occurred in November 1852, three years into the marriage. The subject was whether to attend a rally in support of abolition. James wanted to go. Margaret also wanted to go, but felt it would draw unwanted attention to their household, given that they were already conspicuous as an interracial couple.

"I said something I shouldn't have," James told me. "I said she was more worried about respectability than about what was right."

"What he said," Margaret corrected, from across the room, "was: 'You would rather be comfortable than courageous, and I am beginning to think you always will be.' It was November 14, 1852. It was raining. He was wearing his brown coat—the one with the torn lining he refused to mend—and he was standing by the window, and the light was coming in gray behind him, and I could see that he didn't entirely mean it but that he had said it anyway, which was worse than if he'd meant it completely."

A silence followed this recitation. James, I noted, did not dispute a single detail.

"That is the thing people do not understand about my ability," Margaret continued. "They think the pain is in remembering the words. It isn't. The words are just words. The pain is in remembering everything *around* the words—his exact face, the exact quality of the light, exactly how much I loved him at the precise moment he said something terrible. I can't flatten it. I can't let the feelings go vague. I am perpetually standing in November 1852, and it is perpetually raining."

They did attend the rally. They did not speak to each other for eleven days afterward, a personal record that would stand until 1917.
