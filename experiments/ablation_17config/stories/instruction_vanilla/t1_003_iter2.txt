# The Collectors

## Part One: What Arrives

**Marion**

The letter couldn't fit through the mail slot.

This was not unusual. Very little fit through Marion Tate's mail slot anymore, on account of the seventeen years' worth of magazines, catalogs, and coupon circulars she'd stacked behind the door in columns that rose to her collarbone like a paper city skyline. The mailman had to wedge the envelope diagonally, and it jutted out at an angle that bothered her for a full forty minutes before she navigated the canyon between her foyer's twin walls of bankers' boxes to retrieve it.

Plain white envelope. No return address. Her name typeset in a font that looked medical.

She opened it standing in the only clear square footage in her kitchen—a patch of linoleum roughly the size of a bath mat, surrounded on all sides by stacked cookware, broken appliances she intended to repair, and nine identical toasters she'd purchased during what her therapist gently called "an episode."

*Dear Marion,*

*Twenty years is a long time to keep something buried. But I suppose you'd know about keeping things, wouldn't you?*

*Camp Abenaki. July 19th. Midnight.*

*Bring what you can carry. You'll need to retrieve what you left behind. You have until dawn.*

*Come alone, but not too alone. The others are receiving this same letter. All four must attend, or what I know becomes what everyone knows.*

*You buried a boy. I buried my silence. One of us is ready to dig.*

Marion read the letter twice. Then she folded it, placed it in a Ziploc bag, labeled the bag with a date and category (*CORRESPONDENCE—THREATENING*), and filed it in the box she kept for items that made her hands shake.

Then she sat on a stack of National Geographics from 1987 through 1994 and pressed her palms against her eyes until the colors stopped.

**Dee**

Dolores "Dee" Kowalski read her letter in the bathroom of a Nordstrom in Tysons Corner, Virginia, with a pair of unworn $340 Louboutin pumps in her oversized handbag and a heart rate she could feel in her teeth.

She hadn't meant to take the shoes. She never meant to take anything. That was the central joke of her condition, if you had the kind of humor that found it funny—that a woman who earned $220,000 a year as a corporate litigation attorney at a Washington firm could not walk through a department store without her fingers developing their own gravitational agenda.

The shoes were red. She didn't wear red. She didn't even like these shoes. But her hand had closed around the left one while she was reading a text from her assistant, and by the time her prefrontal cortex caught up with her limbic system, she was already in the bathroom stall, hyperventilating in that specific way that meant the thing had happened again.

She read the letter. She read it once more.

Then she put the shoes back, which she managed about thirty percent of the time—a personal best she was cautiously proud of—and walked to her car with her fists balled in her coat pockets, fingernails biting crescents into her palms.

She hadn't thought about Camp Abenaki in four years, which was down from every single day for the first sixteen.

She sat in her Audi and called a number she hadn't dialed since the last time one of them had broken the pact.

It rang eleven times. No voicemail.

"Marion," she said to the dead air. Then she started the car.

---

**Cal**

Calvin Reeves was filming a workout video in his garage gym when the letter was delivered, so his wife Jenna signed for it for him, which meant he didn't see it until after he'd finished his Optimal Morning Sequence (forty-five minutes of calisthenics, mobility work, and affirmations delivered to a ring light) and his Optimal Midday Fuel (a meal-prepped container of chicken breast and sweet potato that he ate in precise, timed bites while standing at the kitchen counter, because Cal ate every meal standing up, because sitting made it too easy to eat too much, and eating too much made it too easy to—).

He did not finish this thought. He was very practiced at not finishing this thought.

Cal had 1.2 million followers on Instagram under the handle @CalReevesBuilt. His bio read *Discipline Is Freedom.* His content was shirtless motivation. His DMs were full of men who wanted his macros and women who wanted his attention and he answered all of them with the same bright, empty enthusiasm because the alternative was silence, and silence was where the thoughts finished themselves.

He read the letter in the bathroom. Door locked. Fan on.

*You buried a boy.*

His stomach clenched with the old, familiar electricity. The one that started in his gut and ended in his throat. He breathed through it. Four counts in. Seven counts hold. Eight counts out.

He turned the fan up higher.

"Babe?" Jenna called from the other side of the door. "You okay?"

"Great," Cal said. "Totally great."

He pressed his forehead against the cool tile wall and counted backward from one hundred.

---

**Nora**

Nora Vásquez read her letter at a poker table in the back room of a laundromat in Revere, Massachusetts, during a hand she was losing with money she didn't have.

She'd been up eleven thousand. Then she'd been up six. Then she'd been down two, which was the point at which a reasonable person would have left, and then she'd been down nine, which was the point at which Nora's brain released the chemical that felt like God reaching down and lighting a match behind her sternum, the one that said *the next hand, the next card, the next spin of whatever it is that's spinning*, and she couldn't leave now because leaving meant the number was real and the number could not be real.

The letter was in her jacket pocket. She'd read it in her car before coming in, and it had made her feel the thing she always felt before she gambled—the tidal pull toward action, any action, the need to be in motion toward an outcome, any outcome, because uncertainty was the only state in which Nora felt fully alive and fully annihilated at the same time.

*You buried a boy.*

Danny Resko. Twelve years old. Asthmatic. Freckled. Could not swim.

She folded. The table looked at her like she'd spoken in tongues. Nora Vásquez did not fold.

She drove home to her apartment, which was mostly empty because she'd sold most of her furniture during a bad month in February, and she sat on the floor with her back against the wall and her phone in her hand.

She did not call Marion. She did not call Dee. She did not call Cal.

Instead, she Googled "Camp Abenaki, New Hampshire" and found nothing. The place had been wiped from the internet as thoroughly as they'd tried to wipe it from themselves.

She booked a rental car for July 18th. Then she opened an online poker app and played until four in the morning, winning enough to feel like she existed and losing enough to feel like she deserved to.

## Part Two: What Remains

The camp was two hours north of Concord, down a logging road that had decomposed into something more suggestion than path. Marion's Subaru went first because Marion had the highest clearance and because Marion refused to leave her car behind, having packed it with what she described as "essentials" and what the others, arriving in their respective vehicles behind her, recognized as approximately three hundred pounds of categorized survival gear, organized into labeled bins.

"You brought a laminator," Dee said, staring into the open hatchback.

"It's for the labels," Marion said.

"What labels?"

"For the bins."

"The bins are already labeled."

"Those are temporary labels. The laminated ones are permanent."

Dee looked at Cal. Cal looked at Nora. Nora was staring at the tree line with the expression of someone calculating odds.

It was 11:47 PM on July 19th. The moon was three-quarters full, which cast the remains of Camp Abenaki in the kind of blue-silver light that made everything look like a memory even while you were standing in it. The main lodge had partially collapsed, its roof caved in on one side like a hat worn at a rakish angle. The cabins—eight of them, arranged in a semicircle around the cold fire pit—were still standing, though several had been colonized so thoroughly by vegetation that they appeared to be in the process of becoming trees. The lake was invisible from where they stood, but they could smell it—that mineral, algal scent that operates on the brainstem like a key in a lock, opening rooms you thought you'd sealed.

"Well," Cal said. He clapped his hands once, the way he did in his videos before a set. "This is bleak."

"You look enormous," Nora said to him. It was the first thing she'd said to any of them. "You look like you ate the Cal I remember and then worked out about it."

"Nora."

"Cal."

They regarded each other across twenty years of silence. Then Nora laughed—a cracked, startled sound—and Cal's face did something complicated, and he crossed the distance and hugged her, lifting her a full foot off the ground, and for a moment they were twelve and thirteen and the world had not yet taught them what they were capable of.

Dee hung back. Marion hung back further. There was a geometry to the four of them that had always been asymmetric—Cal and Nora were the combustion, the ones who ran toward things, and Dee and Marion were the collecting, the ones who picked up what got left behind. It had been this way when they were children and it was this way now, watching Cal set Nora down and turn to them with his arms open and his smile uncertain.

"Group hug?" he offered.

"I'd rather eat glass," Dee said.

"Dee."

"Don't 'Dee' me. I haven't heard from any of you in four years. Six, in your case." She pointed at Cal. "The last time I called you, your wife told me you were at a fitness retreat in Sedona, and I could hear you in the background telling her to say you weren't home."

"That's—" Cal's jaw worked. "That's actually exactly what happened. Yeah."

"And you." Dee turned to Nora. "I sent you money. Twice. Because you called me at three in the morning saying you were going to lose your apartment. And you never called back. Either time."

Nora's expression didn't change. "I paid you back."
