# The Collectors

## Part One: What We Keep

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Nora Chen would later consider appropriate, since Tuesdays had always been the day things went to shit.

She found it wedged between a ConEdison bill and a coupon mailer for a grocery store that had closed in 2019, which she kept anyway because the paper stock was good and you never knew when you might need card-weight paper in a warm cream tone. The envelope was handmade—she could tell by the slightly uneven edges, the way the flap had been folded by hand against a ruler rather than machine-cut. She appreciated that. Inside, on a single sheet of typewritten paper, were forty-seven words that collapsed the careful architecture of her adult life:

*Dear Nora,*

*I know what happened at Camp Wychwood. I know what you buried. I know what you've become. Come to the camp at midnight on June 14th. Bring the others. Dig up what you left behind, or I'll dig it up for you—and deliver it to everyone who matters.*

There was no signature.

Nora read it three times. Then she folded it back into its handmade envelope and placed it inside a shoebox labeled CORRESPONDENCE — THREATENING on the shelf nearest the door of her apartment, which was the only shelf she could still reach without climbing over the waist-high canyon walls of cataloged debris that constituted her home.

Her hands were shaking, but that was fine. She had a system for shaking hands. She picked up the nearest object—a 2017 calendar featuring Impressionist paintings, March, a Monet of water lilies—and held it against her chest and breathed until the world stopped vibrating.

Twenty years. She had not spoken to any of them in twenty years.

She pulled the letter back out.

*Bring the others.*

Three hundred miles southwest, in the Georgetown townhouse he'd purchased for an amount that required a hyphen when spoken aloud, Declan Moriarty opened the same letter with a Montblanc letter opener he had stolen from a Bethesda stationery shop nine days prior. He read it once, felt his pulse detonate in his wrists, and immediately stole a pen from his own desk—clicking it into his breast pocket with the furtive smoothness that had become, over two decades of practice, as involuntary as blinking.

It didn't count, stealing from yourself. He knew that. His therapist, Dr. Agarwal, had explained at length that it didn't count. But Dr. Agarwal didn't understand that the count was not the point. The point was the click. The moment of *transfer*. The microsecond where something was there and then was here and the world rearranged itself around the absence so seamlessly you'd think nothing had happened at all.

Dec read the letter again. His first thought, honed by seventeen years of practicing law: *This is not legally actionable blackmail unless there's a specific demand for consideration.*

His second thought, from somewhere much older and less litigable: *Oh God. Oh God, they know about Elliot.*

He picked up his phone. Put it down. Picked it up. His contact list still had the old numbers—he'd never deleted them, though he couldn't have said why. He was meticulous about cleaning out his contacts. Clients who'd moved on, opposing counsel from settled cases, women from dates that led nowhere. All pruned. But those three numbers sat in his phone like baby teeth he couldn't bring himself to throw away.

He called the first one.

Margot Vine was standing in her kitchen at 11:47 PM, illuminated by the blue light of an open Sub-Zero refrigerator that cost more than some people's cars, engaged in the nightly ritual she thought of, with grim self-awareness, as *The Ballet.*

First position: stand before the fridge. Second position: open the door. Third position: remove the leftover risotto, the good Parmesan, the crème fraîche. Fourth position: eat standing up, quickly, mechanically, without the ceramic plates and cloth napkins and thoughtful plating she used for her 2.3 million Instagram followers. Fifth position—

Her phone rang. She stared at it. The caller ID said DECLAN MORIARTY, a name she had not seen on any screen since she was fourteen years old, and the risotto turned to cement in her mouth.

She answered.

"I know," she said, before he could speak.

"You got one too?"

"This morning. Dec—"

"Don't say it. Not on the phone."

A pause. She could hear him breathing, and it transported her so violently to the darkness of a July night twenty years gone that she had to grip the counter with both hands. She heard lake water. She heard screaming.

"Have you called Ty?" she asked.

"You're the first. I didn't know if any of you would—I mean, it's been—"

"Twenty years."

"Yeah."

She closed the refrigerator door. Opened it. Closed it.

"I'll call Ty," she said.

Tyler Banks was where Tyler Banks always was at midnight on a weekday: in the back room of a bar in Koreatown, Los Angeles, watching two men he did not respect play poker with a degree of strategic incompetence that made his teeth ache. He had nine thousand dollars on the table. He had been up eleven thousand dollars an hour ago and twelve thousand dollars thirty minutes before that, and some calm, well-lit room in his brain was doing the math on his rate of loss and projecting, with the cold clarity that made him an excellent AP Statistics teacher when he bothered to show up, that he'd be even by 1 AM and broke by 2.

His phone buzzed. He glanced at it preparation for ignoring it and saw MARGOT VINE and felt something move in his chest like a creature turning in its sleep.

He picked up.

"Tell me you got one," she said.

"Got what?"

"A letter, Ty. A letter about Wychwood."

His cards blurred. The nines and sevens and faces swam together into the color of lake water at night, dark and opaque, keeping its secrets.

"I haven't checked my mail in three days," he said, which was true. He hadn't been home in three days either, but that was a different confession for a different moment.

"Check it. Then call Dec. He'll explain."

"Margot—"

"June fourteenth. That's eleven days."

"Margot, I haven't talked to any of you in—"

"I know how long it's been, Ty."

He listened to the bar sounds around him, the clack of chips, the murmur of men trying to outthink each other. Games of chance, Dr. Reeves called his addiction. But nothing about Ty's gambling had ever been chance. He could calculate odds the way other people could read faces. The problem was he kept playing past the math, into the territory where the numbers broke down and the only thing left was the pure annihilating present tense of risk, and in that territory, he felt exactly the way he'd felt at twelve years old on the shore of Lake Wychwood, watching the fire spread toward the screaming.

"I'll be there," he said.

## Part Two: What We Buried

Camp Wychwood sat on fourteen acres of pine forest in the Catskills, on the eastern shore of a lake the color of old tea. It had closed in 2011 after decades of declining enrollment, a pattern that began, if you traced the graph to its inflection point, in the summer of 2004, when a twelve-year-old camper named Elliot Pace was pulled from a burning cabin with third-degree burns over forty percent of his body, and the subsequent investigation revealed "systemic failures in supervision and safety protocol" that the camp's insurance could barely survive.

The official report said Elliot had been alone. That he'd snuck out after lights-out, found a lighter in the activities cabin, and accidentally ignited a stack of old newspapers that the arts and crafts instructor had been saving for papier-mâché. The fire spread to the cabin's dry pine walls. Elliot, panicking, tripped. By the time the night counselor—Dale Fitch, age twenty-two, in his first summer on staff—reached the cabin, the boy was burning.

Every word of the official report was a lie.

What actually happened was this:

Four twelve-year-olds—Nora, Dec, Margot, and Ty—had formed the kind of bond that only exists at summer camp, the pressure-cooker friendship that compresses months of knowing into weeks, years of trust into days. They were the misfits of Cabin 7: Nora, who arrived with three suitcases and cried when the counselors made her send two home; Dec, the scholarship kid who got caught twice in the first week with other people's belongings in his pockets; Margot, who ate nothing at meals and everything between them; and Ty, who turned every activity into a bet—I'll race you, I'll dare you, double or nothing.

Elliot Pace was nine years old and assigned to Cabin 3, and he had the specific, excruciating gift of appearing wherever he was least wanted, at exactly the moment his presence would cause maximum damage. He was also a tattletale of Olympic caliber. He'd already reported Nora for keeping contraband granola bars under her mattress (confiscated, all seven), Dec for stealing fishing lures from the camp store (reprimanded, nearly sent home), and Margot for throwing up behind the mess hall (referred to the camp nurse, a humiliation she wore like a scar).

On the night of July 19th, the four of them snuck out. Ty had dared them—double or nothing, though what they were doubling and what constituted nothing was, like most of Ty's propositions, deliberately vague. They crept to the activities cabin to steal back Nora's granola bars and Dec's fishing lures from the confiscation shelf. Margot, who'd mapped the mess hall kitchen during dish duty, had also liberated a sleeve of Oreos and a jar of peanut butter. They were going to have a feast. They were going to take back what was theirs.

What they hadn't known was that Elliot had followed them. And what Elliot hadn't known was that sneaking into a cabin where four angry twelve-year-olds were rifling through shelves in the dark was, even by his standards, a catastrophically bad idea.

He appeared in the doorway. He said, "I'm telling."
