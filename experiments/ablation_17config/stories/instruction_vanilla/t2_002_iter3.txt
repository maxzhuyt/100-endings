# The HOA Board Will Now Come to Order

## Part One: The Ramp

The fine arrived on a Tuesday, which Meredith Chen later thought was appropriate because Tuesdays were already the worst days of the week. Mondays had the dignity of being universally despised. Tuesdays were just Mondays without the sympathy.

The envelope was cream-colored, printed on the good cardstock that Meredith's HOA dues apparently funded. Inside, on Willowbrook Commons Homeowners Association letterhead, was a violation notice informing her that the wooden ramp affixed to the front of 4412 Larkspur Lane constituted an "unauthorized exterior modification" under Section 7.3(b) of the CC&Rs, and that a fine of fifty dollars per day would be assessed until the structure was removed or a retroactive Architectural Review Application was submitted and approved, a process that, per the same bylaws, took between sixty and ninety business days.

Meredith read it twice, standing at the kitchen counter where she'd been making oatmeal for Richard, who was in the living room in the wheelchair he'd been confined to for three weeks because the tumor pressing on his T4 vertebra had finally won its long campaign against his ability to walk.

She brought him the oatmeal. She did not bring him the letter.

"Meredith," Richard said, because after forty-one years of marriage he could read the atmospheric pressure of her silences. "What?"

"Nothing. Eat your oatmeal."

"You're standing there like you're calculating someone's mortality tables."

"I'm retired."

"You're never retired. You just stopped getting paid for it."

She sat down across from him. Richard's face had the gray translucence of a man whose body was consuming itself from the inside, but his eyes were the same ones she'd fallen in love with at a statistics conference in 1983—sharp, amused, cataloging everything.

"It's a fine," she said. "From the HOA. For your ramp."

Richard looked toward the front door, beyond which the ramp descended at a gentle grade to the walkway. Their neighbor's son, Marco, had built it over a weekend, refusing payment, using pressure-treated lumber and non-slip strips. It was, objectively, the most well-constructed thing on the block, which was more than Meredith could say for the Hendersons' listing pergola two doors down.

"They fined us for the ramp."

"Fifty dollars a day."

Richard absorbed this. "And how many days has it been up?"

"Eighteen."

"So we owe nine hundred dollars for the crime of me being able to leave my own house."

"Apparently."

Richard set down his spoon. "Who signed it?"

But they both already knew.

---

Doug Whitmore had been president of the Willowbrook Commons Homeowners Association for eleven consecutive years, a tenure that exceeded most authoritarian regimes and had roughly the same approval rating if you counted honestly, which no one did because Doug controlled the ballot counting.

He was sixty-three years old, soft-bodied and pink-faced, with the kind of smile that never reached any destination beyond his teeth. He wore khakis year-round and tucked in polo shirts the color of dental office walls. He brought homemade banana bread to every meeting—moist, properly studded with walnuts, wrapped in cellophane with a little bow—and this banana bread had, in Meredith's estimation, done more to consolidate illegitimate power than any baked good since Marie Antoinette's apocryphal cake.

"He's not a bad man," people said about Doug, which was what people said about men who destroyed your life while smiling warmly and asking about your grandchildren.

The truth about Doug Whitmore—and Meredith had spent eleven years arriving at this truth with the slow, inexorable certainty of an actuary examining a data set—was that he genuinely did not believe himself to be cruel. This was what made him dangerous. A knowingly cruel person could be shamed. Doug could not be shamed because he had constructed an interior reality in which every fine, every lien, every enforcement action was an act of community stewardship. He was protecting property values. He was maintaining standards. He was doing the thankless work that someone had to do so that Willowbrook Commons remained the kind of place where people wanted to live, which was to say, a place where every mailbox was black, every lawn was under four inches, and no one's dying husband could leave the house without filling out the proper forms.

Meredith had watched him operate for over a decade. She'd watched him fine the Nguyens for a basketball hoop that was two inches over the permitted height. She'd watched him send violation notices to Carla Vasquez, who'd put up a small plastic Virgin Mary in her garden, citing the prohibition on "lawn ornaments exceeding fourteen inches," while mysteriously overlooking the enormous concrete eagle on Doug's own property, which Doug classified as "architectural detail." She'd watched him force old Tom Berwick to repaint his entire house because the shade of blue he'd chosen was not on the approved palette, even though the difference between "Harbor Mist" and "Harbor Fog" was invisible to every human eye that was not Doug Whitmore's.

And people let it happen. People always let it happen. Because the HOA meeting was on a Thursday at 7 p.m. in the community center, and who wanted to go to that? Who wanted to sit in folding chairs under fluorescent lights and argue about mulch budgets and setback requirements when they could be home? So twelve people showed up, and eight of them were Doug's people, and motions passed, and budgets were approved, and Doug's power grew in the negative space of everyone's collective indifference.

Meredith had been indifferent too. She could admit that now. She'd had a career to manage, a husband to love, a life to live. The HOA was background noise, an annoyance tax, a thing you paid and forgot about. She had not paid attention, and this was the price, and the price was fifty dollars a day, and her husband could not walk.

She called Doug's cell phone that evening. He answered on the second ring.

"Meredith! How's Richard doing?"

The warmth in his voice was genuine. That was the hell of it.

"He's dying, Doug. You know that."

A pause. "Well, we're all praying for him."

"I got the violation notice. For the wheelchair ramp."

"Oh, yes." She heard him shift, heard the creak of the leather recliner she imagined him sitting in, surveying his perfectly compliant kingdom. "I was sorry to have to send that. But you know, we have to apply the rules consistently. If we make an exception for one person—"

"He needs the ramp to leave the house."

"And I absolutely understand that. But the process exists for a reason, Meredith. All you have to do is submit the Architectural Review Application, and the committee will expedite it. I'll personally see to that."

"The process takes sixty to ninety business days. The fines accrue during that time. His doctor gives him four months."

Another pause. She listened to Doug Whitmore process this information and arrive, as she knew he would, at the conclusion that the system was still correct.

"The timeline is what it is," he said gently. "I don't set the bylaws. I just enforce them."

"You've been president for eleven years, Doug. You absolutely set the bylaws."

"The membership voted on them."

"Eight people voted on them. Your eight people."

Something cooled in his voice. Not anger—Doug Whitmore did not do anger. Disappointment. The special disappointment of a man who believed himself generous and was being told otherwise.

"I understand you're upset," he said. "And I'm going to choose to attribute that to the stress you're under. But, Meredith, if we start ignoring the rules every time someone has a difficult personal situation, we don't have a community anymore. We have chaos. I've given eleven years of my life to preventing that."

Meredith hung up without saying goodbye, which was, for her, an act of extraordinary violence.

She went back to the living room. Richard was pretending to read a book, which meant he'd been listening.

"I'm going to destroy him," Meredith said.

Richard lowered the book. "When you say *destroy*—"

"I mean destroy. Completely. Using his own rules."

Her husband studied her for a long time. "You know," he said, "I've been in love with you for forty-one years, and you've never once scared me before."

"Am I scaring you now?"

"A little."

"Good. That means I'm ready."

## Part Two: The Team

Meredith was an actuary, which meant she was a person who looked at systems and calculated exactly how and when they would fail. She'd spent thirty-four years at Midwest Mutual evaluating risk, predicting catastrophe, building models that could tell you to three decimal places the probability that any given thing would go wrong. She had retired at sixty-two with a gold watch, a pension, and a brain that could not stop seeing the architecture of probability in everything around her.

An HOA was just another system. It had rules, vulnerabilities, load-bearing assumptions. It could be stress-tested. It could be broken.

But she couldn't do it alone. She needed people with specific skills, people who understood procedural combat at a granular level, people who had nothing left to lose or who were angry enough to pretend they didn't.

She started with the book club.

---

The book club met every other Wednesday at Janet Howell's house and had been doing so for nine years. They called themselves the Dog-Eared Pages, which had seemed clever in 2015 and now just was. There were seven members: Meredith, Janet, Patricia Okafor, Linda Suzuki, Beverly Armitage, Dolores Finch, and Ruth Ann Waverly. They read literary fiction, mostly, with occasional forays into popular nonfiction and one disastrous attempt at Finnegans Wake that had nearly ended the group.

They were all women in their sixties and seventies. They were all homeowners in Willowbrook Commons. And every single one of them had been personally wronged by Doug Whitmore.

Janet Howell had been fined for "excessive holiday decoration" when she'd put thirteen pumpkins on her porch instead of the approved twelve. Patricia Okafor had been denied permission to paint her door red—her grandmother's tradition in Igbo culture—because red was not in the approved palette. Linda Suzuki had watched Doug's Architectural Review Committee take seven months to approve her fence replacement, during which time her dog escaped twice and was hit by a car the second time, surviving with three legs and an expression of permanent betrayal. Beverly Armitage had been threatened with a lien over a birdbath. Dolores Finch had simply hated Doug from the moment she met him, purely on principle, which Meredith respected as a form of advanced intuition. And Ruth Ann Waverly—sweet, seventy-eight-year-old Ruth Ann, who crocheted afghans for every new baby on the block—had been publicly humiliated at a board meeting when Doug questioned whether her garden gnomes violated community standards, and she'd been too polite to fight back, and she'd removed the gnomes, and she'd never really forgiven herself.

Meredith convened an emergency session of the Dog-Eared Pages on a Friday night, which was unprecedented. She served wine. She told them about the ramp, the fine, the phone call.

Then she said: "I want to take over the HOA."

Six faces stared at her.

"Take it *over*?" Beverly said.

"Through an election. At the annual meeting. Using the bylaws."

"Can you do that?" Patricia asked.

"The bylaws provide for it. Article IV, Section 2. Board positions are elected by a simple majority of voting members present at the annual meeting, provided a quorum is established. The president serves a one-year term with no term limits, which is how Doug has held power for eleven years. But the same mechanism that keeps him in power can remove him."

"So we just... vote him out?" Janet said.

"It's not that simple. Doug controls the meeting. He sets the agenda, he chairs the proceedings, he decides who speaks and when. He's also spent eleven years stacking the room with loyalists. To beat him, we need to control the floor, challenge his procedures, and bring enough votes to overwhelm his base. We need it to be airtight. We need it to be flawless. Because if we give him any procedural opening—any point of order he can use to shut us down—he'll take it."

"So what do you need from us?" Dolores asked. She had not touched her wine. She was sitting forward in her chair with the expression of a woman who had been waiting nine years for someone to say what Meredith was saying.

"I need bodies," Meredith said. "Each of you knows people in this neighborhood. People who've been fined, people who've been bullied, people who stopped coming to meetings because they felt powerless. I need you to find them. I need you to bring them. I need thirty-seven people in that room."

"Why thirty-seven?" Ruth Ann asked.

"Because Article III, Section 1, defines a quorum as twenty percent of dues-paying households. There are one hundred and eighty-two households in Willowbrook Commons. Twenty percent is thirty-seven, rounding up. Doug typically gets fifteen to twenty people in the room. If we bring thirty-seven of our own, we control the quorum, we control the vote, and we control the outcome."

"And Doug just lets this happen?" Linda said.

Meredith smiled. It was not a warm smile. "Doug won't know it's happening until it's too late. That's why I need more than just votes. I need specialists."

---

The first specialist was a woman named Gail Pratchett.

Gail was fifty-nine years old and had been a Cook County court stenographer for twenty-three years before a professional catastrophe that she referred to only as "the incident" and that the Chicago Tribune had referred to as "Stenographer Allegedly Transcribes Entire Murder Trial in Personal Shorthand, Rendering Record Unintelligible; Mistrial Declared."

The truth, as Gail told it over coffee in Meredith's kitchen, was more nuanced. She had developed, over two decades, her own evolved shorthand system that was faster and more efficient than standard stenographic code. The problem was that no one else on Earth could read it, a fact that had not mattered until an appeals court needed the transcript and found what appeared to be an alien language rendered at two hundred and sixty words per minute.

She'd been let go. The Illinois court system had been polite about it, citing "procedural incompatibility," which was employment termination's version of "it's not you, it's me." She now lived in Willowbrook Commons, Unit 78, where she'd been fined twice by Doug Whitmore—once for having a welcome mat that exceeded the approved dimensions, and once for wind chimes.

"I need a record," Meredith told her. "A real-time, verbatim record of the annual meeting. Every motion, every second, every point of order. I need it captured so precisely that if Doug tries to dispute what happened, we have documentation that would hold up in court."

Gail's eyes lit up. "Standard steno or my system?"

"Standard. I need other people to be able to read it."

Gail deflated slightly, then rallied. "I can do standard. I still have my machine. It'll be like going back to riding a bicycle. A slow, boring bicycle."

"Can you also prepare a transcript of the last three years of HOA meeting minutes? I want to compare the official minutes Doug publishes with what actually happened."

"You have recordings?"

"Linda Suzuki has been secretly recording meetings on her phone since 2021, when Doug told her that her motion to discuss fence policy was 'not germane to the agenda' and then moved on before she could object."

Gail picked up her coffee. "What time do you want me here tomorrow?"

"Seven."

"I'll be here at six-thirty."

Harold was seventy-one years old, a retired high school civics teacher, and the single most dangerous parliamentarian in the state of Illinois. This was not an exaggeration. Harold had competed in—and Meredith had not known this was a thing until she'd met him—parliamentary procedure competitions, which were formal events where teams of people argued about the correct application of Robert's Rules of Order the way other people argued about fantasy football.
