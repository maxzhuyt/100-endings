# The Lineup

## Part One: The Whistle

The blood had dried in the lane lines.

Marcus Welty—equipment manager, tire inflator, jersey mender, halftime orange-slicer, and the only adult who ever asked the girls of the Riverside Raptors how their *days* were going before asking how their *chairs* were rolling—lay face down between the free-throw line and the three-point arc of the Riverside Community Center gymnasium, and the fluorescent lights above him buzzed the way they always buzzed, which is to say indifferently, because light does not know to mourn.

He had been struck from behind with the portable ramp the team used to access the court from the south entrance. It was a heavy thing, aluminum and rubber, built to bear the weight of girls and their chairs both, and it had done its final work sometime between 10:15 p.m. Tuesday—when the center's security camera caught Marcus entering through the side door, gym bag in hand—and 5:48 a.m. Wednesday, when JaMarcus the janitor unlocked the main entrance and followed the smell.

The police came. Then the detectives. Then the yellow tape.

By 9:00 a.m. the Raptors' group chat was a scroll of disbelief.

By noon, it was something worse.

---

Detective Andrea Pruitt of the Riverside Police Department did not, as a general rule, enjoy easy cases. They made her suspicious the way a clear sky in tornado season made her grandmother pack the basement. But when Gerald Fisk—former head coach of the Eastlake Eagles wheelchair basketball program, fired eighteen months prior for what the school board euphemistically termed "conduct unbecoming"—walked into the station at 2:00 p.m. Wednesday and said, "I killed Marcus Welty, and I'd do it again," Pruitt's suspicion had to contend with Pruitt's caseload, which was heavy, and Pruitt's captain, who was heavier.

"He's got motive," said Captain Alderman, leaning back in his chair with the contentment of a man watching a pot roast finish itself. "Marcus Welty testified at the firing hearing. Only witness who showed. Without Welty, Fisk keeps his job."

"He walked in off the street, sir."

"Confessed. Detailed knowledge."

"The ramp was described in the initial police report logged at 7:15 a.m. Anyone with a connection to the department could have seen it."

"Andrea."

"Sir."

"The man says he did it."

Gerald Fisk sat in Interview Room B with his arms folded and his jaw set at an angle that suggested not guilt but defiance—the posture of a man who has been humiliated so thoroughly that even punishment feels like reclamation. He was sixty-one. He had coached wheelchair basketball for twenty-three years. He had never won a championship, and Marcus Welty, his former equipment manager before moving to Riverside, had told the school board things about how Fisk treated his players that could not be untold.

Fisk hated Marcus Welty the way a drought hates a river: completely, and from a great distance, and with the patience of something that has nothing left to lose.

But he had not killed him.

This is the truth that the omniscient voice can offer and the detectives cannot yet confirm: Gerald Fisk had been in his apartment all Tuesday night, drinking Canadian Club and watching recordings of old Eastlake games, pausing and rewinding a state semifinal from 2019, whispering adjustments to players who could not hear him. He had no alibi because he had no life. He claimed the murder because it was the first consequential thing anyone had accused him of in eighteen months, and because he had imagined killing Marcus Welty so many times and in such granular detail that confessing to it felt, to his liquor-eroded mind, less like lying than like remembering.

He would not recant. The satisfaction was too pure.

---

Nayeli Rojas-Quinn, point guard, team captain, seventeen years old, learned about Gerald Fisk's arrest from a push notification on her phone while sitting in the passenger seat of her mother's adapted minivan in the parking lot of a Wendy's drive-through. She was holding a Frosty she no longer wanted. Her mother was saying something about the funeral arrangements. Nayeli was not listening.

She was doing arithmetic.

Nayeli's mind worked in timelines the way other people's minds worked in melodies: instinctively, structurally, always seeking the wrong note. This tendency had been nurtured across four summers at Camp Alder Lake Junior Detective Academy—a fact her teammates knew and teased her about and a fact she had used, more than once, to solve problems considerably less serious than murder. (The Case of the Missing Practice Jerseys. The Case of Who Left the Van's Lift Down in the Rain. The Case of Coach Burke's Anniversary, where she had orchestrated a surprise party with the planning precision of a heist.)

Now the arithmetic.

Marcus was in the gym at 10:15 p.m. The security camera at the south entrance confirmed this. But the same camera—and Nayeli knew the camera's range because she had studied the community center's security layout two years ago when someone was stealing from the locker rooms—covered the only ground-level accessible entrance on that side of the building. The north entrance had stairs. The east entrance had been locked for renovation since September. The west entrance required a key card assigned only to staff.

If Gerald Fisk had entered after Marcus, the camera would have caught him.

It had not. Pruitt's own preliminary report, leaked to the *Riverside Herald* by someone whose identity is not relevant to this story, stated that no other individuals appeared on the south entrance footage between 10:15 p.m. and JaMarcus the janitor at 5:48 a.m.

Which meant either Fisk was already inside the gym waiting—possible, but for how long? and how did he enter?—or he came in through the west entrance with a staff key card.

Or he was never there at all.

Nayeli set down the Frosty. She wiped her hand on her joggers. She pulled up the group chat and typed nothing, because what she wanted to type was *I don't think he did it*, and what she could not afford for anyone to ask was the question that would follow, which was *Why do you care so much?*

She cared because Marcus Welty was dead and she had loved him the way the whole team had loved him, fiercely and with the particular gratitude of young people for the rare adult who sees them as complete.

She also cared because Marcus Welty had known.

Three weeks ago, in this same gym, after practice, Marcus had found her alone. He'd been inventorying the equipment closet. He came out with a clipboard and a look she had never seen on his face before, one that was kind and sorry and absolutely unyielding.

"Nayeli," he said. "We need to talk about your classification."

The International Wheelchair Basketball Federation classifies players on a scale from 1.0 to 4.5, based on functional ability. A team's five on-court players cannot exceed a combined total of 14.0 points. This is the rule that makes the sport equitable, that ensures teams cannot simply field five athletes with the highest physical function and overpower everyone else. It is, in the vocabulary of the sport, sacred.

Nayeli was classified as a 2.0. A player with limited trunk movement, limited forward and sideways reach. Her medical records, submitted when she was fourteen, supported this.

But Nayeli was not a 2.0. She was, by any honest functional assessment, a 3.0—possibly a 3.5. Her spinal cord injury was real, but her functional capacity had been deliberately understated on her initial paperwork by a doctor who owed her mother a favor and who did not understand or care what the numbers meant. As a 2.0, Nayeli gave her team a strategic advantage: her coach could surround her with higher-functioning players while staying under the point cap. It was the architecture on which the entire Raptors roster was built.

Nayeli had not asked for the falsification. She'd been fourteen. Her mother had handled the paperwork. But Nayeli had understood by fifteen what the numbers meant, and by sixteen she had built her identity—her assists, her court vision, her captaincy—on a foundation of fraud, and the distance between understanding this and confessing it had grown so vast that she could no longer see across it.

Marcus had seen across it. Marcus, who watched more closely than anyone, who tracked every player's movement on and off the court, had noticed the discrepancy between Nayeli's classified function and her actual function. He had not threatened. He had not judged. He had said, simply and with his hand on her shoulder: "We have to make this right before the championship. I'll help you. But we have to."

The championship was in seventy-two hours.

Marcus was dead.

And the thing Nayeli knew, sitting in that Wendy's parking lot with her Frosty melting—the thing she held in her chest like a swallowed stone—was that Marcus's desire to expose the classification fraud gave *her* a motive. That if anyone ever discovered what Marcus had found, Nayeli Rojas-Quinn would not be a heroic point guard seeking justice for a murdered friend. She would be a suspect.

So when she told herself she needed to solve this case, she was telling the truth. She needed to find the real killer before anyone else started looking at who had reasons to want Marcus Welty silenced.

The altruism was real. The self-preservation was also real. Nayeli was seventeen and had not yet learned that these can coexist without either one being a lie.

She opened the group chat.

**Nayeli:** *Team meeting. My house. Tonight. 7pm. Not about the championship.*

## Part Two: The Roster

They came.

Of course they came. They were the Raptors, and the Raptors came when called—to practice, to games, to each other's birthday parties and hospital visits and the awkward school talent shows where they cheered too loud because the world so rarely gave them a stage to be loud from.

Six players on the roster. One gone now, not a player but the person who made playing possible. The coach, Pat Burke, had texted that she would be at the school board meeting until late—the community center was a crime scene, so the question of where they could practice, if they could practice, if the championship would even proceed, was being debated by people who had attended perhaps one of their games combined.

Nayeli's living room was small and warm and smelled like the tamales her grandmother was perpetually steaming in the kitchen. The furniture had been pushed to the walls years ago to make room for chairs—the rolling kind—and the team filled the space the way they filled any space, which was completely.

There was Tomás Reyes, the center, six-foot-two even sitting, with hands the size of dinner plates and a gentleness that contradicted all of it. Tomás had been deaf since birth. He navigated the hearing world through lip-reading of such precision that he often understood what people were saying before they finished saying it, and he navigated the basketball court through a combination of peripheral vision and vibration, feeling the rhythm of the game through the hardwood and the rubber of his tires. His interpreter, when he wanted one, was usually Marcus. Marcus had learned enough ASL to be useful and enough to be funny, and Tomás had loved him for both.

What Tomás knew and had not told anyone:

He had seen Marcus's last phone call.

Not heard it. Seen it. Tuesday evening, 8:00 p.m., in the community center parking lot. Tomás had been leaving a tutoring session in the center's north wing. He'd come out the side door and there was Marcus, thirty feet away, standing beside his car, phone pressed to his ear, speaking with the urgent gestures of a man who is being disagreed with. Tomás could not hear the words, but he watched Marcus's lips with the focus of a lifetime's practice, and this is what he read:

*"I'm not going to let this go, Pat. It's a violation. I don't care about the championship."*

Pat.

Coach Pat Burke.

And then, after a pause in which Marcus listened to whatever Pat said in return, his lips moved again:

*"If you won't report it, I will. Tonight. I'm going to the gym to get the files."*

Tomás had driven home. He had eaten dinner with his mother. He had done his homework. He had gone to sleep. And then Marcus was dead, and Tomás was holding a piece of information that pointed not at Gerald Fisk but at their own coach, and the weight of it was so enormous that he had spent all of Wednesday in a silence even deeper than his usual one.

He told himself he was protecting the team. He told himself he needed to be sure. He told himself many things, and none of them were sufficient, and he knew it, and he came to Nayeli's house anyway because she had called, and Tomás always came when the team called.

---

Beside Tomás, spinning a basketball between her palms with the unconscious ease of a pianist running scales, sat Destiny Carter, shooting guard, the team's leading scorer, sixteen years old, braids this week in Raptors red and gold. Destiny played basketball the way some people play music—less with her body than through it, as if the ball and the chair and the court were extensions of a single instrument only she could hear.

Destiny's mother, Sharon Carter, worked the night shift in medical records at Riverside General Hospital. This was not, in the ordinary course of things, relevant to wheelchair basketball. But it was relevant to investigation, because Sharon Carter had access to patient files, insurance records, and the kinds of documentation that people spent considerable effort keeping private.

Destiny did not think of her mother's job as a tool. She thought of it as a fact of life, like her chair or her jump shot. But she was aware—and this awareness would become relevant—that when Nayeli had called the meeting "not about the championship," something shifted in the group chat's energy, and Destiny had felt an impulse she hadn't expected: she'd texted her mother and asked, casually, whether Marcus Welty had been to Riverside General recently.

Sharon had texted back: *Why?*

Destiny had texted: *Just wondering.*

Sharon had texted: *I can't share patient information, baby. You know that.*

And then, forty minutes later, Sharon had texted: *He wasn't a patient. But he came in three weeks ago requesting copies of someone else's file. I can't tell you whose. But I can tell you the request was denied because he wasn't next of kin.*

Destiny did not know whose file Marcus had tried to access. She had theories. She kept them to herself.

---

And then there was Leah Okonkwo, the bench player, fifteen, the youngest on the roster, with a quiet so complete that people often forgot she was in the room. This was, in Leah's experience, less an insult than a superpower. She had spent her life being overlooked—by teachers who saw the chair before the student, by classmates who saw neither, by opponents who assumed that a player who averaged four minutes per game was a player who didn't matter.

Leah had a key card.

This fact bears explaining. The community center issued staff key cards to employees and, occasionally, to regular volunteers. Marcus had been both, and when his key card had stopped working one evening six months ago—a dead battery, it turned out—he had borrowed a spare from the front desk, fixed his own, and forgotten to return the spare. It lived, as far as Leah knew, in the equipment closet, in the front pocket of the orange duffel bag where Marcus kept his personal tools.

She knew this because she had watched Marcus put it there. Leah watched everything. She catalogued the gym the way a novelist catalogues a room—details, always details, because agency for Leah had always come not from action but from knowledge, and knowledge came from seeing what others did not bother to see.

The key card opened the west entrance.
