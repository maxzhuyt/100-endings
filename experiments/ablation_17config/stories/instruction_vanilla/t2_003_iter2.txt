# The Patience of Dry Country

## I. Now

The drought came to Perdition Gulch the way all true catastrophes arrive—not as a sudden violence but as a slow subtraction, one green thing dying at a time, until the living could not remember what the country had looked like when it was alive.

This is not precisely true. There was one woman who could remember.

Constance Ryle could remember the exact day the cottonwoods along Alkali Creek turned from green to the pale exhausted yellow of a body drained of blood. She could remember because she remembered everything—the way each leaf curled, the specific quality of light on October 14th, 1884, at half past three in the afternoon, the grasshopper perched on the fencepost outside the assayer's office with its left hind leg missing. She remembered the color of the sky on the day she was born, and the sound of her mother's heartbeat from inside the womb, and every face she'd ever seen in the thoroughgoing way that a daguerreotype remembers—not with meaning but with merciless precision.

She was, in the autumn of 1887, burning her journals.

She did this in the yard behind her house on Gideon Street, feeding the pages into a fire that seemed obscene in the heat, a column of smoke rising into a sky that had not produced rain in eleven months. There were forty-seven journals in total, one for each year of her life, and they contained a record of the world so complete that any university back East would have wept to possess them. Constance burned them with the methodical patience of a woman performing her own last rites. She did not read them as they went. She did not need to.

She was burning them because she had woken that morning and, for the first time since the raw red hour of her birth, she had forgotten a thing.

It was a small thing. The name of the dun horse that had been tied outside Kessler's dry-goods store on the morning of March 7th, 1879.
