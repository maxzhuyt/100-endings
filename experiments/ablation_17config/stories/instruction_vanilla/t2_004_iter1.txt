# The Hateful Immortals: A Documentary History of the Greenbriar-Chen Marriage (1847–2024)

**By Dr. Amelia Thornton, Ph.D.**
*Radcliffe Chair in Supernormal Historical Studies, Harvard University*
*Cambridge University Press, 2024*

## Foreword

I want to state, for the record, that both Margaret Greenbriar and James Chen cooperated fully with this project. They cooperated separately, of course, as they have maintained separate residences since 2019 — Margaret in a brownstone on Beacon Hill and James in a houseboat in Sausalito — though as of this writing they still speak on the phone every evening at nine o'clock Eastern, a ritual they have maintained without interruption since the invention of consumer telephone service in 1877.

I also want to state, for the record, that in the course of my research, Margaret provided me with 14,200 pages of handwritten journals, personal correspondence dating back to 1843, and a series of audio recordings she began making in 1928, which she described as "evidence." James, for his part, showed me a shoebox containing a single daguerreotype of their wedding day, a pressed flower of indeterminate species, and six words written on the back of a Portland takeout menu: *She is my whole entire life.*

When I showed James the 14,200 pages, he said, "That sounds about right."

When I showed Margaret the shoebox, she was quiet for a very long time. Then she said, "He keeps that? Still?"

Then she said, "Don't you dare put that in the book."

I have put it in the book.

— A.T., Cambridge, Massachusetts, March 2024

## Part One: The Convergence (1843–1852)

### Chapter 1: Two Manifestations

The year 1843 was, by most accounts, unremarkable. John Tyler was President. Charles Dickens published *A Christmas Carol*. And in a modest clapboard house on Milk Street in Boston, Massachusetts, a thirteen-year-old girl named Margaret Eliza Greenbriar woke from a fever dream and discovered that she could remember everything.

Not "everything" in the colloquial sense. Not "everything" in the way that a bright child remembers her lessons or a grieving widow remembers her husband's face. Everything. The precise weave pattern of the blanket she had been swaddled in as an infant. The number of bricks in the eastern wall of her father's warehouse (4,211, of which fourteen were cracked). The exact cadence and inflection of every word her mother had ever spoken to her, preserved in her mind with the fidelity of recordings that would not be invented for another thirty-four years.

"It was as though God had lit a lantern inside my skull," she would write in her journal some forty years later, "and all the dark rooms I had walked through in my life were suddenly illuminated, and I found that nothing — nothing — had ever been thrown away."

Her Manifestation — we capitalize the term now, though it would not enter the medical lexicon until Dr. Friedrich Wendt's groundbreaking 1901 monograph *Die Übernatürlichen* — was not the first on record, but it was among the earliest to be thoroughly documented, largely because Margaret herself would become the documentation. Every text she had ever glanced at was now permanently inscribed in her memory. Every conversation. Every face on every street corner. The smell of lye soap on a Tuesday. The sound of her father's boots on the stairs, and how the rhythm differed when he'd been drinking, which was Thursdays and sometimes Mondays.

She told no one.

This is important. In 1843, supernormal abilities were not understood as biological phenomena. They were understood as witchcraft, or divine punishment, or — in the more progressively minded households of Beacon Hill — as "nervous hysteria." Margaret Greenbriar, who was thirteen and had already read enough to know what happened to women who were different, simply closed her mouth and kept it closed. She performed forgetfulness. She pretended to misplace things. When her mother asked whether she remembered some minor domestic detail, she would furrow her brow and say, "I'm not sure, Mama," while the detail in question sat in her mind as sharp and permanent as an engraving on glass.

She would maintain this performance, with varying degrees of success, for the next nine years.

---

James Chen was born in 1830 to a Cantonese father and an Irish mother in the Five Points neighborhood of New York City, which — I feel compelled to note for the benefit of students who may not have studied the period — was at that time among the most wretched and dangerous places in the Western Hemisphere.

His father, Chen Wei-Lin, had arrived in New York in 1822 aboard a merchant vessel and had found work as a launderer. His mother, Bridget Mahoney, had arrived in 1825 aboard a coffin ship and had found work as a seamstress. They met, according to family lore, when Wei-Lin brought a torn shirt to the shop where Bridget worked and she mended it so beautifully that he came back the next day with another shirt he had torn on purpose. They married in a civil ceremony in 1829, which was legal but, in the parlance of the era, "not done."

James was their only child. He grew up speaking Cantonese, English, and Irish Gaelic with equal fluency and unequal utility. He was, by all surviving accounts, a sweet-tempered boy with a serious face and a talent for getting hit.

This is not a metaphor. He got hit constantly. The Five Points in the 1830s and 1840s was a place where a half-Chinese, half-Irish child existed in a sort of Venn diagram of contempt: too Chinese for the Irish gangs, too Irish for the small Chinese community, too poor for everyone else. James Chen was beaten up behind saloons, in alleys, on the waterfront, in the street in broad daylight. He developed no particular skill at fighting. He simply got extremely good at surviving.

His Manifestation came at fourteen, in 1844, one year after Margaret's. He was — per the only account he has ever given of the event, in a 1979 interview with *Rolling Stone* that he has since repeatedly disavowed — "stabbed fairly comprehensively" by a member of the Bowery Boys who objected to his existence on ethnic grounds.

"I remember looking down and thinking, well, that's a lot of blood," James told *Rolling Stone* journalist David Felton. "And then I thought, actually, I feel fine. And then the other fellow looked at me, and I looked at him, and I think we were both quite alarmed."

The wound closed. It closed the way a mouth closes — seamlessly, completely, as though the skin had simply remembered that it was supposed to be intact. The Bowery Boy reportedly dropped his knife and ran. James picked up the knife, looked at it, and — in a detail that I find enormously telling about his character — walked it to the nearest constable and turned it in.

He too told no one. Or rather: he told his mother, who crossed herself and told him to tell no one, and he told his father, who said nothing for a long time and then said, in Cantonese, "This is a gift from your ancestors, and you should not waste it on being careless."

James Chen has not wasted it on being careless. He has wasted it on a great many other things — I refer here specifically to the incident in 1923 when he jumped off the Woolworth Building on a dare from Ernest Hemingway, about which more later — but not on carelessness.

### Chapter 2: The Meeting

They met on October 14, 1847, at the Boston Athenæum. Margaret was seventeen. James was seventeen. She was reading Emerson. He was, by his own later account, "trying to look as though I belonged there, which I absolutely did not."

There are two versions of their meeting, and I have heard both so many times that I can recite them from memory — a fact which Margaret, when I mentioned it, found "charmingly ironic."

**Margaret's version** (from her journals, corroborated by audio recordings made in 1931, 1958, 1974, and 2003, each consistent in every particular down to the placement of commas):

"He was standing by the window in the reading room with a book in his hands — it was Gibbon, Volume III, open to page 247, which concerned the Emperor Julian — and the light was coming through the glass in that way it does in October, when everything turns golden and slightly desperate because winter is near. He was wearing a brown coat that was too large for him and had been mended at the left elbow with thread that did not quite match. His hair was very black and his eyes were very dark and he had the most serious face I had ever seen on a living person, as though he were not reading about the fall of Rome but experiencing it personally. I thought: that is a person who knows what it is to lose things. And I thought: I never want to see that expression on his face again. And I thought: what an extraordinary and inconvenient thought to have about a stranger."

**James's version** (from the *Rolling Stone* interview):

"She had red hair. I was done for."

These two accounts — Margaret's forensically precise, emotionally exhaustive, 247-word recollection and James's seven-word summary — represent, in microcosm, the fundamental dynamic that has sustained and tormented their marriage for 177 years. Margaret remembers everything and is compelled to articulate it. James feels everything and is compelled to compress it. They are, respectively, an encyclopedia and a haiku, and they have been trying to read each other across this gap since the administration of James K. Polk.

---

Their courtship was, by Victorian standards, irregular. Margaret's father, Ezekiel Greenbriar, was a modestly successful shipping merchant who harbored modest ambitions for his daughter — a suitable marriage, a respectable household, a life of quiet domestic industry. James Chen was none of these things. He was a mixed-race orphan (his father had died of typhus in 1846; his mother would follow in 1849) with no profession, no connections, and no particular prospects beyond an inability to die, which was not, in 1847, a marketable skill.

They should not have met again after that day in the Athenæum. But Margaret Greenbriar, who forgot nothing, also forgot nothing about the way the light had fallen across James Chen's serious face, and James Chen, who could not be killed, turned out to be equally unkillable in his determination to see her again.

He found work at a printer's shop on State Street. The printer's shop was four blocks from the Athenæum. The Athenæum was where Margaret went every Wednesday afternoon. I will leave the reader to draw their own conclusions about coincidence.

Their early letters survive — Margaret kept hers, naturally, and James kept his in a way that tells us something about the man: he kept only the ones that made him laugh. The collection begins with a letter dated November 3, 1847, in which Margaret recommends that James read Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein*, "as it concerns a creature who cannot be destroyed by conventional means and may therefore strike you as autobiographical." James's response is lost, but Margaret's next letter — November 10, 1847 — begins: "I did not say you were a MONSTER, I said the THEMATIC PARALLELS were —" and continues for six pages.

Their correspondence reveals two young people engaged in the sort of passionate intellectual combat that the Victorians considered foreplay. They argued about Emerson ("a windbag of the first order" — James; "you simply haven't read him properly" — Margaret; "I have read him four times" — James; "then you have been improperly reading him four times" — Margaret). They argued about abolition (they agreed on the principle but spent eleven letters arguing about tactics). They argued about the nature of memory, which was — obviously, in retrospect — the argument that mattered.

"You say you cannot forget," James wrote on March 12, 1848. "I say I cannot die. Which of us is more imprisoned?"

Margaret's response, dated March 15, 1848, runs to nine pages. The relevant passage:

"You ask who is more imprisoned, and I answer: you are free, James, because every wound you suffer heals and leaves no mark, and so each day you may begin again as though the world has not yet touched you. But I carry every wound forever. Every unkind word, every slammed door, every look of disappointment on my father's face — these are not memories, they are *residents*. They live in me. They eat at my table. So do not speak to me of imprisonment until you have spent a single week unable to forget the sound of your mother weeping through a wall."

James's reply, dated March 18, 1848, reads in its entirety:

"You're right. I'm sorry. May I take you to dinner?"

### Chapter 3: The Marriage

They married on June 6, 1852, at a small ceremony in the parlor of Margaret's family home. Ezekiel Greenbriar had been persuaded to consent — or, more precisely, had been exhausted into consent by a five-year campaign of attrition waged jointly by his daughter and her suitor. Margaret's mother, Harriet, wept throughout the ceremony, though whether from joy or despair no record indicates.

The officiant was a Unitarian minister named Reverend Thomas Hale, who, Margaret would later note, mispronounced James's surname as "Chin" three times during the ceremony. "I corrected him each time," she recorded. "James told me afterward that I ought to have let it go. I told him I could not let it go. He said, 'Yes, I'm beginning to understand that.'"

The marriage certificate — which I have personally examined at the Massachusetts State Archives — lists James's occupation as "printer" and Margaret's as "none." It is signed in James's careful, slightly cramped hand and Margaret's confident, forward-leaning script. It is the most ordinary document in the world, and it contains, in its boilerplate language, the phrase that would become the central joke of their existence:

*Till death do us part.*

"We laughed about it at the time," Margaret told me during one of our interviews. "We were twenty-two years old. We thought it was funny. We thought: well, James can't die, and I can't forget, so we shall simply have to be very happy forever. That was the plan. Be very happy. Forever." She paused. "The word 'forever' does different things to you at twenty-two and a hundred and ninety-four."

## Part Two: The Long Century (1852–1952)

### Chapter 4: The First Quarrel, and Its Aftermath

The first true quarrel — as opposed to the intellectual sparring that characterized their courtship — occurred on September 3, 1852, approximately three months after the wedding. The subject was oysters.

I realize this seems trivial. I assure you it is not. Or rather: it is, and that is precisely the point. The great discovery of the Greenbriar-Chen marriage, the insight that has made them a subject of scholarly interest and popular fascination for over a century, is this: *it is never about the oysters.*

The facts, as Margaret has recounted them to me (and to anyone who will listen, including a visibly uncomfortable waiter at a restaurant in Portland in 2016, per witness accounts):

James purchased oysters from a vendor on the waterfront. Margaret noted that the oysters smelled "off." James maintained they were fine. Margaret said she could recall with perfect clarity the smell of the fresh oysters her father had brought home on the evening of April 7, 1839, and these were not that. James said that not everything required comparison to a memory from thirteen years ago. Margaret said, "It is not a matter of *requiring* it. I have no choice. The memory is simply there." James said, "Must every moment of our lives be compared to some prior moment and found wanting?" And Margaret said — she contends she said it "calmly," though James has used the word "volcanic" — "I do not find this moment wanting, James. I find these oysters wanting. There is a difference, and a man of even moderate intelligence would perceive it."

James ate the oysters.

James — who could not die — suffered approximately sixteen hours of catastrophic food poisoning, which his body fought with its customary supernatural resilience but which was, by his account, "the most miserable experience of my life to that point, and I had been stabbed."
