# The Thirty-Seven Letters of Monk Hideo

### As Compiled, Annotated, and Offered Without Peace by Yoshida Katsuro

*Published posthumously by arrangement with the Yoshida estate and the Eihō-ji monastery archive. Translated from the Japanese by R. Ōmura.*

---

## Editor's Foreword

The volume you hold requires little introduction. Yoshida Katsuro completed his editorial annotations on the fourteenth of October and died eleven days later, having refused treatment for the cancer he had known about for two years. His instructions to this press were precise: publish nothing before his death, alter nothing after it, and on no account contact the monastery at Eihō-ji for comment or correction.

We have honored these wishes with a single exception. Upon notification of Yoshida-sensei's passing, Eihō-ji's abbot responded with a brief statement: *The monk called Hideo died in the spring of this year. He left no instructions regarding correspondence.*

The overlap is therefore confirmed. Hideo's final letter, dated the eleventh of July, preceded Yoshida's receipt of his diagnosis by nine days. Yoshida did not learn of Hideo's death until we informed his estate. The two men, as far as can be determined, never spoke and never met again after the ninth of March, forty-one years ago, on the raised platform of the Enshū Budōkan.

What happened that day has been told so many times that it has acquired the quality of myth, which is to say it has been sanded smooth of everything that was true. Most accounts agree on the essentials: Hideo, undefeated through six rounds and widely regarded as the finest kendōka of his generation, stepped onto the platform for the championship final against Yoshida Katsuro—his junior by three years, a technician of no particular fame, who had advanced through the bracket by discipline rather than brilliance. They assumed their positions. The referee called *hajime*. Hideo stood motionless for eleven seconds, then laid his shinai on the floor, bowed, and walked out of the arena without speaking. He was never seen in competition again.

Yoshida accepted the championship in silence, competed for four more years with a record of mixed distinction, and retired to teach at his family's dōjō in Hamamatsu, where he produced three national champions and one volume of essays on posture (*On Stillness in Motion*, Takeda Press, now out of print). He was by all public accounts a measured, private, courteous man. His students describe him as "incapable of praise or blame in equal measure." He never discussed the Enshū final.

The letters began fourteen months after the match.

—*S. Nishikawa, Takeda Academic Press*

## Yoshida's Preface

I will say only what is necessary and not one word that is true.

This is not a contradiction. The truth of this matter has been true for forty years, and being true has accomplished nothing—has healed nothing, ended nothing, freed no one. If truth were sufficient, Hideo would have spoken it on the platform, or I would have asked for it in the years when asking was still possible, and we would both have been released into the ordinary suffering of ordinary men instead of this.

What is necessary: I received thirty-seven letters. The first arrived in May, the year after the match, in a plain envelope bearing the return address of the Eihō-ji monastery. The last arrived this July. I answered none of them. This was not from cruelty, or indifference, or strategy, though I have spent years wondering whether it was, in fact, a kind of strategy so deep I could not see its shape from inside. I answered none of them because I understood, from the first letter, that Hideo was trying to tell me something he could not tell me, and that my silence was the only space in which he could continue trying.

I was wrong about this. Or I was right. Forty years did not produce the confession, and my silence did not produce it, and therefore I was wrong. But the letters continued for forty years, and therefore the space held something, and therefore I may have been right. I cannot tell.

I am dying. This is not metaphor. The specific name of the disease is unimportant; what matters is the deadline, which is the first real deadline either of us has faced since the referee's clock. I have chosen to publish these letters as my final act because I have nothing else to offer and because silence, which I once believed was generosity, has revealed itself to be merely the form my cowardice preferred.

If Hideo is alive when this reaches print—and he may not be; he would be eighty-one and his letters have grown shorter—then I must ask him to understand that I am not exposing him. I am exposing myself. The flaw was always mine.

He will understand what I mean by this.

Yoshida Katsuro
Hamamatsu
October, ——

## Letter One

*Received May ——. No envelope preserved. Paper identified as standard Eihō-ji monastery stationery by watermark. Hideo's calligraphy throughout the correspondence is of remarkably high quality, consistent with reports that he took up shodō formally upon entering monastic life.*

Yoshida,

I address you without honorific because I am no longer sure what honorific applies between us. You are champion. I am nothing. The forms that once organized our relation to each other—opponent, peer, rival, whatever we were—were dissolved by my action, and I have found no new forms to replace them. In the monastery we are called by our given names alone. I offer you mine as I offer it to the man who ladles soup. Without rank.

I am writing to you because I have been unable to meditate. The abbot says this is common in the first year among men who were previously Athletes. He uses this word with the particular care of a man who considers it a diagnosis. He says the body, accustomed to being the instrument of will, cannot immediately accept its demotion to the instrument of breath. He has prescribed additional hours of zazen and the copying of sutras.

I have copied the Heart Sutra four hundred times. I mention this not to demonstrate diligence but because I wish you to understand the context in which I am writing: I have spent months reducing my mind to the shape of another man's words, and still my own words come back, and the words that come back are the ones I did not say to you.

I will not say them here either.

That sentence, which I have just written and cannot bring myself to cross out, may tell you everything or nothing. I am aware that it is the kind of sentence a man writes when he wants to confess but also wants credit for the nobility of his silence. I am aware of this and I am sending the letter anyway. The abbot says that awareness of one's self-deception is not the same as overcoming it. This has proved to be the truest thing anyone has ever told me.

I hope your knee has healed. I noticed, in the quarterfinals, that you favored it.

Hideo

---

**Yoshida's annotation:** *He noticed the knee. I record this without additional comment, because additional comment would be the thing itself, and I have committed to saying only what is necessary. But: he noticed the knee. In the quarterfinal. Which means he was watching me two rounds before we met. I have thought about this sentence for forty years and it has not finished meaning what it means.*

## Letter Two

*Received August ——. Envelope preserved; postmark confirms Eihō-ji.*

Yoshida,

The summer here is of a particular density. The cedar forests hold the humidity like cupped hands. I wake at 3:40 each morning for the first meditation bell and by 4:00 my robes are dark with sweat. There is something clarifying about this—the body so occupied with its own discomfort that the mind can slip past it, the way a second swordsman might enter while the first is engaged.

I have been thinking about the Enshū Budōkan and whether the building still stands. It was not beautiful. The light came from high windows on the eastern side and fell across the platform in bars that shifted over the course of a match, so that a bout beginning in full illumination might end with both competitors half in shadow. I mention this because I do not think the architecture of that place has been given proper consideration. We speak of a match as though it occurs between two people, but it occurs between two people *in a place*, and the place shapes what is possible.

I am being evasive. I will try not to be.

In the seconds before our match began—after the rei, after kamae, before hajime—I saw something. I need you to understand that the word *saw* is imprecise. A man with thirty years of training does not *see* in the way a spectator sees. The spectator sees positions. The trained man sees futures. He looks at the body before him and reads not what it is but what it will become in the next quarter-second, the way a man who has lived by a river can look at the current and see the shape of the stone beneath.

In that moment I saw

I cannot complete this sentence today. Forgive me. I will try again in my next letter.

The plums here are exceptional this year. The monastery receives a crate each week from a farmer in the village who considers us a worthy charity. We are not. We eat the plums and fail to achieve enlightenment and he would be better served donating to the fire brigade.

Hideo

---

**Yoshida's annotation:** *This is the first approach. There will be many more. Each time the sentence breaks at the same joint—not at the fact itself, but at the moment of telling. The thing he saw (and I know what he saw; I have always known; the not-knowing that I have maintained for forty years is a performance so thorough that I have at times convinced even myself, the way a man telling the same lie for decades wakes one morning and finds it has replaced the truth in his memory, so that the truth, when it surfaces, feels like the lie)—the thing itself is not what silences him. What silences him is the act of placing it between us, where it would become real in a way it has not been real while locked inside his monk's skull. A thing known by one man is a burden. A thing known by two men is a fact. He cannot bear to make it a fact. I could not bear to ask him to.*

## Letter Four

*Letter Three is omitted here at Yoshida's instruction. His note reads: "The third letter concerns only monastery life and the death of a cat. It is the only letter in which Hideo sounds at peace. I will not publish a man's peace."*

*Received February ——.*

Yoshida,

I have been reading Musashi again. I know this is predictable—the monk reading *Go Rin no Sho* the way the drunkard returns to the bottle—but I find I cannot help myself. There is a passage in the Water Book that I have read perhaps a thousand times over the course of my life, and I have understood it differently at each reading, which either means the text is bottomless or my understanding is shallow. Likely both.

He writes: *When your opponent is going to use a technique on you, let him do the useless ones but stop him from doing the useful ones.*

I used to read this as tactical advice. Now I read it as something else entirely. What does it mean to "stop him"? In Musashi's world, you stop him with the sword. But suppose one has set down the sword? Suppose one has encountered the useful technique—the decisive one, the one that would end the bout—and has chosen neither to use it nor to counter it but to leave the hall altogether? What then? Has one stopped him? Has one failed to stop him?

Has one, by refusing to act, achieved a kind of stopping more complete than any block or parry—a stopping that extends not for the duration of the technique but for the rest of both men's lives?

I am aware that I am still being evasive. The abbot says that I use philosophy the way I once used the shinai—as an instrument for maintaining distance. He is a shrewd man and I do not like him very much.

Tell me: do you still compete? I have no access to tournament results here and would not read them if I did, but I find that the question interests me more than the answer. Not whether you compete, but whether you stand on the platform and assume kamae and feel, in that moment, the thing that I—

No.

The plum farmer has died. His daughter continues the deliveries.

Hideo

---

**Yoshida's annotation:** *I competed for four more years after Enshū. My record was thirty-one wins, fourteen losses, and one draw. The draw was against Ozeki, at the Shizuoka prefectural, and it haunts me almost as much as the Enshū final, because Ozeki was not a strong fighter—he was a man with good reflexes and mediocre instincts—and I believe the draw occurred because I could not bring myself to strike his kote when it appeared open. I had developed, by then, a hesitation. An uncertainty that lived in the half-second between seeing the opening and committing to the strike. I knew what caused it. I could not say what caused it. These two facts are different.*

*Everyone who teaches kendō will tell you that hesitation is death. That the space between perception and action must be zero. That the strike must arise from mu-shin, no-mind, the way a mirror reflects without deciding to reflect. I taught this to my students for thirty years. I said it with conviction. Meanwhile, inside me, a dead man was asking: but what if the mirror sees something it cannot reflect? What if what it sees would break it?*

*I retired from competition because I was afraid that I would kill someone. Not with the shinai—the shinai is bamboo, the armor is armor, and men are not killed in modern kendō. I was afraid I would kill someone by defeating them the way Hideo could have defeated me: by seeing the thing they could not see in themselves, the flaw that lived below technique in the structure of the body itself, and exploiting it so completely that what died was not the opponent but his belief that he had ever been whole.*

*I know this sounds grandiose. I know a dying man is entitled to grandiosity and should resist the entitlement. I record it anyway.*

## Letter Seven

*Received November ——. This letter is notable for its length—nine pages, the longest in the collection—and for the fact that it appears to have been written over several days, with visible changes in ink consistency.*

Yoshida,

I must talk to you about bushido, and I am aware that this is like saying I must talk to you about the sea: where does one begin, and at what depth does one stop, and how does one avoid drowning in one's own metaphor?

The code, as I was taught it, rests on seven pillars: rectitude, courage, benevolence, respect, honesty, honor, and loyalty. I have recited them since childhood. I have engraved them on wooden plaques for my students. I have delivered speeches about them at ceremonies where men in suits nodded gravely and understood nothing, and I nodded back and understood, I now realize, approximately as much as they did.

Here is what I have learned in five years of sitting in silence: the pillars are not allies. They are opponents. They stand in a circle facing inward, and in the center of the circle is a man trying to act, and each pillar forbids the action that another demands.

Rectitude says: *the truth must be upheld*. Benevolence says: *the suffering of others must be prevented*. But what happens when the truth, upheld, would cause the suffering? What happens when the only righteous act is also the most cruel?
