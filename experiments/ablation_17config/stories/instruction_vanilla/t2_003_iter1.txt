# The Five Mercies of Perdition Gulch

## I. Now

The sickness came the way most things came to Perdition Gulch—slowly, then all at once, like the dry creek beds that remembered, in some mineral recess, the shape of water.

It began with Jonah.

Nobody noticed at first. A boy of eleven who goes quiet earns little remark in a town where silence is currency and noise a debt. But Maren noticed, because Maren noticed everything—every face, every figure, every shift in light through the warped glass of the general store where she kept accounts she never needed to write down. She noticed that Jonah had stopped flinching.

For six years, the boy had moved through Perdition Gulch like a man wading through briars, his small body tensing at every passerby, his eyes squeezing shut when the saloon crowd thickened on Saturday nights. Six years of other people's thoughts pressing against the inside of his skull like hornets in a jar. He'd told Maren once—only Maren, because she would remember it perfectly and therefore never need to be told again—that the thoughts didn't come as words, not exactly. More like weather. Some people carried storms. Some people were just wind.

Now the boy sat on the porch of the assay office, swinging his legs, and two cattlemen walked by close enough to stir the dust at his feet, and he did not flinch.

Maren watched from across the street. She cataloged the observation. She cross-referenced it against every other instance of Jonah in proximity to strangers, a vast internal library whose shelves stretched back to the first moment she'd seen him—a feral creature of five, shaking behind a water trough, hands clamped over his ears, screaming at voices no one else could hear.

Something was different.

Something was wrong.

---

The town of Perdition Gulch occupied a crease in the earth where the Sonoran scrubland buckled against the first shy assertions of the Dragoon Mountains. It had been founded in 1849 by prospectors who were certain the surrounding hills held silver. They did not. What the hills held was chalcite and disappointment, and by 1860, the prospectors had gone, leaving behind a name and a scatter of buildings that looked like the discarded set pieces of a play about human ambition performed for an audience of lizards.

It should have died then. Towns like it did die, all across the Territory—quietly, without ceremony, their lumber cannibalized, their names struck from maps no one was making yet anyway.

Instead, it persisted. New people drifted in, the way people do to places that have been abandoned: slowly, gratefully, with the specific relief of those who have run out of elsewhere. Ranchers. A blacksmith. Two women who ran a boarding house and slept in the same room and whom nobody questioned because nobody in Perdition Gulch wanted their own questions answered either.

By 1881, it was a town of perhaps three hundred souls, remote enough that the Territorial government took no particular interest, close enough to the border that certain men found it convenient, and dry enough that everyone understood thirst as a permanent state of being rather than a temporary complaint.

By 1883, it had the Five.

By 1887, it was the safest, strangest settlement in the Arizona Territory, and no one—not the cattlemen, not the miners who filtered through, not the Burlington & Pacific Railroad scouts who kept riding out to survey and kept, inexplicably, riding back confused and dissuaded—could quite articulate why.

---

Silas Cain was the one who named it.

"The sickness," he said, standing in the back room of Maren's store, holding his left hand up to the lamplight. There was a cut across his palm, a neat red line where he'd drawn a skinning knife across his own flesh with the methodical calm of a man conducting an experiment.

The cut was not closing.

Maren watched blood collect in the valley of his hand. She observed the precise color—not the bright arterial red she'd cataloged a thousand times from Silas's previous demonstrations, when the wounds would seal like mouths deciding against speech, but a darker, more honest red. A red that intended to keep flowing.

"How long?" she asked.

"Since Wednesday." Silas looked at his palm the way a man looks at a letter from a creditor. "Wednesday I nicked my thumb on a fencepost. Bled for twenty minutes. Twenty minutes, Maren. I haven't bled for twenty minutes since I was fourteen years old."

He was forty-three now. Maren knew his exact age because she knew everything—every date, every offhand remark, every numerical scrap that had ever passed within range of her senses. Silas Cain, born March 7, 1844, in a farmhouse outside of Joplin, Missouri. Had first discovered his ability at sixteen, when a hay rake punctured his thigh and the wound closed before he'd finished screaming. Had arrived in Perdition Gulch in 1882, fleeing—though he never used that word—a town in Colorado where people had started to notice that the doctor's hired man never scarred.

"And now?" Maren asked.

"Now it's Saturday." Silas closed his fist. Blood dripped between his fingers and onto the floorboards. "And I can feel it, Maren. Or—I can feel the *absence* of it. Like a tooth that's been pulled. You keep tonguing the socket."

"Jonah's stopped hearing."

Silas looked up. In the lamplight, his face performed its usual trick—a roughness that suggested cruelty but housed, in its deeper architecture, only exhaustion. "Stopped hearing thoughts?"

"I believe so."

"Good," Silas said, and then caught himself. "No. Not good."

"No," Maren agreed.

They stood in the back room with its smell of tallow and dried beans and the particular dust that was Perdition Gulch's primary export, and they did not say what they were both thinking, because what they were both thinking required the kind of honesty that could only survive in the dark and they were standing directly beneath a lamp.

What they were thinking was: *the Garza family. This is about the Garza family. This is about what we did, or didn't do, and now the bill is here.*

## II. Then

Understand this about the Five: they did not choose each other.

There was no meeting by moonlight, no ritual, no pact signed in blood or formalized with handshakes. There was only the gradual, startled recognition that comes when a person who has believed themselves to be the only example of a species discovers they are not.

Maren Elsbeth Voss arrived first, in 1878, carrying two carpetbags and a memory so total that it constituted, in its way, a form of imprisonment. She could not forget. She had never been able to forget. Every moment of her thirty-one years sat in her skull with the crispness of a daguerreotype, equally accessible, equally vivid—her mother's face the day she died (Maren was four), the way the light fell through the church window at her father's funeral (she was nine), the exact number of bricks in the facade of every building she had ever passed on every street in every town she had lived in or moved through.

It was not a gift. She wanted to be clear about that. When people imagined perfect memory, they imagined convenience—never losing a key, never forgetting a name. They did not imagine what it meant to relive, with complete sensory fidelity, every moment of grief, every humiliation, every cruelty received or inflicted. They did not understand that a woman who remembered everything was a woman who could never heal, because healing required the gentle erosion of time against the sharp edges of the past, and for Maren, there was no erosion. Every edge stayed sharp.

She'd come to Perdition Gulch because it was small and remote and because she'd calculated—she was always calculating—that a town with fewer people would give her fewer things to remember.

She was wrong, of course. A small town was dense with detail in ways a city was not. In a city, faces blurred into crowds. In Perdition Gulch, every face was singular, and every face had a story she could not stop recording.

Silas came next, in 1882. He'd walked into Maren's store to buy salt and she'd asked about the scar on his wrist—the only scar she'd ever seen on him—and he'd gone pale, because the scar was old, from before, from when he was still breakable, and no one had ever been observant enough to notice that a man who worked with his hands every day bore no other mark.

"You see too much," he told her.

"Yes," she said. "I see everything."

He did not leave the store for two hours. They sat in the back room—the same back room where, five years later, he would stand bleeding from an ordinary wound—and they traded their impossibilities like children showing each other contraband.

The twins came together, because of course they did. Luce and Vera Cahill, twenty-two years old, arrived in Perdition Gulch on the spring coach of 1883 carrying between them a single carpet bag, a single parasol, and a single consciousness that operated two bodies with the unsettling fluency of a pianist playing with both hands.

"They" were, in truth, more like an "it"—or, more precisely, a "she." One awareness, one internal voice, one unbroken stream of experience that happened to express itself through two sets of limbs, two mouths, two pairs of dark eyes that moved in a synchronicity so precise it made observers uneasy without being able to say why. The twins had learned, early, to fabricate the appearance of individuality—to disagree in public, to feign surprise at each other's remarks, to perform the theater of separate personhood that the world required. But in private, there was no Luce-and-Vera. There was only the Cahill girl, singular, who happened to occupy two rooms at once.

Maren recognized them on sight. Not the specifics of their ability—she was not, herself, a mind-reader—but the texture of the performance. She knew what it looked like when a person was pretending to be less than they were. She had been doing it for thirty-six years.

Jonah was last, and Jonah was an accident.

A woman named Clara Peck had arrived in the spring of 1883 with a small son who would not stop crying. She'd looked ill, drawn, desperate— Maren remembered (she always remembered) the exact quality of Clara Peck's desperation, which was the flat, affectless kind that comes after the sharper forms of despair have been exhausted. Clara had rented a room at the boarding house. Three days later she was found dead in the creek bed behind it—dead of what the town's informal doctor (a horse veterinarian named Pratt) called "exhaustion and exposure," which was the frontier's term for everything it could not explain or did not wish to examine.

The boy was left behind. Screaming, inconsolable, a howling knot of nerve and sinew no larger than a yearling calf, and impossible to approach because every time someone came near him, he would shriek things—private things, true things, things the approaching person had been thinking but had certainly not said aloud.

*You think I look like my mother the day she died and that frightens you.*

*You want to leave but you're worried what the woman beside you will think.*

*You're counting the buttons on your coat because the numbers calm you down.*

Silas found him. Or, rather, Silas's body found him—Silas, who healed from everything, whose flesh could not hold a wound, and who radiated, to Jonah's battered receivers, a broadcast signal of such profound biological calm that the boy stopped screaming for the first time in three days and simply stared.

"You're quiet inside," Jonah whispered.

And Silas, who had spent twenty-seven years knowing he was not like other people but not knowing why or what to do about it, knelt in the dust and said, "Yeah. I suppose I am."

## III. Now

The Cahill twins kept a house at the southern end of town, a narrow clapboard structure that had once been the assayer's residence and that they had purchased with money Maren suspected—but did not ask about—had been acquired through the strategic deployment of two-bodied omnipresence in poker games across three territories.

Vera answered the door. This, in itself, was information, because in the private architecture of the Cahill consciousness, "Vera" was the face they wore when they wanted to be careful. Luce was for warmth. Vera was for walls.

"Come in," Vera said, and as Maren crossed the threshold, she saw Luce sitting at the kitchen table, motionless, staring at a cup of coffee that had gone cold enough to show a faint skin on its surface.

Luce did not look up.

This, too, was information—and information of a catastrophic variety, because the Cahill twins did not operate independently. They did not have independent impulses. When one body moved, it was because the single intelligence that inhabited both had directed it to move. Luce sitting motionless while Vera moved and spoke was like watching a woman operate her right hand while her left lay paralyzed on the table.

"When?" Maren asked.

Vera closed the door. What happened next was the thing Maren would carry—had already begun carrying—with the same crystalline precision she carried everything. Vera's face, which had been performing composure, simply stopped. The mask didn't crack; it was set aside, consciously, the way one sets aside a plate.

"Three days ago," Vera said. "Three days ago I woke up and she—" Vera stopped. The pronoun, Maren understood, was wrong. There had never been a "she" that referred to only one of them. "I woke up and I could feel the edge of her. Like a wall where a hallway used to be. I could still reach across but it was—there was resistance. Today I can barely—"

Vera looked at Luce. Luce looked at her coffee.

"Today she is thinking things I cannot hear," Vera said.

The quiet in the kitchen was the particular quiet of a house in which something has broken that cannot be named because no one has ever needed a name for it. What vocabulary exists for the loss of a connection that the world insists cannot exist? What word describes the act of becoming singular when you have never, in forty-four years of shared consciousness, been alone?
