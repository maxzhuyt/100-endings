*September 9. First day. I'm in the garage, level three, and I should go home but I wanted to get this down while it's still—okay.*

*Start with the administrative stuff. I had to request this case three times before assignment came through. Filing called me back the first time to say it had been given to Deborah Wu. Then Deborah was reassigned — no, not reassigned, that's not what they said. "No longer available." Then it went to a contract reporter, Steven something, I have his name somewhere, and three days later he was also "no longer available" and they still hadn't empaneled the jury. So when I put in the third request, Maria in the clerk's office actually laughed and said, "You want this one? Okay, Margaret, it's all yours." Like she was handing me the last donut. Anyway.*

*The trial itself. Derek Solis is prosecuting, and he's good, the jury likes him, and Paul Koenig is defending, and he is — God, I shouldn't say this on a recording — he is not good. He is the kind of public defender who makes you understand why people hate public defenders. Shuffling his papers. Couldn't find a pen. The defendant, Eleanor Vance, sat there watching him fumble for his pen with this expression of — I don't know. It wasn't anger. It was the look you'd give a child who's trying to build something and doesn't have the right parts.*

*But that's not why I'm sitting in the parking garage talking into this thing.*

*The outburst. The transcript says "unintelligible." I typed "unintelligible." That is what I put into the record because it's the closest available notation for what happened, but it is not accurate. It was not unintelligible. It was the opposite of unintelligible. She stood up from the defense table mid-sentence — Solis was mid-sentence — and she spoke in a voice that was not the voice she'd used to say "Good morning, Your Honor" thirty seconds earlier. It was not shouting. It was not glossolalia. It was measured, rhythmic, and specific. Syllables I could not identify but could hear with absolute clarity. Each one distinct. Like someone reading aloud from a text in a language I don't speak, except that's not right either because my hands— this is the part. My hands tried to take it down. The steno machine is muscle memory; you don't think about the keys, you hear and your fingers translate, and my fingers were reaching for key combinations that don't exist. Not hunting for approximations the way you do with a foreign name or a technical term. Reaching. Like they knew where the keys should be and the keys simply weren't there. I have been a court reporter for fourteen years. I have never—*

*It lasted maybe six seconds. The bailiff reached her within four, but she was already sitting back down by the time he got there, and she was quiet, just looking at her hands on the table. Solis was white. Koenig was looking at his notes like the answer was in his notes.*

*And then after the recess they brought her back in, and the judge admonished her, and she was perfectly composed. Calm. Calm the way a doctor is calm when they're telling you something bad. She said: "I am sorry, Your Honor. It is not me who is speaking."*

*And the judge moved on, and Solis continued his opening, and the rest of the afternoon was standard. Jury's attentive. Solis laid out the timeline. Three children placed, escalating injuries over seven months, removal in October 2017, grand jury indictment February 2018, competency hearings delayed everything for a year and here we are.*

*But here's the thing that's going to stay with me. When Eleanor said that line — "It is not me who is speaking" — she wasn't looking at the judge. I'm certain of this because I was looking at her, and she was looking at me. Not at me. At the machine. At my hands on the stenotype. Her eyes tracked from my face to my hands and stayed there while she finished the sentence, and then she looked away, and I put my fingers back on the keys, and the trial went on.*

*She wasn't apologizing to the judge. She was explaining something. To me.*

*Okay, it's almost nine. I have a cat to feed.*

*September 10, evening. Second day of trial.*

*The pediatrician was credible. The injuries are bad. I've reported on abuse cases before, I know how to maintain professional distance, but the detail about the three-year-old moving her hands — I had to stop for a second. Just a second. Nobody noticed. My hands kept going.*

*I keep thinking about that. "Like someone typing." Bethany Flores is three years old. She has never used a keyboard. She has never seen a stenotype machine. But I watched the surveillance footage that Solis entered into evidence this afternoon — footage from the emergency department — and I can see the girl's hands moving, and I want to be very precise about this: the movement pattern is a steno chord progression. Not typing. Steno. The simultaneous multi-finger depression of keys that is specific to shorthand stenography. I have been making those motions for fourteen years. I know what they look like.*
