# The HOA Board Will Now Come to Order

## I.

The ramp was good work. Meredith had made sure of that. She'd hired Garza Brothers, who'd been pouring concrete in the northeast end of town for thirty years, and they'd done it right: thirty-six inches wide, gentle slope, handrails on both sides, a non-slip surface aggregate that caught the light in a way that made the concrete look almost blue in certain hours of morning. It had taken them four days, and on the last day the younger Garza had leveled himself up from his knees, wiped his forehead with the back of his glove, and said, "That's not going anywhere, Mrs. Chen." He'd meant it as reassurance. It had felt like a promise.

Two days later someone from the homeowners' association had zip-tied a laminated notice to the left handrail.

Meredith stood in the driveway reading it. The notice was printed in a serif font — Times New Roman, she was almost certain, which struck her as the kind of choice that revealed something about a person, though she wasn't sure what. The relevant passage was: *Exterior modification installed without prior Architectural Review Committee approval. Violation of Section 4.11(b): "All exterior structural additions, including but not limited to ramps, decks, pergolas, and raised garden beds, must receive written approval from the ARC no fewer than thirty (30) days prior to the commencement of construction." Fine: $50.00 per day, retroactive to date of installation, until violation is remedied or approval is obtained.*

Retroactive to date of installation. She did the math because she couldn't help it. Four days of construction, two days since completion. Three hundred dollars already. Fifty more tomorrow. Fifty more the day after that.

"Mere?" Arthur's voice came through the screen door, thin and scratchy, a recording of the voice she'd married played back on worse equipment. "What are you looking at?"

She folded the notice in half, then in half again, and put it in the pocket of her cardigan. "Nothing," she said. "Sun's nice."

"Come inside. There's that thing at four."

That thing at four was his occupational therapy session via the tablet, which he hated, and which she propped on the hospital tray at the correct angle and volume while he did hand exercises that made him look, he said, like a man trying to milk an invisible cow. She sat in the kitchen where he couldn't see her and listened to the therapist's bright, patient voice say, "Good, Arthur, now the other hand," and she pressed the heels of her palms against her eyes until she saw colors.

After dinner — canned soup she'd doctored with lemon and fresh dill, because she'd read somewhere that ALS patients lost taste gradually and she was trying to stay ahead of it — she helped him from the wheelchair into the hospital bed, which had lived in their living room for three months now, where their sectional used to be. The sectional was in the garage. His breathing settled into its new rhythm, the one that sounded like a man perpetually about to say something, and she went to the hall closet.

The bylaws were in a white three-ring binder on the top shelf, behind a box of Arthur's old tax software and a tote bag from a conference she'd attended in 2011. She didn't remember putting them there. She didn't remember receiving them. But there they were: ninety-four pages, including appendices, printed on paper that had gone faintly yellow in the way of documents that believe they are permanent.

She brought them to the kitchen table with a cup of tea she didn't drink and a pencil. She read the way she'd once read mortality tables — not for narrative, not for meaning, but for structure. For the logic underneath the language. For the load-bearing walls.

It took her three hours and forty minutes. She filled nine pages of a yellow legal pad with notes, section references, cross-citations. She used a system of symbols she'd developed in her twenties when she'd first started at the firm: a circle for a vulnerability, a square for a fixed rule, a triangle for something that depended on interpretation or timing. By the time she put the pencil down, the pad looked like it had been annotated by a particularly obsessive surveyor mapping terrain for an invasion.

The quorum requirement for the annual meeting: 15% of homeowners in good standing, present or by written proxy.

The process for removing a sitting board president: a motion from the floor, seconded, requiring a simple majority of those present and voting.

The requirement for any bylaw amendment passed at an emergency session: fourteen days' written notice to all homeowners, delivered by mail or hand, with proof of delivery.

The annual meeting was sixty-three days away. It was already scheduled. It couldn't be moved without a two-thirds board vote and thirty days' notice, and the board was Doug Whitmore and two people who did whatever Doug said and one vacancy that had been a vacancy for two years.

She looked at the pad. She looked at the binder. She thought about the ramp — the good ramp, the solid ramp, the ramp her husband needed to leave his own house — and the laminated notice zip-tied to it like a toe tag.

Then she picked up the phone.

---

Linda Mancuso arrived first because Linda arrived first to everything. It was a compulsion that had survived her career and the end of her career in roughly the same way a tick survives the death of the dog.

"You said it was important," Linda said, standing on the porch with a canvas bag over one shoulder that, Meredith happened to know, contained a stenotype machine. Linda had once been a court stenographer for the superior court, seventeen years, until an incident involving a judge's off-the-record comments that Linda had, in fact, recorded and subsequently shared with a reporter for the local paper. The judge had not been removed. Linda had. She now did freelance transcription for law firms and carried her machine the way other women carried knitting.

"It is important," Meredith said. "Come in. Don't mind the bed."

Linda looked at the hospital bed in the living room, at Arthur sleeping with his mouth slightly open, at the accordion tubing of the BiPAP machine, and said nothing, which was the correct thing to say, and which Meredith noted and appreciated.

Phil Rekowski came next, exactly on time, carrying a slim leather portfolio and a paperback copy of *Robert's Rules of Order Newly Revised, Twelfth Edition*, which he held not like a book but like a badge. Phil was sixty-eight, retired from something in municipal planning that he described differently each time he was asked, and he lived alone in a townhouse on Birchwood Lane that was, by his own account, maintained in full compliance with every applicable covenant, bylaw, and community standard. He had attended the annual meetings of the Creekside Homeowners' Association for nine years. He had also attended the annual meetings of the Pinewood Estates HOA, the Laurel Heights Community Association, the Fox Run Condominium Board, the Briarcliff Neighborhood Council, and at least seven other residential governance bodies to which he had no connection whatsoever. He attended them the way some men attend air shows: for the spectacle, for the machinery, for the rare and beautiful moment when someone who doesn't understand the rules encounters someone who does.

"You said this involves a procedural question," Phil said, settling into the chair Meredith had set out for him.

"It involves a procedural campaign."

His eyes sharpened. He opened the portfolio.

The book club came together, as they always did, in a configuration that suggested less a social gathering than a formation. There were five of them: Janet Okafor, seventy-one, retired hospital administrator; Diane Phelps, sixty-seven, retired high school principal; Bev Whitaker, sixty-four, semi-retired real estate attorney who still consulted "when things get interesting"; Connie Zhao, sixty-nine, who had run the regional office of a major insurance company for twenty-two years and who described her management style as "cheerfully undeniable"; and Ruth Moran, seventy-three, who had been an Air Force wife for thirty years and had moved fourteen times and said that the thing about packing a household into boxes repeatedly was that it taught you what was structural and what was decorative. They'd been meeting every other Thursday since 2013, ostensibly to discuss books. They had discussed books. They had also, over eleven years and approximately 286 meetings, discussed the following: two divorces (Diane's and Janet's), one federal workplace discrimination complaint (Connie's, settled), one adult child's addiction and recovery (Ruth's), three estrangements, one bankruptcy, and the slow, relentless, inexorable process by which the Creekside Commons Homeowners' Association, under the presidency of Douglas R. Whitmore, had become a small empire of laminated notices and selective enforcement.

They settled around Meredith's dining table — the same maple table where, fourteen years ago, she and Arthur had hosted dinner parties with wine pairings she chose and jokes he told, the two of them performing the marriage like musicians who knew each other's timing so well they could improvise without rehearsal. Now the table was covered in paper: the bylaws, the legal pad, a spreadsheet Meredith had started at two in the morning.

"Before I start," Meredith said, "I want to be clear about what this is."

She told them about the ramp. She told them about the fine. She did not tell them about saying "Nothing" to Arthur, because some things were not for the table, not even this table.

"Fifty dollars a day," Janet said.

"Retroactive," Meredith said.

"Do you have ARC records?" Bev asked, already in attorney mode. "Did you submit an application?"

"I submitted one four months ago, when the neurologist told us it was time. I got an acknowledgment letter. I never got a decision."

"Four months," Bev said.

"The bylaws require a response within sixty days. Section 7.3. I followed up twice, in writing. I have copies."

Bev made a sound that was not quite a word, the sound of a person encountering a legal position so poor that language temporarily fails.

"We could sue," Bev said. "ADA, FHA, reasonable accommodation —"

"We could," Meredith said. "It would take eight months minimum. Arthur doesn't have eight months."

The room recalibrated. It was a subtle thing — a shift in posture, a quieting — the collective recognition of a timeline that was not legal but biological.

"What do you want?" Connie asked.

Meredith laid it out. Not a lawsuit. Not a petition. Not a letter-writing campaign or a shouting match at a board meeting or any of the other instruments of complaint that Doug Whitmore had spent fifteen years absorbing and deflecting like a sea wall absorbs waves. She wanted to remove him. At the annual meeting. Using the bylaws — his bylaws, the ones he'd written and amended and wielded for a decade and a half. She wanted a motion from the floor, seconded and voted upon by a quorum of homeowners who understood exactly what they were voting for. She wanted it procedurally immaculate. She wanted him to have no recourse — no appeal, no technicality, no motion to reconsider. She wanted it to be, in every way that mattered, his own system doing exactly what his own system was designed to do.

"A coup," Phil said, and his voice carried the faintest tremor of pleasure, the way a bird dog's body trembles at the scent.

"A vote," Meredith said.

"Same thing," Phil said, "done properly."

"What about the embezzlement?" Linda asked.

Everyone looked at her.

"I've been transcribing the meeting minutes for three years," Linda said. Her fingers moved as she spoke, tapping the edge of the table in patterns that seemed involuntary, as though she were transcribing her own words in real time. "There's a landscaping company — Whitmore & Associates, no relation except it's his brother-in-law — that's been billing the mulch fund quarterly. The amounts are small. Fifteen hundred here, two thousand there. But they don't correspond to any actual landscaping schedule, and the receipts — when there are receipts — are handwritten."

"How do you know about the receipts?" Bev asked.

"I requested them. The bylaws guarantee homeowner access to financial records within ten business days of a written request. Section 11.2. I've been requesting them quarterly for two years."

"Why?" Meredith asked.

Linda's fingers stopped moving. "Because he revoked my community garden plot in 2021 for 'failure to maintain,' and the garden was fine. The garden was *fine*."

This, Meredith thought, was how it worked. Not one wound but many. Not a single injustice but an accumulation, a slow silting-up, until one day you looked around and realized you were standing in the company of other people who had also been accumulating, and the combined weight of it was enough to move something.

"I'll need everything," Meredith said. "Every receipt, every minute, every discrepancy. Can you build a timeline?"

"I already have one," Linda said.

Of course she did.

They divided the work. Phil would map the procedural sequence: the exact order of motions, the points of order, the fallback positions if Doug tried to adjourn or table or filibuster. He would prepare for seventeen different parliamentary scenarios, which he described the way a chess player describes variations — "if he goes Queen's Gambit, we play the Slav; if he tries to adjourn, we invoke Section 6.14(a), which requires a second and a majority vote to adjourn and he won't have the majority if we've done the proxy math right." Bev would handle legal review, ensuring nothing they planned crossed the line from procedural hardball into actionable territory. Connie and Janet would lead the proxy campaign — quiet, personal, conducted over kitchen tables and front porches and the kind of conversations that happened when you brought someone a casserole and stayed for forty minutes. Diane and Ruth would manage logistics: the room, the sign-in sheet, the verification of credentials, the physical choreography of where people sat and in what order things happened. Linda would prepare the financial documentation.

Meredith would build the PowerPoint.

"One more thing," Janet said, as they were gathering their things. "Doug's going to know something's happening. He always knows."

"He'll know something," Meredith agreed. "He won't know what."

"You're sure about that?"

"He's been president for fifteen years. Nobody's ever challenged him. He won't believe it's possible because he's never had to believe it's possible."

Janet looked at her for a moment. "All right," she said. "Then we'd better make sure it is."

After they left, Meredith cleared the table and found, underneath the stack of bylaws, an index card that had fallen from Linda's bag. On it, in Linda's small, precise hand, was a transcription of something — a line from a meeting Meredith didn't recognize: *It's not personal. It's for the good of the community. Motion carries.*

She put the card on the refrigerator with a magnet shaped like a lobster that Arthur had bought in Maine on their anniversary trip, years ago, when he could walk, when they could travel, when the future was a place they assumed they'd both get to.

---

Arthur was awake when she went to check on him. The BiPAP mask was off, which meant he'd removed it himself, which meant he'd had enough strength in his hands to do that, which was a good sign, which she noted and filed the way she'd once noted and filed favorable mortality indicators.

"Lot of people," he said.

"Book club."

"Book club doesn't use that tone of voice."

"What tone of voice?"

"Your work tone. Low and clear and everyone stops talking when you talk."

She sat on the edge of the bed, carefully, on the side where the rail was lowered. His hand found hers. His fingers were thinner than they'd been a month ago. She could feel the bones now, the architecture of him.

"They fined us for the ramp," she said. She hadn't planned to say it.

He was quiet for a moment. "How much?"

"Fifty a day."

"Ah," he said. And then: "The bastards."

She laughed. She didn't expect to. It came out short and raw, like a cough. "Yes."

"You're going to do something about it."

"Yes."

"Good," he said. He closed his eyes. His hand stayed in hers. "You'll be good at that."
