# The Collectors

## I.

The first thing I noticed was that Mara's rental car was packed wrong.

Not packed *full* — though it was that, trunk to ceiling, every window except the driver's a wall of fabric and cardboard — but packed with the wrong things. A standing lamp. A toaster still in its box. What looked like an entire winter coat collection pressed against the rear glass like pelts in a fur trader's window. She had driven here from Portland, an eleven-hour trip, and she had brought a standing lamp to an abandoned summer camp in New Hampshire.

She saw me seeing it. She didn't explain. She locked the car twice, checked the handle three times, and walked across the parking lot toward me with her keys balled tight in her fist, the way you'd carry a weapon.

"Caleb," she said.

"Mara."

Twenty-two years. She was forty-four now. So was I. Her hair was shorter and she'd developed a specific kind of posture I associated with people who spent their lives standing between things and the people who might take them — shoulders forward, arms close, the body as barricade. She looked at my face the way you'd look at a house you used to live in: searching for the familiar, finding mostly renovation.

"You got one too," she said, looking at the envelope in my hand.

"I got one too."

A Subaru pulled in. Blue, mud-specked, New York plates. The driver sat behind the wheel for a long time. I could see his hands moving — adjusting the mirror, adjusting it back, touching the dashboard, touching the gearshift, a restless circuit of contact that looked less like fidgeting and more like inventory. When Jay finally got out, i watched his right hand trail along the Subaru's flank the way a child trails fingers along a fence, and when it reached the back bumper his fingers closed around something and his hand went into his jacket pocket in a single smooth motion. He didn't look down. His hand had done this without consulting his eyes.

Later I'd learn it was a rusted lug nut, half-buried in the gravel. At the time I just saw a man whose hands were working a different shift than the rest of him.

"Jesus," Jay said, looking at us. "Jesus Christ."

He was taller than I'd predicted. You carry a version of everyone you knew as a child, a twelve-year-old's body scaled up by imagination, and it's never right. Jay at twelve had been small, fast, light-fingered even then, though we'd called it something else. Jay at forty-four was six-two, broad, wearing a suit jacket over a t-shirt like a man who'd come from work and torn off whatever he could. His hands kept moving — touching the fence post beside him, and then his right hand dipped again and came back with a small plastic reflector, the cheap kind bolted to roadside posts, and it disappeared into his pocket with the lug nut. Two objects in ninety seconds. His face showed no awareness of either acquisition.

"Where's Dana?" he said.

A Honda Civic was parked at the lot's far edge, driver's door open, engine ticking as it cooled. I'd been so focused on Mara's rolling storage unit that I'd missed it pulling in. The driver's seat was empty.

"She went that way," Mara said, pointing toward the tree line. "About four minutes ago."

We waited. The parking lot of Camp Kenoza was mostly gravel and weeds now, milkweed pushing through the cracks in what had been the welcome lane, a faded wooden sign reading CAMP K—OZA hanging by a single chain. Behind it the main gate listed on its hinges, and through the gap I could see the first of the cabins, squat and dark, their roofs soft with moss. The mountains behind them were black against a sky just beginning to lose its blue. It was a quarter past seven in the evening in late October. We had, according to the letters, until dawn.

Jay's hand found a bottle cap in the gravel. Pocketed.

Dana came back from the tree line after ten minutes, wiping her mouth with the back of her hand. She was holding a sleeve of Ritz crackers, or rather the cellophane ghost of one — empty, crumpled, already being compressed between her palms into something she could hide. She was shorter than I'd expected. The child-to-adult prediction was off in the other direction. She looked at the three of us and something in her face performed a complicated negotiation between relief and horror, and horror won.

"I know," she said. "I know, I know."

I wasn't sure what she knew. I wasn't sure any of us were talking about the same thing yet.

"I'm going to read the letter," I said, because someone had to organize this, and I could feel the four of us vibrating at different frequencies — Mara guarding her car, Jay accumulating gravel, Dana hiding her cracker sleeve — and if nobody took hold of the situation it would fly apart before it started. "Everybody got the same one, I assume. But I'm going to read it out loud so we're all on the same page. Okay? Can we do that?"

Nobody objected. I noticed they all looked at me the way people always look at me when I do this — with a relief so instant it passes for agreement. I've been told I have that kind of voice. Calm. Organized. The voice you want when the situation is bad but not yet out of control. I've built my whole life on that voice and what it can manage.

I opened the envelope.

"'Dear Caleb,'" I read. "'Dear Mara, Jay, and Dana. On the night of August eleventh, two thousand and two, the four of you buried something in the woods behind Cabin Nine at Camp Kenoza. You know what it is. You have not spoken of it in twenty-two years, and you have not spoken to each other.'"

Mara's hand tightened on her keys. Jay went very still — the first time his hands had stopped moving since he'd stepped out of the car.

"'In those twenty-two years,'" I continued, "'you have each become something. Mara Ellison-Ward has applied to adopt a child through the Pine Tree Adoption Collaborative. Her home study is in February.'"

Mara's face drained. Not slowly, not dramatically, but like a plug had been pulled somewhere behind her eyes. She looked at her packed car and then back at me and I understood, in that moment, exactly what was at stake for her and exactly why her house was full and exactly why a social worker walking through that house would end her application before it started.

"'Jay Okafor-Stein has been a junior associate at Cahill Gordon for fourteen months. The firm's background check did not surface his two sealed juvenile shoplifting charges or the adult misdemeanor in 2019, but a detailed letter to the managing partner would.'"

"Don't," Jay said quietly. Not to me. To the letter.

"'Dana Reeves is engaged to Michael Kessler, who does not know about her years at Renfrew or the relapse in 2021 or the relapse in 2023.'"

Dana sat down on the gravel. Just folded her legs and sat, the way a child sits when standing becomes theoretical. She didn't say anything. She pressed the flattened cracker sleeve between her palms again and again, a rhythmic compression, her body finding a repetitive motion to absorb what her mind couldn't.

"'Caleb —'"

I stopped. The next line named me. Named what I had become and what could be sent and to whom. I read it silently, then folded that part of the letter with my thumb, a casual gesture, a magician's misdirect.

"The rest is logistics," I said. "We have until dawn. We retrieve what's in the woods behind Cabin Nine. We bring it to the main office. If we don't, copies of a detailed accounting of the night in question and our current situations go to Mara's agency, Jay's firm, Dana's fiancé, and —" I paused just long enough that the pause itself was a kind of object, something they could see me holding. "And a relevant party in my case. It doesn't matter. What matters is the timeline. We have roughly ten hours."

"Who sent it?" Dana asked from the ground.

"It's not signed."

"You know who sent it," Jay said. "We all know who sent it."

I did know. We all knew. But none of us said the name, because saying it would make the parking lot of Camp Kenoza feel like the parking lot of Camp Kenoza, which would make us twelve again, which would put us back in the woods, which would make us feel the weight of the shovel, and we weren't ready for that yet. I wasn't ready for that yet. I needed to manage the sequence. First, the practical reality: enter the camp, locate Cabin Nine, find the woods, dig. A simple operation. I could do simple operations. I could break any crisis into steps. You go through it the way you eat an elephant — one organized bite at a time.

"Let's go in," I said. "We can figure out the rest inside."

I went first, because someone had to, and it might as well be someone who could keep their head on straight. The gate groaned when I pushed it. The camp opened up before us in declining rows of cabins and common buildings, a landscape of low structures being slowly consumed by forest. Birch saplings had colonized the old volleyball court. The dining hall's windows were dark.

We crossed the threshold, all four of us, single file, and the moment Jay — the last one through — cleared the gate, something happened.

The lights came on.

Not all of them. But enough. Floodlights on the dining hall's eaves, footpath lanterns we hadn't seen buried in the overgrowth, a strand of bare bulbs strung between the first row of cabins like a carnival midway. The camp illuminated itself in sections, a rolling wave of light pushing across the grounds, and before the last bulb had flickered on we heard the hiss — a crackle of ancient PA speakers, mounted on poles we hadn't noticed, coughing to life along the main path.

Static. Then a voice.

"Welcome back to Camp Kenoza," it said. "Cabin assignments will be posted in the morning. For now, please proceed along the marked path to the main activity center for evening programming."

The voice was older. Of course it was older. But it had the same cadence, the same camp-counselor brightness stretched over something harder, the same way of making an instruction sound like an invitation.

We all recognized it. I saw it hit each of them differently: Mara flinched and stepped closer to me, Jay's hands went into his pockets, Dana stood up from the gravel at last and wrapped her arms around herself. Each body responded the way a body responds to a frequency it learned before it had words — involuntarily, cellularly, the muscle memory of childhood authority snapping their postures back twenty-two years.

"Tonight," the voice continued, "we have a number of special activities planned. The path is lit. Please stay on the path."

The PA clicked off. The camp hummed with electric light. The trees pressed in on all sides, and the path was lit — a double row of lanterns leading away from the gate and deeper into the grounds, bending left around the dining hall toward the activity buildings we remembered from a time when we were small enough to be told where to go and afraid enough to go there.

"So," Jay said.

"So," I said. And because the silence was threatening to become the kind of silence where people make individual decisions, where the group fragments and nobody is steering and the whole thing falls apart: "We stay together. We go where the path goes. We figure out what this is. Okay?"

"Okay," Mara said, too quickly, the way she'd said it twenty-two years ago when I was the one who decided we should bury it, when I was the one who said *I'll handle this, just follow me, it's going to be okay* —

But that's not the order things go in. That part comes later. Right now we were four adults on a lit path in the dark, and I was the one walking in front, and that was enough. That was the thing I could manage.

## II.

The path led us through the camp's old bones. Cabins we'd slept in, though we couldn't agree on which ones. A tetherball pole, its rope long rotted, standing like a dead stalk in a clearing. The shower block, collapsed on one side, tiles visible through the broken wall like the inside of a mouth. Jay's hands were busy the entire walk — a piece of broken tile, a hinge pin, a screw — each object liberated from the landscape and deposited into his pockets with the frictionless efficiency of a coin sorter. I counted seven acquisitions in the first two hundred yards and then stopped counting because counting was its own kind of compulsion and I was organizing again, cataloguing everyone else's behavior so I wouldn't have to catalogue my own.

The first building the path funneled us into was not Cabin Nine. It was not the woods. It was a long, low structure I recognized, after a moment, as the old arts and crafts pavilion, though its interior had been gutted and rebuilt into something else.

A corridor. Narrow, lit by bare bulbs on a wire, and lined on both sides with shelving — industrial shelving, floor to ceiling, and on every shelf: objects. Teapots, wooden boxes, porcelain figurines, folded quilts, vintage cameras, snow globes, picture frames with strangers' photos still in them, mason jars of buttons, cigar boxes of stamps, a taxidermied owl missing one eye, a child's rocking chair with a needlepoint cushion, a brass telescope, four mismatched candlesticks, a sewing machine, an accordion. The shelves went on and on, deeper into the building than the building should have been able to contain, and everything on them shared a single quality: they looked abandoned. Not arranged for sale or display but *left* — the detritus of estate sales and foreclosures and storage units that no one came to claim.

Mara made a sound.

Not a word. A sound — something between recognition and hunger, the way a person gasps when they round a corner and see the ocean. She stepped forward and her hand came up and touched a ceramic pitcher on the nearest shelf with such tenderness that I thought for a moment she was greeting it.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, look at this."

She picked up the pitcher. Then a wooden box beside it. She opened the box — it was full of glass doorknobs — and said, "These can't just be *here*, someone needs to —" and stopped herself, but her hands didn't stop. They moved from shelf to shelf with the desperate efficiency of a person pulling things from a burning building. A snow globe. A set of brass letter openers. A doll with a cracked face and real human hair.

"Mara," I said. "Mara, these aren't yours. Someone put these here. This is — these were placed here for you. Do you understand?"

She looked at me, and I watched her know it and not be able to use the knowledge. Her arms were full. She was already calculating how to carry more.

"I know," she said. "I *know*. But I can't —" She looked at the shelves stretching away into the corridor's depths. "Someone just *left* all of this. If I don't —"

"You don't have to save them," I said.

"You don't know that."

Jay was watching from the corridor's entrance with his hands in his pockets, touching his collection.

"Put them down, Mara," he said. Not unkindly. He said it the way you say it when you recognize the territory.
