# The Lineup

## I.

The outdoor court had not been repainted since the Reagan administration, and its three-point line was a suggestion more than a boundary—a faded white arc that stopped, reconsidered, and picked up again two feet to the left. Maya Arnette had dragged five folding chairs from the janitor's closet in the science wing and set them in a half-circle at the free-throw line, and when the last chair wobbled on a crack in the asphalt, she wedged a torn piece of cardboard under its front leg with the focus of someone defusing ordnance. She was a point guard. Details were the job.

They came in ones and twos across the faculty parking lot, backpacks slung wrong, faces doing the work of not showing what was underneath. Tameka Williams arrived first because the center always sets the screen before anyone else moves, and because she had not slept and the apartment was unbearable. She sat in the chair nearest the baseline and stretched her legs into the key and looked at the sky, which was the faint dishwater gray of early November in a city that could not commit to autumn. Reena Watts, the shooting guard, came next, earbuds in, nodding to something with too much bass, her phone held at an angle that let her read it without appearing to read it. Then Jessie Ocampo and Dana Morrison together, Jessie bouncing a tennis ball she'd found on the walk over, Dana carrying two coffees and looking for a third hand.

"Kiesha's not coming?" Dana asked.

"Kiesha tore her ACL in August," Maya said. "I need people who can move."

They understood she did not mean on the court.

"Okay," Maya said, and she stood because she could not say this sitting down, and because standing at the top of the key was the only posture in which her body knew how to organize a play. "Coach Richmond confessed. You know that. Everybody in the district knows that because Eyewitness News put his mugshot next to our team photo and the anchor made a pun about 'foul play' and my mother called me four times."

Nobody laughed. Nobody was supposed to.

"Here's what the confession sounds like." Maya pulled a folded printout from her back pocket—a transcript Reena's cousin at the public defender's office had texted as a photograph at 2 a.m. She read it aloud. The words were precise, sequential, causal. At 9:15 p.m. I entered the gymnasium through the east door. I confronted Marcus Bell about his continued harassment of my former players. The confrontation became physical. I struck him with a hand weight from the equipment room. He fell. I did not call for help. I left through the same door at approximately 9:40 p.m.

Maya folded the paper. "That's not how people talk when they've killed someone. That's how people talk when they've practiced."

Tameka tilted her head. The way she did when a play was developing and she needed to see the whole floor from above.

"His alibi for the hours before and after," Maya continued, "is that he was home alone watching the Celtics game. No witnesses. No food delivery. No phone calls. Which is exactly the alibi you'd pick if you wanted it to collapse. A guilty man with a bad alibi looks more guilty than a guilty man with a good one." She paused. "Richmond hated Marcus Bell. Everybody knew that. But hatred isn't evidence, and his confession is too clean, and the timeline fits like someone measured it with a ruler."

She looked at each of them, one at a time, the way she looked down the court before initiating: reading, calculating, assigning.

"Tameka. You lip-read. You've been doing it since you were six. You told me once you could reconstruct a conversation from twenty feet through a car window."

"Fifteen," Tameka said. "Twenty's a stretch."

"Marcus Bell made a phone call from the gym lobby fourteen minutes before he died. The security camera caught him on the phone but there's no audio. I need you to pull that footage and tell me what he said."

Tameka nodded, once, a motion so minimal it might have been the wind.

"Reena. Your mom works intake at Mercy General. I need you to find out if Marcus Bell visited the ER in the two weeks before his death. Any visit, any record, anything."

Reena removed one earbud. "My mom could lose her job."

"She could."

Reena put the earbud back in. She did not say no.

"Jessie. You're six feet tall and nobody on this campus knows your name. You can walk into any room in this building at any hour and no one will stop you because no one sees you. I need you in the equipment room. I need to know how someone got in the night Marcus Bell died. The police report says the lock was forced. I don't believe it."

Jessie stopped bouncing the tennis ball. She held it in her palm and stared at it as though it were a planet she was deciding whether to keep.

"Dana, you and I are going to reconstruct the timeline from the outside. Every camera angle, every entrance log, every janitor's schedule."

"What about Coach Warren?" Dana asked. Their current coach, the one who had replaced Richmond two years ago when Richmond was fired for what the school board called "philosophical differences" and what everyone else called getting drunk at the booster banquet and telling the superintendent his daughter had no business being on the JV squad.

"We'll need to talk to him eventually," Maya said. "But not yet. Not until we know enough to ask the right questions."

Dana nodded.

They all agreed. Of course they did. They were a team, and a teammate was asking, and the cause was just: an innocent man rotting under fluorescent lights for a murder shaped too perfectly to be real.

The omniscient eye rises, now, above the key's cracked paint and the five chairs and the November sky, and looks down the way a coach looks down from the top row of the bleachers during walkthroughs—not at any single player but at the system, the geometry, the five bodies and the vectors humming invisibly between them.

Maya Arnette knew that Marcus Bell, in his capacity as an assistant athletic director, had discovered thirteen days before his death that her disability classification—the one that qualified her for the adaptive scholarship track and made her eligible under the conference's accessibility provisions—was built on a diagnosis she did not have, documented by a physician who had been paid, supported by test results that had been altered. Bell had left her a voicemail that she had not deleted because deleting it felt more suspicious than keeping it. *Maya, we need to talk about your file. I'm not trying to end your career. But I can't sit on this past Friday.* Friday was four days after he died. If the investigation she was now building reached that voicemail, it would find her at the center of the motive they were supposed to be pulling apart through the arrested man.

She knew this. She had done the math—not the moral math, because moral math was for people who had time, and she had forty-eight hours before the police would start reinterviewing the team—and the math told her that the safest place to stand in an investigation was at its head. You could not be ambushed by a finding you were the first to see.

Tameka Williams had told Maya the truth about her lip-reading range, and the truth was that it extended well beyond twenty feet on a clear day with good sightlines, a fact she usually downplayed because the things you catch people saying when they don't know you're watching are rarely things you want to have caught. Seven weeks before Marcus Bell's death, Tameka had been walking the perimeter of the tennis courts after a conditioning session and had stopped at the chain-link fence that bordered the south parking lot. Through the window of Coach Warren's office, thirty-two feet away and one floor up, she had watched Marcus Bell lean across the desk, his mouth forming shapes she could only partially reassemble because the angle was oblique and the afternoon light was cutting hard across the glass. She caught: *I know about the payments*. She caught: *Since last year*. She caught Bell's index finger pointing at Warren's chest, and Warren's mouth forming a single word she could not decipher because he turned his head at the critical instant—but whose shape, in her memory, carried the contour of *please*. She had told no one. She had spent three weeks trying to convince herself she'd misread *payments* as *patience* and *since last year* as *since last here*, which was not even a phrase, which is how she knew she was lying to herself. If the investigation she had just joined reached Coach Warren's finances, it would find whatever Bell had found, and the thread from that spool would lead, potentially, to the chain-link fence and the woman standing behind it, and the question of how long she had known and why she had said nothing.

Reena Watts had not waited for Maya's request. Forty hours earlier—six hours after the murder, two hours after Richmond's arrest—she had texted her mother a careful, casual question: *Hey did a Marcus Bell come through Mercy Gen intake recently? Someone at school said he fell and I wanted to see if he was okay before, you know.* Her mother, who triaged sixty patients a shift and maintained the cheerful boundary-lessness of a woman who had been doing the job too long to pretend the rules still mattered, had texted back a photograph of an intake form. Bell, Marcus J. Admitted 11/2, 10:47 p.m. Chief complaint: contusions to left forearm, laceration above right eyebrow. Patient states injuries sustained in a fall from a stepladder while hanging shelves at home. Nurse's note, handwritten in the margin: *Injuries inconsistent with reported mechanism. Contusions appear defensive in pattern. Patient became agitated when asked to clarify. Declined follow-up.* Below the nurse's note, in the field for emergency contact, a name that Reena recognized immediately and had not spoken aloud to anyone in the forty hours since: Diana Arnette. Maya's mother. Who was not Bell's wife, not his family, not his listed next of kin in any school directory. Reena did not know what it meant. She knew that it meant something that would change shape once she said it, and she was not ready for the new shape.

Jessie Ocampo had been in the equipment room the night Marcus Bell died.

This was a fact she had held in her body for forty-eight hours the way muscle holds a cramp—tightly, involuntarily, in a place she could not reach to release. She had gone back at 8:50 p.m., twenty-five minutes before the murder according to the police timeline, to retrieve the knee brace she'd left on the shelf behind the ball rack after afternoon practice. She had a key. She'd had a key since sophomore year, when Coach Warren gave her one because she was the only player who consistently stayed after to clean up and the only one he trusted not to lose it. She had entered through the side door, crossed the dark gym—the overhead lights were off but the emergency exit signs made the space navigable in a red, submarine glow—and pushed open the equipment room door, and the brace was where she'd left it, on the third shelf between a box of pinnies and a deflated volleyball. She took it. She turned to leave. And she heard the east door open.

Not the sound of someone entering casually. The sound of someone entering the way you enter a room you've already decided to enter hours ago: no hesitation, no pause to orient, the footsteps mapped and committed.

She did not see who it was. She pressed herself against the shelving and held her breath and listened to the footsteps cross the gym floor—sneakers on hardwood, a sound she knew as intimately as her own pulse—and then another set of footsteps, lighter, already inside, coming from the direction of the lobby. Two people converging. She heard a voice, too low to identify, say something with the cadence of a question. She heard a second voice answer. She did not hear the words. She left through the side door while the voices were still talking, her brace clutched against her chest, and she crossed the parking lot in the dark and drove home and sat in her car in the driveway for eleven minutes before going inside.

She had heard two voices. Two people in the gym, twenty-five minutes before a man died there. She could not identify them. She told herself she could not identify them. She had spent forty-eight hours telling herself this with the frantic, repetitive energy of someone trying to sand a scratch out of glass, and the scratch was still there, and beneath it was the faint, inadmissible knowledge that one of the voices had carried the particular downward cadence—the way the pitch dropped at the end of a sentence like a stone into water—that she had heard a thousand times in a thousand practices, in a thousand plays called from the top of the key.

She could not identify the voice. She could not. She had joined this investigation to make sure of that.

---

These five women agreed to find the truth. The reader may note that not one of them wanted it.

## II.

Seventy-two hours compressed the way a season compresses in memory—long stretches of tedium punctuated by moments that rearranged everything, and afterward you could not remember the tedium at all, only the rearranging.

Tameka obtained the security footage on Tuesday morning. The camera was mounted above the lobby's trophy case at an angle designed to capture the entrance, not the phone alcove, which meant that Marcus Bell's face during his final call was visible only in a three-quarter profile, his mouth intermittently obscured by the hand holding his phone. She watched the footage eleven times on her laptop in the back row of her statistics lecture. She watched it seven more times in the library bathroom, her forehead nearly touching the screen.

She could reconstruct approximately sixty percent of his side of the conversation. She wrote down what she caught in a notebook she would later wish she had burned:

*—done protecting this—*
*—told you in September, I told you what would—*
*—they'll all find out Friday—*
*—I don't care what happens to the program. I don't care what—*
*—not my problem anymore—*

The last phrase was the clearest. His hand had dropped to his side and his mouth was fully visible and he said it with the exhausted precision of someone who had rehearsed the boundary and was speaking it aloud for the final time. *Not my problem anymore.*

He was arguing with someone about the team. About protecting something—or refusing to continue protecting it. About a Friday revelation that he controlled and someone else feared.

Tameka presented the reconstruction to the group in the student union on Tuesday afternoon. She read the phrases aloud. She presented them as the complete and honest product of her lip-reading. She did not mention that at the 1:47 mark, Bell had turned fully toward the camera for approximately two seconds—a compensatory angle shift, the kind you make when you're pacing—and that his mouth, in those two seconds, had formed a sequence she had not included in her notebook.

The sequence was: *Tell Maya I'm sorry it has to be this way.*

She omitted this. She told herself the angle was bad and the reading was uncertain and the light was poor, and two of those three things were lies.

"He was talking about the team," Maya said, reading Tameka's notes. "About exposing something."

"About a lot of teams," Dana offered. "He worked with six programs."

"He said *the* program," Tameka corrected. "Not *a* program."

The room absorbed this.

"So someone on a team, or connected to a team, had a reason to keep him quiet," Reena said. She said it looking at her hands. She said it having already read the intake form with Diana Arnette's name in the emergency contact field, a detail she had carried alone for three days, a stone in her pocket growing heavier with each hour, and she said it knowing that *the program* could mean their program, and that the person Bell was threatening to expose could be sitting in this room, and that the intake form in her phone was a thread that, if pulled, would unravel things she could not predict and did not want to see.

"I got the hospital records," Reena said.

She told them about the visit. The date. The contusions, the laceration, the nurse's note about defensive wounds. She told them Bell had claimed he fell from a stepladder.

"Defensive wounds," Maya repeated. "Someone hit him. A week before he died, someone hit him."

"Or he was in a fight," Dana said.

"A fight he lied about," Maya said.
