# Form 27-B: Application for Continued Existence

## I.

The printout was eleven meters long and Captain Lev Olshansky held it the way a man holds a snake he's just found in his bed—at arm's length, with the kind of trembling that can't decide if it's fear or fury.

"Rejected," he said.

The bridge was quiet. It was always quiet these days, the *Provisional Remainder* being a ship of three thousand people who had grown very good at not making unnecessary noise, the way your body gets good at not wasting calories when calories become theoretical. But this was a different quiet. This was the quiet of a courtroom when the verdict comes in wrong.

"All of them," Olshansky continued, his voice finding its register now, climbing toward the place it went when he needed the ship to remember he was the kind of man who hit things on tables. He hit the printout on the table. "Four thousand six hundred and twelve petitions. Seventeen years of documentation. Every birth, every death, every poem Anika Cho wrote for the Cultural Preservation attachment, every gene sequence we catalogued, every—" He stopped, breathed, hit the table again for good measure. "They didn't even read them."

He was right about that, though not for the reasons he thought.

"The forms were not *denied*," said Commander Tahan, who had the printout's header on her own screen and was reading with the expression of a woman performing her own autopsy. "They were rejected as improperly formatted. Filing series discrepancy."

"What does that mean in a language spoken by people who *feel things*?"

Tahan opened her mouth, but she didn't have the answer, because the answer was not a military answer or a leadership answer or even a common-sense answer. The answer belonged to a category of knowledge so specific and so universally disrespected that only one person on a ship of three thousand possessed it, and she was sitting in the third row of bridge-adjacent seating, where non-essential personnel were permitted during all-hands transmissions, holding a stylus she'd been using to update the seed bank's germination failure log.

Dara Kessler picked up the printout. She didn't pick up the end that Olshansky had been waving. She picked up the other end, the part that had coiled on the floor, where the rejection codes were printed in a font so small it seemed philosophically opposed to being read. She flipped a section over. Read it. Flipped another section. Read that too.

"Oh," she said.

Olshansky turned. He had the look of a man who has been shouting at the ocean and has just noticed someone behind him with a tide chart.

"They needed 27-B," Dara said. "We've been sending 27-A."

The bridge processed this.

"We've been sending the wrong form," Tahan said.

"Not the wrong form, the wrong series. The A-series is for civilizations requesting a hearing before processing. It's pre-processing petition protocol. The B-series—" Dara ran her stylus along a line of code so dense it looked like a graphical error. "The B-series is for civilizations requesting continued existence after processing has already occurred."

"And?"

"And Earth was processed. Eight days after we launched. It says that here, in the second footnote of our original receipt of departure—which, to be fair, is printed in a spectral range we can barely see." She looked up. "We're a post-processed species filing pre-processed paperwork. It's like—I once saw a man lose his house because he checked 'renewal' instead of 'replacement' on a title transfer. His signature was fine. His identity was verified. He owned the house. But the form said 'renewal,' and the system for renewals had a different jurisdiction pathway, and by the time anyone figured out what happened his property had been reclassified as a drainage easement."

"You're comparing the extinction of humanity to a title transfer."

"I'm comparing it to a *filing error* on a title transfer," Dara said, and the distinction, to her, was clearly important.

Olshansky stared at her for a long time. Dara was forty-three, with the kind of face that looked like it had been designed for waiting rooms—patient, unspectacular, lived-in. Before the launch she had worked at the Department of Motor Vehicles on Sherman Way in Reseda, California, a place that no longer existed in any meaningful sense except insofar as it had produced the only person who looked at a species-extinction rejection notice and saw a *routing error*.

"So we refile," Olshansky said.

"We refile with 27-B."

"And if they reject that too?"

"Then we appeal the rejection. There's always an appeal process. If there weren't, there wouldn't be a code for it, and there's a code for it. It's in the footer. Code 11-QR-7, subsection—here." She held up the printout. "A bureaucracy that doesn't allow appeals isn't a bureaucracy. It's a wall. Bureaucracies have doors. They're just not where you expect."

Olshansky looked at Commander Tahan. Tahan looked at Olshansky. Neither of them had the Etgar Keret short story expression for this moment—the face you make when you realize the punchline *is* the situation.

"Seventeen years," Olshansky said, very quietly. "We've been writing to the wrong department for seventeen years?"

"They probably have a room somewhere," Dara said, not unkindly. "Full of our petitions. Filed correctly as incorrectly filed. Very organized about it, I'm sure."

Olshansky sat down.

---

Three decks below, in the school module that smelled like recycled air and the particular brand of despair that clings to educational spaces operating on a civilization's last reserves, a seven-year-old named Yotam Garner was drawing with the last of the wax crayons.

He had the red, the blue, and a yellow so worn down it was essentially a waxy fingernail. The paper was the back of a decommissioned maintenance log, which his teacher, Priya, had cut into rectangles because they hadn't had fresh paper since Year Eleven.

The picture showed a big triangle. The triangle was the ship. Next to it was a circle, which was a planet—not Earth, because Yotam had never seen Earth and thought of it the way some children think of grandparents who died before they were born: real, technically, but not *available*. This planet was the one they were going to. The one with the dirt and the sky. Between the triangle and the circle, stick figures held hands in a line, like a bridge made of people.

"That's nice, Yotam," Priya said, and pinned it to the wall next to thirty other drawings, most of which depicted the same three subjects that dominated the artistic output of children born in transit: the ship, a planet, and the space between them filled with wanting.

Nobody noticed that the compositional arrangement—large angular form, circular form, bilateral connective figures arrayed in descending scale between them—precisely mirrored the glyph-syntax the Auditors used for formal petitions. This was because nobody on the *Provisional Remainder* had ever seen an Auditor formal petition, and because the notion that a seven-year-old could produce one by accident belonged to a category of events the universe preferred not to explain.

The yellow crayon finally gave out. Yotam looked at the blunt, paperless stub, turned to Priya, and said, "Can I have another one?"

Priya had been teaching for four years on a ship where "another one" was the most heartbreaking phrase in any language.

"Let's see what we can find," she said, which was what she always said, which was the teacher's version of a 27-B: a petition for continued existence filed against absurd odds, in a format that might or might not be correct, but submitted anyway because the alternative was sitting in a chair and saying nothing.

---

## II.

The corrected form took Dara nine days to prepare.

Nine days was remarkable, given that the Auditor procedural codex for the B-series alone ran to what, if printed, would have constituted roughly 1.3 million pages of extraordinarily fine print—a document so dense that reading it felt less like processing information and more like being processed *by* it. The codex had margins that contained footnotes with their own margins. It referenced appendices that referenced sub-appendices that, in at least one case, referenced the original document circularly, creating a textual loop that Dara suspected was either an error or a koan.

She worked in the seed bank, because that was where her desk was, surrounded by vacuum-sealed packets of genetic material labeled in handwriting that got progressively less optimistic as the years advanced—the early labels careful, hopeful (*Triticum aestivum – winter wheat, high-yield hybrid*), the recent ones terse (*Wheat #8, germ. unknown*). She drank a great deal of coffee, or rather the substance the *Provisional Remainder*'s galley called coffee, which was to actual coffee what a hostage note is to a letter.

On the tenth day she submitted Form 27-B, filing code 27-B/EX-CONT/SOL-III/POST-PROC, with seventeen attached appendices, two supporting cultural exhibits (she used Anika Cho's poems; they were good poems), and a cover letter that she spent four hours on and that read, in its entirety: *To Whom It May Concern: We would like to continue existing. Please find enclosed the relevant documentation. Thank you for your time.*

The response came in eleven minutes.

It was not a rejection. It was not an approval. It was an invitation.

---

"'You are hereby invited,'" Tahan read aloud, "'to present your petition in person at Auditor Processing Node 7781-Provisional, Liminal Administrative Zone, Non-Temporal Jurisdiction.' There are coordinates."

"Not spatial coordinates," said the ship's navigator, Emil Reyes, who had been staring at the numbers for several minutes with the expression of a man asked to find the square root of the color blue. "These aren't—they're not a *place*. They're more like an instruction set. 'Fold here, fold here, arrive.'"

"Can you follow them?"

"I can follow anything. I'm saying what I'm following might not be a location in any sense I understand."

Olshansky looked at Dara.

"We should go," Dara said.

"Into non-temporal jurisdiction."

"They're seeing us without an appointment. That's the equivalent of—do you know how hard it is to get a walk-in at the DMV? When I was at Sherman Way we had people camp out. This is a gift. We should go before they change their mind."

"Do bureaucracies change their mind?"

"No. But they *lose files*."

That settled it.

---

The fold, as Emil would later describe it to the ship's log, was like someone had picked up the universe by one corner and given it a sharp, decisive crease.

The *Provisional Remainder* dropped out of normal space the way a coin drops out of a conversation—one moment present, the next somewhere else entirely, leaving behind only the vague sense that something had been there.

What they dropped into was a waiting room.

It was enormous. It was white—not the white of blank paper or of snow, but the white of a space that had never been asked to be any color at all and had defaulted to the absence of a decision. Rows of chairs extended in every direction, arranged in configurations that suggested the room served clients of radically different anatomies. Some chairs were recognizable. Most weren't. One appeared to be designed for a being that was primarily a gas. Several were just holes in the floor with cushions around the rim.

There was a numbered ticket dispenser mounted on a pedestal near where the ship had materialized, which was a parking space—Dara recognized the signage logic instantly, the universal language of "you may exist in this area temporarily."

Their number was 4,311,702.

The display board on the far wall, visible despite being what appeared to be several kilometers away (distances were suggestive here, not definitive), read:

**NOW SERVING: 4,311,558**

"Hundred and forty-four ahead of us," Dara said.

"That's not—that could be—" Olshansky started.

"It'll move."

"How do you know?"

"Because there's a number system. If they weren't going to serve anyone, they wouldn't need numbers. Numbers imply sequence. Sequence implies progress."

There was also a water cooler.

It stood nearby, the kind of object so mundane that its presence in a room outside spacetime felt like either a profound commentary on the universality of waiting or a clerical error. It dispensed a liquid that was not water. The liquid was clear and had no smell and when Dr. Anya Roth, the ship's medical officer, analyzed a sample she said, "It's not toxic. It's not nutritive. I genuinely don't know what it is."

"It's waiting-room water," Dara said. "It's not supposed to hydrate you. It's supposed to give you something to do with your hands."

They sat in the chairs that most closely approximated human seating, which had clearly been designed for a species with a vaguely similar body plan but slightly longer torsos and, Dara suspected, tails.  She sat with her back straight and her stylus out, reading the codex on a tablet propped against her knee.

They waited.

---

Time, in the waiting room, was a guideline.

Days passed, or what felt like days, though the light never changed and no one's watch agreed with any other watch. The ship remained where it had materialized, looking embarrassed, the way a car looks in a hospital parking lot—functional but irrelevant. The *Provisional Remainder*'s three thousand occupants established a rotation: groups of fifty would disembark to sit in the waiting room in shifts, maintaining their place. Olshansky insisted on this. "We are not losing our spot," he said, as though the Auditors operated on a deli-counter honor system. He might have been right.

The number advanced. It advanced with the speed of continental drift. 4,311,558 became 4,311,559 with no discernible transition, like watching a sunset—you never saw the moment it changed, only realized afterward that it had.

Yotam loved the waiting room.

He loved it because it was *big*, bigger than anything on the ship, bigger than the cargo bay where Priya sometimes let the children run. He loved the weird chairs. He tried to sit in all of them. The gas-chair made a sound when he sat in it, a kind of soft harmonic hum, and he sat in it fourteen times on the first day alone. He loved the water cooler. The not-water tasted, he reported, "like if you licked a cloud. A clean cloud." He loved the other waiting species.

There were not many, but there were some—beings occupying distant clusters of chairs, visible as shapes and colors and, in one case, a sound that persisted in an area without any visible source. Yotam waved at all of them. Occasionally, something waved back.

And he drew.

He drew constantly. Priya had found a cache of grease pencils in the ship's maintenance closet—not crayons, but they made marks, and for Yotam making marks was as essential as breathing, possibly more so, since he sometimes forgot to breathe when concentrating but never forgot to draw.

He drew the waiting room. He drew the chairs. He drew the water cooler ("the cloud juice box"). He drew an Auditor functionary—the first any of them had seen up close—that had drifted through the waiting area on what appeared to be routine rounds.

The Auditor resembled, to adult eyes, a translucent geometrical distortion roughly two meters tall, something like a prism made of fog, carrying what might have been a clipboard or might have been part of its body. To Yotam's eyes, it was "a big cloud with a stamp."

His drawing showed exactly this: a rounded, puffy form with two dots for eyes and a flat rectangle in its middle section. He walked up to the functionary—Priya lunged after him, too slow—and held up the picture.

"This is you," Yotam said.

The functionary stopped. It regarded the drawing. It regarded Yotam. It regarded the drawing again. The process took four minutes, during which Priya stood frozen three meters away, vibrating at a frequency that only parents and caregivers recognize as the specific terror of watching a child offer a sandwich to a bear.

The functionary took the drawing. It held it—or incorporated it, or filed it; the motion was ambiguous—and moved on.
