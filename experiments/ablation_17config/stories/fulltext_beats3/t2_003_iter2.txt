# Perdition Gulch

## I.

The child woke and the world was only the world.

She lay still for a long time in the narrow cot beneath the window where the morning was doing what morning does in the high desert — arriving all at once, without the graduated courtesies of dawn in kinder country. The light came through the flour-sack curtain and fell across her chest and she listened and heard nothing. Not nothing the way silence is nothing, which is always, if you attend to it carefully enough, full of something — the tick of cooling stovepipe, the settling of a house into its own joists, the respiration of the town outside cycling through its sleepers like a bellows working an organ no one has tuned. She heard all of that. But beneath it, where for every morning of her life there had been the murmuring river of Perdition Gulch thinking itself awake — the ordinary private litany of a hundred and forty-three souls surfacing through their first arguments and appetites and grudges and the wordless hydraulic surge of bodies remembering they were alive — there was nothing.

She was eleven years old. She did not panic, because she had never been taught the particular fear that attaches to the loss of a sense. She had, in fact, never been taught much of anything. What she knew she had stolen from minds that did not know they were being read, and what she had learned from that immense and unasked-for education was that most people did not think in ways that were useful to a child. But the silence was new, and new things, in her experience, were almost always someone else's thought arriving before the event it described, which meant she had a habit of experiencing novelty as confirmation. This was the first new thing that had no precedent in another person's mind. It was hers alone. She did not know what to do with it.

She dressed. She braided her hair with fingers that worked by the practice of fingers and not because she had once, years ago, borrowed the muscle-memory of a woman in the dry goods store who braided her own hair so beautifully that the child had simply kept the knowledge, tucking it behind her own thoughts like a letter slipped into someone else's book. She walked outside.

Perdition Gulch had a single street, which was both its principal shame and its principal advantage. It meant that everyone within it could be observed from any point along its length, as though the town had been designed not for habitation but for surveillance — or, if you preferred the gentler reading, for *community*, which in a town of this size was so often the same thing. The street ran north-south along the spine of a dry wash that still, in certain seasons, remembered it had once been a creek. On the east side: the saloon, the assayer's office, the land office with its federal seal that no one had bothered to update since the territory had become a state two years prior, and the dry goods store with its false front that the proprietor, Mr. Kass, insisted made the building look substantial from a distance. On the west side: the livery, the boarding house, the lot where a church had been framed but never finished, and the house where the memory-keeper lived behind curtains that were always drawn.

The child walked the length of this street and read lips.

She had always been able to do this — it was a skill she had developed not out of necessity but out of a kind of redundant craftsmanship, the way a carpenter who owns a lathe might still occasionally turn a spindle by hand for the pleasure of the older discipline. She had used it before as a cross-reference, checking what the mouth said against what the mind meant, and the gap between those two texts had constituted the bulk of her education in human character. Now she used it because it was all she had. Mr. Kass, sweeping his porch, moved his lips around a complaint about the heat, and the child heard the words a half-second after she saw them form, which was how everyone else experienced conversation, and it struck her as grotesquely slow, this dependence on the physical, this waiting for the air to carry meaning when meaning had always simply *been there*, prior to its articulation, like heat before the match.

No one noticed. This was the part that would have broken her had she been old enough to understand what it meant: no one in Perdition Gulch had ever noticed how she listened. They did not know their thoughts had been heard. They did not know their disputes had been settled, their crimes predicted, their intentions catalogued and weighed by a child who sat on the porch of the boarding house with her feet not reaching the ground and her face arranged in the blankness that adults attributed to the vacancy of youth. She had been, for her entire sentient life, the most important civic institution in the town, and the town had mistaken her for furniture.

She found the memory-keeper in the house behind the drawn curtains.

The memory-keeper — whose name was Constance, though no one used it, not even herself, because a name is a thing that changes with context, and her gift had always been to hold things fixed — was sitting at the desk where she wrote in her journals every morning. The journals filled three shelves of a cabinet that she had commissioned from a furniture-maker in Silver City, and they contained, in a handwriting of almost pathological regularity, everything that had ever happened in Perdition Gulch, to anyone, in any order, recoverable upon demand. She could recall the color of the sky on any given Tuesday. She could reproduce, word for word, a conversation she had overheard nine years ago between two men she had never met again. She remembered the interval between lightning and thunder on the night the boarding house roof caught fire, and she remembered the smell of the char, and she remembered how many nails had been required to rebuild the southwest corner, because she had watched the carpenter count them out and her mind had kept the number the way other minds keep scars — automatically and forever.

The child stood in the doorway and said, "Something's wrong with the quiet."

Constance looked at her. She catalogued the child's face — she could not help this, it was reflex, the gift operating beneath volition the way a heart operates beneath intention — and in the act of cataloguing she reached, by habit, for yesterday's entry. What was the child wearing yesterday? Constance had seen her in the street at midday, had passed within six feet of her, had certainly registered every detail with the comprehensive fidelity that was not so much her talent as her condition.

She could not find it.

She searched. Not the journals — she did not need the journals, the journals were transcription, redundancy, the external record maintained out of a discipline she had imposed on herself because she understood, in a way the others did not, that a gift without practice is a gift preparing to leave. She searched her own mind, the vast and faultlessly indexed architecture of it, and for the first time in her life found a drawer that would not open. Not empty — she could feel the weight of the memory behind the latch — but inaccessible, as though the mechanism had rusted overnight.

She said nothing about it.

She looked at the child and the child looked at her, and between them passed a communication that required neither telepathy nor perfect recall but only the shared grammar of fear, which is the oldest language and the only one that does not improve with fluency.

"Sit down," Constance said. "I'll make coffee."

She did not make coffee. She sat with her hands on the desk and the child sat in the chair by the window and they occupied the silence together, each understanding that something had broken and neither yet willing to say the word that would make the breaking real.

Outside, Perdition Gulch continued. It did not know it had lost anything. It had never known what it had.

Seven years before this morning — before the child understood what her gift was, before Constance had begun her journals, before the council existed or the word *council* had attached to any of them — five people stood on a ridge above the Atchison creek road and watched the dark gather.

They were not yet acquainted. This is important. They were not friends, not allies, not a body constituted for any purpose. They were five residents of a town of moderate size who happened to be, by various routes and various reasons, on the same ridge at the same hour, looking down at the same scene: a party of fourteen settlers — three wagons, nine adults, five children — camped along the creek bed where the road widened enough to allow a fire without blocking passage. The settlers had come from Missouri. They were bound for California. They had stopped because one of their oxen had thrown a shoe and because a child among them was ill, not dangerously but persistently, with the kind of low fever that required rest and time and cool water, all of which the Atchison creek road provided.

The healer knew they were going to die.

He had found the body two hours earlier — an outrider, one of the settlers' forward scouts, shot twice in the back at a distance that suggested the shooter had not been in a hurry. The outrider was not yet dead when the healer came upon him. He was dying in the particular way that men die of abdominal wounds in open country, which is slowly and with a terrible clarity, the pain burning off every pretension and leaving behind a mind of almost surgical precision. The outrider told the healer what he had seen: six riders, coming from the south, following the road. The Garrity brothers. Everyone in the territory knew the name. Everyone had heard the stories, which were not stories but reports, verified and plain. They did not rob. They did not negotiate. They killed, and they moved on, and the territory absorbed their passage the way a body absorbs a poison — in small enough doses, over long enough time, that the dying felt like the normal condition of living.

The healer had closed the outrider's wounds — had laid his hands over the ragged exits and felt the flesh knit beneath his palms with the warm, uncanny obedience that he had never been able to explain and had long since stopped trying to — but the man was past mending. Too much blood already in the dirt. The healer stayed with him until it was over, then climbed the ridge because from the ridge he could see the road in both directions, and he wanted to see, with his own eyes, whether the outrider had been right.

He was right. Six riders, south. Moving at the pace of men who had nowhere to be except where the killing was.

The twins were already there.

Alma and Petra, twenty-three years old, born eight minutes apart in a room above the saloon that had since been converted to storage, stood at opposite ends of the ridge — Alma at the northern spur, Petra at the southern — and between them passed a single field of perception, unbroken, the way a stereo pair of lenses produces depth from two flat images. They could see the settlers' camp in the creek bed. They could see the Garrity riders coming through the mesquite breaks two miles south. They could see, with a lateral range that no single pair of eyes could achieve, the full geometry of what was about to happen: the angles of approach, the distance to cover, the estimated time until convergence. They had come to the ridge for the same reason the healer had — because something was wrong in the valley, and their shared body of sight had, for days, been registering the small seismic disturbances that precede violence: unfamiliar horses, smoke from an unexpected direction, the pattern of birds breaking from the treeline in a sequence that, read correctly, described the passage of men who did not want to be seen.

Constance was there because she had been here before. Once, four years earlier, she had ridden this ridge with a survey team, and her memory of the terrain was so precise that she could, even now, plot a path from the settlers' position to the northern pass that would be invisible from the southern approach — a dry wash that bent behind a basalt uplift, wide enough for the wagons if they unhitched the teams, passable in forty minutes if someone led them who knew where the wash narrowed and where it opened and where the footing was sand and where it was stone. She had not come for this purpose. She had come because she had heard the same rumors as everyone else and because her gift made her constitutionally unable to leave a situation unrecorded. She was there to witness, which she had always believed was a form of duty.

The child was there because she was always there. She was four years old. She followed the healer sometimes, and the memory-keeper sometimes, and the twins sometimes, because their minds were different from other minds — louder, stranger, fuller — and she was drawn to them the way certain insects are drawn to light, not understanding the attraction, only obeying it. She sat on a rock near Alma's position, her legs too short to hang over the edge, and she listened to the Garrity brothers' minds the way someone might listen to a menu being read aloud in a restaurant — with a detached assessment of each item, a browsing among intentions. She heard what they planned to do. She heard it in detail. She did not have the developmental context to fully understand what she heard, but she understood enough: these men were going to hurt the people by the creek, and they were looking forward to it, and the anticipation had a flavor she had never encountered before in anyone's thoughts — not hot, like anger, but *flat*, like hunger, like the dull mechanical need of a body that has learned to feed on a thing it should not need.

She said nothing. But her face, in the failing light, was a text that anyone on that ridge could have read.

Five people. Fourteen settlers and five children below them. Six riders approaching. The light going. And everything — everything — available to them.

The healer could have descended to the camp. He could have warned them. He could have, if warning failed, laid hands on the wounded once the shooting started, pulled them back from the threshold, given the survivors a chance to flee while the Garrity brothers paused to reload.

The twins could have directed the evacuation. Alma at the north end, Petra at the south, their shared sight encompassing every escape route, every line of fire, every second of advantage — they could have moved those settlers through the dark the way a conductor moves an orchestra through a score, each part arriving precisely where it was needed, the whole assembly navigating by a perception that no single person in the party would have understood but all of them would have obeyed, because in crisis people obey confidence, and the twins' confidence, rooted in their bifocal omniscience, was absolute.

Constance could have drawn the map. She carried, in the architecture of her recall, a route to safety so precise that she could have described it in the dark by feel — every turn, every gradient, every place where a wagon wheel would catch on stone and every place where the sand was packed hard enough to bear it. She could have walked it blindfolded. She could have told them exactly how much time they had.

And the child — the small, quiet, terrifying child — could have told them what was coming. Not in the vague terms of rumor or the calculated terms of probability but in the specific, inarguable, nauseating terms of six men's actual thoughts, which she carried in her skull like a court transcript of a crime that had not yet been committed.

They did not act.

There was no moment of decision. The narrator wishes to be precise about this. There was no scene in which one of them proposed intervention and another argued against it. There was no weighing of risks, no articulation of the calculus by which five gifted people might determine that their secrecy was worth more than fourteen lives. None of them said we cannot. None of them said we should not. None of them spoke at all.

What happened was slower than that, and worse.

The healer looked at the twins. The twins looked at each other — or rather, Alma looked at the space where Petra was and Petra looked at the space where Alma was, and between those two gazes was an exchange so compressed that it had no duration a human clock could measure. Constance watched all of them and committed every detail to the record she was already composing in her mind, which she understood, even then, was a way of transforming responsibility into archive. The child sat on her rock and listened to the Garrity brothers getting closer and said nothing, because she was four years old and no one had ever told her that the things she heard in other people's minds were things she was allowed to say aloud, and so she had learned — the way children learn everything, by observation and inference — that her gift was a secret, and secrets were kept.
