# The Hateful Immortals

---

## I.

The marriage certificate is Episcopal, printed on stock that has aged to the color of weak tea, and it currently resides in a climate-controlled drawer at the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History, where it is catalogued not under "Religious Documents" or "Vital Records" but under "Anomalous Persons: Primary Source Materials," a designation that Dr. Eleanor Thornton, the Harwick Professor of Anomalous Historical Studies at Columbia University, has publicly called "a bureaucratic euphemism that tells you everything about how this country processes the existence of people it cannot explain."

The certificate reads:

*This is to certify that Mr. James Aloysious Greenbriar and Miss Margaret Yun Chen were united in Holy Matrimony according to the rites and ceremonies of the Protestant Episcopal Church in the United States of America, on the fourteenth day of June, in the year of our Lord eighteen hundred and forty-seven, in the City of Boston, Commonwealth of Massachusetts.*

Below the officiant's signature and the signatures of two witnesses — a Mr. Harold Potts and a Mrs. Constance Delacroix, about whom almost nothing is known — the certificate bears the standard liturgical language of the Book of Common Prayer, reproduced in small type along the bottom margin, including the phrase that Dr. Thornton, in the opening narration of her 2024 documentary *Till Death: The Greenbriar-Chen Marriage and the Limits of Human Contract*, has called "the most consequential piece of boilerplate language in recorded history":

*...to have and to hold, from this day forward, for better, for worse, for richer, for poorer, in sickness and in health, to love and to cherish, till death do us part...*

The joke, Thornton observes — and she uses the word *joke* deliberately, in that dry, faintly mordant register that has made her public lectures unexpectedly popular on YouTube — operates on three levels. Legally, the clause is unenforceable: death is, for James Greenbriar, an event that has been conclusively and repeatedly demonstrated not to occur, meaning the contract contains an exit provision that references a physical impossibility, which would, in any other context, render the agreement void. Theologically, the vow invokes divine authority over a boundary — the boundary between life and death — that at least one of the parties has been empirically shown to exist beyond, which raises questions about ecclesiastical jurisdiction that no synod has been willing to address. And domestically — here Thornton pauses, in the documentary, and adjusts her glasses, and the pause is a beat of comic timing so precise that it is difficult to believe it was not rehearsed — domestically, the couple has had one hundred and seventy-seven years to discover exactly what they promised.

James Greenbriar was twenty-three years old when he signed the certificate. Margaret Yun Chen was twenty-one. They had been courting for approximately fourteen months, a period that, by the standards of 1840s Boston, was neither scandalously brief nor tediously protracted. James was the son of a Cantonese merchant and an Irish seamstress, employed as a clerk at a shipping firm on Long Wharf, notable primarily for his height — six feet two inches, unusual for the period — and for the fact that, two years prior to his marriage, he had fallen from a warehouse roof onto a cobblestone loading dock, a distance of approximately thirty-five feet, and had stood up. He had, in the words of one contemporaneous newspaper account unearthed by Thornton's research team, "risen from the ground as though he had merely tripped over a curbstone, brushed the dust from his trousers, and inquired as to whether any damage had been done to the cargo below."

This was not the incident that confirmed his unkillability. That came later — 1849, two years *after* the marriage — when a mechanical loom at the Appleton Cotton Mill malfunctioned and a steel shuttle rod pierced James's chest cavity, passing through what the attending physician's report described, with clinical bewilderment, as "the approximate region of the heart." James walked to the infirmary. The rod was removed. The wound closed within the hour. Dr. Thornton reproduces the physician's final case note in full: *I have no diagnosis. I have no prognosis. I am recording that this occurred, and I am going home.*

Margaret's manifestation had preceded James's by several years. Her parents — her father, Dr. Chen Wei-Lin, a physician trained in both traditional Chinese medicine and at Harvard Medical School, one of the first Chinese men to attend; her mother, Anna Hale Chen, a Boston Brahmin whose family had disowned her upon the marriage — first noticed the anomaly when Margaret was twelve. She had recited, verbatim, the entirety of a sermon she had heard a single time, three years prior, at a church the family had visited while traveling. Not merely the words. The cadence. The minister's breathing pattern. The sound of rain on the vestibule roof. The smell of the hymnals. Upon gentle questioning — Dr. Thornton notes that both of Margaret's parents were, by all accounts, patient, empirically minded people who treated their daughter's difference with curiosity rather than alarm — Margaret revealed that she could reproduce, with equivalent fidelity, every experience she had ever had since approximately her fourth birthday. Every conversation. Every meal. Every dream she could recall having had at the time of dreaming. The archive was total, sensory, and continuous, and it did not dim.

They married, then. Young. In love. In a Boston that treated its emerging population of "special persons" — the term used in the broadsheets; the clinical nomenclature would not be standardized until the 1920s — with cautious fascination, which is to say, with the politeness of a society that prides itself on tolerance and the watchfulness of a society that does not entirely trust what it is tolerating.

The earliest known photograph of the couple dates from the wedding day itself. It is a daguerreotype, produced by a studio on Tremont Street whose name has been lost, and it shows exactly what daguerreotypes of the period always show: two people sitting very still, their expressions carefully neutralized against the long exposure time, their postures rigid, their clothing elaborate and uncomfortable. Margaret's right hand rests on James's left forearm. His jaw is set. Her eyes are focused on a point slightly to the left of the camera. They look, Thornton observes, like every other young married couple in 1847, which is to say, they look like people performing the fact of their own union for an apparatus that requires them to hold perfectly still.

In a 2019 interview — one of the rare long-form interviews Margaret has granted, conducted by a journalist at the *Atlantic* whose questions, Thornton notes with academic disapproval, were "well-intentioned but frequently insufficient" — Margaret was asked about the photograph. She said:

"The lace at my wrist was Honiton, borrowed from my mother. It was starched to the point of cruelty — I could feel the individual fibers pressing into the skin below my pulse. The studio smelled of bromine and iodine and something underneath that I later learned was mercury fumes, which I suppose should have worried us, though it did not. The photographer told us not to move. We were not moving. James leaned toward me — not visibly, you would not see it in the image, but I could feel the shift in pressure on my forearm — and he whispered, 'If I must hold this face much longer I will begin to look embalmed,' and I nearly ruined the exposure."

The journalist asked a follow-up question that does not appear in the published transcript.

Margaret continued:

"The first cruel thing he said to me was on March the seventh, 1852, at approximately ten minutes past eight in the evening, in the parlor of our house on Myrtle Street. We were arguing about household accounts. He said that I was 'relentless' and that my memory was 'not a gift but a weapon' and that I 'wielded it like a person who has never been cut by her own blade.' He was standing by the fireplace. The fire had been banked but not extinguished. He was wearing a brown waistcoat with ivory buttons, the second button missing since the previous November. His voice was not raised. That was the worst part. He said it as though he were reporting the weather."

Thornton notes, in her companion essay to the documentary, that Margaret has offered these two memories — the daguerreotype studio, the Myrtle Street parlor — in every interview she has granted across three centuries. She presents them unprompted. She presents them in sequence. She presents them as a matched set.

The academic observation Thornton appends to this fact is restrained, almost parenthetical, and it is the sentence that made the documentary's opening segment go briefly viral among scholars of anomalous studies before it was even released: "Mrs. Chen does not sort her memories by valence. She is not telling you two stories. She is telling you one."

---

What Thornton means — and what the remainder of the documentary methodically, sometimes brutally, demonstrates — is that Margaret Yun Chen's memory does not perform the triage that every other human brain performs. It does not flag the joyful and file the painful in a separate, less accessible drawer. It does not soften. It does not erode. It does not allow the passage of time to do the thing that the passage of time does for every other person who has ever lived, which is to blunt the edge, to lower the resolution, to replace the raw footage with a summary, and to replace the summary, eventually, with a feeling, and to replace the feeling, eventually, with nothing at all.

For Margaret, 1847 is now. The pressure of the Honiton lace on her wrist is now. The smell of mercury and bromine is now. James's whispered joke is now — not recalled, not reconstructed, but *accessed*, with full sensory resolution, as though a window into that afternoon had been left permanently open in some alcove of her consciousness and she could turn toward it at any moment and find the light still falling through it at the same angle, the dust still suspended in the same beam.

And 1852 is also now. The banked fire is now. The missing button is now. The word *relentless* in James's uninflected voice is now.

Both. Always. At the same fidelity. At the same volume.

This is the first thing the documentary wants you to understand, and Thornton, who is a careful enough scholar to know that argument by juxtaposition is the most potent form of argument, lets the juxtaposition do the work. She does not editorialize. She does not tell you that the marriage of James Greenbriar and Margaret Yun Chen is the longest, the strangest, and — depending on which historian you consult — the most important or the most appalling union in recorded history. She places the marriage certificate under its glass. She places the daguerreotype beside it. She lets Margaret's voice play once, describing the lace, the chemicals, the whispered joke, and then, without pause or transition, the parlor, the fire, the waistcoat, the cutting calm of a young husband's voice.

And then Thornton says, in the documentary's only direct address to camera in the entire opening segment:

"The vow said *till death do us part*. It did not specify whose death. It did not contemplate the possibility that death might not come at all. And it did not — could not — anticipate that one of the parties to the contract would be incapable of the single act of mercy that makes every other marriage in human history survivable. Not forgiveness. Forgetting. Margaret Chen cannot forget. James Greenbriar cannot die. And the certificate under this glass is the lock on a door that will never open, signed by two young people who loved each other very much and had absolutely no idea what they were promising."

The screen cuts to black.

The title card reads: *Part One: 1847–1890.*

## II.

Thornton calls it the Pattern.

In her published scholarship she uses more precise language — "the Greenbriar-Chen Relocation Cycle" — but in the documentary she permits herself the colloquialism, perhaps because the documentary, unlike the scholarship, is attempting to make you *feel* something, and what it is attempting to make you feel is the weight of repetition. The Pattern is this: the couple arrives in a new city. They establish a life. They embed themselves in a community — neighbors, friends, sometimes employees or business partners. They are, by most surviving accounts, a couple that people enjoy knowing: James is described, across a dozen independent sources spanning three centuries, as gregarious, warm, an excellent cook, a man who "fills a room without crowding it"; Margaret is described as sharp-tongued, funny, intensely private, a person who "remembers your birthday and your sins with equal enthusiasm." They build what looks, from the outside, like a normal life. Fifteen years pass. Twenty. Thirty. And then someone notices. Someone always notices. The neighbor's daughter who has seen the wedding photograph. The local journalist who begins comparing records. The colleague who remarks, too casually, that Margaret looks exactly the same as she did when they met in 1943. And then the Pattern completes itself: leases broken, friendships severed without explanation or with explanations so thin they constitute their own kind of confession, possessions abandoned or shipped to a storage facility in Delaware that Thornton's team discovered holds approximately thirteen thousand cubic feet of the Greenbriar-Chen household goods, accumulated across nearly two centuries, catalogued by Margaret from memory.

Thornton traces the Pattern across its major iterations, and this is the documentary's long middle movement, its most structurally ambitious passage, because it must compress approximately a hundred and thirty years of peripatetic domestic life into something that communicates both the scope and the suffocating sameness.

Paris. 1891 to 1923.

They are, for this period, James and Marguerite Beaumont — one of approximately two dozen known aliases, most constructed by James with new papers obtained through methods Thornton characterizes as "almost certainly illegal and admirably thorough." The apartment is on the Rue Lepic, in Montmartre, fourth floor, with a narrow balcony overlooking a courtyard where a chestnut tree grew and, according to Margaret, "never once produced an edible chestnut, which James regarded as a personal insult." James cooks. He has, by this point, a repertoire that Thornton's team estimates at several hundred dishes, anchored by the Cantonese recipes his father taught him as a boy — char siu, lo mai gai, a clay pot rice that Margaret, in a 2011 interview, described as "the single most reliable good thing in my life, which is a damning thing to say about a dish and a more damning thing to say about a marriage, and I mean both compliments sincerely." Margaret sings. Not professionally, not publicly, but for friends — the small, rotating cast of neighbors and acquaintances that the couple accumulates in every city — in the apartment on evenings when the balcony doors are open and the chestnut tree is doing its annual impression of agricultural futility. She has, a surviving neighbor's diary records, "a voice that makes you feel she has known you a long time." She has known everyone a long time. She has known everyone she has ever met for exactly as long as she has been alive, because none of them have ever left her memory, and she can return to the first moment of every acquaintance with the same ease with which she returns to the daguerreotype studio, the parlor on Myrtle Street, the smell of her mother's perfume on a specific Tuesday in 1840 when she was seven years old and the light through the kitchen window was the color of pear skin.

They leave Paris because a neighbor's daughter — Thérèse Moulin, age nineteen, an art student, fond of Margaret, a frequent dinner guest — finds, in a secondhand shop on the Rue des Abbesses, a photograph. It is one of the couple's earlier portraits, taken in Boston in the 1870s, sold or donated at some point during a previous relocation. Thérèse, who is young and therefore direct, brings it to dinner and says, to the table at large, that Madame Beaumont looks "exactly like her own wedding photograph, which I have *seen*, which is here in my hand, and which was taken fifty years ago, and I would like very much for someone to explain this to me."

They are gone within the month. Thornton reproduces the final entry in Thérèse Moulin's diary that mentions the couple: *The Beaumonts have left without saying goodbye. I cannot decide if I am offended or frightened. Marguerite took the good copper pot. She left everything else. I do not understand people who can leave everything else.*
