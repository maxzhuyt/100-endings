# The HOA Board Will Now Come to Order

## I.

The banana bread was still warm. Doug Whitmore had carried it from his kitchen in a glass dish with a tea towel over the top, the way his mother had carried banana bread to church potlucks for thirty years, and he set it on the folding table beside the coffee urn and the sign-in sheet and said, "Help yourselves, there's plenty," before he even took his seat at the front of the community room, which was the multipurpose room of the Cedarbrook Estates clubhouse, which was also where the water aerobics people stored their pool noodles in a bin marked AQUA FIT — DO NOT REMOVE.

"All right, let's get ourselves going," Doug said. He adjusted his reading glasses. He wore a polo shirt the color of good health. "First off, I want to recognize Barb Lipinski for what she's done with those hydrangeas along the north entrance. Barb, you out there? Those are just — they're spectacular. They really are. Round of applause for Barb."

There were eleven people in the room. Nine of them applauded. Barb Lipinski, who was seventy-four and had nursed those hydrangeas through two droughts and a case of powdery mildew that she'd treated with a baking soda solution she'd found on a University of Maryland extension website, put her hand over her heart and mouthed *thank you*.

"Beautiful. Just beautiful," Doug said. "Okay. Moving on. I've got a few items from the Architectural Review Committee, and then we'll open things up."

He lifted a sheet of paper and read from it with the serene cadence of a man delivering the Sunday scripture readings at Grace Lutheran, which he also did, second and fourth Sundays, and had done without missing a rotation since 2011.

"Item one. 4412 Briarfield Court. The Muñoz residence. Wind chime installation, front porch, not submitted for prior approval per Section 7.3.2. Fine of twenty-five dollars. And I'll just note for the record, Tony, it's a lovely chime, I could hear it from the sidewalk, but we do need the form."

Tony Muñoz was not present. Doug moved on with the warmth of a man who assumed Tony understood.

"Item two. 4508 Briarfield. The Okonkwo residence. Flagstone path, side yard, extending from patio to property line. Not in the approved materials palette. Fine of twenty-five dollars, plus remediation within sixty days."

"Item three. 4327 Winding Creek Lane. The Petersen residence. Basketball hoop. Portable style. Measures one hundred and twenty-two inches from base to rim. Maximum allowable height per Section 9.1.4 is one hundred and twenty inches. Fine of twenty-five dollars. And Jim, I know it's just two inches, but — " Doug smiled the way a pediatrician smiles before the needle. "Rules are rules, right? We start making exceptions, where does it stop?"

Jim Petersen, who was present and whose son was twelve and had just made the travel team, opened his mouth and then closed it. He had three years left on his mortgage. He took a piece of banana bread.

"Item four." Doug turned a page. "4415 Cedarbrook Drive. The Chen residence."

He read it exactly as he had read the others, with exactly the same tonal frequency and exactly the same half-smile, as if the words were interchangeable with wind chimes and flagstone and basketball hoops, as if the content were merely administrative and the delivery merely pleasant, which, in Doug's experience of himself, it was.

"Construction of a wheelchair ramp, front entrance, without prior architectural review or approval. Non-conforming materials — pressure-treated pine, not on the approved exterior materials list. Non-approved gradient — measured at one-to-ten, where ADA recommends one-to-twelve but Section 6.2.1 requires one-to-fourteen for aesthetic consistency with existing walkway grades. Fine of fifty dollars per day until the structure is brought into compliance or removed."

Doug looked up. His reading glasses caught the fluorescent light.

"And I know this is a sensitive situation," he said. "We all know Richard, we all care about the Chens. But the board can't start making exceptions based on — well, based on circumstances, however sympathetic, because then we don't have a covenant, we just have opinions. And what I always say is — "

"Rules are rules," said the nine people who had applauded for the hydrangeas, or six of them, or three. It didn't matter. It was the room's consensus, spoken or ambient, the way a church agrees to a hymn.

Doug set down the sheet. "That's the ARC report. Now, Barb, you wanted to talk about the pool hours?"

The meeting continued for another forty minutes. Someone ate the last of the banana bread. The coffee urn made its exhausted gurgling sound. Eleven people signed out on the sign-in sheet and went home to Cedarbrook Estates, which had been voted one of the Triangle's Best Planned Communities by *Triangle Living* magazine in 2019, and which looked, in the particular amber light of that April evening, like a place where nothing had ever gone wrong and nothing ever would.

---

The violation letter arrived in Meredith Chen's mailbox the next morning, a Tuesday, in an envelope with the Cedarbrook Estates logo — a stylized cedar tree that had been designed by a homeowner who once took a graphic design course at the community college and who had, everyone agreed, done a very nice job.

She read it standing in the kitchen. She read it again sitting down. Richard was in the living room in the hospital bed that hospice had delivered three weeks ago, the bed she had to pass every time she went from the kitchen to the front door, and from the kitchen she could hear the oxygen concentrator's rhythmic hum, which sounded like a machine that was trying very hard to breathe on someone's behalf and was not entirely confident in the project.

She set the letter on the kitchen table beside the amber bottles — oxycodone, lorazepam, ondansetron, methylphenidate for the fatigue that no amount of methylphenidate could touch — and the hospice brochure, which was called "The Journey Ahead" and featured a photograph of a sunset that she found personally offensive. A sunset. As if her husband of thirty-one years were a Hallmark card. As if pancreatic cancer were an aesthetic experience.

Meredith was sixty-three. She had retired from Aon two years ago after a career spent pricing catastrophe. She had modeled earthquake risk in the New Madrid Seismic Zone. She had built mortality tables for long-term care insurers. She understood, in a way that most people could not, how large systems processed individual human suffering into statistical noise — how a death became a data point, how a wheelchair ramp became a line item, how fifty dollars a day became the specific, annualized cost of a man being able to leave his own house.

She opened her laptop. She opened a new spreadsheet. In cell A1 she typed: *Daily fine.* In cell B1: *$50.* In cell A2: *Estimated remaining days.* In cell B2 she paused. She looked toward the living room, where the oxygen concentrator hummed. She typed a number. She let the formula calculate.

Then she closed the spreadsheet and opened a different file: the Cedarbrook Estates Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions, 127 pages, last amended 2016. She began reading the way she had once read actuarial tables — not for narrative, not for meaning, but for structure. For load-bearing assumptions. For the weight a system could hold and the points where it would crack.

She read for six hours. She took notes in a second spreadsheet. At 11 p.m. she helped the night nurse turn Richard and check his IV line. At midnight she went back to the bylaws. At 2 a.m. she found Article XII, Section 4, the amendment provision, buried between a paragraph about pool furniture and a paragraph about holiday lighting displays.

At 2:17 a.m. she picked up her phone and called Janine Kowalczyk.

---

Janine answered on the first ring because Janine always answered on the first ring and because Janine had not slept well since 2018, when she had been let go from the Wake County Superior Court reporter pool for what her supervisor called "editorial interpolations in the record." What Janine had done was type *(witness is clearly lying)* during a deposition, which she maintained was strictly accurate and which the judge maintained was grounds for termination. She now freelanced, transcribing podcasts and city council meetings and, occasionally, in her darker hours, the conversations of strangers at Panera, which she recorded on her phone and transcribed at home for practice and which she stored in a folder on her desktop labeled FIELD NOTES.

"I need a stenographer," Meredith said.

"It's two in the morning."

"I know what time it is."

"For what?"

Meredith told her.

"I'll bring my machine," Janine said.

---

Leonard Pratch arrived the next evening at 6 p.m. with a rolling briefcase and a copy of *Robert's Rules of Order Newly Revised, Twelfth Edition*, which he had tabbed with forty-six color-coded sticky notes and which he had read, by his own careful estimate, more times than any other single human being on the Eastern Seaboard, a claim no one had ever thought to challenge because no one had ever met anyone else who would make it.

Leonard was fifty-eight. He had managed a Kinko's. He had coached JV debate at Broughton High School. He had served on the boards of eleven nonprofit organizations, not for the mission statements but for the meetings, the way some men do not fish for the fish but for the ritual of casting. Parliamentary procedure was not Leonard's hobby. It was not even his passion. It was his art form, and he had spent thirty years mastering an art form for which the world had provided no serious venue, no concert hall, no gallery — only community rooms with folding chairs and coffee urns, where his expertise was either ignored or openly mocked, where people said "Let's just vote" when he tried to explain the difference between a motion to table and a motion to postpone definitely, which were *not the same thing* and the conflation of which was, in Leonard's view, a symptom of civic decay so advanced that it bordered on the moral.

He set his briefcase on Meredith's dining table and looked through the sliding glass door at the wheelchair ramp. It was six feet long. It was made of pressure-treated pine. Richard had built half of it himself, before the diagnosis made that impossible, and a neighbor named Clay had finished it, using YouTube videos and a circular saw he borrowed from another neighbor, and it was not elegant and it was not to code and it was the reason Richard Chen could sit on his own front porch in the mornings and watch the dogwoods bloom, which he had done every morning for the past three weeks, wrapped in a blanket because the disease had taken his ability to regulate heat, and which he would not do for many more mornings, which everyone in the room knew and no one said.

"Show me the bylaws," Leonard said.

Meredith showed him. She showed him the quorum provision — Article III, Section 2 — which required a majority of homeowners in good standing, and which she had cross-referenced against the sign-in sheets from the last four annual meetings, obtained through a records request Doug had been legally required to honor and had honored with a three-week delay and a note that said *Happy to help! 😊*.

"He's been short," Meredith said. "Every year. He counts proxies that were never properly submitted. He counts homeowners who are ninety days delinquent on dues. He counts himself twice — once as homeowner, once as board president."

Leonard's hand went to his copy of Robert's Rules. He did not open it. He touched it the way a priest touches a Bible when the truth is being spoken and the book merely confirms what the heart already knows.

"The quorum was never valid," he said.

"No."

"Which means every action taken at those meetings — "

"Is voidable."

Leonard looked at the wheelchair ramp again. He looked at the bylaws. He looked at Meredith, and in his eyes was an expression she recognized because she had seen it in her own mirror every morning for the past two weeks: the expression of a person who has found, after a long time in the wilderness, work that is adequate to their anger.

"What about amendments?" he said. "Can we force a special session?"

"Article XII, Section 4. Fifteen homeowners in good standing can petition for a special meeting with fourteen days' notice. But we don't need a special meeting. The annual is in six weeks."

"So we go to the annual."

"We go to the annual."

Leonard unzipped the rolling briefcase. Inside, in a clear plastic folder, were his own color-coded copies of eleven different organizations' bylaws, each annotated in a hand so precise it looked typeset. He removed them and set them aside. He removed a dry-erase marker. He looked at the sliding glass door and then at Meredith.

"Do you have a whiteboard?"

She did not have a whiteboard. She had a shower curtain liner, still in the packaging, bought for a bathroom renovation that had been abandoned when Richard got sick. Leonard taped it to the dining room wall. It buckled slightly. He uncapped the marker. He drew a vertical line down the center. On the left he wrote THEIR MOVES. On the right he wrote OUR MOVES. At the top, in block letters, he wrote: *CEDARBROOK ESTATES ANNUAL MEETING — PARLIAMENTARY SEQUENCE*.

"All right," he said. "Let's walk the order of business. Doug will open with the call to order. He'll ask for approval of last year's minutes. If we challenge the minutes — "

"We don't challenge the minutes," Meredith said. "Not yet. We let him get comfortable."

Leonard looked at her. He capped the marker. He sat down. He understood, in that moment, that he was not the strategist in this room. He was the technician. He was the safecracker's fingers. The heist itself belonged to the woman with the spreadsheet and the dying husband and the ramp outside the sliding glass door.

"Tell me the plan," he said.

---

The book club came next. There were five of them, not counting Meredith, and they had been meeting every third Thursday for nine years, and in those nine years they had read every John Grisham novel in publication order and had supplemented this with Scott Turow and Lisa Scottoline and, during a brief experimental phase in 2021, a Michael Connelly, which they had enjoyed but which Phyllis Dykstra had felt "lacked Grisham's moral seriousness," a judgment the group had accepted by consensus without a formal vote.

Their names were Gloria Munn, Phyllis Dykstra, Sandra Osei, Yvette Fontaine, and Helen Park. They were between the ages of fifty-seven and seventy-one. Between them they possessed a retired pharmacist, a former school board member, a woman who had once run a regional Mary Kay distributorship of 340 consultants, a CPA, and a grandmother of eight who had, in 1988, escaped Communist Romania by walking across a frozen river at night. They did not consider themselves an army. They considered themselves a book club. But they had read *The Firm* and *A Time to Kill* and *The Runaway Jury* and in particular *The Runaway Jury*, which was about jury selection and manipulation and which Gloria Munn had called "essentially a manual for community organizing if you think about it," and they had the one skill that every campaign requires and that no amount of money can buy: they knew every single person in Cedarbrook Estates by name, by kitchen, by ailment, by grievance, and by dessert preference, and they were not afraid to ring a doorbell.

"We need fifteen signatures to force a quorum count," Meredith said. "We need a majority of valid proxies to carry the no-confidence vote. That's at least fifty-two, assuming a hundred and three homeowners in good standing."

"I can get you fifty-two," Sandra said. She was the former Mary Kay regional. She said it the way a contractor says *I can get you the permits*.

"We don't want fifty-two. We want seventy. We want it to not be close."

Sandra looked at Gloria. Gloria looked at Helen. Helen — the one who had crossed the river — looked at the wheelchair ramp through the sliding glass door and said, "We'll get you seventy."
