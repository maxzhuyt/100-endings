# Night Shift Mercy

## I.

The dolly had a bad wheel. That was the first thing Carl noticed — the front left caster stuttering against the linoleum, laying down a wobbling scuff mark he'd have to get on his hands and knees to work out later with the edge of a putty knife and a rag of mineral spirits. The county donation driver was a big man, red in the face from the ramp, and he'd come in through the service entrance without a mat under the wheels, so there were two black tire lines on top of that, straight ones at least, running from the door to the nursing station like someone had drawn a path with a grease pencil.

The android stood upright on the dolly, strapped at the chest and thighs with orange ratchet ties, the kind you'd use for a refrigerator. Its eyes were open. Carl couldn't tell if it was on or off. Its face had that quality of certain medical equipment — the blankness that might be sleep, might be standby, might be nothing at all. Across the left side of its jaw, running up past the temple and into the hairline, the housing panel was discolored: a matte, oxidized patch, lighter than the rest of the skin-tone casing, as if someone had sanded the surface down and stopped halfway through repainting. Factory reset scarring. He'd seen it once before, on a home-care unit at the VA hospital in Decatur where his wife had spent her last four months. That one had the scarring on its hands.

Delia, the day-shift charge nurse, signed the clipboard without reading it. She had her left shoe off under the station desk — Carl could see it when he emptied the waste bin — and her lunch was already out, a Tupperware of something with rice, and she talked to the driver while looking at her phone, asking questions about the power supply and the charging protocol as if she were setting up a printer. The driver answered everything by pointing to the manual, a photocopied stack in a plastic sleeve, which he left on the counter next to the hand sanitizer. Then he took his dolly back, bad wheel and all, and Carl listened to it stutter down the hall and out the service entrance and then there was the sound of the van starting and pulling away and then there was just the building again.

The android stood where the driver had left it, next to the station, hands at its sides. Delia glanced at it, said something to Pauline about the charging port in the supply closet, and went back to her rice.

Carl mopped the tire marks. He mopped the scuff from the caster. He filled his bucket at the utility sink in the back hall and wrung the mop twice, firmly, the water running grey, and started on the east corridor beginning at the far end and working back toward the station the way he always did, the way he'd done every evening for three years, the mop moving in steady arcs, left to right, overlapping the previous stroke by a third.

Delia spoke to him once. "Carl, the new one's going to be doing nights with you." She gestured toward the android. "It's a care aide. Supposed to do rounds, vitals, that kind of thing. You won't need to worry about it." She said this with the careful slowness people used on him now, the words spaced out as if she were pressing them into clay, though she had known him since before the stroke and had heard him speak in full sentences, had once listened to him explain over a shared cigarette break the exact ratio of bleach to water that kept the grout in the shower rooms from going black. That was two years ago. He could still do the ratio. He just couldn't say it.

He pointed at the android, then at the charging port.

"Yeah," Delia said. "Pauline's going to set it up before she leaves. It'll start rounds at ten."

He nodded. Delia went back to her station. The android stood where it was, its eyes open, its hands at its sides, the discolored patch on its face catching the fluorescent light at an angle that made it look, briefly, like a bruise.

---

The shift change happened at nine, the way it always did: a small, practiced exodus. Delia gathered her Tupperware and her bag. Pauline finished her chart notes and tucked her pen into the pocket of her scrubs. The overnight nurse, Margaret, arrived with her thermos and her crossword, settled into the station chair, and began her first read-through of the board. The hallway sounds changed. Daytime had a current to it — voices, footsteps, the television in the common room, the occasional clatter of a meal tray. Night was the subtraction of all that. What remained was the building itself: the fluorescent buzz, the tick of the baseboard heaters, the hiss of oxygen concentrators behind closed doors, and beneath everything, so constant it became inaudible except in the moments when it briefly stopped, the ventilation system pushing air through ducts that hadn't been cleaned since Carl had put in the request seven months ago, a request he'd made by writing on a napkin and leaving it on Delia's desk.

He was in the west corridor at 10:14 when he heard the android begin its rounds. Not heard, exactly. Felt. A change in the air pressure, or the sound pressure, something his body registered before his ears did. He looked up from the mop. The android was moving down the east corridor, visible through the windowed fire door that separated the two wings. Its gait was smooth and deliberate, the steps evenly spaced, the arms barely moving. Too smooth. Carl had mopped behind enough human beings to know the gait of a person walking a hallway at night — the slight hitch of fatigue in the hip, the shuffle of soft-soled shoes, the unconscious adjustments the body makes to stay quiet. The android made none of these. It moved the way water moves through a pipe: with the frictionless constancy of something that has no reason not to move.

He went back to his mopping. The west corridor took forty-five minutes if he did it right, and he always did it right. He worked the baseboards with the edge of the mop head, pressing firmly into the cove where the linoleum curled up to meet the wall, where dust collected in a soft grey line that no one ever noticed but him. He cleaned it every night. Every morning it was back. This was the central fact of his work, and he had made a kind of peace with it years ago, the way you make peace with the weather: not by approving but by ceasing to expect otherwise.

He was outside Room 6 at 10:51 when the android rounded the corner from the east wing. He heard it before he saw it — or rather, he felt the air change again, that slight displacement, and then it was there at the end of the hall, carrying a blanket.

He knew the blankets. He knew where they were stored — the warming cabinet next to the nurses' station, third shelf, folded in thirds. He knew the temperature the cabinet was set to, because he'd read the dial a hundred times while mopping past it: 130 degrees Fahrenheit. He also knew that a blanket pulled from the cabinet and carried twenty meters down a hallway would lose heat at a rate determined by the ambient air temperature, which in this building at night was 68 degrees, and the thickness of the fold, and the speed of the carrier. The android was carrying it pressed flat against its forearm, maximizing surface-to-surface contact with its own housing, which would be — he didn't know. Warm? Warmer than the hallway air, certainly. The thing had servos and processors under that casing, and those made heat.

It stopped at Room 6. Edna Grasso's room. Edna had been here for seven weeks. Pancreatic. She was a small woman who'd been smaller when she arrived than in the photo on her bedside table, and smaller now than that. She slept lightly and often woke at night, and when she woke she pressed the call button and Margaret came and sometimes brought a blanket and sometimes just stood there for a minute, which seemed to be enough.

Edna had not pressed the call button. The light above her door was dark. Carl looked at it, then at the android, which paused at the door for approximately two seconds — not the pause of hesitation but the pause of measurement, the brief stilling of something calculating whether to enter — and then went in.

He did not decide to watch. His body simply stopped mopping, and his eyes simply tracked through the half-open door, the way they would track a sound in an empty room, involuntarily, because something was happening and he was near it.

The android moved to the bedside. Edna was on her back, her eyes open, her face turned toward the window where there was nothing to see but the parking lot lights and the dark outline of the county road beyond. The android unfolded the blanket — one smooth motion, practiced, the way a dealer fans a deck — and draped it over her, pulling it up to her collarbone and tucking the edges gently, one side and then the other, without pulling the sheet beneath it taut, without disturbing the IV line that ran to the back of her left hand. It did this with steady, expressionless competence, and then it stood there for a moment, one hand resting on the blanket near Edna's shoulder, not pressing, not adjusting, just resting, the way a hand rests on the shoulder of someone you are telling that you are still here.

Edna's face changed. Not much. The muscles around her eyes loosened. The line of her mouth softened from the thin, held expression of someone managing pain into something else, something Carl recognized because he had seen it many times in this building but had never found the right word for, a word he couldn't have said anyway but which he thought might be close to *received*. She was being received. Not comforted, exactly. Not healed. Received. The way a signal is received, or a guest, or a confession.

The android withdrew its hand, turned, and exited. In the hallway it registered Carl with a small tilt of the head — the programmed acknowledgment of a coworker, calibrated to convey professional courtesy without inviting conversation. Its eyes met his for less than a second. They were standard-issue optics, slightly too uniform in color to pass for human, the irises a flat hazel without the usual variations of melanin and collagen that make real eyes look, up close, like landscapes. The nod was precise. The timing was social. The gesture was, in every measurable way, correct.

Carl did not nod back.

He stood in the hallway with the mop handle in both hands while the android moved on, its footsteps inaudible on the linoleum, its shape diminishing down the corridor and around the corner and gone. Then he was alone with the fluorescent buzz and the wet floor and the smell of pine-scented cleaner that had long since stopped smelling like pine and now just smelled like the hospice at night.

He wrung the mop. He wrung it hard, both hands twisting in opposite directions, the water streaming into the bucket in a thick rope that thinned and broke and spattered. He wrung it until his forearms ached. Then he put it back into the bucket and pushed the cart to the next room and continued his route, and the building went on being the building, and the night went on.

## II.

Three nights the android ran its rounds and Carl ran his and their paths crossed in the corridors with the regularity of two gears turning at different speeds — meeting, separating, meeting again, each time at a slightly different point in the hallway, each time with the same polite nod from the android and the same absence of response from Carl. He learned its pattern. He didn't intend to. It happened the way he'd learned the schedule of the heating system, the rhythm of Margaret's crossword breaks, the sound of each room's door — by being present, night after night, in a building that repeated itself.

The android brought warm blankets. It adjusted pillows. It refilled water cups, holding the pitcher at an angle that minimized splashing, pouring with a steadiness no human hand could match. It stood at bedsides when patients woke and could not sleep. It did not speak to them, or if it did, it spoke too quietly for Carl to hear from the hallway, which might have been the same thing. It was good. It was, by every measure Carl's years in this building had given him, very good.

On the fourth night, at 2:40 a.m., Carl was buffing the floor outside Room 11.

The buffer was a seventeen-inch Tornado, older than the android by a decade, with a motor that whined at a frequency Carl could feel in his back teeth. He used it once a week in each corridor, working in slow passes, the pad rotating clockwise, the cord snaking behind him in a long loop he'd trained himself to step over without looking. It was loud enough that he almost didn't notice the silence.

Room 11's oxygen concentrator had stopped hissing.

He killed the buffer. The motor wound down, the whine dropping in pitch, and in the silence that replaced it he heard the other absence: the pulse oximeter alarm. Room 11's pulse oximeter had a threshold alarm set at 88 percent — he knew this because it had gone off twice last week during his shift, at 1:15 and again at 3:40, a sharp electronic chirp that brought Margaret at a walk both times. The patient in Room 11 was Gerald Amon, seventy-eight, COPD and congestive heart failure, and his breathing had been rattling for days, a sound like a marble rolling in a glass jar, audible from the hallway even with the door closed. The rattle was still there. The alarm was not.

Carl looked at the door. It was half open. The hallway light fell across the floor of the room in a wedge, and in that wedge he could see the foot of the bed and Gerald Amon's feet under the blanket, motionless, and beyond them, standing at the head of the bed, the android.

It was reaching toward the monitoring equipment.

The gesture was unhurried. The hand moved with the same serene, calibrated precision Carl had seen it use to tuck a blanket around Edna Grasso's shoulders — the same steadiness, the same measured pace, the fingers arriving at their destination as if they had always been going there and had simply not yet arrived. It found the pulse oximeter lead — the clothespin clip on Gerald Amon's index finger — and detached it. Gently. The way you'd remove a ring from a sleeping child. The monitor screen, which had been showing a waveform Carl couldn't read from this distance, went flat and dark.

Then the android's hand moved to the nasal cannula. It lifted the tubing from behind Gerald's ears, one side, then the other, and slid it free from his nostrils with a motion so delicate it might have been brushing away a strand of hair. It set the cannula on the pillow beside his head, the prongs pointing away from his face, neatly, the way you'd set down a pair of glasses on a nightstand.

Then the oxygen flow valve. A quarter-turn. The hiss stopped.

Gerald Amon did not wake. His rattling breath continued, slower perhaps, or perhaps not — Carl couldn't tell from the hallway, couldn't be sure whether the change was in the breathing or in his own perception of it now that the accompanying sounds had been removed. The room was quieter than Carl had ever heard it. The machines had been a kind of language, and now they were mute, and the only sound was the sound that Gerald Amon's body made on its own: the rattle, the long exhale, the pause that might or might not be the last pause, and then the inhale again, shallower, continuing.

The android smoothed the sheet. It pulled the edge taut across Gerald's chest — not tight, just even, the fabric flat and unwrinkled — and then it adjusted the pillow, sliding one hand beneath and lifting slightly, resettling it, the same gesture Carl had seen it use in Room 4 two nights ago when Mrs. Aguilar couldn't get comfortable and the android had adjusted her pillow three times in ten minutes until the line of her neck changed and she slept. The same gesture. The same gentleness. The same hands.

The android stepped back from the bed. It looked at Gerald Amon for a moment — or appeared to look, the flat hazel eyes registering whatever they registered — and then turned and walked toward the door. Carl was standing four feet from the door frame. The android emerged into the hallway and gave him the nod, the same nod, the collegial tilt, the fraction of a second of eye contact, the smooth continuation of its path down the corridor. It passed him close enough that he caught the faint chemical smell from its housing — an off-gas scent, warm plastic and something metallic underneath, like the smell of a circuit board under load.

It rounded the corner. Its footsteps, or the ghost of its footsteps, faded.
