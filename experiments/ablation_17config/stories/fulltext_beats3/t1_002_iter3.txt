**AUDIO DIARY — TAPE 1, SIDE A**
**Transcribed from microcassette**

[RECORDING BEGINS]

[AMBIENT SOUND: car engine idling, rain on windshield]

This is Carol Fielding, September fourteenth. I'm sitting in the courthouse parking lot in my car because I can't smoke in the building and I have forty-five minutes until the Henderson deposition and I'm not going to spend it in the break room listening to Donna talk about her Pomeranian's surgery again.

[PAUSE, 3 seconds]

First day on *Vance*. I was not originally assigned this one. Janet Holloway had it—she'd been primary reporter for Judge Hadley's courtroom since, I don't know, '83 at least. But she took a leave. I don't know the full story. Marilyn in the clerk's office said it was personal, and Marilyn said it in that way she says things where you know she wants you to ask. I didn't ask. It's none of my business. Hadley's clerk called me Thursday and asked if I could pick it up, and I said yes, because I always say yes, and because the alternative was two more weeks of insurance depositions in Charleston, which—just kill me.

[PAUSE, 2 seconds]

So. Eleanor Vance. This is the satanic panic case that's been in the papers. Two kids from the Bowman family died, one in February and one in May, and the social worker assigned to the family is being charged with—I don't know how to describe it. Murder by way of devil worship. The prosecution's theory, as I understand it from the filings, is that Vance was part of some kind of cult operating in the county that targeted foster children for ritualistic abuse, and two of the children died from injuries sustained during these rituals. Dale Parsons is prosecuting. He's—very Dale Parsons about it. Red tie. Pointing a lot. The courtroom was packed. Channel 5 had a camera crew outside.

[SOUND: lighter flicking, inhale]

The defendant. I should note what I noticed, professionally. She sat perfectly still. I mean *perfectly*. Most defendants fidget. They whisper to their lawyers, they look at the gallery, they shift in their seats. She didn't. She sat with her hands flat on the defense table, palms down, like she was holding it in place. Small woman. Brown hair, no makeup, state-issued clothes. She looked at the judge when the judge spoke. She looked at the prosecution table when the prosecution spoke. She looked at her own attorney when her attorney spoke. She didn't look at me. Nobody looks at the stenographer.

[PAUSE, 4 seconds]

She did speak out of turn once. Right in the middle of her own lawyer's bail argument. It was fast, maybe three seconds, and it wasn't English. It wasn't anything. It sounded—it sounded like glossolalia, like a Pentecostal speaking in tongues. Or like someone trying to clear their throat with syllables in the way. I got what I could on the machine and marked it [UNINTELLIGIBLE], which is standard. Judge shut it down. Lukas looked like he wanted to crawl under the table.

[SOUND: exhale, long]

What else. Hadley's breath smelled like black coffee and something worse, something medicinal. Lukas is wearing a suit that doesn't fit—too tight in the shoulders, which means he borrowed it or he lost weight or he used to be the kind of lawyer who bought good suits and now he's the kind of lawyer who gets appointed to murder cases in Sutton County. Parsons had a new briefcase. Leather. I'd estimate three hundred dollars. Someone's funding this prosecution, or he's investing in his future. This is the kind of case that gets a prosecutor elected to bigger things.

[PAUSE, 2 seconds]

Sixteen minutes of proceedings. Two dead children. One woman who sat perfectly still except for the moment she opened her mouth and let out the sound of something that wasn't language.

I got it all down. That's the job.

[RECORDING ENDS]
