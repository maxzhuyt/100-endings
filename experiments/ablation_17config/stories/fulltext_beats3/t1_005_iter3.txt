# The Unfinished Play

## I.

The gym doors were chained at 6:14 a.m. on a Tuesday, which was not early enough to keep Aïda Cole from pressing her face against the wire-glass window and seeing what she would spend the next fifty-two hours deciding how much of to share.

By the time the rest of the team arrived for what should have been their final practice before the Southwest Regional Championship, the Riverside County Sheriff's Department had already removed the body of Gerald Voss—equipment manager, volunteer statistician, and the only person in the program's history who hand-taped every player's wheels before a game because he believed the factory tape bunched at speed and cost them a half-second on cuts. The gym was taped off. Two deputies stood at the east entrance drinking gas-station coffee with the distracted air of men for whom this was already a closed matter. It was. Within four hours, Coach Mike Devlin of Eastlake Prep had confessed. He'd come to the gym to confront Voss about a scouting dispute—Devlin said Voss had stolen his playbook files during a shared tournament last spring—and the confrontation had turned physical, and Devlin had shoved the older man into the corner of the scorer's table, and the older man had not gotten up, and Devlin had driven home to Eastlake in his windbreaker and sat in his own gym until the guilt made him call the sheriff at dawn.

It was neat. It was public. Two local stations ran it by noon, one of them with a photograph of Devlin in which he looked puffy and already convicted.

The team's relief was immediate, communal, and—to one person—obscene.

Mariana Reyes, whom everyone called Wheels and who called herself nothing because she did not believe in the luxury of self-narration, sat in her TiLite at the far end of the corridor outside the chained gym and watched her teammates process the news. She watched Tomás Quintero nod and wheel away without comment, which was what Tomás did with every piece of information he received—he stored it in the same neutral place, like a man filing receipts he might need later for a tax audit or a prison appeal. She watched DeAndra Jackson call her mother, speak for eleven seconds, and hang up without saying goodbye, which DeAndra did only when the conversation contained something she did not want overheard. She watched Aïda Cole sit perfectly still by the wire-glass window and blink three times in rapid succession, which Aïda did when she was translating something she'd seen into something she could live with.

Mariana watched all of them. Then she took out her phone and began texting, not to the group thread—never to the group thread—but to each player individually. The messages were different. To Tomás: *You told me last week the east-corridor keycard reader logs to a local server. Does the loading-dock reader log the same way?* To DeAndra: *Your mother works intake at Riverside General. I know you know that's not a question. Auxiliary gym, tonight, 9.* To Aïda: *I know you were at the window this morning before the deputies. I'm not asking what you saw. I'm asking you to come to the auxiliary gym tonight and tell me what you didn't see. There's a difference.*

She did not send a group text because a group text would have created a shared document, a single version of events, and Mariana did not want a single version of events. She wanted four versions, each carrying only the weight its author chose to load onto it, so that when the versions converged she could see the shapes of what had been left out. This was how she'd been taught to think at the junior-detective summer camp she'd attended at eleven, twelve, and thirteen—the camp her mother had sent her to because the brochure said it built "analytical confidence in exceptional young women" and because the camp was one of three in the state with accessible cabins. Mariana had loved that camp without irony or embarrassment. She had loved the deduction exercises, the mock crime scenes, the slightly absurd field trips to a retired FBI agent's ranch where they dusted for prints on horse-stall latches. She had loved it, and she had carried its logic into her life the way some people carry a religious conversion: totally, and with the understanding that it would eventually be used against her.

Because what Mariana did not include in any of the four texts, and what she had not told anyone, and what the chained gym doors and the taped-off court and the deputies' gas-station coffee had done nothing to resolve, was this:

Gerald Voss, forty-eight hours before someone killed him, had found the documents. He had found them in the filing cabinet he was organizing as part of his offseason project to digitize the program's records—a project nobody had asked him to undertake, which he'd begun because he believed a well-organized program was a form of respect for the athletes it served. The documents were Mariana's original disability-classification paperwork, submitted four years ago when she first joined the team. The classification—which determined her eligibility to compete in the league's wheelchair basketball division—had been falsified. Not by Mariana, who was fifteen at the time, but by Coach Ray Salazar, their head coach, who had adjusted the functional-capacity scores to ensure Mariana qualified for the classification tier that would let him build his offense around her speed. Mariana's disability was real. Her SCI was real. She belonged in a wheelchair on a basketball court. But the specific classification that made her eligible at the level she competed—the number on the form that determined whether she was a 2.0 or a 3.5, which determined whether Riverside's roster math added up within the league's point system—was wrong by enough to matter and had been wrong for four years.

Voss had found the documents. He had come to Mariana first. He had sat across from her in this same corridor, on the bench that visiting parents used during games, and told her what he'd found with the exhausted gentleness of a man delivering a diagnosis. He told her he understood she'd been fifteen. He told her he didn't believe she'd known. He told her he was giving her one week to self-report to the league before he filed the discrepancy himself, because he believed self-reporting was better for everyone—for her, for the team, for the program's integrity, a word he used without self-consciousness because he was the kind of man who still believed integrity was a thing you could hand to an institution and have it mean something.

Mariana had thanked him. She had wheeled back to her apartment, sat in the kitchen for two hours, and then driven to Coach Salazar's office and told him everything.

She did not self-report.

She went to the coach who had falsified the documents, and she told him that the man who had found the documents was going to report them, and she did this because she was twenty years old and the championship was in five days and her scholarship—not just to play, but to attend the university at all—was tethered to her roster status, and because the analytical confidence she'd built at junior-detective camp had taught her to see five moves ahead but had not taught her what to do when every move was a betrayal of someone.

She told Coach Salazar. Then she went home. Then Gerald Voss died in the gym with his clipboard on the scorer's table and his play diagrams half-finished.

The clipboard was still there. The deputies had bagged the items near the body but not the items on the scorer's table, because the scorer's table was twenty feet from where Voss had fallen against the bleacher supports, and because the case was already closed, and because a clipboard full of basketball diagrams is not what anyone pictures when they picture evidence. On the top page, in Voss's careful draftsman's hand—he had once studied architecture for two semesters before switching to kinesiology—a pick-and-roll was half-drawn. Five circles represented five players. Arrows indicated movement. One circle, labeled *M*, had three arrows radiating from it, each to a different location on the court, because Voss had been designing options. He had been designing options for the player whose eligibility he was about to end.

Mariana had not yet seen the clipboard. She did not know about the arrows or the options or the small notations in the margins. She would not see them until the morning of the championship, when seeing them would be the thing that broke the analytical confidence into something less confident and more analytical—into something, perhaps, that the retired FBI agent at summer camp might have recognized as the beginning of an honest investigation, the kind that turns inward.

But that was Beat 3. This was Beat 1. And in Beat 1, four texts went out, and four players received them, and what each player did before responding told a different story than the response itself.

Aïda Cole, twenty-one, center, six-foot-one even seated, with hands so large she could palm a basketball from her wheelchair like other people palmed a grapefruit, received the text in her dorm room at 4:47 p.m. She read it twice. Then she set the phone face-down on her desk and closed her eyes and replayed, for the eleventh time since dawn, what she had seen through the wire-glass window at 6:08 a.m.

She had not seen the body. The body was already behind the bleacher supports, out of her sightline. What she had seen was Gerald Voss's phone on the gym floor, face-up, its screen still lit, and—reflected in the gym's polished court surface, warped but legible—the last outgoing call's contact name. She had been able to read it because the font was large. Voss used large fonts. He was fifty-seven and vain about not wearing glasses.

The contact name was MARIANA R.

No—that was not all she had seen. She had also seen, three days earlier, Gerald Voss making a phone call through that same wire-glass window while she waited in the corridor for the training room to open. She had not intended to read his lips. She read lips compulsively; it was an old habit from the two years of partial deafness in her childhood, before the surgery, and it had never fully switched off. She read lips the way other people eavesdropped—automatically, guiltily, and with better accuracy than they would ever admit. Through the wire-glass, she had watched Voss say, into his phone: "I need to talk to someone about a classification issue. There's an insurance component too…" And then a name. The name was Mariana.

He'd been calling a lawyer.

Aïda knew this because the contact name on his phone, which she'd seen at 6:08 a.m., was not MARIANA R. The outgoing call—the last call—had gone to MARIANA R., yes, but the call she'd lip-read three days earlier, the call to the lawyer, had been placed to someone else. She knew this because she'd watched him scroll through his contacts, and the name he'd tapped was followed by the letters ESQ.

So: Voss had called a lawyer about Mariana's classification. And Voss's last phone call had been to Mariana herself. And now Voss was dead, and Coach Devlin from Eastlake had confessed, and Aïda Cole sat in her dorm room and replayed the things she'd seen and carefully, surgically, edited out the name. Not forever. Not to protect Mariana—or not only to protect Mariana. She edited it out because Aïda had also lip-read something else during the lawyer call, something she had not yet fully decoded: the word *insurance*, followed by what looked like *Coach* and then *billing* and then a phrase she could not catch because Voss had turned away from the window. And this fragment—*insurance, Coach, billing*—did not fit the classification story. It fit a different story, one Aïda did not yet have enough information to tell, and she was not the kind of person who spoke before she could tell a complete story.

She told herself this was intellectual discipline.

It was not intellectual discipline. It was the particular cowardice of intelligent people who have learned that withholding information feels the same as being careful with it.

Aïda picked up her phone and texted back: *I'll be there.*

Three words. No indication of what she would bring, what she would leave behind, or what it had cost her to separate the strands of what she'd seen into the ones she was willing to share and the ones she would carry alone until carrying them alone became impossible.

DeAndra Jackson, nineteen, shooting guard, the best pure shooter on the team and the most private person in any room she entered, received the text at 5:02 p.m. while sitting in her mother's apartment. Her mother, Claudette, was at work—a twelve-hour shift at Riverside General's intake desk, where she had been employed for nine years and where she had, in those nine years, developed the habit of remembering faces long after HIPAA required her to forget them.

DeAndra did not open the text immediately. She opened her mother's laptop, which was on the kitchen table beside a cold cup of coffee and a pen with the hospital's logo on it. The laptop was already open to the hospital's patient-records portal. The browser had a bookmark in the favorites bar: INTAKE LOG—SEPT. DeAndra did not create this bookmark. Her mother had created it two nights ago, after DeAndra asked a question during dinner so casually that it could only have been rehearsed: "Mom, did anyone from the athletic department come through intake recently? Like, in the last month?"

Claudette had looked at her daughter for a long time. Then she had said, "I can't tell you that." Then she had bookmarked the page and left the laptop on the kitchen table, which was a different sentence.

DeAndra clicked the bookmark. The intake log loaded. She scrolled to September 14, two weeks before the murder, and found what she had already found the first time she looked, the night her mother left the laptop open: Gerald Voss, admitted at 11:42 p.m. via the ambulance bay. Accompanying person listed as RAY SALAZAR, identified as "colleague." Chief complaint: fall. Diagnosis: fractured left zygomatic arch, fractured orbital floor, soft-tissue contusion to the left temporomandibular region.

The injuries were to the face. The left side of the face. The kind of injuries you get when you fall, or the kind you get when someone who is right-handed hits you and you do not turn away in time because you do not expect to be hit.

DeAndra had seen Coach Salazar's hands a thousand times. She had watched those hands diagram plays, adjust wheel camber, grip the push-rims of a demonstration chair. Those hands were thick-fingered, blunt-nailed, and right-dominant in everything they did.

She closed the laptop. She picked up her phone. She read Mariana's text. She texted back: *I'll bring what I have.* She did not specify what she had because she did not yet know how to explain that what she had was her mother's career sitting on a kitchen table in the shape of a bookmarked page, and that bringing it anywhere meant risking the only job her household had, and that she was going to bring it anyway because Gerald Voss had come to intake at 11:42 at night with his face broken and the man who brought him in had called it a fall and the hospital had believed it because hospitals believe colleagues the way juries believe confessions: quickly, and with relief.

Tomás Quintero, twenty, bench player, the kind of player who entered games in garbage time and exited them without the announcer noting the substitution, received the text at 5:11 p.m. in the middle of an experiment.

He was in the athletic building's east corridor, the one that connected the main gym to the training rooms and the loading dock, and he was wheeling. Just wheeling. Back and forth, at various speeds, through the corridor's three keycard-access doors, at various times, with his own keycard and—when no one was looking—with the backup card he'd taken from the unlocked equipment closet six hours ago because the equipment closet was always unlocked because Gerald Voss had believed in keeping things accessible and no one had yet thought to change the policy now that Gerald Voss was dead.

Tomás was timing how long it took to get from the loading dock to the main gym in a wheelchair. He was testing whether the keycard readers logged timestamps. He was checking whether the east-corridor security camera—the one mounted above the loading-dock door, angled outward toward the parking lot—captured anyone entering the building from that side, or whether its field of vision stopped at the threshold.
