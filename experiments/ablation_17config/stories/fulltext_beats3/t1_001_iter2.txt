# The Thirty-Seven Letters of Monk Hideo, As Compiled by His Opponent

---

## Editor's Preface

I will not waste the reader's time with modesty or apology.

In the autumn of 1961, at the Seventeenth All-Japan Kendo Championship in Osaka, Hideo Takamura defeated every opponent in the individual division without conceding a single point. His movement through the bracket was the subject of considerable discussion. Several referees noted afterward that his strikes seemed to arrive before the intention to strike had fully formed in his body — a quality sometimes described in classical texts but not, before that week, observed in modern competition.

I was his final opponent.

The match lasted eleven seconds. Hideo entered the court, assumed his stance, stepped forward, and bowed. He laid his shinai on the floor. He left the gymnasium. He was not seen again in the competitive martial world.

I was awarded the championship by forfeit.

In the forty-two years since, I have been asked to explain the forfeit perhaps six hundred times. I have never been able to do so. For the first decade, I believed this was because I did not understand it. For the second, I believed it was because I understood it and could not prove what I understood. For the third and fourth, I stopped distinguishing between these positions.

This collection may clarify the matter, or it may not. I am presenting it because I am dying — liver, inoperable, three months or less, the doctors politely vague in the way that indicates precision would be unkind — and because the letters exist and should not be destroyed by my heirs, who would not know what they contained. Hideo has not been asked for permission. I am aware of what this means.

The first letter arrived in February 1962, four months after the tournament. It was postmarked from a monastery in Niigata Prefecture, though the name on the postmark corresponded to no monastery I or anyone I consulted could locate. I did not reply for seven months. When I did reply, I do not recall what I said. He wrote again. I replied again. This continued for forty years.

There are thirty-seven letters in all. I have arranged them chronologically. My annotations are limited to matters of factual correction, contextual clarification, and — in certain instances I could not prevent — personal observation. I have attempted to be precise. Where I have failed, the failure is itself a kind of information.

The letters begin.

---

## Letter One (February 1962)

The monastery wakes at half past three. I did not know this before arriving. I had imagined that monastic life would be organized around significant hours — sunrise, noon, the canonical divisions — but 3:30 is not a significant hour. It is an hour no one would choose. I think this is the point.

We sit zazen until 5:00. Then there is a meal: barley rice, pickled radish, sometimes a broth made from kelp. The barley has a particular quality when it has been cooked for a long time in a large quantity of water. It does not become soft. It becomes less resistant. These are not the same thing. I am still learning the difference.

The cold here enters the knuckles first. The other monks wrap their hands in cloth during the early morning sitting, but I have not done this. I am aware this may be vanity rather than discipline. The abbot has not commented, which could mean either thing.

My cell has a single window that faces northeast. It is adequate for seeing what is necessary.

I will write to you again.

>**Annotation [1]:** The tournament took place in October 1961, not autumn generally. The specific date was October 14. It rained that morning and cleared by noon, a detail that has no bearing on anything but which I remember.
>
>The postmark reads "Shingetsu-ji," which translates roughly as "New Moon Temple." Inquiries to the Sōtō Zen administrative offices produced no record of a monastery by this name in Niigata Prefecture. Inquiries to the Rinzai offices produced the same result. It is possible the name is informal, or historical, or invented. I did not pursue the question further at the time. I should have.

## Letter Two (June 1962)

The monastery garden is maintained by a monk called Daishin, though I do not think this is the name he was born with. He rakes the gravel each morning in lines that follow the contours of the stones. When it rains, the lines are destroyed. He rakes them again the next day, identically. I asked him once whether the repetition frustrated him. He said the question was like asking whether he was frustrated that his lungs kept breathing.

I have been assigned to the kitchen. The work is not complex, but it requires a specific quality of attention — the kind that empties itself of preference. A knife used with preference cuts the vegetable into what you want it to be. A knife used without preference cuts the vegetable into what it is. I have not yet managed the second kind of cutting.

The barley has not improved. I suspect it is not meant to.

>**Annotation [2]:** No correction necessary. I note only that in June 1962 I was preparing for the national team trials and had not yet replied to the first letter. I carried it in my equipment bag for four months. The edges became soft.

## Letter Three (November 1962)

Winter arrives in this region the way a decision arrives — not all at once but in a series of small concessions. First the color goes. Then the sound. Then the warmth. By the time you notice winter has come, it has been here for some time and has been waiting for you to acknowledge it.

I sit zazen six hours each day now, sometimes seven. The abbot says I am sitting too long. He says sitting zazen like a man trying to accomplish something is not zazen. I said I was not trying to accomplish anything. He said: "Exactly. You are trying very hard not to accomplish anything, which is the same problem wearing different robes."

The window in my cell has developed a crack in the lower right corner. When the wind comes from the north, it produces a sound like a single sustained note on a shakuhachi. I have not reported it for repair. The sound is adequate for hearing what is necessary.

>**Annotation [3]:** He always spoke like this. Everything meant two things. I never knew which one he was offering.
>
>Regarding the window: in his first letter, Hideo describes it as "adequate for seeing what is necessary." Here the phrasing recurs with variation. I do not know whether this is a deliberate echo or the habitual architecture of a mind that cannot say a thing once.

## Letters Four Through Six (1963–1964)

*I summarize. These letters concern the routines of the monastery in greater detail: the schedule of services, the rotation of duties, a sustained description of the method by which the monastery produces its own miso, which requires eighteen months of fermentation and a particular quality of attention during the turning of the paste. Letter Five describes the death of an elderly monk whose name Hideo does not give, and the funeral rites, which involved the chanting of a sutra for five continuous hours. Hideo notes that during the fifth hour something changed in the quality of the chanting — "not louder, not more sincere, but less separate from the silence around it, as though the voices had stopped trying to fill the room and had become the room." Letter Six describes the spring thaw and the discovery, beneath the retreating snow, of a set of stone steps leading to a lower garden no one in the current community had known existed. Hideo helped clear the steps. At the bottom was a small stone basin, perfectly intact, containing frozen water and a single dead leaf.*

*None of these letters mention the tournament, kendo, or me.*

>**Annotation [4-6]:** The miso-turning technique Hideo describes in Letter Four corresponds to no method I have been able to verify in standard references on monastic food preparation. This does not mean it is invented. It means the monastery's practices may predate the standardized traditions, or may have diverged from them. Or Hideo may be describing the process incorrectly. He was never interested in being correct. He was interested in being precise, which is a different pursuit conducted with the same tools.
>
>I note the hidden garden. I note the stone basin. I note the dead leaf. I do not annotate them.

## Letter Seven (March 1965)

You did not reply to my last two letters. I consider this a form of reply.

I have been thinking about *bushido* and about whether it is a living practice or a memory that moves through the body like an old habit, the way a man who has lost his arm still reaches for things in the night. There is a difference between a practice and a haunting, but the difference may be less than I once believed. A practice asks: *what is the correct action?* A haunting asks: *what was the correct action?* The haunting is the practice after the body has stopped but the mind has not.

I do not practice kendo here. There are no shinai, no armor, no court. But yesterday I was cutting firewood and I noticed that the motion of the axe — the specific rotation of the wrists at the apex, the way the weight transfers through the hips — was not the motion of a man cutting firewood. It was the motion of a man executing *men-uchi*. The firewood split perfectly. I set down the axe and did not cut firewood for the rest of the day.

The abbot asked me why the wood was not cut. I said I did not have an answer. He said: "Good. Answers are for people who have stopped listening to the question."

>**Annotation [7]:** He began writing about combat here. I mark it.
>
>*Men-uchi* — a downward strike to the head — is the most fundamental technique in kendo. Every practitioner learns it first. Every master returns to it last. Hideo's observation that the motion of cutting firewood reproduced the motion of *men-uchi* is technically accurate. The biomechanics are nearly identical. What he does not say — what he circles — is that a motion practiced ten thousand times does not live in the mind. It lives in the body. And the body does not forget. The body does not renounce. The body does not bow and lay its weapon on the floor and walk out of a gymnasium. Only the mind does that, and the mind was always the weaker partner.
>
>I replied to this letter. I do not include my reply. My replies are not the subject of this collection.

## Letter Nine (September 1966)

*Letter Eight, which primarily concerns the monastery's preparations for the Obon festival and a digression on the nature of offering food to the dead, is omitted. Its contents are not essential to the collection's trajectory, though I note that Hideo describes arranging rice on a tray with a precision he calls "architectural," and breaks off the description mid-sentence. The letter resumes after a visible gap in the brushwork, as though time passed between the two halves. The second half discusses weather.*

---

There is a novice here, fifteen years old, who cannot hold a broom correctly.

I do not mean that his grip is wrong. His grip is technically sound. His fingers close in the right places. His thumbs align. But when he sweeps, the bristles touch the ground the way a hand touches a surface it expects to be hot — testing, hesitant, prepared to withdraw. He sweeps as though the floor is a thing that might object.

The abbot has corrected him perhaps forty times. Each time, the novice adjusts, and each time the adjustment holds for the span of the courtyard and no longer. By the time he reaches the gate, his grip has returned to its original form, like water finding its own level.

I recognize this. The body has a shape it considers home, and every correction is a departure from home, and every departure creates a longing to return. A man can train for thirty years and still, in the moment before action, feel his body reach for the posture it knew first rather than the posture it was taught.

This is not failure. This is the body's memory asserting itself against the body's education. The novice's broom grip is his first grip — the one he learned before anyone told him how to hold things. It is the shape of his hand in the absence of instruction. It is what he would do if no one had ever told him what to do.

The question is not how to correct it. You cannot correct a thing that is native to the body. The question is whether you need to.

I think about the geometry of a man's stance sometimes. The way the feet find their distance without being told. The way the leading hip opens or closes in accordance with some law the man himself does not know he is obeying. These are not decisions. They are confessions. The body confesses its history in every posture it assumes. A man who has injured his left knee twenty years ago will still, in his stance, make a single concession to that injury — a degree, perhaps less, of rotation in the leading foot. An observer would not see it. But a man who knew to look for it would see nothing else.

>**Annotation [9]:** No comment.

## Letter Twelve (1968)

I have not written about what I wish to write about, and I suspect you have noticed. You are not a man who fails to notice things. This was always the problem.

I want to discuss the concept of *suki*. You will know the term. For the reader who does not: *suki* refers to an opening — a gap in a fighter's defense, a moment of vulnerability, a place where the armor of technique and intention fails to cover the body's truth.

Every fighter has *suki*. The novice has many. The intermediate fighter learns to close them. The advanced fighter has only one or two, and these are so deeply embedded in the architecture of the body that they are invisible to anyone who does not already know they exist. They are not errors. They are the cost of being a particular body with a particular history. The left knee that healed slightly inward. The right shoulder that compensates. The chin that lifts — by a degree, by less than a degree — in the instant before a counter technique, because the eyes want to see the target and the body has learned to give the eyes what they want.

A master can be defined as a fighter whose remaining *suki* are indistinguishable from his strengths. The opening and the virtue occupy the same gesture. The very habit that makes his counter-strike devastating is the same habit that exposes his throat in the instant before the counter-strike arrives.

This is not a flaw in the system. This is the system. Mastery does not eliminate vulnerability. Mastery makes vulnerability and power identical, so that they cannot be separated, so that to attack the weakness you must also forfeit the advantage of the strength, and to use the strength you must also accept the exposure of the weakness.

I have thought about this for a long time.

>**Annotation [12]:** The technical content is accurate. The term *suki* translates variously as "opening," "gap," or "opportunity," depending on whether you are the one who has it or the one who sees it. In competitive kendo, identifying an opponent's *suki* is the fundamental strategic objective. Exploiting it is the fundamental tactical act.
>
>What Hideo describes — the *suki* that is identical to the strength — is a real phenomenon. It is discussed in the Ittō-ryū tradition under the concept of *ken-tai-ichi*, the unity of sword and body. But the classical texts discuss it as philosophy. Hideo is not writing philosophy here. He is writing anatomy.
>
>I do not recognize the body he is describing. That is not the same as saying I do not know whose body it is.

## Letter Fifteen (February 1971)

I will describe something now with the precision it requires.

A certain posture — I will call it *seigan-gamae* in its modern form, though the roots are older — places the sword's point at the opponent's eyes. It is the most common central guard. Its virtues are balance, adaptability, and the speed with which it permits both offensive and defensive transitions. A fighter who adopts this posture well can be attacked from any direction and respond cleanly.

But "well" and "correctly" are not synonyms. A fighter who adopts this posture *correctly* — feet at a precise shoulder-width distance, weight distributed sixty-forty between front and back foot, the point of the shinai aligned with the opponent's left eye — has closed every major *suki* the posture permits. A fighter who adopts this posture *well* has done something different: he has inhabited the posture the way a body inhabits a room, with small adjustments that make the geometry his own. The left hip slightly forward. The rear heel a quarter-inch higher than the text prescribes. The grip pressure asymmetric — stronger in the left hand's last two fingers, lighter in the right — in a way that accelerates the lateral cut by a fraction of a second.

These adjustments are improvements. Each one adds speed, or power, or adaptability. Each one is a refinement born of ten thousand hours of individual practice.
