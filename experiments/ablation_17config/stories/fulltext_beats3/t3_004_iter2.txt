# The Commissar's Confession

## I.

The room smelled of tallow and the particular sourness of men who have not left a building in three days. Someone—Collot, perhaps—was coughing into a handkerchief in the manner of a person who believes his cough to be quieter than it is. Papers covered the long table in overlapping tiers, white and cream and the pale blue of requisition forms, and the ink on most of them was not yet fully dry, so that when Citizen Robespierre set his hand upon a stack and then lifted it, his palm carried a faint mirror-script that he did not notice or did not care to wipe away. The committee chamber had no windows. Two lamps. An inadequate fire. The session was technically the fourth of the day but felt, as all sessions now felt, like a single unbroken effort that had been running since September and would run until it had consumed its fuel, which was names.

He read them without emphasis.

Desmoulins. Arnault. The widow Morel. Couthon's former secretary, whose surname the inspector had heard three different ways and now heard a fourth. Brissard. Laine. The printer Gâtien, already dead but listed because the Committee's records must be complete, because completion was itself a revolutionary act, because a republic that could not account for its enemies could not account for itself. A schoolteacher from Lyon whom the inspector had met once in a cellar that smelled of onions and lamp oil and who had spoken, with a sincerity that approached physical pain, about Condorcet's vision of universal education, about a world in which every child—but that was not relevant. Delacroix. Fourneau. The widow Varennes.

Fourteen names. The inspector heard each one register in the chamber of his mind with the clean mechanical action of a typesetter's lever pressing a letter into place.

Eleven were his.

Eleven people he had recruited, or vetted, or sheltered, or passed documents to in the back rooms of printers' shops and the kitchens of taverns whose owners asked no questions because to ask a question in the Year II was to generate an answer that might kill you.

The remaining three—Desmoulins, Couthon's secretary, Delacroix—were the Committee's own quarry, caught by the Committee's own methods, unrelated to his network except by the accident of a shared list. Bureaucracy had its own geometry. Lines converged.

"Citizen Mériaux."

He met Robespierre's eyes. This required no performance. He had been meeting those eyes for two years across tables and in corridors and once in the gallery of the Convention when Robespierre had turned to him during a vote and said, very quietly, as though commenting on the weather, *The Republic has no need for mercy; mercy is the privilege of kings*—and the inspector had nodded, because nodding was his function, because agreement was the oxygen that kept a man alive in a room full of men who were slowly suffocating each other.

"The Committee recognizes your diligence in the matter of the Faubourg Saint-Marcel section. You will oversee the transfer and execution of the condemned. The schedule is as follows."

A scheduling detail. A clerical matter. The assignment of a task to the man best suited to perform it, as one might assign an inventory to a clerk who knows the warehouse. Robespierre's voice carried the flat assurance of a man who had long since ceased to distinguish between administration and justice. The schedule: two days hence. Morning. The carts from the Conciergerie at eight. The walk. The Place de la Révolution by ten, because the Committee preferred morning executions—afternoons attracted larger crowds and larger crowds generated unpredictable sentiments and the Republic could not afford unpredictable sentiments, only predictable ones, channelled and measured and deployed.

"I am honored by the Committee's trust," the inspector said.

This was the correct sentence. He had heard variations of it spoken by forty men in this room over twelve months and had noted, with the part of his mind that never stopped noting, that the men who meant it and the men who did not produced the same sentence with the same intonation, as though sincerity and its counterfeit were manufactured in the same factory, by the same process, indistinguishable on delivery. Collot coughed. Someone turned a page. Saint-Just was writing something in the margin of a report and did not look up.

He walked out.

The corridor was long and dim and smelled of stone and the faintly sweet decay of a building that had housed more bodies than its ventilation could accommodate. His boots struck the floor in a regular cadence. He counted the steps because counting was what his mind did when the alternative was something else.

Fourteen names. Eleven his. Three unknown to him. Eleven minus the already dead Gâtien: ten living people he had placed, personally, into the moderate network over the course of three years, seven months, and an indeterminate number of days that he could calculate precisely if he allowed himself the exercise, which he would not, because the exercise would take him back to the beginning, and the beginning was a room above a printer's shop where a woman whose husband had been dead for four months had set a cup of chicory in front of him and said, *Tell me what you believe*, and he had told her things he had not yet told himself.

The widow Varennes.

Her name sat in the fourteenth position on Robespierre's list, which was alphabetical, because the Revolution was nothing if not orderly.

He did not think about her yet. There would be time. There would be the walk. For now: the corridor, the arithmetic, the three names the Committee had not read aloud because the Committee did not possess them.

Fournier. The typesetter's apprentice, nineteen years old, currently in a room above a tannery in the Marais, waiting for a set of forged transit papers that would never arrive because the forger, Brissard, was now on the list.

Madame Leclerc. A former actress who ran a safe house near the Pont-Neuf and who had once, in a moment of exhaustion or abandon, told the inspector that she had stopped believing the moderates would succeed approximately eight months before he had and that she continued because stopping was a form of dying she was not yet willing to perform.

Joly. A clerk in the Ministry of the Interior whose position gave him access to arrest warrants forty-eight hours before they were executed and who had, by the inspector's careful accounting, allowed eleven people to vanish from Paris before the gendarmes arrived at their doors. Joly, who was still in place. Joly, whose name the Committee did not have.

Three. He held them in his mind the way a man holds coins in a closed fist—by pressure, by the ache of the hand that holds them.

The question was mechanical: if the ten condemned included anyone who knew those three names, then the interrogations preceding the executions might extract those names, and the three would join the fourteen, and the list would grow as lists grew in the Republic, by a logic that was not logical but organic, cellular, the division and multiplication of suspicion.

Did Brissard know about Joly? He did. The inspector had introduced them himself because Joly's access to warrants had required a secure channel for the forged documents, and Brissard was the channel, and this connection—which had saved eleven lives—was now the filament along which a flame would travel from the interrogation chamber to the Ministry of the Interior.

Then Joly was already dead. Not yet, but already. Dead in the future tense, which was the only tense the Terror recognized.

Could Joly be warned? Could Fournier? Could the actress?

Each question performed itself in his mind and produced its answer with the efficiency of a machine designed for this purpose, which was what he was—a machine, a device for calculating risk and proximity and exposure and loss, assembled from the parts of a man who had once sat in a room above a printer's shop and spoken about justice, about Condorcet, about the rights of man, about the possibility that a republic could be built not on the blade but on—but that was not relevant. That had not been relevant for some time.

If he warned Joly: Joly might flee. If Joly fled, his absence would be noted. If his absence was noted within the window of the interrogations, the connection to Brissard would be inferred, and the inference would travel backward along a chain of association that terminated at the inspector himself, because he was the node, he was the point where all the lines converged, and his exposure would not save the ten condemned but would add to the list every name he carried in the closed fist of his mind, names the Committee did not have, names of people in Rouen and Bordeaux and a farmhouse outside Nantes where a family was sheltering a priest who was sheltering a constitutional monarchist who was sheltering a Girondin who was sheltering a woman whose only crime was that her brother had once—

No.

He would not warn Joly. He would not warn Fournier. He would not warn the actress. He would perform his function. He would oversee the transfer and the walk and the execution and he would return to this building and he would report that the schedule had been maintained, the names verified, the Republic's will made manifest in the efficient administration of its justice, and the three names he carried would remain unspoken because the only vault in France the Committee could not open was the one inside his skull, and he would keep it locked, and the cost of keeping it locked was ten people, eleven counting Gâtien who was already beyond cost, and among the ten was a woman who had once set a cup of chicory in front of him and said, *Tell me what you believe*.

He reached the end of the corridor. He opened a door. Daylight struck him like a verdict.

The streets were full. The streets were always full. Paris in the Year II was a city that could not stop moving because stillness attracted attention and attention attracted suspicion and suspicion attracted lists, and so the citizens walked and the citizens shopped and the citizens attended to the revolutionary business of appearing to be citizens, and the inspector walked among them and carried his arithmetic through a city that did not know it was a calculation performed by a man who had forgotten, with the precision of someone who has not forgotten at all, the taste of chicory in a room above a press.

## II.

Morning, and the carts.

He had not slept. This was imprecise. He had lain on his bed in the room he rented on the Rue du Bac—a narrow room, a desk, a basin, a window overlooking a courtyard where a dog barked at intervals so regular they seemed administrative—and his mind had performed its operations in the dark the way a mill continues grinding after the miller has gone. The operations were not useful. They were recursive. They circled the same set of numbers and produced the same outputs: zero, zero, zero. No stratagem that saved one without exposing three. No calculation whose remainder was not someone's life. At some point in the night the operations had shifted register—this had happened before, he had learned not to resist it—and the numbers became not numbers but scenes, not outputs but textures, and he was in the cellar with the schoolteacher from Lyon who was explaining Condorcet with such earnestness that his hands shook, he was in the actress's safe house listening to her describe the exact moment she had stopped believing, he was standing in a field outside Montmorency at six in the morning watching his handler, Duclos, bury a packet of letters in ground that was still frozen and would not yield, and Duclos was swearing softly and methodically in a way that struck the inspector then as comic and struck him now, in the dark, on the bed, as the most human sound he had ever heard, and Duclos was dead, had been dead since March, was in a mass grave near the Barrière d'Enfer that the inspector had visited once and would not visit again because the lime—the lime had a smell that did not leave, that had entered something deeper than memory, some stratum of the body where sensation was stored without the permission of the mind, and it surfaced now, in the room on the Rue du Bac, the sweet chemical burn of it, and his throat closed and his eyes opened and the dog was barking and it was not yet dawn.

The carts from the Conciergerie arrived at eight, as scheduled. The Republic was punctual. He stood outside the prison gate with a list—the same fourteen names, transcribed in his own hand onto a fresh sheet because the Committee required each stage of the process to generate its own documentation, a paper trail that would survive the event it documented the way a fossil survives the animal—and he watched the condemned descend.

They came in the order they had been loaded, which was the order of their cells, which bore no relation to the alphabetical order of Robespierre's list, and so the names as he checked them arrived jumbled, disordered, a sequence stripped of the logic that had organized it. This should not have mattered. It mattered. The schoolteacher from Lyon—Fourneau—came third, and his hands were bound in front of him, and he did not look at the inspector because he did not know the inspector's face, they had met once, in a cellar, in the presence of four other people, and the inspector had been introduced under a name that was not his name, because names in the network were disposable, interchangeable, temporary, the way names in the Revolution were temporary, the way everything—

He made his mark beside Fourneau and moved on.

Brissard came fifth. Brissard knew his face. Brissard had known his face for two years, had forged documents for eleven people on his instruction, had once stayed up for thirty consecutive hours producing a set of transit papers so convincing that the checkpoint guard at the Porte Saint-Denis had waved the bearer through without a second glance, and that bearer was now in Geneva, alive, because of Brissard's hands, which were thick and stained with ink in a way that reminded the inspector of another pair of hands, and Brissard looked at him.

He looked at Brissard.

What passed between them was not communication. It was the recognition by two men of a shared knowledge that neither could speak, a mutual understanding that the air between them was full of names they would not say, and the inspector made his mark beside Brissard's name and moved on, because moving on was the procedure, and the procedure was the only architecture still standing in a city where every other structure—moral, political, personal—had been requisitioned by the Republic and repurposed as scaffolding for the blade.

The widow Varennes came eleventh.

She was smaller than he remembered. This was wrong. She was not smaller. She was the same. He was the one who had changed, who had expanded in some direction that was not physical, who carried more inside his body than his body had been designed to hold, and so by comparison everything outside him had contracted, scale had shifted, the street was narrower and the carts were smaller and the widow Varennes was a diminished figure in a grey dress whose hands were bound with the same cord that bound the schoolteacher's hands and the forger's hands, the Republic not being in the business of bespoke restraints.

She saw him.

He could not avoid this. He was standing with the list. He was the functionary. She knew his name—his real name, not the name he had used in the network, but the name his mother had given him, because the widow Varennes was the person who had recruited him, the origin, the first link in a chain that now terminated here, at a cart, outside a prison, with a list.

She did not speak. She descended from the cart with the care of a woman navigating a step in a dress that was too long, which it was not—it was too short, if anything, its hem muddied, torn on one side—but her body remembered the habit of care, the habit of a life in which hems mattered, in which a step was a step and not a station on the way to a blade, and she placed her bound hands against the side of the cart to steady herself and he saw the ink.

It was still there. Three years later, and the ink was still there, not the same ink—ink did not last, ink faded and was washed away and replaced—but new ink on old stains, an archaeology of work, the sediment of a practice that had continued even in the Conciergerie, because the widow Varennes had been arrested in possession of a press and the press had been seized but the ink had not been seized, the ink had been on her hands and under her nails and
