# The Perdition Depositions

---

## I.

You will understand that I kept a house of particular standards. This was not Abilene. This was not Dodge. The men who came to me were men of the mines, yes, but I maintained linens, I maintained order, and I maintained the health of my girls with a regularity that would shame the practices of most licensed physicians west of the Mississippi. I examined them myself each Sunday. I kept a solution of mercuric chloride for irrigation and insisted upon its application prophylactically rather than therapeutically, which is a distinction, I have found, that even educated men fail to grasp. So when you ask me what I knew of the Krebs woman before she arrived, I will tell you: I knew she was unnecessary.

She had been expelled — this much was common intelligence — from the Women's Medical College of Kentucky for publishing a paper on the vascular anatomy of the uterine cervix so explicit that the journal's compositor refused to set the type. A compositor. A man who daily handled advertisements for Madame Restell's abortifacient pills and Dr. Doddridge's Galvanic Belt for Masculine Debility could not bring himself to arrange the letters of the words she had written about the interior of a woman's body. This tells you everything, I think, about the distance between what a civilization will sell and what it will know.

But I get ahead of myself. You asked me what she did that was unforgivable.

I will tell you what she did. She cured them. Every last one. And then she made it so they couldn't forget how.

---

You want to know about that woman doctor. I will tell you about that woman doctor. I will tell you what she was, which was a drunk and a menace, and what she was not, which was any kind of proper healer. I have attended two hundred and eleven births in this territory. I have turned breech babies in the dark with my own two hands. I have performed podalic version by candlelight — that is when you reach in and find the feet, sir, and I'll thank you not to look at me that way because it is the Lord's own work and I answer to Him for it — and I have lost only nine, which is a rate I will put before any man's record in any hospital in Philadelphia. And she came to Perdition with her instruments and her Latin and her drunk's confidence and told me I was a danger to my patients. That is what she told me. A danger.

She arrived in September. By October the men were sick with it. By November she had the whole town talking about things no decent woman speaks of, and by Christmas she was gone and three men were dead and Mrs. Gault was ruined and here you sit asking me to account for it as though it were some kind of mystery. It is no mystery. She was a judgment sent upon us and I said so from the first.

Now — I will have more tea, if you have it, and then I'll tell you about the day she came.

---

I don't write so good which is why you are writing what I say which I understand. I was deputy to Harlan Crisp who was marshal though he was mostly not marshal if you take my meaning, he was mostly at the Lucky Chance or in his house with a bottle and it was me who kept the peace, which I done. Which I did. I will say she come on the stage. I believe it was September 14 or 15 because the assay office had just opened back up after the diphtheria scare and I was stood outside it when the coach pulled in. She was a tall woman. She was not pretty but she was something. She had a trunk that took two men to lower and when they lowered it wrong she said a word I will not repeat and pulled it the rest of the way herself. Her hands were like a man's hands, large, and her knuckles were scarred across the tops like she had hit something, or someone, more than once.

I heard later she had been run out of her schooling for doing surgery on a woman what didn't need surgery. That was the talk. I did not ask what surgery. It was not my business. I am telling you what I saw, which was a woman getting off the stage with a trunk and a look on her face like she already knew what this place was and had decided to stay anyway. That was September. She was dead by February. That is all I know in the way of a summary and you may ask me the particulars now if you want.

---

## II.

She came by stage, as I have said, and she came sober. This is important because Mrs. Cribbins — the midwife, I assume you have spoken to her, the woman is a font of grievance and misinformation — will have told you she arrived inebriated. Mrs. Cribbins was not present at the stage depot on the fourteenth of September. I was. I had sent one of my girls, Lacey Delacroix — a professional name, naturally, her Christian name was Bertha Mudd of Council Bluffs — I had sent Lacey to collect a shipment of French letters from the express office and I accompanied her because the express agent had a habit of opening parcels addressed to my establishment and subjecting my girls to commentary. So I was there. I saw the Krebs woman descend from the coach.

She wore a traveling suit of dark brown serge, much mended at the cuffs. She carried a medical bag of the Gladstone type and a carpetbag and nothing else. No trunk. Mrs. Cribbins may tell you there was a trunk. There was no trunk. A woman traveling alone by stage from — I understood it was from St. Louis, though others said Chicago — does not bring a trunk because a trunk requires porters and porters require trust and a woman alone trusts nothing she cannot carry herself. This I know from experience.

I will tell you what else I noticed. Her boots. They were cavalry boots, the square-toed pattern, and they were worn through at the sole in the manner of boots that have been walked in far beyond their designed purpose. And the way she scanned the town as she stepped down — not the way a newcomer looks, with apprehension or curiosity, but the way a field surgeon scans a ward. Triage. She was performing triage on Perdition before she had taken two steps into its dust.

She asked the express agent where she might find a room suitable for a medical practice. He laughed at her. She did not alter her expression. She asked again, and he told her there was an empty tent behind the assay office where the last dentist had operated before he was shot over a card debt, and she said, "That will do," and she walked toward it without asking directions, as though she could smell the ether and the blood from where she stood.

---

She come on horseback, not on the stage. I don't care what anyone says. She come on a bay horse that was half dead with lather and she come from the south road, not the coach road, and she could barely stand when she got down from the saddle. I do not say she was drunk. I say she could barely stand. There is a difference. She may have been riding hard for a long time. She may have been ill. I know a drunk woman when I see one and I saw plenty in this town and I am not saying she was drunk on *that* occasion.

She had a trunk. It was strapped across the back of the saddle with hemp rope and a cinch hitch that was done wrong, any horseman could see it, it was a miracle the thing hadn't fallen off in the hills. Two men from the livery helped her lower it and she cursed at them when they handled it rough. This I heard from Jacob Nell who worked the livery. He said she had large hands. That is a detail I recall because I thought to myself when he said it: good. A midwife needs large hands. A woman who calls herself a doctor needs something else entirely, and I told her so later, but I am getting ahead of myself.

She asked after lodgings for a medical practice and was directed to the tent behind the assay office. I did not see this. I was told. I did not meet her until the following week when she had the temerity — the *temerity* — to present herself at my door and ask to observe me attending a birth. She said she wished to understand "local practices." As though my work were some species of folklore. As though twenty-three years of keeping mothers alive were a quaint regional custom she might take notes on.

I told her the door was behind her and the street was beyond it.

---

I don't recall whether it was a horse or the stage. I think it was the stage but I may be wrong. I recall her face and her hands. I recall later she told me or maybe told someone who told me that she had been at two institutions before she come out here and both times it went bad for her. The first time she was let go from a hospital back east for I believe they said she was intemperate which might mean drinking or might mean she said what she thought which in a woman amounts to the same charge. The second time I heard was the surgery business. I did not ask. She set up in the tent and put out a shingle and for two weeks nobody went to see her and then Charlie Buell broke his arm at the stamp mill and they carried him to her because Mrs. Cribbins was in the foothills attending a birth and the company doctor had been dead for six months and she set the arm and Charlie Buell said it was the cleanest set he had ever known. He said she spoke to him the whole time in a voice that was calm and strange and she told him exactly what she was doing as she did it, every movement, so that his mind was occupied with the knowledge and could not attend to the pain.

That was the first I heard her spoken of with anything other than suspicion. It did not last.

---

## III.

The affliction. Yes. You will want to know about the affliction.

It began, as I best understood it, in October, though the men were slow to speak of it and the first reports reached me only through Lacey, who heard it from a client who could not complete his transaction and wept about it afterward. He was a man of forty, a shift boss at the Consolation mine, and he told Lacey it had happened to him once before — with Mrs. Gault, not at my establishment — and that the paralysis had started in his legs and climbed through his groin and seized him so completely that he could not move from the waist down for nearly two hours. He had to be carried out. He said it was as though his body had turned to plaster. He said Mrs. Gault had watched him from across the room with no expression on her face and had not called for help.

I should be precise. When I say paralysis I mean a genuine motor failure, not the ordinary failure of nerve or desire that afflicts men in certain conditions. I examined one of the men myself — this was later, after Eulalie had begun her work and the talk was everywhere — I examined him because he came to my house seeking a girl and was struck down in the parlor before he reached the stairs. His legs were rigid, the muscles in sustained contracture, the reflexes — when I tested with a hatpin, which is crude but effective — entirely absent below the iliac crest. This was not performance. This was not shame. This was a conversion paralysis of the kind described in Charcot's published lectures, which I had read, though I could not tell you how they came to be in my possession, and it is not germane to your inquiry.

It happened only in association with Mrs. Gault. This was the peculiar and, to the men, the enraging thing. They could perform adequately elsewhere — at my house, with their wives, in whatever solitary arrangements men make for themselves — but the moment they attempted to visit Mrs. Gault, the moment that specific desire organized itself and directed them toward that specific woman, their legs turned to stone.

---

The Lord's judgment is not mysterious to those with ears to hear. Those men were visiting a woman outside the bonds of matrimony — a widow, yes, and there is no law against a widow, but there is a law above the law and it was being enforced. I said so. I said so from the first. When Mike Trautwein was carried out of that woman's house like a plank I said to his wife, I said, Margaret, there is your proof and you should get on your knees and thank the Almighty for it.

Mrs. Gault. You want to know about Mrs. Gault. She was a harlot with a house. That is what she was. She did not work at the madam's establishment, no, she was too fine for that, she had a house left to her by Mr. Gault who died of silicosis in the first year of the mine, and she received men there in what she called her parlor as though the word could sanctify the act. She was not beautiful. She was — I cannot describe it. She was the kind of woman that men lose their minds over and other women cannot see why and I include myself in that. She was thin and she had a scar on her neck from I know not what and she did not speak much. She was not friendly. She did not come to church. She did not come to the store unless she had to and then she came early, before other women were about, and she purchased what she needed and left.

I believe she was a witch. I do not say that lightly. I say it as a woman of faith who has read her Bible and knows that there are forces in this world that —

No. You are right. You did not ask me what I believe. You asked me what happened. Very well.

---

Mrs. Gault. I seen her some. She kept to herself. She was of some quality, I mean to say she had a manner about her that was above the run of women in this town though I could not tell you where she come from. Her husband was Thomas Gault who I knew slightly, a quiet man, died in the first cave-in before they shored up the lower drift. After that she was alone. She did not work, not in the way the madam's girls worked, and I believe the talk about her was crueler than it ought to have been. Men visited her. That is true. Whether she received payment I do not know and did not inquire as it was not a crime as such without a formal complaint.

The sickness in the men, well. I first heard of it from Otto Pfaff who come to me one morning white as paper and said he couldn't feel his legs and he thought he was dying and I said Otto where were you last night and he said he had been at Mrs. Gault's and I said well go to the doctor, meaning Mrs. Krebs, and he gave me a look like I had told him to go to the devil. By October there were four or five men I knew of who had the same thing. All in connection with Mrs. Gault. Only Mrs. Gault. They would go to her door and their legs would lock up. Or they would sit in her parlor and it would take them. Or they would get so far as the — well. It took them at different points but always in that house and always with that intent.

I will tell you what was odd. She did not turn them away. She did not bolt her door. She did not seem surprised. It was as if she expected it or had seen it before or did not care. That woman sat in the middle of a town of paralyzed men and did not change her expression one degree.

Nobody asked the Krebs woman to come. I want to be clear about that. No man went to her for it and Mrs. Gault sure as hell did not send for her. She come on her own. She heard about it — I suppose the whole town had heard about it by November — and she walked up to Mrs. Gault's house and knocked on the door and said she was a physician and she believed she could help.

I know this because I was stood across the street and I saw it. Mrs. Gault opened the door about halfway. She looked at the Krebs woman for a long time. Then she opened it the rest of the way and the Krebs woman went in and the door closed behind her.

That was the beginning, I reckon. Of all of it.

---

## IV.

I will describe the treatments as I understood them, because you will not get a coherent account from Mrs. Cribbins, who was not present for any of them, and you will get only fragments from the deputy, who I believe was too embarrassed to inquire closely and may, for reasons I will leave to your inference, have had personal cause to avoid the subject.
