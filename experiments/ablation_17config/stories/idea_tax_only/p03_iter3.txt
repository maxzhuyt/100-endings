# The Mud Shelf

It was 1963. The canyon was the Paria, southern Utah, where the rock is the color of arterial blood when it's dry and goes black when it's wet like something ashamed of itself. There had been no rain for eleven days, and then there had been all of it.

My aunt's name was Delia Byrne. She had been a prostitute in Flagstaff for nineteen years and had retired the way a horse retires, which is to say someone decided she was done. The taxidermist was called Hollis Greer. He had one arm. The left one ended four inches below the shoulder in a knot of skin that looked, Delia said later, like the tied end of a sausage. That was later. At first she said worse things.

The mule was dead. It had broken both front legs in the flash and then the water had held it under for a while and then set it on a mud shelf about eight feet by twelve, a ledge the canyon had coughed up like a table. Delia and Hollis were on the shelf with the mule. I was six feet above them on a rock ledge, dry, untouched, looking down.

I was seventeen. I was on my way to a seminary in Provo. Delia was taking me because no one else would. Hollis had been traveling the same road with a cart of supplies for his shop in Kanab, and when the sky opened we all ended up in the same drainage, the way water decides things for you.

I am going to tell you something about grace now. I have used that word maybe ten times in my life and each time it came out of my mouth tasting like a stone I'd sucked on too long, smooth and mineral and not quite mine. I don't know that I believe in grace. I know that I saw it. I am not sure those two things need to be the same.

---

The water was shin-high on the shelf and not moving much, just sitting there like it planned to stay. The mule lay on its side with its legs pointing east and its mouth open. Its tongue had gone gray already. The saddlebags were still attached and half-submerged. The sky was white. There was no wind.

Delia noticed Hollis before she noticed much else. She was waist-deep when she'd crawled onto the shelf, and she was pressing water out of her skirt with both hands, and she looked up and saw him sitting against the canyon wall with his one arm holding his knees and his stump just resting there in the open air like it didn't know it was supposed to be hidden.

She said he smelled like formaldehyde and failure. She said it straight to his face the way you'd tell someone the time. Hollis looked at her for a long moment and said at least his failures were preserved. He said he had some in a jar in Kanab that still looked fresh after six years, which was more than could be said for her, although he did not say *her*.

I was above them. I had my Bible and my suitcase, both dry. I told them I could see a seam in the canyon wall to the northwest that might be a trail out and Delia said if I had anything useful to contribute she'd be sure to faint from the surprise.

They went back and forth. I will not reproduce all of it. What I can tell you is that within the first twenty minutes Hollis had made reference to Delia's profession twice without using the word directly, relying instead on a metaphor about rental properties and another about the hourly rate for unskilled labor. Delia had asked him whether he practiced on the animals because he couldn't get a living woman to hold still long enough or whether the preference had come first. Hollis said nothing to that for a while.

Their cruelty was specific. That is what I want you to understand. It was not the loose, general cruelty of people who don't see each other. Each knew exactly where to press, the way you know where the bruise is on a piece of fruit by the color. She saw that his missing arm was the thing he carried not with pain but with a rehearsed indifference that was worse than pain. He saw that her retirement was not something she'd chosen but something that had been done to her, a door shut from the other side, and that the shutting had broken something in her understanding of her own aliveness.

They attacked these things in each other because the alternative was to sit quietly with a dead mule and rising water and the knowledge that the boy above them on the dry ledge was the closest thing either of them had to a rescuer and the boy was seventeen years old and reading the book of Job and weeping.

I was weeping. I want to be clear about that. Not from fear. From the size of things. The canyon was three hundred feet deep and the sky was white and the two people below me were destroying each other with words and I could not climb down and I could not help and I could not stop listening. It was the first time I understood that there are positions in the world from which you can see everything and do nothing, and that some people build entire theologies out of calling that position good.

---

The water was alkaline. The whole drainage was. It tasted like licking a battery, which I knew because I'd put my hand in it when I first scrambled up the wall. That taste stayed on my fingers for an hour. I cannot imagine what it did in the gut.

Hollis was the first. Maybe two hours after the bickering settled into a sullen quiet. He stood up suddenly, and his face did something I had never seen a face do, a kind of total surrender of the muscles, and he walked three steps toward the downstream edge of the shelf and he did not make it.

I will say what happened because I said I would not look away in the telling even though I looked away on the ledge. He lost control of his bowels completely and he was standing when it happened and he was facing Delia and there was no mystery about what was occurring. His trousers went dark. The sound was the sound a body makes when it has been ambushed by itself. He did not fall but he bent forward at the waist as if he'd been hit, and his one hand went to his knee, and his stump moved in the air the way a hand moves when it's reaching for a wall that isn't there.

Delia turned her back. That is the thing I remember. She did not laugh. She did not speak. She turned her back so completely and so fast that it was itself a kind of violence, the violence of decency when decency has no room to operate. The shelf was eight feet by twelve. There was nowhere for her gaze to go that wasn't him or the mule or the water or the boy above, and she chose the water.

Hollis cleaned himself as well as a man could with one arm and alkaline water and no privacy. It took a long time. I could hear the water against his legs. When he was done he sat down against the canyon wall and he said nothing.

Then, forty minutes later or an hour — the light moved but I cannot say how much — Delia stood. And I saw the same thing on her face. The same surrender. She made it one step farther than he had, which is to say she made it to the edge of the shelf, where the water was deepest, and it happened to her standing in water up to her shins with her back to both of us, but the sound carried, and the water carried, and on a mud shelf eight feet by twelve there is no privacy, there is only the direction people choose to point their eyes.

Hollis did not turn away. I have thought about this for fifty-one years. Delia had given him the gift of her back, the pretense of not-witnessing, and he could have returned it. He did not. He watched her, and his face carried nothing — not amusement, not pity, not disgust. He watched her the way you watch weather. She was a thing happening, and he was present for it, and his presence was neither punishment nor mercy. It was just witness.

When she finished she stood in the water for a long time without turning around. The canyon was quiet. The water moved.

She turned around and she looked at him and he looked at her and I saw something pass between them that I could not name at seventeen and cannot name now except to call it a first clearing, the way you clear a field not by planting but by burning, and what is left is black and bare and capable of receiving seed.

That was the first time I saw one of them look at the other without disgust, and I mistook it for something lesser than it was. I thought it was exhaustion. I thought they had simply run out of cruelty. But that was not it. Cruelty is a renewable resource. What they had run out of was the need for it.

---

The mule's saddlebags held three things of use: a leather case with a folding knife and a length of cord, a can of peaches that had been dented but not opened by the flood, and a small brown bottle of Hamlin's laudanum tincture, three-quarters full. I could see Hollis register the bottle. His eyes moved across it and his breathing changed in a way I recognized even from six feet above, the breathing of a man who has met something he has a history with.

It was Delia who found the bottle, pulling it from the sodden leather with her thick red fingers. She held it up and looked at it and then she looked at Hollis and something passed between them again, some negotiation completed entirely in the eyes.

She set the bottle on the flattest patch of mud between them. Neither of them reached for it. It sat there like a third person.

The dead mule was the only other resource. It was Hollis who said it first. He said the hide could be cut into strips that would be useful. He did not say for what. Useful was as far as he went, but his voice had changed. When he was insulting Delia his voice had been high and thin and carried an edge like a man who'd spent decades sharpening his wit because it was the only weapon that didn't require two hands. When he talked about the mule his voice dropped and leveled out and became the voice of a man who knew something, and knowing it gave him ground to stand on.

He told her where to cut. Along the belly, following the line where the hair thinned. Short strokes, he said, and keep the blade angle shallow, you're not butchering, you're undressing it. She kneeled next to the mule with the folding knife and he kneeled across from her with his stump braced against the mule's flank for leverage. He pressed his weight into the carcass to keep it from rolling while she opened it.

I watched from above. I had read Genesis. I had read the part about the coats of skins the Lord God made for Adam and Eve after the shame of their knowledge, and I had pictured something clean, an act of divine tailoring. I had not pictured the smell. The mule's insides came into the air all at once, a wave of heat and sweetness and something deeper and older than sweetness, and Delia gagged but did not stop, and Hollis pressed his stump into the flank and murmured instructions like a man speaking to a horse. *There, now follow the fascia, that white line, see it. Don't force it. The knife will tell you where to go if you let it.*

His voice when he instructed her was the finest thing about him. I mean that. It was patient without condescension. It was knowledgeable without performance. He had spent his life opening animals, cataloguing their interiors, and that knowledge came out of him now not as grotesquerie but as tenderness, the tenderness of a man who has studied the body's architecture and found it worthy of respect.

Delia's hands were quick and sure. This surprised no one more than Hollis, who had expected to spend the afternoon correcting her, and instead found himself watching a woman who understood blades and pressure and the logic of flesh from her own education, which was different from his but adjacent in ways neither of them would have acknowledged on a dry road in sunlight. They both knew what it was to make a living from what others would not touch. I think they began to see that in each other over the mule, though they would have cut out their tongues before saying so.

She peeled two broad pieces of hide and laid them on the mud to drain. The underlayer she cut into strips that could be used for lashing or binding. Hollis directed her to the stomach, which she opened, and the contents spilled and she did stop then, she stopped and breathed through her mouth for a moment, and Hollis said, without mockery, *I know. Keep going.* The stomach lining itself he said could hold water and filter out the worst of the alkali if they stretched it across the mouth of the peach can and poured slowly.

It worked. Not well, but it worked. They drank in turns, tilting the can to each other's mouths, because his one hand was filthy and her hands were filthy and neither of them could hold the can and wipe their mouth simultaneously. So they drank like children. Or like the very old. Each tilting the can while the other swallowed.

The laudanum they did not discuss. It sat on the mud. Both of them looked at it when they thought the other wasn't looking and both of them were always wrong about that.

---

Delia's wound became visible in the late afternoon when the exertion of cutting shifted her dress and the fabric pulled away from her left side and the cloth underneath was not the color it should have been. She had been carrying it all day, a gash along her ribs from something in the flood, a branch or a fencepost or a stone edge moving at the speed of water and water moves at whatever speed it wants.

She did not mention it. Hollis saw it. He went quiet in a way that was different from his previous silences, which had been the silence of a man reloading. This was the silence of a man recalculating.

He said, *Show me.*

She said something about how she'd shown enough men enough things to know where it led.

He said, *Show me the wound, Delia.* He used her name. I don't think he'd used it before.

She looked at him for a long time and then she pulled the fabric away from her side and the gash was six inches long and deep enough to show the white layer beneath the red and the edges had gone a color I did not like. I was above them and the angle was such that I could see the wound plainly and I could see Hollis's face as he looked at it and what I saw on his face was not pity or revulsion or clinical detachment. It was recognition. He looked at the wound the way he had looked at the mule: as a body that needed tending, yes, but more than that, as a thing that was hurt and still alive and therefore demanded his specific attention.

He told me — the first time he'd spoken to me directly in hours — to tear a strip from my shirt and drop it down. I did. He told Delia to soak it in the filtered water and then he told her to sit against the canyon wall and he knelt beside her.

He had one hand. The work was impossible and he did it anyway. He pressed the wet cloth to the wound and held it there and she hissed and he said nothing, just held it, and with his stump he braced himself against the wall so he wouldn't lose his balance. The stump was perhaps eight inches from her face. She could see the scar tissue, the puckered seam where they'd closed him up. She looked at it. She looked at it the way he had looked at her wound.

After a while he said he needed to bind it and that required wrapping a strip of mule hide around her torso which required her to lift her arms which required her to trust him not to lose his balance and fall against the wound and this small chain of necessary trust took longer to negotiate than the actual binding. They argued about it. He said lift your arms. She said if he pressed too hard she'd knock him into the water. He said he'd managed seventeen years with one arm and hadn't fallen yet. She said there was still time.

But she lifted her arms. He wrapped the hide around her ribs, passing it from his hand around her back and catching it again on the other side, a maneuver that required him to lean close enough that his face was near her neck. I could see this from above. I could see the moment he paused there, not long, a half-second, his face near her skin, and whether he smelled her or simply rested in the proximity of another living body I cannot say. He finished the binding and tied it with a knot he managed against his own knee, pulling the end with his teeth.

He sat back. She lowered her arms. The hide was dark against her dress. She looked at him and said, with no inflection I could read, *You're gentle for a man who works with dead things.*

He said, *Dead things are easy to be gentle with. They don't flinch.*

She said, *I didn't flinch.*

He said nothing.
