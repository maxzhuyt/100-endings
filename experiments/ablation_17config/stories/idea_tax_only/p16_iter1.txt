# The Lost Willoughby Materials

## A Partial Recovery

---

### A Note on Provenance

The materials collected here were acquired in 2019 from the estate of Mrs. Dorothy Calloway (née Willoughby) of Batavia, Illinois, following her death at the age of ninety-one. Mrs. Calloway was the granddaughter of Elaine Willoughby (1849–1932), who served as an attendant nurse at Bellevue Place Sanitarium in Batavia from 1874 to 1882. Among the effects were three tin boxes containing: (1) a series of interview transcripts, conducted by an unidentified interviewer in 1911, when Mrs. Willoughby was sixty-two; (2) personal letters retained by Mrs. Willoughby, including correspondence with individuals connected to Bellevue Place and its most prominent patient; (3) photocopied institutional records, some bearing handwritten annotations in Mrs. Willoughby's hand; and (4) a notebook, partially water-damaged, containing what appear to be drafts of scenes Mrs. Willoughby composed late in life, reconstructing events she could not have witnessed firsthand. These last items she labeled, in pencil, "Fillings for the gaps."

The materials are presented here in an order that reflects the editors' best reconstruction of chronological and thematic coherence. Where lacunae exist, they are noted. Where Mrs. Willoughby's later reconstructions fill those lacunae, they are identified as such.

No account of this kind is complete. No account of this kind is neutral. The decision to publish was not made lightly. It was made because the alternative—another century of silence from a woman paid to be silent—seemed worse.

—*The Editors, 2022*

## I.

**[Transcript excerpt. Interview with Elaine Willoughby, 1911. Interviewer unidentified. Transcript appears to begin mid-conversation; preceding pages missing or never transcribed.]**

—and I would have been twenty-six. Just twenty-six. I'd had two years at Cook County, on the surgical ward, which was mostly holding men's hands while they died of sepsis, and before that I'd done a year with Dr. Henshaw's practice in Aurora, which was mostly holding women's hands while they delivered children they did not want, and none of that, I should tell you, none of it, had prepared me for Bellevue Place. Not because Bellevue Place was a hospital. It was not a hospital. I understood that on the first day. A hospital is organized around the pretense that its patients will leave. Bellevue Place was organized around the pretense that its patients had chosen to stay.

**Q: How did you come to be assigned there?**

My aunt knew Dr. Patterson's wife. That was the whole of it. I was told there was a position for a sensible young woman who did not gossip, and I needed the income, and I was told the work was light. The patients were women of a certain class. Nervous cases, most of them. Exhaustion, neurasthenia, melancholia. The husbands paid handsomely and visited on Sundays. The building was clean. The grounds were beautiful. It was, by every measure I then possessed, a pleasant situation.

**Q: And you were not told who your patient was.**

I was told the morning I arrived. Dr. Patterson drew me into his office and informed me that I would be attending primarily to one patient, a woman of some public reputation, and that discretion was paramount. He used the word "paramount" three times in six minutes. I counted. Then he told me her name. I don't recall what I said. I recall that my hands went cold.

**Q: You knew who she was.**

Everyone in Illinois knew who she was. Everyone in America. She was the president's widow. She was the madwoman who'd been dragged out of the Grand Pacific Hotel by her own son and committed in open court. The papers had been quite thorough. I'd read the testimony. The hotel manager said she'd tried to descend in the elevator wearing only her nightgown and a shawl. Robert—her son—said she believed an Indian spirit was pulling wires from her eyes, and that a man was living inside the walls of her room, and that the city of Chicago was going to burn again, specifically to consume her. He said she had spent six hundred dollars on lace curtains she did not hang. He said she carried bonds and currency sewn into her petticoats, that she would not be parted from them, that the weight of the securities under her skirts caused her to list to one side when she walked. The doctors testified—six of them, I believe, though only one had examined her—that she was unsound. The jury deliberated ten minutes.

So yes. I knew who she was. I knew she was mad. I walked to her room expecting—I suppose I expected something theatrical. A woman talking to the air. A woman in torn clothing. What I expected was the woman the newspapers had described, which is to say I expected an illustration, not a person.

**Q: And what did you find?**

A closed door. I knocked. A voice from inside said—and I am giving you her exact words, because I have never forgotten them—she said: "If you are Dr. Patterson, I am indisposed. If you are staff, enter and state your business and your name, in that order. If you are my son, you may go to hell by whatever route is most convenient."

**[Laughter from interviewer.]**

Yes, it is amusing. It was not amusing in the moment. Or rather, it was—I did nearly laugh—but the amusement sat on top of something else that took me a moment to identify. She had, in three sentences, sorted the entire universe of people who might knock on her door into categories and dispensed with all of them. It was a hostess's instinct. A way of controlling a room before anyone had entered it.

I opened the door. The room was—well, it was not a cell. Dr. Patterson was very clear about that, and in fairness, he was not wrong. There was a carpet, somewhat threadbare but patterned. A bed with an actual headboard, walnut I think, not an iron frame. A window with curtains—yellow, a pale butter yellow—that had been drawn but did not quite meet in the center, so a strip of afternoon light fell across the floor. There was a wardrobe. A washstand with a porcelain basin. A small table with a vase of dry flowers that no one had replaced. Two chairs, one upholstered. No writing desk. I noticed the absence of the desk before I noticed anything about the woman, because the room was so clearly organized around concealing what it was, and the missing desk broke the illusion. Every other piece of furniture said *guest room.* The absence of the desk said *You are not to write unsupervised letters.*

She was sitting in the upholstered chair, very straight, her hands in her lap. She was stout—that was the first thing I registered, because the newspapers always called her stout, as though it were a symptom. She wore a dark dress, bombazine or something near it, with a high collar, impeccably buttoned. Her hair was pinned. Her face was—how do I describe her face? It was a face that had been beautiful and knew it had been beautiful and was adjusting, with imperfect grace, to the loss. She had large eyes, very clear, and a mouth that pulled slightly downward on the left side, which gave even her neutral expression an air of dissatisfaction. She was sixty years old. She looked at me for a long time.

She said: "You are very young. They must imagine I require someone energetic."

I said my name and told her I was to be her attendant.

She said: "My attendant. Yes. That is the word Dr. Patterson prefers. It has a whiff of the lady's maid about it. So much more palatable than 'guard,' don't you think?"

She held my gaze while she said this, and what I saw in her expression was a kind of test. Not of my sympathies—she did not yet care about my sympathies. It was a test of my face. She was watching to see how I absorbed the word "guard," whether I flinched, whether I denied it, whether I fumbled. She was calibrating me. I recognized the technique because I had seen Dr. Henshaw use it: you say something slightly too sharp for the social context and observe how the other person manages the discomfort. It tells you whether they will be useful.

I said nothing. That was evidently the correct response, or at least a tolerable one, because she smoothed her skirt and gestured to the other chair and said: "Sit down, then. You might as well be comfortable while you watch me."

I sat. She began talking—not to me, exactly, but at me, in the way that women of her generation and her station addressed servants, with a flow of commentary that assumed an audience without requiring a response. She told me that the flowers were dead and had been dead for a week and that she had requested fresh ones. She told me the curtains did not close properly and that she had asked for pins. She told me that breakfast had been adequate but the coffee criminal, that she had been promised writing paper and received only four sheets, that the window in the hallway outside her room had a draft she could feel under the door.

These were complaints. But they were also, I understood even then, a map. She was showing me every place where the institution's surface cracked, every point where the fiction of *comfortable rest home* gave way to the reality of *confinement.* She was doing this within the first ten minutes of our acquaintance. Not because she trusted me—she trusted no one, and she was right not to—but because the recitation itself was an act of resistance. Every complaint was a small assertion: *I am a person who notices things. I am a person with standards. I am not what this room has been designed to make me.*

I listened. I said, "I'll ask about the flowers."

She looked at me again, and something shifted—not warmth, but a slight recalibration, a fractional adjustment in the way she held her shoulders.

She said: "You will find, Miss Willoughby, that asking is the easy part."

**Q: You said earlier that you knew within the hour she was not entirely well. What did you observe that led to that conclusion?**

I've thought about this for thirty-six years, and I want to be precise, because precision matters in this particular case, given what she was accused of and what was done to her on the basis of those accusations.

She was not raving. She was not incoherent. She was not disoriented. She knew the day, the date, the name of the president. She could converse with intelligence and wit. She read. She remembered conversations verbatim—better than I could. Her mind, in its operations, was sharp, sometimes laceratingly so.

But there was a—I don't know how to express this without sounding as though I am pathologizing grief, which is the very thing I am trying not to do. There was a quality of *vigilance* about her that exceeded the situation. She watched the door. Not glanced at it—watched it, the way a woman watches a door when she believes somebody is going to come through it. Her gaze returned to it every few minutes. And her hands—during that first conversation, she spoke with great composure, great rhetorical control, but her left hand, the one in her lap, was working at the fabric of her skirt, pressing and releasing a fold, pressing and releasing, in a rhythm that had nothing to do with what she was saying. The hand was operating on a different clock than the voice.

And once, only once during that first meeting—she was telling me about a particular slight, a request for a newspaper that had been denied—she stopped mid-sentence. Not a pause for breath. A stop. Her mouth remained slightly open. Her eyes fixed on a point over my left shoulder, which was the wall, which was yellow wallpaper of a faded floral pattern. She stared at the wall for perhaps four seconds. Then she resumed the sentence at the exact syllable where she had stopped, as though nothing had happened. She did not acknowledge the interruption. Neither did I.

Four seconds. It was only four seconds. But in those four seconds I saw something behind her face that looked like—I want to say terror, but that's not quite right. It was *listening.* She was listening to something I could not hear.

I knew within the hour that she was not entirely well. I also knew within the hour that this fact had almost nothing to do with why she was there.

**Q: That is a significant claim.**

It is a factual observation. Her son did not commit her because she sometimes stared at walls. He committed her because she would not stop spending his inheritance and she would not stop embarrassing him and she would not stop being *visible,* publicly and inconveniently visible, and in 1875 a man had the legal machinery to convert embarrassment into diagnosis. Robert Lincoln did not need his mother to be ill. He needed her to be *manageable.* The illness was the instrument, not the cause.

But I want to be clear. She *was* ill. I will not do her the disservice of pretending otherwise. The question was never whether Mary Todd Lincoln was well. The question was who got to decide what her illness meant, and what it permitted them to do to her.

**Q: And that question—you felt it was yours to consider? As a nurse?**

I was twenty-six. I was nobody. I was being paid four dollars a week to sit in a room with the most famous madwoman in America and make sure she did not swallow lye. No. I did not think it was my question. I thought it was simply the weather I was living in. You don't decide whether to consider the weather. You put on your coat.

## II.

**[Transcript resumes. Elaine Willoughby, 1911.]**

—think it was the third week. Perhaps the fourth. Dr. Patterson had scheduled what he called a "conversational assessment," which meant he sat in the morning room with Mrs. Lincoln and took tea and observed her behavior and wrote notes afterward that would determine her privileges for the following month. Whether she might walk the grounds. Whether she might receive visitors. Whether the four sheets of writing paper might become six.

I was present for this because I was always present. That was the nature of the position. She was never unattended—never unwatched, to use her word—except when she slept, and I suspected she did not always sleep.

The morning room was the finest room in the house, the room Dr. Patterson used when the patients' families visited. Real china. Drapes that were not merely functional but decorative. A pianoforte that no one played. He had set the table with a lace cloth, and there was a small arrangement of forced flowers—hothouse roses, pink and slightly too open—and when Mrs. Lincoln entered and saw the table, I watched her clock every detail the way a general clocks terrain. The china pattern. The quality of the lace. The flowers. She was assessing what performance was expected of her and calibrating accordingly.

She entered, and she was—transformed is too strong. She was *adjusted.* The quality of her movement changed. She crossed the room with a kind of deliberate graciousness, not hurrying but not dawdling, the walk of a woman who knows that a drawing room is a stage and the entrance is the first line of the performance. She extended her hand to Dr. Patterson—not to shake, but to be received, the way a woman of rank permits her hand to be taken—and she said: "Doctor. How kind. What lovely roses."

Her voice was different. Not different in content, but in pitch and placement. It sat higher in her throat, lighter, with an almost musical lilt that I had never heard in the room upstairs, where her voice was lower and flatter and carried the weight of a woman too exhausted for ornament. This was the voice she had used in Washington. I was certain of it. This was the voice that had received ambassadors.

She sat. She accepted tea. She asked after Mrs. Patterson's health—she had somehow learned that Mrs. Patterson suffered from rheumatic headaches, and she offered a remedy involving camphor and rosemary that her own physician had recommended, and the way she said "my own physician" quietly reminded Patterson that she inhabited a world in which one *had* a personal physician, in which medical care was a service one selected rather than a regime one endured.

Patterson was charmed. He was not a stupid man—I want to be fair to him, as I am trying to be fair to all of them—but he was susceptible to a particular kind of female social performance because he had been trained to read its presence as evidence of mental health.
