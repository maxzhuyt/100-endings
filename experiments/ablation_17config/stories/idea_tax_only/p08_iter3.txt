# The Pratfall Prophecy of Mx. Jean Delacroix

---

## I.

The cat's name was Gérard, and he had learned, in the way of all creatures who share quarters with inevitability, to sleep with one eye open.

Jean Delacroix stood before the bathroom mirror in their apartment on the Rue des Capucins, fourth floor, no elevator, the kind of Lyon walkup where the radiators clanked like prisoners tapping messages through stone walls, and studied a face they were still learning to claim. They had been up since five-forty. The binding had taken three attempts. The first was too tight — a punishment, not a garment — and the second too loose, a concession they hadn't authorized their hands to make, so the third attempt found the narrow country between suffocation and surrender, and they pressed their palms flat against their chest and breathed once, deeply, testing whether the architecture of the morning would hold.

"Elle," Jean said to the mirror. Then: "Il." Then, with the exhalation that always accompanied the truest word, the one that still felt like jumping from a height: "*Iel.*"

The face in the mirror did not change. It never did. It was a good face, objectively — Salomé had once described it as "the face of a nineteenth-century poet who has been told a very good joke" — but Jean could not stop auditing it for evidence. Evidence of what, they could not have said precisely. Legitimacy, maybe. Some visible proof that the inside and the outside had reached a détente. Their hair was cut short on the sides and long on top, a style they had chosen from a magazine photograph of a person who seemed to know, with a savage and effortless certainty, who they were. Jean had taken the photograph to the salon on Rue Mercière. The stylist had done an immaculate job. Jean had looked in the salon mirror and seen a stranger who might, with time, become a relative.

They reached for the comb on the shelf above the sink. Their elbow caught the mug — the blue one, the one Salomé had bought at the marché aux puces with LYON'S #1 GENDER DISASTER hand-painted on it in gold nail polish, a gift that had made Jean laugh until they couldn't breathe and then cry in the shower afterward because the laughter had felt too close to an admission — and the mug leapt from the shelf with the resigned determination of an object that has always known its fate, struck the edge of the sink, and shattered across the tile floor into five clean pieces.

Jean stood very still.

There was a theory — they had read it in a book, or perhaps several books, or perhaps they had assembled it themselves from the salvage of too many late-night internet spirals — that the body carries knowledge the conscious mind refuses. That the muscles and tendons and the soft dumb meat of the limbs will act out what the psyche cannot say. Jean had been clumsy their entire life, but the clumsiness had accelerated in the last year, the year of the new pronouns, the new name, the new way of walking into a room and saying *this is what I am, please see me, I'm sorry, I'm sorry for asking,* and they did not want to think about what that acceleration meant, so they thought about it constantly, an underground river eroding the limestone of every other thought.

They stepped around the shards, caught their hip on the doorframe — the doorframe they had passed through twice daily for two years, the doorframe whose exact width their body knew the way a tongue knows the inside of a mouth, and yet — and stumbled into the hallway, where Gérard had, against all better judgment, positioned himself in the precise center of the runner rug for his seven o'clock bath.

Jean stepped on Gérard.

Gérard screamed.

It was not a meow. It was not a yowl. It was the sound of a small French cat discovering that the universe is not only indifferent but *specifically hostile,* and it echoed through the apartment with the moral authority of a cathedral bell. Jean lurched sideways, overcorrected, caught the hall table, and sent a stack of unopened mail — bills, a postcard from their mother that they had been avoiding for nine days, a flyer advertising a drag brunch they desperately wanted to attend and could not explain why they were afraid to — cascading to the floor.

From the bedroom, underneath the duvet that she had colonized completely: "Jean? Ça va?"

"Oui, oui, everything's fine, everything is —"

A framed photograph — the two of them at the Fête des Lumières, Salomé's chin on Jean's shoulder, both of them made spectral and gorgeous by projected light — detached from the wall with the gentleness of a leaf falling in October and landed face-down on the hallway floor.

Jean closed their eyes.

*The self,* they thought, standing amid the wreckage of a Tuesday morning, *is not a destination. The self is a negotiation between what you know and what you are willing to survive knowing, and the body is the table where the negotiation happens, and my table has a broken leg, and everything slides off it.*

They opened their eyes. They picked up Gérard, who accepted this indignity with the stiff fury of the wronged. They held him against their chest — against the binder, against the ribs that held the lungs that held the breath that kept the whole trembling enterprise aloft — and they whispered, "I know. I'm sorry. I know."

Gérard purred grudgingly.

Jean put on the shirt. The shirt was a good shirt. It was linen, slightly oversized, the color of a twilight they had watched from the Fourvière hill the night they told Salomé everything, and they buttoned it with fingers that trembled only slightly, and they tucked it in, and they looked at themselves in the hallway mirror — the one that was still on the wall, the survivor — and they thought: *You again.*

Salomé appeared in the bedroom doorway. She was wearing Jean's old university sweatshirt, the one that had, through some alchemy neither of them examined too closely, become hers. Her hair was a psalm of disorder. She was holding a coffee cup in each hand, and she looked at Jean — at the broken mug in the bathroom, at the mail on the floor, at Gérard's expression of moral devastation — and she said, "The baguette place on Pentes Croix-Rousse?"

"The baguette place on Pentes Croix-Rousse," Jean agreed.

"Okay." She kissed them on the cheek, on the hinge of the jaw where the skin was softest, a kiss so practiced and so deliberate that it constituted a thesis statement. "Put shoes on. Both of them, this time."

"That was *once.*"

"It was twice."

"The second time doesn't count because —"

"Shoes, Jean."

Jean put on shoes. Both of them. They tied the laces with the concentration of a person disarming a bomb, because everything — the laces, the shoes, the hallway, the morning, the woman holding coffee in the doorway — was something they did not believe they were allowed to keep, and their hands had a way of proving them right.

## II.

The patisserie was called Maison Vernal, and it occupied the ground floor of a sixteenth-century building on a street so narrow that sunlight entered it only by appointment. The front window was ancient, hand-blown, full of the small imperfections that made old glass look like it was remembering something. Behind it, every morning, Madame Ouédraogo arranged her éclairs and tartes aux fraises and religieuses and the tiny, architecturally improbable croquembouches that she built for weekend display with the seriousness of a woman constructing arguments for the existence of God.

Jean loved this shop with a love that was structural, load-bearing, built into the foundations of who they had become.

"It was right here," Jean said, standing inside the door, one hand holding a baguette they had purchased ostensibly for tonight's dinner and were currently using as a pointer, a baton, a conductor's wand for an orchestra of memory. "Right *here,* Sal, by the éclair case, you had that — what was it — the Paris-Brest, and there was choux pastry cream on your lip, right here —" They touched their own lip. "— and I thought, if I don't kiss this person right now I will die, I will actually medically die, my organs will shut down in sequence, and so I —"

"You missed," Salomé said. She was leaning against the far counter, arms folded, smiling in the way she smiled when Jean was being exactly themselves: an involuntary, slightly helpless widening, as if her face could not contain the specific thing Jean did to her composure. "You missed my mouth entirely and kissed my chin."

"I did *not* miss. I made a *strategic choice.* The chin is an underrated —"

"You were shaking," Salomé said, and the smile changed, deepened, became a thing with roots. "Your whole body was shaking, and you kissed my chin, and then you apologized, and then you did it again, properly, and you tasted like almond cream."

"And you tasted like choux."

"And I tasted like choux."

Jean was glowing. They were standing in the narrow aisle between the display case and the window, the baguette aloft, their free hand painting the memory in the air between them, and they were so full of the remembered architecture of that first kiss — the flour dust in the light, Salomé's eyelashes, the small startled sound she'd made, like a bird discovering flight — that they did not notice the baguette's trajectory until it made contact with the rotating display rack on the counter to their left.

The rack was a tiered affair, five levels, each shelf holding a careful arrangement of Madame Ouédraogo's éclairs — chocolate, coffee, pistachio, rose, and, on the top tier, the salted caramel éclairs that she had been perfecting for thirty years and considered not pastries but *children.* The baguette struck the rack at the second tier. The rack did not fall immediately. It considered its options. It swayed with the deliberative patience of a tree choosing a direction, and then it committed — southward, toward the window — and the top three tiers emptied their éclairs onto the marble counter in a landslide of choux and crème pâtissière that swept, with the gathering momentum of historical inevitability, into the large metal tray where the morning's croissants had been cooling, and the tray — ovoid, unanchored, buttered on its underside — became a sled.

Jean watched, baguette still raised, as the tray of croissants sailed across the counter, collected a small ceramic bust of Paul Bocuse that served as Madame Ouédraogo's unofficial household god, and struck the front window at its thinnest point, the place where the sixteenth-century glass was most tired and most full of memory.

The window exhaled.

There is no other word for what happened. It did not shatter in the manner of modern glass, all safety cubes and engineered surrender. It *exhaled* — outward, in large curved petals, each one catching the morning light as it fell, so that for a moment the street outside was filled with what looked like luminous, slowly turning wings — and then the petals struck the cobblestones and broke, and the sound they made was the sound of something very old agreeing, at last, to let go.

Silence.

Pastry cream dripped from Jean's left eyebrow.

Madame Ouédraogo stood behind the counter, one hand still holding the piping bag she had been using to fill a Saint-Honoré. Her mouth was open — not screaming yet, that would come, it was gathering like weather — and the piping bag continued to extrude a thin, accusatory line of crème mousseline onto the countertop, an unbroken thread of evidence.

Then the scream came, and it was magnificent, a scream with generations of professional pride behind it, a scream that contained within it the names of every éclair that had ever been ruined by the careless and the clumsy, and Jean stood in the center of it, baguette still aloft, and thought:

*I touched the memory and it broke. I reached for the beautiful thing and I knocked it into the world and the world cannot survive what I do to it. This is what happens. This is what always happens. You love something and your body turns that love into velocity, into angles, into a chain of stupid irreversible physics, and the window that held the light breaks, and the éclairs are on the ground, and the woman is screaming, and you are standing here with pastry cream on your face in the exact spot where you first kissed the only person who has ever made you feel possible, and you have ruined it, you have reduced it to broken glass and insurance claims, because you cannot hold a beautiful thing without your elbow finding the nearest catastrophe, because the body does not believe what the heart is trying to build —*

"I am so sorry," Jean said aloud, their voice high and splintered. "Madame, I am — I will pay for — I can — the window, the éclairs, Monsieur Bocuse — I can —"

Across the shop, standing precisely where she had been standing when the cascade began, Salomé held two intact macarons — pistachio and raspberry — one in each hand, like small pastel planets she had plucked from the disaster and saved. Her expression was unreadable. Not blank. Unreadable. A book in a language that adjoined the one Jean spoke but whose grammar worked differently, whose verbs conjugated in directions Jean's did not yet reach.

She looked at Jean.

She looked at the window.

She bit into the pistachio macaron and chewed slowly, her eyes on the space where the glass had been, where the morning air now moved freely through the shop for the first time in four hundred years.

She did not say anything.

Jean put down the baguette.

## III.

The canal in Vieux Lyon runs beneath stone bridges that have been holding their breath since the Renaissance, and its water is the color of certain secrets — dark, green-tinged, deep enough to keep what it is given.

Jean sat on the ledge above the canal at the Quai Romain Rolland, their feet dangling, Salomé beside them. It was evening. The light was performing its daily act of departure, turning the Fourvière basilica into a gold tooth in the city's upper jaw. A pair of herons stood in the shallows downriver with the poised indifference of creatures that have never once doubted their right to occupy space.

This was the spot.

Jean knew it by the iron railing, third post from the bridge, where someone had scratched *A+M* inside a lopsided heart, and by the plane tree that leaned over the water at an angle that suggested lifelong eavesdropping, and by the particular quality of the stone beneath their thighs, which was worn smooth in a shape that had, months ago, on an evening almost but not quite like this one, cradled two bodies sitting closer together than architecture required.

It had been September. The air had smelled like the river and like rain that was thinking about arriving. Salomé had been talking about her dissertation — social structures in Balzac, the way Balzac's Paris was a machine for sorting people into categories that pretended to be natural — and Jean had been listening, and also not listening, because underneath the listening a sentence had been assembling itself with the slow, terrifying patience of a thing that knows it will be said.

Two sentences, actually. Braided together. Inseparable.

"I love you," Jean had said, and the words had come out with a crack in the middle, a fissure in the syntax, and then, before the crack could heal, before Salomé could respond, before the moment could become only the one thing: "And I need you to use *they.* For me. When you talk about me. I need — *iel.* I need that. I'm — I think I've always —"

And Jean had not finished, because there was no finishing, because the sentence was not the kind that ends, it was the kind that opens, and Salomé had looked at them with an expression that was — Jean remembered this with the exactitude of a wound — *not surprised.* Not confused.
