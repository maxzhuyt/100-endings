### I. FRANCHISE OVERVIEW AND LEAGUE CONTEXT

The Global Catastrophe Prevention League, in the configuration that existed during the 2019 season, comprised thirty-two franchises distributed across six continental divisions, each competing for the right to deploy against certified Omega-class threat events through a system of regular-season scoring, postseason seeding, and — in the case of the championship round — a live, adjudicated response to an actual or simulated planetary emergency broadcast on the Paramount Heroics Network to an average viewership of 14.2 million households.² Teams were evaluated across seven performance axes: Threat Neutralization Efficiency (TNE), Style and Presentation (S&P), Civilian Retention Rate (CRR), Property Damage Minimization (PDM), Teamwork Coefficient (TC), Post-Event Infrastructure Recovery Contribution (PIRC), and a discretionary category titled, with no apparent irony, "The Intangibles."³

The scoring rubrics, which had been revised eleven times since the GCPL's founding in 1987,⁴ produced composite rankings that determined playoff eligibility, broadcast scheduling priority, and — through a complex revenue-sharing formula administered by the League Office in Geneva — the bulk of each franchise's operating budget. Teams that finished in the bottom quartile of composite rankings for three consecutive seasons were subject to "developmental restructuring," a euphemism whose specific consequences had never been publicly clarified, because no franchise had ever tested the threshold.⁵

Table N.1 reproduces the GCPL composite standings at the conclusion of the 2019 regular season. The reader's eye will descend naturally to the bottom.

Several observations must be made about this table, though the editors are aware that no amount of contextualization will salvage the Neutralizers' dignity through statistics alone.

Dayton's composite score of 60.24 placed them thirtieth out of thirty-two franchises — not, it must be noted in the interest of accuracy, last, a distinction that editorial laziness and narrative convenience sometimes obscure.⁶ They were last, however, among franchises with continuous active status, as both Tulsa and Anchorage spent portions of the 2019 season under League-imposed operational holds due to scandals unrelated to performance.⁷ In uninterrupted play, the Neutralizers were last. They had been last, or within statistical noise of last, for seven consecutive seasons. Their Style and Presentation score of 44.7 was the lowest in the modern era.⁸ Their Civilian Retention Rate of 72.4% meant that in a typical deployment, roughly one in four civilians in the designated threat zone experienced what League paperwork termed an "adverse outcome," a category that ranged from "minor psychological disturbance" to "death." Their TNE of 61.2 meant they neutralized the threat — the single thing the franchise existed to do — approximately three-fifths of the time.

The Neutralizers played their home events at the Gem City Civic Arena, a converted minor-league hockey stadium on Edwin C. Moses Boulevard with a listed capacity of 7,242, which had not been tested since the 2014 season opener.⁹ Average home attendance in 2019 was 2,107.¹⁰ The team's jersey sponsor was Rapid Lube Plus, a regional oil-change chain with thirty-one locations across the Miami Valley, which paid $85,000 annually for the privilege of having its logo appear on the left sleeve of a uniform seen primarily in loss highlights. The broadcast arrangement consisted of a local-access cable deal with a Kettering-based production company called TriState Sports Vision, whose coverage area did not include parts of Dayton itself.¹¹

This was the franchise. These were the numbers.

What follows is an account of the 2019 season as it was experienced — not by the composite rankings, which cannot experience anything, but by the people who reported to the Gem City Civic Arena every morning and did a job that the numbers say they did badly.

The author was not present. The author has relied on institutional records, League archives, postgame interview transcripts, the team's internal communications (released under the Freedom of Heroic Information Act, 2020), and fourteen months of interviews with surviving staff, family members, and the one active-roster player who was not in the building on the day the author has been trying, for the length of this project, to reach without flinching.

This is a reference work. It should be read as one.¹²

### II. FACILITIES, PERSONNEL, AND THE TEXTURES OF INSTITUTIONAL LIFE

The Neutralizers' operational facility occupied the entirety of the Gem City Civic Arena and a connected two-story annex that had been added in 2008 with a League development grant. The annex housed the coaching offices, the medical suite, the film room, the weight room (such as it was),¹³ and the break room.

The break room requires its own entry, because to write about the 2019 Dayton Neutralizers without writing about the break room would be to write about a body without mentioning the heart.
