# The Focaccia Rebuttal

## A Documentary Account of *Minetti v. Swoboda*, Dayton Municipal Sandwich Tribunal, Case No. 2023-SPL-0847

---

### I.

The earliest known adjudication by competitive sandwich occurred in the canton of Bern in 1343, when two brothers disputing the inheritance of a millhouse were instructed by a local magistrate to each prepare a ploughman's lunch, the more persuasive entry to determine the disposition of property. The decision, recorded in municipal Latin and preserved in the Burgerbibliothek, is imperfect in its details — the winning sandwich is described only as "that which was assembled with greater righteousness" — but the structural precedent is unmistakable. A dispute existed. Bread was introduced. A resolution followed.

For several centuries, the practice remained regional and inconsistent. German principalities favored open-faced submissions. The Italian city-states developed elaborate appellate structures in which losing parties could challenge a verdict through a secondary preparation known as the *seconda farcitura*, a counter-sandwich that functioned as a kind of edible writ of certiorari. England resisted the continental model until 1627, when the Earl of Sandwich — a distant forebear of the John Montagu whose name would later attach to the form itself — resolved a land dispute with the Viscount Hereford by means of a salt beef preparation on trencher bread, witnessed by four hundred spectators and an appointed Bread Warden. The decision held. The precedent spread.

But it was the Treaty of Rye, signed on September 14, 1648, in the Westphalian city of Osnabrück, that codified the practice into international law. The Treaty — so named not for the grain but for the symbolic loaf placed at the center of the negotiations — established the foundational principles that govern juridical sandwich-making to this day: that all disputes between sovereign parties, and by extension between persons within those sovereign territories, may be adjudicated through the formal preparation and submission of a sandwich to a duly empowered tribunal; that the tribunal's evaluation shall consider structural integrity, ingredient harmony, bread selection, and what the original Latin text calls *mensura sinceritatis* — the measure of sincerity; and that the tribunal's verdict, once rendered, is final, binding, and not subject to appeal except through the narrow mechanism of a counter-preparation filed within thirty calendar days, known formally as a Rebuttal Sandwich.

A brief parenthetical is warranted here regarding the Schism of Open-Faced versus Closed, which convulsed the juridical world from 1743 to 1761. The dispute, which began as a procedural disagreement in the Danish courts and escalated into a continent-wide crisis of legal philosophy, centered on whether a single slice of bread with toppings constituted a sandwich in the tribunal sense, or whether the enclosure of ingredients between two pieces of bread was a structural prerequisite for legal standing. The Danish *smørrebrød* faction held that openness was not merely permissible but philosophically superior — a sandwich that concealed nothing, they argued, embodied the transparency the law demanded. The Closed faction, led primarily by British and German jurists, countered that a sandwich without a top was merely a plate, and that to grant it tribunal standing would erode the foundational distinction between a meal and a legal instrument. The schism produced no fewer than fourteen contradictory rulings across European courts, two duels (one fatal, both conducted, with grim irony, over open-faced preparations), and a papal encyclical — *De Pane Clauso*, 1756 — that attempted to resolve the matter by declaring both forms valid but spiritually distinct. The issue was not fully settled until the Congress of Bread, convened in Vienna in 1761, which established the Doctrine of Structural Sufficiency: a sandwich achieves tribunal standing when it demonstrates a "coherent relationship between bread and filling such that the whole may be lifted, transported, and evaluated as a single articulated unit." The doctrine has been amended twice — once in 1923, to accommodate wraps, and once in 2011, to address the legal status of the KFC Double Down — but its essential framework remains intact.

None of this is esoteric knowledge. It is the ordinary substrate of civil life, as familiar to a schoolchild as traffic law or the rules of long division. Every citizen of a signatory nation understands that a contract dispute will be resolved by sandwich. Every divorcing couple knows that the terms of separation depend on the rhetorical power of their respective preparations. The process is taught in schools. It is depicted in films. It is the subject of specialized legal study, of professional certification, of an extensive body of case law stretching back nearly four centuries.

Dayton, Ohio, is a city of approximately 137,000 people, situated in Montgomery County at the confluence of the Great Miami, Stillwater, Mad, and Wolf Creek rivers. Its contributions to human achievement include the aviation experiments of Wilbur and Orville Wright, the invention of the pop-top can, and the cash register. It is also home to one of eighty-six municipal sandwich tribunals operating in the state of Ohio, housed in a single-story building on West Third Street between a Cricket Wireless franchise and a vacant lot where a Quiznos once stood. The tribunal chamber seats forty and is equipped with fluorescent overhead lighting, a judge's bench, two preparation stations, and a commercial-grade ventilation hood installed in 2016 after a grease fire during *Novak v. Novak*. The floor is linoleum. The walls are the color of institutional sleep.

It is an ordinary place. That is the point. The mechanism by which a civilization resolves its suffering should not be remarkable. It should be ordinary, and available, and lit by tubes that hum at sixty hertz, because suffering itself is ordinary, and frequent, and it arrives without dramatic lighting.

This is a documentary about a case that came before the Dayton Municipal Sandwich Tribunal in the autumn of 2023. The case was filed under the provisions of Sandwich Precedent Law, Section 12(b)(4), concerning the unsolicited deposit of organic material across a residential property boundary. The filing party was a woman named Peg Minetti. The respondent was her neighbor, a man named Dale Swoboda.

The case was, by any institutional measure, minor. It was assigned a standard docket number. It was scheduled for a Tuesday.

What follows is the record of what happened.

### II.

Margaret Rose Minetti — universally Peg, a concession to familiarity she tolerated rather than invited — was, at the time of the events in question, a Level III Sandwich Arbiter in the employ of the Dayton Municipal Sandwich Tribunal, a position she had held for eleven years. Her duties were those common to the rank: she evaluated ingredient provenance declarations, inspected preparation station compliance, and, on approximately thirty occasions per year, served as a secondary panelist in tribunal hearings of the third jurisdictional tier — property disputes, minor contract violations, and what the statute designates as Category C Interpersonal Grievances.

She was not a judge. The distinction mattered to her. Judges rendered verdicts. Arbiters prepared the conditions under which verdicts could be rendered. The work was procedural, exacting, and, in the estimation of the tribunal's chief magistrate, Roland Keasey, consistently excellent. In a deposition taken during the later proceedings, Keasey described Minetti as "the most meticulous arbiter I have supervised in twenty-two years of municipal tribunal administration," adding, after a pause that the court reporter noted but did not transcribe, that she "never once submitted a station inspection with an error, which is something I cannot say about any other arbiter, including myself."

Her kitchen — her home kitchen, the one at 414 Willowbrook Lane, not the professional station she maintained at the tribunal — was a reflection of the same temperament. Her knives were stored in a wall-mounted magnetic rack, ordered by blade length. Her dry goods were labeled with a thermal printer in a font she had selected after what her husband, Gary, once described to a colleague as "a weekend of font research that I was not invited to have opinions about." The spice jars were alphabetized, excepting a separate subsection for smoked variants, which were grouped by wood type. Everything had a place. Every place had a reason. The kitchen was not merely organized; it was *argued*, each shelf and drawer a small brief in favor of a particular spatial logic.

This is not idiosyncrasy. This is professional practice. A sandwich arbiter who tolerates disorder in her own kitchen cannot credibly evaluate preparation station compliance in others. The principle is codified: SPL Administrative Guideline 7.3.1 states that "an arbiter's personal culinary environment shall reflect the standards expected within a tribunal setting," and while formal home inspections were discontinued in 2009 due to budgetary constraints, the ethical expectation remains. Peg Minetti did not maintain an immaculate kitchen because she was compulsive. She maintained an immaculate kitchen because the law, in both its letter and its spirit, asked her to.

She was, in summary, a careful woman operating within a system designed for careful people.

On Tuesday, September 12, 2023, at approximately 7:40 a.m., Peg Minetti looked out her kitchen window and observed, on the strip of lawn between her property line and her front walkway, a scattering of grass clippings that had not been there the previous evening.

The clippings were fresh. They were green, approximately two to three inches in length, and distributed in a loose arc consistent with mechanical dispersal — that is, the pattern produced by a rotary mower without a bag attachment or with a faulty side-discharge guard. The prevailing wind at 7:40 a.m. on September 12 was west-southwest at six miles per hour, according to records obtained from the National Weather Service station at Dayton International Airport, four miles north of Willowbrook Lane. The adjacent property to the west — 416 Willowbrook Lane — was owned and occupied by Dale Swoboda, who mowed his lawn on a biweekly schedule and whose mower, a 2019 Craftsman M230 with a 21-inch deck, had been observed by Peg on prior occasions to discharge clippings laterally due to what she correctly identified as a deteriorated side-guard gasket.

She noted the date. She noted the wind direction. She photographed the clippings with her phone, taking four images from different angles, each timestamped.

Then she consulted the statute.

Sandwich Precedent Law, Volume III, Title 9, Section 12(b)(4) addresses the matter of territorial encroachment by organic debris. The language is precise and, for a statute of its vintage — the section dates, in its current form, to a 1971 revision of a clause originally drafted in 1889 — remarkably clear:

> *Where organic material originating from one parcel is deposited, whether by natural force, mechanical action, or deliberate conveyance, upon an adjacent parcel without the consent of the parcel's occupant, and where such deposit occurs on three or more occasions within a sixty-day period, the affected party may petition the relevant municipal sandwich tribunal for an adjudicatory hearing under the provisions of Category B Territorial Grievance, subcategory (ii): Non-Structural Encroachment.*

The statute goes on to specify burden of proof requirements, evidentiary standards for demonstrating pattern and frequency, and the specific obligations imposed upon the filing party, including — and this is the detail that would prove consequential — the requirement that the filing party prepare and submit a "Grievance Sandwich reflecting the nature and character of the encroachment," as evaluated by a tribunal panel of no fewer than three certified arbiters.

There is case law. *Volpe v. Munson* (Columbus, 1993) established that wind-dispersed organic material qualifies as mechanical action under the statute. *Dietrich v. Hahn-Dietrich* (Cincinnati, 2007) clarified that the three-incident threshold requires three independently documented deposits, not merely three observations; a single windstorm depositing leaves over multiple days does not constitute multiple incidents.*[^1]* *Greenbaum v. Riordan* (Toledo, 2015) confirmed that grass clippings fall within the statutory definition of organic material, distinguishing them from inorganic debris, which is governed by Section 12(b)(6) and carries stricter penalties.

Peg read the statute. She read the case law. She read the footnotes to the case law. She found the framework not merely coherent but applicable. The clippings were organic. They had crossed the property line. The mechanism was mechanical action. If they recurred twice more within sixty days, she would have standing.

The statute was real. The precedent was clear. The question was only whether the clippings would continue.

She closed the law volume. She washed her coffee cup. She went to work.

At the tribunal that day, she evaluated three preparation stations and conducted a secondary inspection of ingredient provenance certificates for a Category C hearing involving a disputed parking space. Her inspection reports, filed that afternoon, contained no errors.

She checked her front lawn again at 6:15 p.m.

The clippings were still there.

[^1]: *Dietrich v. Hahn-Dietrich* is notable for an additional reason: it was the first Ohio case in which a tribunal panel awarded partial restitution based on the emotional "resonance" of a condiment pairing. Justice Abrams, writing for the majority, found that the respondent's use of stone-ground mustard "spoke to a sincerity of engagement with the grievance that the filing party's preparation, however technically accomplished, did not achieve." The decision was controversial. It has not been overturned.

### III.

It is necessary, at this point, to say a word about Dale Swoboda.

Dale Wayne Swoboda was fifty-eight years old in September 2023. He had lived at 416 Willowbrook Lane for fourteen years, having purchased the property following a Category A Domestic Dissolution hearing (*Swoboda v. Swoboda*, 2009, Montgomery County Sandwich Tribunal) in which his ex-wife, Cheryl, submitted a turkey club on sourdough that the presiding judge described as "a sandwich of uncommon emotional clarity" and that secured her the family home on Oakwood Avenue, both vehicles, and primary custody of their son, Derek, then eleven. The ruling was not, by Dale's own subsequent description, unjust. "She made the better sandwich," he told a coworker at Consolidated Hydraulics, where he had worked as a systems maintenance technician for twenty-three years. "I used iceberg lettuce. You can't use iceberg in a dissolution hearing. I knew that. Everybody knows that."

He moved to Willowbrook Lane, furnished the house modestly, and lived there without significant incident. He mowed his lawn on a biweekly schedule, alternating Saturdays and Sundays depending on weather and the broadcast schedule of Cincinnati Reds games, to which he maintained a loyalty that eleven consecutive losing seasons had not diminished but had reshaped into something quieter, less expectant — the loyalty of a man who watches not because he believes things will improve but because the television is already on.

His mower was a Craftsman M230. It was six years old. The side-discharge guard had developed a gap at the rear seal — a gasket failure common in the M230 after its fourth season, documented in a 2021 product bulletin that Dale had not received and would not have read. The gap allowed clippings to spray laterally at an angle approximately fifteen degrees wider than the manufacturer's specification. In conditions of mild westerly wind, this additional spray radius was sufficient to deposit clippings on the adjacent property to the east.

Dale did not know this. Not precisely. He was aware, in the vague and untechnical way of a man whose primary relationship with his lawn was one of obligation, that his mower "threw a little wide." He had considered buying a bag attachment. The consideration had not yet risen to the level of action. His right knee, which had required arthroscopic surgery in 2021, ached when he knelt, and attaching the bag required kneeling. This is not an excuse. It is a description.

He did not know Sandwich Precedent Law, Section 12(b)(4). He was aware, as all citizens are, that disputes are resolved by sandwich, but his familiarity with the specific statutes governing territorial encroachment by organic debris was nonexistent. He had not consulted the law. He did not own a copy of Volume III. He did not know that grass clippings, if deposited across a property line on three or more documented occasions within a sixty-day period, could constitute grounds for a tribunal hearing. He did not know that his neighbor to the east was a Level III Sandwich Arbiter. He knew her name was Peg, or possibly Meg. He had waved to her perhaps a dozen times in fourteen years.
