# The Extraordinary Bureaucratic Odyssey of Pyotr Lunchkin

## I.

Pyotr Alexeyevich Lunchkin woke on a Tuesday in March — a month that in this particular province had not yet decided whether it was late winter or early mud season, and so had compromised, as provinces do, by being both — and performed, with the unthinking fluency of a concert pianist running scales, the entire orchestral suite of his morning.

He shaved. He shaved with a Sputnik safety razor, model 1978, which had been manufactured in a factory outside Tula that no longer existed, in a country that no longer existed, by workers who had presumably gone on to do other things or else nothing at all. The razor still worked. Pyotr saw no reason to replace a thing that still worked. He made two passes: one with the grain, one against. He patted his face with a towel that had once been green. He examined the result in a mirror whose silver backing was beginning to separate from the glass in the upper left corner, producing a small foggy archipelago through which Pyotr's left eyebrow appeared to float, unmoored, in a private atmosphere. He did not notice this. He had not noticed it for four years.

He boiled an egg. He boiled it for exactly seven minutes, because seven minutes produced a yolk that was firm throughout but had not yet taken on the grayish-green penumbra of sulfurous betrayal that eight minutes inevitably produced. He had determined this empirically in 1996 and had not revisited the question. He ate the egg with a quarter teaspoon of salt, applied in two pinches. He drank a glass of tea, dark, no sugar, from a cup whose handle had been broken and reglued so many times that the handle was now primarily glue — a philosophical object, a ship of Theseus rendered in epoxy and ceramic, which Pyotr gripped each morning without existential hesitation.

He dressed. His suit was charcoal gray, or had been, and was now the color of a cloud that has been trying to rain all day and has resigned itself to merely threatening. The left elbow of the jacket shone with a faint, almost pearlescent luster, like the spot on a marble step worn smooth by a century of genuflection. This was because Pyotr had rested his left elbow on the same spot of the same desk for fourteen years while processing applications with his right hand, and the fabric, undergoing this daily devotion, had been polished to a secular reliquary. The right elbow remained matte.

He took the number 11 bus. He stood, though seats were available, because the available seats were not *his* seat, which was the third from the back on the right-hand side, and which was presently occupied by a woman with a net bag full of beets. Pyotr did not resent the woman. He did not approve of her. She was simply a meteorological fact — an obstruction, like weather — and he stood with the patient, weightless tolerance of a man who has made peace with all the minor injustices of public transit.

He arrived at the Provincial Bureau of Permits and Licensing at 8:53. His shift began at 9:00. Seven minutes. It was always seven minutes. Not six, which would have suggested anxiety about punctuality and therefore a lack of confidence in one's own rhythms. Not eight, which would have been ostentatious. Seven minutes was the correct margin — enough time to remove one's coat, adjust one's chair, align one's stamp pad with the edge of one's desk, and review the first file in the queue, so that when the clock (which was mounted on the east wall, between a portrait of the current regional governor and a fire safety placard whose edges had curled with age into a permanent expression of mild alarm) struck nine, one was not *beginning* to work but was already *working*, had in fact been working for some fluid, uncountable interval, as though work were not something one commenced but something one had always been doing and would always be doing and the clock was merely a witness.

The clock, incidentally, ran two minutes fast. It had run two minutes fast for eleven years, since the Tuesday — also a Tuesday — when a maintenance man named Zhirkov had adjusted it incorrectly and then retired the following Friday, taking the error with him into a pensioned obscurity from which it could never be recalled. Everyone in the office knew the clock was fast. No one corrected it. To correct it would have been to admit that for eleven years the office had organized its life around an error, and this was a thing that could not be admitted, not because anyone would be punished but because the admission itself would constitute a crack in a surface that derived its entire structural integrity from having no cracks. So the clock remained fast, and everyone arrived two minutes late by its reckoning and perfectly on time by every other measure, and this was understood by all and discussed by none, and the clock ticked on in its gentle, delusional way, like a man who has been wrong so long that being wrong has become its own kind of authority.

Pyotr's desk was the fourth desk in the third row. There had been a time, early in his career, when he had occupied the second desk in the fifth row, which was closer to the window but farther from the filing cabinets, and then the first desk in the fourth row, which was closer to the filing cabinets but directly beneath a fluorescent tube that buzzed at a frequency that seemed to have been specifically engineered to vibrate the fillings in one's back teeth. The fourth desk in the third row was ideal. It was equidistant from the window and the cabinets. The fluorescent tube above it buzzed at a tolerable frequency. The chair, though its pneumatic cylinder had long since failed, had been shimmed with a folded copy of the 2003 Regional Permit Codex — Volume II, Appendices — to a height that suited Pyotr's lumbar region exactly. He had, over the years, worn a groove in the desk's surface with the base of his stamp, a shallow depression in the laminate that received the stamp with a satisfying solidity, the way a river stone fits into the bed it has spent a century carving for itself.

He was content.

Let us say that again, because it is important, and because what follows will make it difficult to remember: Pyotr Alexeyevich Lunchkin was content. Not happy — happiness is an event, a spike, a thing that happens *to* you and then stops happening — but content, which is a state, a dwelling, a house one has built around oneself out of small rituals and familiar objects and the precise knowledge of how long to boil an egg. His contentment was not the absence of desire but the *completion* of it: he desired exactly what he had, and he had exactly what he desired, and the distance between the two was zero, and in that zero he lived.

The office hummed. It hummed with a quality of silence known only to government buildings in mid-morning, a silence produced not by the absence of sound but by the *particular kind* of sound made by eleven people who are not working hard but are working continuously — the shuffle of paper, the muted percussion of stamps, the occasional creak of a chair, the distant clatter of a typewriter from the second floor (where Marya Grigoryevna still refused to use a computer on grounds that she described as "principled" and everyone else described as "Marya Grigoryevna"), the faint wet sound of someone eating a sandwich they had promised themselves they would not eat until noon but it was 10:40 and what was twenty minutes, really, in the grand architecture of a life?

Pyotr opened the first file. He reviewed it. He found it satisfactory. He stamped it. The stamp struck the desk in its groove with a sound like a small, decisive heartbeat, and the sound was good, and the morning was ordinary, and the world, such as it was, worked.

## II.

The woman's name was Krechetova, Yelena Mikhailovna, and her file concerned the renewal of a retail food-handling permit for a shop that sold, according to the application, "confections, dried fruits, and such items as may be reasonably classified under Category 14(b) of the Provincial Commercial Inventory." Her documentation was nearly complete. Nearly. She was missing, as Pyotr could see with a glance that had been refined by fourteen years of seeing exactly this, the signature of a district health inspector on Form 27-B, Subsection 3, which was the subsection that pertained to ventilation standards in commercial premises where sugar-based products were stored at ambient temperature.

This happened perhaps three times a week. A signature missing. A date omitted. A form that had been filled out in blue ink when the instructions — printed in a gray so faint they might have been ashamed of themselves — specified black. Each time, Pyotr explained the deficiency with a politeness so thorough it had become indistinguishable from indifference, and each time the citizen received the news with an expression that traveled, in the space of two or three seconds, from disbelief to outrage to a gray, collapsing resignation, like a soufflé observed through a time-lapse camera.

Pyotr opened his mouth to explain the deficiency.

He heard: *If this little man tells me I need Form 27-B again I will set this building on fire and feel nothing.*

Pyotr closed his mouth.

The thought had arrived with the quality of a radio signal suddenly tuned in — crisp, intrusive, undeniable, and located, with spatial precision, approximately one meter in front of him and slightly to the left, which was exactly where Krechetova, Yelena Mikhailovna, was standing. She was smiling. The smile was the particular smile of a person in a government office: a muscular arrangement that communicated not pleasure but *cooperation*, the willingness to be seen as cooperative, which was a different thing entirely.

*I will set this building on fire and feel nothing.*

"Are you all right?" Krechetova asked.

Pyotr looked at her. Her face was the face of a woman in her late fifties who had spent a meaningful portion of those years standing in lines: patient, weathered, and organized along principles of defensive pleasantness. Nothing in her expression suggested arson. Nothing in her posture suggested the capacity for total affective extinction. And yet the thought had been hers — he was certain, with a certainty he could not explain, the way one is certain that a sound has come from the left and not the right — and it had been as vivid and unambiguous as a headline.

"Yes," said Pyotr. "One moment."

From behind Krechetova, from the man who was second in the queue — a tall man with a mustache that appeared to be compensating for something, though for what, it was difficult to say — came: *I have been standing here for forty minutes. I am the only person in this line who has a real reason to be here. Everyone else is an impostor. That woman with the confections permit is an impostor. The man behind me with the yellow folder is definitely an impostor. I am the only genuine case. My case is urgent. My case is a matter of some gravity. Why does no one see this? Why does no one see me?*

And from the elderly gentleman by the door, who was leaning on a cane and whose overcoat was buttoned incorrectly, one buttonhole off, so that the left side hung lower than the right in a way that made him look as though he were slowly sliding off himself: *My feet hurt. My feet hurt. My feet hurt. My feet hurt. My feet hurt.*

And from the young woman filling out a form at the standing desk by the window: *If I write small enough in this box I can fit the full address but if I write too small they will say it is illegible and if I write large I will have to abbreviate and last time I abbreviated they said the abbreviation was nonstandard, but what is the standard abbreviation, nobody knows, nobody has ever known, the standard abbreviation is a fiction, a myth, a lie — I hate this desk, I hate this pen, I hate this box and all boxes everywhere — God please let the ink not run out—*

And from Kuznetsov, at the desk behind Pyotr, his colleague of nine years: *Sausage. I should have bought sausage. Why didn't I buy sausage?*

And from Desyatkina, the woman who managed the archived files and who had once, at a New Year's party in 2011, sung a folk song with such unexpected beauty that the entire office had fallen silent and stared at her and then never mentioned it again: *If they move my filing cabinet one more time I will write a letter. I will write such a letter. I will write a letter so devastating that — no. I will not write a letter. I never write the letters. I compose them, exquisitely, completely, in my head, and then I do not write them, and they sit inside me like stones.*

And from everyone else — all of them, the citizens in the queue and the clerks at their desks and the woman on the second floor whose typewriter had briefly stopped clattering — a rising, overlapping, unendurable chorus of thoughts that were not Pyotr's thoughts, that had never been Pyotr's thoughts, that were the thoughts of other people, delivered to him without permission, without context, without the membrane of social performance that makes other people bearable:

*—does he think he's better than—*
*—if I don't get this permit by Thursday—*
*—what is that smell, who brought fish, who would bring fish to—*
*—lonely, lonely, lonely—*
*—I could have been a—*
*—sausage—*

"Excuse me," said Pyotr.

He walked to the lavatory. He closed the door. He sat on the toilet lid without unfastening his trousers and pressed his palms against his temples.

The thoughts followed him through the wall. They were slightly muffled now, attenuated by plaster and tile, but still legible, the way a neighbor's radio is still legible through a shared wall — you cannot make out every word but you can follow the general program, and the general program was: resentment, boredom, hunger, the formless conviction that life was happening to someone else in some other room, and sausage.

Pyotr sat for eleven minutes. He did not panic. He was not a man who panicked. Panic was an improvisatory response, and Pyotr was not an improviser. He sat, and he waited, and he hoped that whatever was happening would stop happening the way a headache stops happening — not through any action on one's part but simply through the passage of time, through the body's decision to quietly revoke whatever error it had made.

It did not stop.

After eleven minutes he washed his hands — not because they were dirty, but because he was in a lavatory and the procedure upon leaving a lavatory was to wash one's hands, and Pyotr was, above all things, a man of procedure — and returned to his desk, and called Krechetova back to the window, and informed her, in a voice that was almost but not entirely steady, that her Form 27-B, Subsection 3, lacked the requisite district health inspector signature.

*I will burn it,* she thought, smile immaculate. *I will burn it all.*

"Thank you," she said. "I'll come back Thursday."
