# THE PINE SMELL

### A Sobibor Document

---

## I.

Mornings began with counting.

Not the SS Appell — though that too, that mechanical liturgy of numbers shouted into fog — but the private counting a boy does to stay tethered to the world. I counted the planks in the bunk above me. Eleven. I counted the steps from our barracks to the sorting shed. Two hundred and fourteen, if you walked the way the kapos wanted you to walk, which was quickly and with your eyes down. I counted the seconds the diesel engine ran each time a transport was processed in Camp III, because counting seconds was better than hearing what the seconds contained. On a full transport — and by October 1943, the transports were always full — the engine ran for approximately thirty minutes. I was fifteen years old and I knew this the way children in other places knew bus schedules.

I should describe the camp. I have described it so many times now — in courtrooms in Hagen and Hamburg, in depositions taken in fluorescent rooms in Tel Aviv, in a book I wrote and rewrote across decades, sitting in offices in Santa Barbara and Warsaw and places where the air smelled of coffee and paper instead of what our air smelled of — that the description has acquired the smoothness of a pebble handled too long. I worry about that smoothness. I worry that fluency is a kind of betrayal, that when the words come too easily the listener hears eloquence instead of evidence. But I will describe it again, because the story I have to tell requires you to understand the shape of the machine before you can understand what it meant to break it.

Sobibor was not large. You could walk its entirety — if you had been permitted to walk its entirety, which you were not — in perhaps twenty minutes. It sat in the Lublin district of eastern Poland, in a clearing hacked from dense pine forest near the Bug River. A railway spur connected it to the main line. That spur was the camp's alimentary canal: everything entered through it; nothing left. The Germans had divided the camp into sub-camps with Roman numerals and high fences, a bureaucrat's dream of compartmentalization. Camp I held the prisoners' barracks and the workshops — the tailor shop, the cobbler's shop, the carpentry. Camp II held the sorting barracks where the belongings of the murdered were processed, catalogued, packed into crates. Camp III held the gas chambers. Between these zones ran corridors of barbed wire threaded with pine branches, so that a prisoner working in Camp I could not see into Camp II, and a prisoner in Camp II could not see into Camp III, and no one — no one except the SS and the Ukrainian guards and the handful of Jewish Sonderkommando forced to work inside — could see what happened at the end of the tube, which was what the Germans called the fenced pathway that led the naked and the deceived from the undressing barracks to the chambers. *Der Schlauch*. The tube.

I worked in Camp II. I sorted clothing. Coats, dresses, children's shoes — so many children's shoes that sometimes the pile achieved a height and mass that seemed architectural, as though the shoes themselves were trying to build something, a monument or a barricade. I knew the names of some of the people who had worn these clothes, because sometimes, in the linings and the pockets, there were documents. Identity cards. Letters. A photograph of a woman in a garden. You learned not to look at the photographs. You learned this quickly or you did not survive the learning.

The SS officers moved through the camp on rough schedules. Oberscharführer Karl Frenzel managed the labor details in Camp I and kept his rounds predictable because he was a man who loved predictability — it gave him the feeling of order, and order gave him the feeling of justification. Frenzel killed casually and frequently, the way a man walking through a field might kick at stones: not from passion but from the ambient understanding that the stones were there and his boot was there and no one had told him to stop. Untersturmführer Johann Niemann, the deputy commandant, oversaw operations from the main building and visited the workshops in midafternoon, usually between two and three o'clock, because that was when the tailors had new garments ready for inspection. Oberscharführer Siegfried Graetschus trained the Ukrainian auxiliaries and kept dogs that he had taught to attack on a one-word command; the word was ordinary, almost gentle, a word you might use to call a child to dinner. I will not write it here. Some details are evidence; others are contagion.

I knew their names the way you know seasons. Frenzel was autumn — regular, methodical, stripping things bare. Niemann was a species of winter. Graetschus was something else, something not seasonal. The Ukrainians rotated among guard towers on four-hour shifts, and the shifts changed on a pattern that, if you watched long enough — and watching was all I had, watching was my only craft, the only skill the camp had not yet found a way to exploit — you could learn. I watched.

The minefields lay in a belt around the camp's outer perimeter, between the inner wire and the tree line. This was not widely known among the prisoners because it was not the kind of information the SS announced, but it was known, in the way that fatal geography always becomes known — through the evidence of bodies. Twice during my months in Sobibor, prisoners attempted to flee through the wire at night. Both times, the mines found them before the guards did. The sound was distinctive: not quite thunder, not quite a door slamming — something between the two, something the body recognized before the mind named it. After the second attempt, the forest perimeter obtained a new quality in my perception. It was no longer landscape. It was wall.

And everywhere, threading through everything — the diesel fumes, the latrine stench, the smoke from the perpetual fires in Camp III where the bodies were burned on iron rails after the mass graves proved unmanageable — there was the smell of pine. The trees pressed close around the camp on all sides. In October, with the air cold and the sap still faintly resinous, the pine smell was overwhelming. It came through the barracks walls at dawn. It was in the fabric I sorted. It lived in my hair, my skin, the spaces between my fingers. Visitors to forests smell pine and think of Christmas, of holidays, of the word *fresh*. I smelled it and it was the smell of the world continuing — indifferently, beautifully — around a place designed to end the world for everyone inside it. The pine smell was the camp's mask. The Germans had planted extra trees along the railway approach so that arriving transports would see only forest, only the soothing green ordinary world, right up until the moment the train stopped and the doors opened and SS-Oberscharführer Michel, whom the prisoners called "the Preacher," began his speech about the showers, the disinfection, the wonderful work opportunities ahead.

I want to say the names now, while there is still time in the telling before the action begins and the names become too numerous and the dead accumulate faster than my voice can hold them.

Léon Feldhendler, who had been the head of the Judenrat in Żółkiewka and who arrived in Sobibor already carrying the specific knowledge of what it meant to negotiate with annihilation. He was perhaps thirty-three years old and had a quality of compressed watchfulness, like a man listening to a conversation happening in the next room. He would become essential.

Shlomo Szmajzner, the goldsmith, who was thirteen years old when he arrived and whom the SS kept alive because his hands could work metal with a precision their own hands could not. He melted gold from the teeth of the dead and made rings for SS officers' wives — gifts sent home to Germany, to houses with gardens and curtains, each ring carrying inside its molecular structure the calcium and phosphorus of someone who had been, until recently, a person with a name. Shlomo was thirteen and he understood what the gold was. He did this work and he lived. I do not ask you to judge the mathematics of that sentence.

Chaim Engel, from the Netherlands, who had been a merchant and who spoke four languages and who sorted currency in Camp II alongside me — guilders, złotys, francs, Reichsmarks — all of it extracted from the luggage of the murdered, and it was Chaim who once, without any particular emphasis, without stopping his sorting hands, remarked to me that the money smelled of perfume because the women had kept banknotes in their purses beside handkerchiefs, and that certain denominations smelled of different perfumes, and that he could sort the currencies as easily by scent as by sight. He said this and then he did not speak again for three hours.

Eda Lichtman, who had been a dressmaker in Lublin and who was young and precise, with hands that could sew so finely the stitches disappeared. The SS valued her because she could mend their uniforms to a standard they associated with their own civilization — the civilization of pressed creases, of polished buttons, of cloth that lay *just so* across the chest. She worked in the tailor shop in Camp I, and because the tailor shop was positioned between the SS quarters and the armory, she had, day after day, observed the routines of entry and exit through the armory door. She had not been asked to observe this. No one had recruited her to watch. She watched because she was alive and her mind was alive and the mind, even inside the machine — *especially* inside the machine — refuses its reduction, continues to map, to track, to store, the way a plant in a sealed room continues to turn toward whatever crack admits light.

I could name more. I will name more, later, as the telling demands them. But I want you to hold these few for now, to feel the specific weight of them — their ages, their skills, the particular texture of their suffering and their attention — so that what comes next is understood not as the movement of a mass but as the coordinated action of *these hands*, *these eyes*, *these particular irreplaceable nervous systems* that the Reich's machinery had tried to render interchangeable and disposable and whose absolute unsubstitutable individuality is the fact on which everything that follows depends.

The camp processed approximately two hundred and fifty thousand people in its eighteen months of operation. The transport documents — I have read them all; I obtained them from the Bundesarchiv in Ludwigsburg and from microfilm collections in Washington, and some of them I found reproduced in the indictments at Hagen — the transport documents record these people as units. *Stück*. Pieces. The daily capacity reports, signed by SS-Obersturmführer Franz Reichleitner, the camp commandant, used this word without self-consciousness because the word was the thought and the thought was the system and the system was the point. The fuel requisitions for the diesel engine that pumped carbon monoxide into the chambers were filed alongside requisitions for boot leather and mess-hall potatoes. I have seen a single-page document from September 1943 that requests, in consecutive line items, six hundred liters of diesel fuel, fourteen kilograms of barbed wire, and a replacement fitting for the kitchen stove. The prose of annihilation is the prose of logistics. It is the prose of ordinary men making sure the ordinary machinery of their ordinary workplace runs on time. I used to read these documents and feel sick. Now I read them and feel something worse, which is a complete absence of surprise.

This was Sobibor in the first days of October 1943. A closed system. A machine so complete in its design that it had abolished the future as a category of experience. We did not think about tomorrow because tomorrow was today repeated, and today was a set of procedures — counting, sorting, carrying, not-looking — designed to keep you alive until the moment the design no longer required you, at which point you would walk down the tube and into the smell of pine and engine exhaust and you would become one more name that someone like me, if someone like me survived, would try to hold in the insufficient vessel of human speech.

There were no exits.

You must understand this. You must feel it in your chest, the airlessness of it, before I tell you what happened when a man arrived who looked at the same walls and the same wire and the same minefields that I had been looking at for months and saw not the absence of doors but the possibility that a door could be *made*.

---

## II.

The transport from Minsk arrived on September 23, 1943, late in the afternoon, when the sun was low enough that the pines cast long shadows across the railway spur and the air had the particular amber quality that October light acquires in eastern Poland — though it was not yet October; it was the late edge of September, which in that latitude already carries October inside it like a premonition.

I was in the sorting barracks when the train came in. We heard it first — the brakes, the specific metallic protest of a transport train decelerating on the spur — and then the shouting began, the SS and the Ukrainians taking up their positions, and through the gaps between the planks of the sorting shed wall I could see the cars, the usual cars, the sealed cattle wagons with their bolted doors, and I was already doing the thing I always did when a transport arrived, which was to stop thinking. To become hands. To become the mechanism of sorting, of lifting, of carrying, the way a clock becomes its gears.

But this transport was different, and I knew it before I understood why.

What I saw, through the planks: the doors opened and the people came out, and they were men, almost all men, and they were not Dutch families in good coats or Polish Jews clutching suitcases addressed to fictional destinations. They were Red Army soldiers. Soviet prisoners of war. Perhaps eighty of them, gaunt and filthy from weeks in transit, but they came off the train with a quality that I had not seen in Sobibor, a quality that I will call *vertical*. They stood the way men stand who have been trained to stand. Not proudly — there was nothing theatrical about it, nothing defiant in the way that word implies a performance — but *structurally*. Their bodies were organized around an internal scaffolding that the journey had not dismantled.

I noticed one man in particular, not because he did anything noticeable but because of the intensity with which he did nothing. He was tall. He stood near the back of the group as the Ukrainians shouted them into lines. He was looking at the camp. Not the way arrivals usually looked — the quick darting panic, the search for information about what was happening, the dawning apprehension — but slowly, with a kind of consumptive patience, the way a man looks at a building he is going to have to build or demolish. His eyes moved along the fence line, paused at the guard towers, tracked the route of a Ukrainian auxiliary walking from one post to another, and then — this is the detail I have carried for sixty years — he looked up. Not at the sky. At the tops of the fence posts. He was measuring their height.

His name was Alexander Aronovich Pechersky. He was a lieutenant in the Red Army, thirty-four years old, from Rostov-on-Don. He had been captured at Vyazma in 1941, had survived two years in POW camps including Borisov and Minsk, and had been sent to Sobibor when a medical inspection revealed what the camp's bureaucracy recorded as his essential characteristic: he was a Jew.

I learned most of this later. Decades later. From Pechersky's own memoir, published in Russian in 1946 and poorly translated and little-read, a document I eventually obtained through a contact in Moscow and had re-translated by a woman in Haifa who wept through the commission and charged me nothing. From testimonies given by other Soviet POWs who survived. From Blatt's own interviews — I should say from my own interviews, though sometimes, at this distance, the boy in the camp and the old man with the tape recorder feel like two different people who happen to share a set of nightmares and a name. From military personnel files in Soviet archives that I spent years trying to access and that ultimately confirmed what the man's bearing had already told me: Pechersky had been trained. He was an officer. He had commanded men. He understood logistics, timing, the architecture of operations.

But that afternoon — that September afternoon in 1943, standing in the sorting shed with my hands still stupid from work and my pulse doing the thing it always did when a transport arrived, which was to become irregular and fast and wrong — that afternoon, I knew none of this. I knew only that a tall man was looking at the fence and that his looking had a quality I could not name.

Some of the Soviet POWs were separated immediately — the SS selected those who appeared strongest for labor details, a temporary reprieve that every prisoner in Sobibor understood. If they did not select you, you walked down the tube within the hour. They selected Pechersky. They selected perhaps two dozen others.
