# Municipal Code

---

## I.

The first thing they got wrong was the staple.

I want to be clear about this because I think it matters, and because my memory of the first eleven or so minutes is mostly reliable, and because when you wake up from what turns out to be two hundred and thirty-one years of cryogenic storage in what turns out to be the basement of what turns out to have once been a Dave & Buster's, you tend to fixate on the details that make sense. The staple was in the upper-right corner. Any intake form worth the paper it's generated on gets its staple in the upper left, at a forty-five-degree angle, a quarter inch from each edge. This is not a preference. This is Section 4.02(c) of the Administrative Procedures Manual for the City of Dayton, State of Ohio, Office of Parking and Traffic Enforcement, revised 2019, which I know because I revised it. I added the bit about the quarter inch. Before me it said "approximately." I have thoughts about "approximately."

The woman holding the form was wearing a jumpsuit the color of an overripe tangerine with an insignia on the breast pocket that I recognized as a parking meter—the old single-head analog kind, the Duncan Automatics we phased out in 2016—rendered in what I can only describe as a devotional style. Like an icon. Like someone had been asked to draw a parking meter from a place of deep personal reverence and had, against all odds, succeeded. She did not offer me water. She did not tell me my name, or where I was, or what year it was, or why my arms felt like they'd been filled with wet cement and left in a walk-in freezer, which, contextually, was more or less accurate.

What she said was: "Gerald David Muntz, formerly of 412 Smithville Road, Unit 2B, Dayton, Ohio, badge number PEO-1187, you are hereby informed that Outstanding Warrant DM-2024-JW-00471 remains active, unresolved, and in violation of the Perpetual Code, and that your physical reactivation has been authorized solely for the purpose of adjudication. Please initial here, here, and here."

She pointed to three lines on the first page. The pen she offered me was attached to the clipboard by a piece of yarn.

I initialed. I have spent the majority of my adult life in rooms where you initial things, and the muscle memory, it turns out, survives cryogenic preservation better than, say, the ability to feel your own teeth. My hand moved. Letters appeared. The woman examined them with the unhurried focus of a jeweler appraising a suspect diamond, turned to the second page, and said, "Date of reactivation is 11-2-2255. You'll note the date field has been expanded to six digits to accommodate post-Silence chronological reclassification."

I noted it. The date field had six digits. It also had a formatting error—the slash between month and day was a hyphen, but the slash between day and year was a forward slash, and if you think that doesn't matter, you have never been deposed.

"Where am I?" I said, or tried to say. What came out was more like the sound a refrigerator makes when you unplug it.

"Tribunal Intake and Processing, Zone 4, Sector 11, formerly the greater Dayton metropolitan area," she said, already moving to page three. "The world you knew is functionally concluded. Civilization as currently practiced is governed under the Perpetual Code, which you may recognize in its foundational elements as the City of Dayton Municipal Code, Title 7 through Title 15, inclusive, as ratified and sacralized during the Reconstruction of Meaning, Year 38 Post-Silence. Do you have any allergies?"

"To what?"

"To anything. It's on the form."

"Penicillin," I said. "And seasonal. Ragweed." I paused. "What do you mean, 'concluded'?"

She checked a box. She checked two boxes. She did not check the box I would have checked, which was the one marked "Environmental/Botanical," because ragweed is botanical, and the box she checked was "Atmospheric/General," which is for things like dust and mold, and I thought about correcting her, and then I thought about the fact that she had just told me that civilization had ended and was now run by parking law, and I let the ragweed thing go, and that was the last time I let anything go, and I think about that sometimes.

They moved me to a holding area. The holding area had fluorescent lighting—tubes, not panels, the old T8s—and a bench made from what appeared to be a highway guardrail, bent. There was a sign on the wall. The sign said:

> **ALL PERSONS AWAITING ADJUDICATION MUST REMAIN WITHIN DESIGNATED RIGHT-OF-WAY ZONES. STANDING IN NON-DESIGNATED AREAS IS A VIOLATION OF PERPETUAL CODE § 7.14.3 AND WILL RESULT IN SUPPLEMENTAL CITATION.**

Section 7.14 of the Dayton municipal code—the real one, the one I carried in a three-ring binder in the back seat of my city-issued Dodge Caliber for eleven years—governs pedestrian conduct in crosswalk zones. Subsection 3 addresses obstruction of pedestrian flow. It was written for sidewalks. The holding area was a room. There were no pedestrians. There was me, and a man asleep on the guardrail bench, and a spider in the corner that, under a strict reading of the code, was arguably also in violation.

I sat down. The guardrail was cold. My body was cold. Everything was cold except the fluorescent lights, which buzzed at a frequency I associated with late shifts at the citation processing center on Third Street, where I had spent eleven years converting the chaos of illegally parked automobiles into the order of triplicate documentation, and where I had been, I was reasonably certain, a person with a life, although the specifics of that life were arriving slowly, like faxes.

The jaywalking citation. I remembered it now. June, maybe July 2024. I'd been off duty, walking to the Kroger on Wayne Avenue, and I'd crossed mid-block because the crosswalk signal was out—it had been out for three weeks; I'd filed the maintenance request myself—and a patrol officer, a new one, a kid who didn't know me, had written me up. Failure to yield to traffic signal. Pedestrian violation, § 7.12.1(a). I'd meant to contest it. I'd filled out half the contestation form. Then my mother had her hip surgery, and I'd been driving her to physical therapy three times a week, and the court date came and went, and a failure-to-appear warrant was issued, and I'd meant to resolve that too, and then—

And then, apparently, the world had ended and I'd been frozen and I was here.

The man on the bench opened one eye. He looked at me the way you look at a new coworker whose name you have already decided not to learn. "First thaw?" he said.

"I think so," I said.

"They do the form with the staple in the wrong corner?"

"Yes."

"They always do that." He closed the eye. "Welcome to the Perpetual Code. Try not to stand anywhere."

---

## II.

They moved me by shuttle, which turned out to be a repurposed city bus—a Gillig Low Floor, 40-foot, the kind Dayton's RTA used to run on Route 11—painted the same devotional tangerine as the jumpsuit. The route number on the front said ∞. The interior smelled like old vinyl and something botanical I couldn't place, and the seats had been rearranged so that half of them faced a small podium at the center of the bus, on which sat a copy of what I can only describe as a book, except that the book was the size of a microwave oven and bound in what appeared to be reclaimed highway-sign aluminum, and on its cover, embossed in reflective lettering of the kind used for street signs, were the words:

> **THE PERPETUAL CODE**
> *Being the Complete and Sacralized Municipal Code of the City of Dayton, Ohio*
> *Titles 7–15, Inclusive*
> *As Interpreted, Expanded, and Made Eternal*
> *by the First Tribunal of the Reconstruction of Meaning*

Below this, in smaller text:

> *"He who obeys the sign shall not be lost."*
> *— Attributed to the Meter-Saint of Third Street*

I sat down in one of the forward-facing seats. A guard—or an attendant, or a deacon; the functional distinctions were, at that point, unclear to me—sat across from me and watched me with the polite alertness of someone monitoring a parking meter that was about to expire. The bus moved. Outside the window, the world went by.

I should describe what I saw. I'm going to try to describe what I saw. I want to be accurate about this because accuracy is the only professional habit I have left and because what I saw was so specifically, so precisely, so granularly derived from a document I once knew the way a mechanic knows an engine—not reverently, not completely, but with the intimate, grimy, working familiarity of daily use—that to describe it without precision would be a kind of professional malpractice, and whatever else I have lost, I have not yet lost that.

The streets were laid out in a grid. This was not unusual for Dayton, which had always been a grid city, but this grid had the quality of intent—the religious kind of intent, the kind where the geometry means something. Every intersection was a four-way stop. Every single one. I counted eleven intersections before I stopped counting, and every one had stop signs on all four corners—not the standard 30-inch octagons, but larger, maybe 48 inches, mounted on poles that were painted in alternating bands of red and white, like barber poles, like ceremonial objects. At each intersection, pedestrians stopped. Not slowed, not glanced. Stopped. They stood at the corner. They looked left, then right, then left again. They proceeded. Every one of them. Every time. With the synchronized, unthinking obedience of people for whom the four-way stop was not a traffic-control measure but a liturgical act.

I watched a child—six, maybe seven—approach a crosswalk, stop, look left, look right, look left, and cross, and I watched the child's mother mouth the words along with the child's head-turns, the way a parent mouths the words of a school recital, and I understood that the looking was not caution. It was catechism. The child was not checking for cars. There were no cars. There were buses and a few cargo vehicles that appeared to run on something I couldn't identify, but there was no private automobile traffic. The child was performing the four-way stop the way a child genuflects: because the ritual contains the meaning, and the meaning is the ritual, and to ask what the ritual is *for* is to miss the point so comprehensively that the question itself becomes a kind of heresy.

The buildings. Fine. I'll do the buildings. The buildings were zoned. And I mean *zoned* the way the Dayton municipal code means zoned, which is to say that the zoning designations—R-1, R-2, C-3, M-1, the whole alphanumeric taxonomy of permitted land use—were not merely regulatory categories applied to structures but *visible, architectural features of the structures themselves.* An R-1 residential building (single-family, low-density, per § 11.03) looked like a house. A C-3 commercial building (general business, per § 11.07) looked like a store. These are not remarkable observations. What was remarkable was the *purity*. There were no mixed-use buildings. There were no buildings that blurred categories. A residential building had no commercial signage. A commercial building had no residential balconies. The zoning code had been taken literally—not as a framework for land-use planning but as an *ontological classification system,* a way of sorting the world into categories that, once assigned, could not be transgressed, blended, or questioned.

And the signs. God, the signs.

Every surface that could hold a sign held a sign. Regulatory signs. Warning signs. Guide signs. Signs that corresponded to real sections of the code I recognized and signs that corresponded to sections I'd never encountered, presumably added during the two centuries of interpretive expansion that had converted a mid-sized Ohio city's parking regulations into a continental theology. The signs were color-coded per the Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices, which I knew because parking enforcement requires a working familiarity with the MUTCD—white for regulatory, yellow for warning, green for guide, orange for construction—and the color coding was *exact,* which meant that someone, at some point, had preserved or reverse-engineered the federal sign standards with the fidelity of a medieval monk copying scripture, and I found this detail, out of everything, the most disorienting, because the MUTCD is not Dayton municipal code, it's federal, and the fact that it had survived alongside the local code suggested either a deliberate act of archival preservation or an accident of proximity, and I was not sure which possibility was worse, and I still am not.

I saw a church. I knew it was a church because it had the architectural grammar of a church—a high central space, a pointed facade, a doorway framed with the solemnity of an entrance meant to be passed through with intention—and because it had, above its door, a symbol I recognized as a stylized crosswalk signal, the walking-man icon, the one that means WALK, rendered in stained glass the color of oxidized copper, and beneath it the words:

> **TABERNACLE OF THE YIELDED RIGHT-OF-WAY**
> *"To yield is to be yielded unto."*
> *— § 7.08.2, The Perpetual Code*

Section 7.08 of the Dayton municipal code governs right-of-way at intersections. Subsection 2 addresses the obligation of left-turning vehicles to yield to oncoming traffic. It was written by a traffic engineer named, if I remember correctly, Phil. Phil had a mustache and a Reds mug and a Dilbert calendar and no evident interest in founding a religion. Phil, to my knowledge, is long dead. Phil's one-paragraph regulation on left-turn yielding, as far as I could tell from the window of the converted Gillig bus, had become the foundational creed of a faith practiced by what appeared to be millions of people across the ruins of the American Midwest.

I missed Phil. I missed Phil suddenly and terribly and without proportion, the way you miss someone you never really knew when everything you did know is gone. Phil had written a subsection about left turns. It was a fine subsection. It was clear, it was enforceable, it was exactly as long as it needed to be. Phil did not deserve this. Neither did the subsection.

The guard, or deacon, or whatever he was, noticed me looking at the tabernacle. "You recognize it," he said. Not a question.

"I recognize the section number," I said. "You've got the subsection wrong on the facade. 7.08.2 is left-turn yield, not general pedestrian right-of-way. You're thinking of 7.08.4, or maybe .5, which was the one about—"

I stopped. He was staring at me. Not with surprise, exactly. With the expression of a man who has just heard his dishwasher speak.

"You know the numbers," he said.

"I memorized most of them," I said. "You pick them up. Eleven years."

He was quiet for a while. The bus passed three more intersections. Three more four-way stops. He was still quiet. Then he picked up a device I hadn't noticed—a handheld thing, like a radio, or a phone, or something in between—and spoke into it, very softly, a string of numbers I recognized as code sections, and I heard, on the other end, a voice say something I didn't catch, and the guard, or deacon, looked at me with an expression that had migrated from surprise through reverence into something I can only describe as *jurisdictional unease,* and he said, "We're making a stop."

We did not make a stop. We made eleven stops.
