# The Stenographer's Silence

## I.

The witness was lying about the timestamps and Elena Marra knew it because his micro-pauses fell in the wrong places. A truthful person pauses before nouns—reaching for the specific detail, the remembered object—but this man paused before verbs. He was constructing actions, not recalling them. She noted this the way she noted everything: without judgment, without opinion, her fingers striking the chorded keys of the stenotype at two hundred forty words per minute, converting the man's fabricated evening into the evidentiary record of Case No. 2024-CF-11567, State v. Provenzano, and it didn't matter that he was lying because that was not her province. Her province was fidelity. Her province was the clean transfer of sound into text, the judicial system's most basic alchemy, and she performed it so well that she had become, in the estimation of judges and attorneys and courtroom clerks across three districts, a kind of high-functioning absence—the best possible version of a person who is not there.

She had worked the Provenzano docket for eleven months. She could tell you that the lead defense attorney cracked the second knuckle of his left index finger exactly once during every sustained objection, that the judge favored a particular cough when she was about to deny a motion, that the bailiff shifted his weight to his right foot in the moment before he called order. She could tell you these things, but no one asked, because no one in eleven months had directed a substantive sentence at her that was not "Could you read that back" or "The court reporter may have Tuesday off." She was the room's most reliable instrument, and instruments do not have observations. They have outputs.

The defendant was Marco Provenzano, twenty-six, heir to a family whose name appeared in county records attached to eleven restaurants, four construction permits, a cement wholesaling operation, and—in the filing cabinets of an investigation that would never graduate to prosecution—a narcotics distribution network that ran from the port to the northern suburbs with the quiet efficiency of municipal plumbing. The trial was for fraud. The fraud was a small bone thrown to the state's attorney to chew while the skeleton walked free.

Elena transcribed it all. She transcribed the timestamps and the contradicted timestamps and the sustained objections and the overruled objections and the sidebar conferences she was asked to approach for and the ones she was waved away from. She transcribed eleven months of language that circled the truth the way water circles a drain—touching it, shaping itself around it, never going down. And when the jury returned its verdict—not guilty on all counts—she transcribed that too, her fingers completing the final phrase of the final session with the same mechanical loyalty they'd given to the first, and she closed the machine's cover and she stood and she smoothed the front of her skirt and she walked out of the courtroom into a Tuesday afternoon that smelled like exhaust and approaching rain.

Her brother Matteo was waiting on the courthouse steps. He always waited on the courthouse steps. He was twenty-three and had the kind of face that looked like it was permanently emerging from a joke—mouth ready, eyes up, the whole arrangement tilted toward laughter. He had come to every session of the trial, and he had sat in the gallery's last row, and Elena had forbidden herself from looking at him because looking at gallery members during proceedings was a deviation she could not afford. But she had heard him. His particular way of shifting in the wooden seat. His particular way of exhaling when testimony turned absurd. She had transcribed the trial while her brother sat behind her and breathed, and now it was over, and the man whose family had ruined them had walked free, and Matteo was standing at the top of the steps with his hands in his jacket pockets and his chin lifted toward her, and he said something she could almost hear over the wind and the traffic.

She started toward him.

Later she would not be able to say with certainty whether the car had been parked there all along or whether it pulled up in the relevant moment. She would be able to say that the rear passenger window descended approximately four inches—she would give this measurement to the police and they would note "approximately four inches" in the report and she would understand, reading the report later, that the word "approximately" was doing no work in that sentence, that it was exactly four inches, she had seen it with the eyes that saw everything—and that the sound was not like the sounds people compare gunshots to. It was not a crack or a bang or a pop. It was an abbreviation. A word that had most of itself removed. Two of these abbreviations, one-point-five seconds apart, and the second one was unnecessary because Matteo was already—

The witness will describe the decedent's position as supine, left arm extended, right arm folded beneath the torso at an angle consistent with a sudden loss of—

She was on the steps. She was on the steps and her knees were in something warm and her hands were on his jacket and the jacket had the wrong weight and the wrong temperature and his face still looked like it was about to tell a joke but the timing was off, the timing was very badly off, and around her there were sounds that her mind would not stop sorting into their component categories—a woman's scream (sustained, soprano range, approximately 90 decibels at source), the car's engine (four-cylinder, slight valve knock, receding east-northeast), footsteps (multiple, varied pace, converging)—and she was aware that these classifications were not appropriate, that something in her was broken in a way that manifested as continued function, that the machine was running without an operator, and she opened her mouth to say his name, to say the word that was the first word she had learned to say after *mama* and *no*, the word that had been in her mouth more than any other word in her life, and

The ambulance arrived in four minutes. Bystanders gave statements. A woman in a gray coat said she had seen the car's license plate but could only remember the first three characters, and these were taken down by a uniformed officer whose handwriting, Elena observed from inside the back of the second ambulance where a paramedic was shining a penlight into her unresponsive eyes, pulled hard to the left on every downstroke, as though the letters were trying to leave the page.

They asked her what happened. She knew what happened. She had transcribed what happened in real time, involuntarily, and the transcript was inside her and would remain inside her with perfect fidelity for the rest of her life: every detail, every ambient sound, every precisely observed angle and measurement and duration.

They asked her what happened and her mouth was open and the shape of the word was there, she could feel it, the tongue in position, the breath lined up behind it, and nothing came out.

The paramedic noted this in the file. *Patient non-verbal. Possible shock response. Recommend follow-up.*

The detective who visited her apartment the next day asked whether she could identify the vehicle. She wrote on a legal pad: *Black Lincoln Town Car, 2019 or 2020 model year, rear passenger window tinted beyond legal limit, plate beginning LXR, slight body damage above right rear wheel well consistent with a parking-structure scrape at approximately 28 inches from ground level, pine-scented air freshener visible through windshield, driver wearing a dark blue or black cap with no logo.*

The detective looked at the legal pad for a long time and then looked at her and said something she processed as concern but that she also processed, because she could not stop processing, as a man performing the facial configuration of concern because his training required it: brow compressed, lips slightly parted, head tilted eleven degrees left. He asked if she had anyone she could stay with.

She wrote: *When will there be an arrest?*

There were phrases from the detective. There were more phrases the following week from a different detective, and then from an assistant state's attorney whose cuticles were bitten to the quick on both hands, a detail Elena cataloged and filed under duress indicators, subheading: professional overextension. There were phrases that contained the words *ongoing* and *investigation* and *at this time* and *every effort*, and these phrases were, she recognized, the exact bureaucratic dialect she had spent her adult life transcribing: the language that institutions speak when they are explaining that they will not act.

No arrest was made. No arrest would be made. The Provenzano name appeared nowhere in the file. The detective's visits stopped.

Her employer, the court reporting service contracted by the Third District, sent a form letter offering three days of bereavement leave followed by a supplemental two days of personal leave followed by a referral to an employee assistance program whose phone number she would not be calling because she could not speak into a phone. A subsequent letter, nine days later, noted that her continued absence required medical documentation. She visited her doctor, who performed a laryngeal examination and found nothing structurally wrong and referred her to a neurologist, who found nothing structurally wrong and referred her to a psychiatrist, who used the term *psychogenic aphonia*—or possibly *conversion disorder*, the two terms circling each other in the professional literature without ever quite resolving—and recommended a course of therapy. The psychiatrist's office filed the relevant paperwork. Her short-term disability was approved. A claims adjuster left a voicemail confirming her monthly benefit, which was fifty-eight percent of her former salary, and reminding her that the benefit period would not exceed twenty-six weeks.

The landlord of the apartment building where Matteo had also lived—the apartment directly below hers, so close that she could feel the vibration of his music through the floor, had felt it, had felt it every night for four years and would now feel its absence every night with the same structural permanence—slid a sympathy card under his door, addressed to "Tenant, Unit 3A," because the landlord had never learned Matteo's name. Two days later, a different envelope appeared under the same door: a thirty-day notice to vacate, as the lease had been in the decedent's name alone. They cleaned it out on a Thursday. Elena carried the boxes upstairs. She set them in the corner of her living room where the reading chair had been. She did not open them.

She was a woman who had lost her brother and her voice and her profession within the span of eleven days, and the combined institutional response to these losses was: three forms, two referrals, one sympathy card addressed to the wrong name, and a twenty-six-week clock.

## II.

On the first morning of what she would later understand was her new life, she walked to the corner bodega to buy coffee, and she watched the man behind the counter lie to a customer about the freshness of the bread.

She could not hear him lie. She was twelve feet from the counter and the radio was on and she had, in any case, stopped tracking spoken language with the attentiveness she had once applied to it—an adaptation that had occurred without her permission, the way a body redirects blood from the extremities in a crisis, conserving what it needs to survive. But she watched his hands. His left hand placed the bread in the bag with a motion that was three-quarters of a second faster than his usual pace—she had been buying coffee here for six years, she knew his usual pace—and his right hand made a small corrective touch to the brim of his cap, which he only did, she now realized, when a transaction involved a claim he did not believe. The customer accepted the bread. The man's shoulders dropped two millimeters—the precise measurement of a lie that has landed.

She took her coffee to the park across the street and sat on the bench nearest the east entrance, and the city unfolded before her in a language she was only now learning to read fluently.

A woman on the adjacent bench was on the phone. Elena could not hear the words and did not try. But the woman's free hand was conducting a symphony of involuntary semaphore: index finger tracing the seam of her jeans in a repetitive downward stroke (anxiety, repetitive self-soothing, the content of the call was medical or financial), left foot bouncing at a frequency of approximately three beats per second (impatience layered over the anxiety, she wanted the call to end but could not end it), and then—a sudden stillness. Every moving part stopped. The woman's spine straightened by perhaps a centimeter. She had just received the information the call was about. Elena watched the woman's throat execute a swallow that took twice as long as a normal swallow, the muscles working against something that was not food, and then the hand resumed its seam-tracing, but slower now, and the foot did not resume at all. Whatever the news was, it had weight. It had settled into the woman's body and changed its physics.

Elena drank her coffee and observed this with the same fidelity she had once given to courtroom testimony, and she felt—what did she feel? She felt the precision. She felt the almost sensual satisfaction of a pattern correctly identified, a signal cleanly read. She felt the world performing itself for the first time without the scrim of language between her and its machinery, and it was—*gorgeous* was not the right word, but it was the nearest word, the way a medical illustration of the circulatory system is gorgeous: all that hidden infrastructure suddenly visible, every line purposeful, every connection traceable if you were willing to look closely enough.

She was willing.

She began to walk. Not with destination but with the systematic patience of a surveyor establishing sight lines. Down Halsted to the corner where the Provenzano family's restaurant, La Fiera, occupied the ground floor of a limestone building whose facade had been repointed recently and expensively. She had walked past this restaurant for years without seeing it; now she sat on the bus stop bench across the street and she *read* it.

The lunch delivery driver arrived at 10:40 and parked illegally in the loading zone for exactly twelve minutes, which meant he had timed the traffic enforcement patrol and knew the gap. The hostess propped the front door open at 11:15, and when she did, her body oriented not toward the street—not toward potential customers—but leftward, toward the alley, where she held the prop for three seconds longer than a door-propping required. She was looking for someone. Or looking for the absence of someone. The kitchen's exhaust fan had a rhythmic catch in its rotation, a click every four-point-five seconds, which meant a bent blade, which meant deferred maintenance, which meant either financial distress or the particular negligence of a business that exists to appear to be a business rather than to function as one.

By 11:45, Elena had cataloged the arrival times and behavioral patterns of four employees, the frequency of the delivery driver's route (she would confirm this over subsequent days), the sightline from the front door to the alley, the window configuration on the second and third floors (offices, she suspected; shades drawn on the third floor, which was the only floor whose windows had been upgraded to double-pane), and the posture of a man in a dark suit who arrived at 11:38, entered through the alley rather than the front, and carried a leather portfolio whose zipper he touched three times between the car and the door in the way that people touch objects that contain things they are afraid to lose.

A bus arrived. She did not get on it. Another bus arrived. She committed the schedule to memory (the 8 bus, southbound, every fourteen minutes at this hour, every twenty-two after 2 PM, the driver of the 11:52 run being the one who braked most gently, indicating either professional conscientiousness or a bad knee that punished sudden stops) and she did not get on that one either. She sat and she watched and the world narrated itself in a language that had no words, and she understood it the way she had once understood the rhythm of testimony: totally, involuntarily, with a fidelity that was its own kind of affliction.

Over the following days, the survey expanded. She mapped the other businesses: the cement wholesaler on West 31st, where trucks arrived before dawn and the foreman—a man whose neck was permanently inclined four degrees to the right, as though he had spent decades looking over his shoulder and the posture had calcified—counted the bags with a lip-movement pattern that tracked perfectly with the numbers she could see stenciled on the loading manifests, except on Tuesdays and Fridays, when his lip-count exceeded the manifest numbers by what appeared to be a consistent margin. She noted this discrepancy and filed it and continued watching.
