# The Last Sneeze

## I.

Meredith Toyle had been holding her breath for eleven seconds when she lost control of her left nostril.

It was a Tuesday. She was standing third in the queue at the Dispensary of Civic Wellness, Southwest District, Requisitions Window C, which handled replacement cartridges for personal atmospheric filters and had, because of a systemic clerical reclassification eighteen months prior, also become the only window in the district that processed renewals of Exemption Status Form 11-NR — colloquially, and in certain circles quite unkindly, known as a "drip slip." She held one in her hand now, folded twice to hide the header. The woman in front of her was picking up filter cartridges and taking an extraordinarily long time about it, examining each foil blister pack as though it might contain a different future. The man behind Meredith was breathing through his nose with the mechanical, soundless ease of someone whose nano-mucosal suite had been updated within the last fiscal quarter: a seamless, poreless, utterly silent inhale-exhale cycle that, if you pressed a sensitive microphone against his septum, would have registered as a faint, contented hum at roughly four hundred hertz. His nose was, by the standards of 2089, an appliance functioning within normal parameters. Meredith's nose was not an appliance. Meredith's nose was an organ, and it was — at this exact moment, in this fluorescent queue, surrounded by functioning appliances — beginning to itch.

She knew the sequence. She had been living inside the sequence for thirty-seven years, which was the entirety of her life, because Meredith Toyle had been born in 2052 to parents who belonged to one of the last528 families in the Northern Governance Zones to decline nasal nano-suppression on the grounds of — and here the record diverged depending on who was recounting it — either "sincere philosophical objection to mucosal automation" or "being difficult." The family had dwindled. The philosophical objection had not. And so here was Meredith, the last practicing nostril in Southwest District, standing in a government queue and feeling the itch climb from the base of her septum toward the soft, traitorous tissue at the bridge of her nose with the slow, hydraulic certainty of a dam about to fail.

She pinched. She pressed one knuckle into the dip above her upper lip. She blinked rapidly, which had never once in the entire history of human sneezing prevented a sneeze but which every person who has ever sneezed has attempted, because the body, in the seconds before convulsion, becomes a superstitious idiot.

What Meredith wanted — and this was the engine of her, the pistons-and-crankshaft of her daily existence — was for her nose to stop. Not to be cured, not to be celebrated, not to be written about in the small, self-congratulatory journals of the Embodied Experience Collective, who had twice sent her pamphlets calling her condition "a gift of radical somatic presence" and whom she had twice asked, in writing, to lose her address. She simply wanted to stand in a queue and breathe like every other person in the building. She wanted to pick up her form, walk to the tram, ride fourteen stops, and go home without incident. This is not a large want. It is, in fact, such a small and tedious want that in most centuries nobody would have thought to write it down. But Meredith lived in the century that had made breathing into an engineering problem, which meant her want — to simply *be unremarkable* — had become structurally impossible, and the impossibility of it had curdled over the years into a low, persistent irritation that she carried in her jaw, her shoulders, and the short, efficient way she spoke to medical intake coordinators who asked her to describe her "episodes."

The itch crested.

She sneezed.

It was not a large sneeze. It was, by the taxonomy used in the biannual reports of the Bureau of Atmospheric Hygiene, a Class 2 expulsive event — involuntary, mono-phasic, lasting approximately 0.4 seconds, productive of a fine aerosol mist at an exit velocity of roughly thirty-nine meters per second. In the year 1987, a sneeze of this magnitude would have earned her a "bless you" from a nearby stranger and no further thought. In the year 2089, it landed on the queue like a gunshot in a cathedral.

The woman at the window flinched. She didn't turn around — turning around would have meant *looking*, and looking would have meant acknowledging — but her shoulders climbed toward her ears and stayed there.

The man behind Meredith stepped backward. One step, precise, calibrated. Not a recoil. A *reclassification of proximity*.

The intake coordinator at Window C paused her typing and, without raising her eyes, pressed a small amber button on the underside of her counter. The button did not trigger an alarm. It triggered a notation: *Unmanaged Expulsive Event, Public Setting, Form 11-NR Holder Present.* The notation would be logged. It would be cross-referenced with Meredith's file. It would not result in any action. It would simply *exist*, in the file, alongside the other notations, accruing mass the way a snowball accrues snow — except that snow was a metaphor that implied innocence, and Meredith's file had not been innocent for some time.

"Sorry," Meredith said, to no one in particular, because she had been saying it for thirty-seven years and the word had worn smooth as a river stone in her mouth. She said it the way a person apologizes for a cough on a tram, except in 2089 nobody coughed on trams either.

The man behind her — and this was the detail that mattered, the one the narrator has been waiting patiently to deliver — the man behind her did not say anything unkind. He did not grimace. He did not make a show of disgust. What he did was pull from his coat pocket a small, translucent dermal patch, roughly the size of a postage stamp, and press it to the side of his own neck, just below the ear. It was an atmospheric immunity booster. It was designed for use in industrial contamination zones. He applied it in a government queue, standing less than a meter from a woman who had sneezed, with the calm, practiced gesture of someone applying sunscreen near the equator.

Meredith watched him do it. She said nothing. She turned back toward the window, Form 11-NR folded in her hand, and waited for the woman with the filter cartridges to finish examining her future.

Her nose itched again, already.

It always did.

There were, in the Southwest District alone, fourteen medical clinics licensed to consult on what the Bureau of Atmospheric Hygiene classified as "residual naso-expulsive reflex," a phrase so meticulously assembled that it managed to contain no word a normal person would associate with sneezing, which was, of course, the purpose. Meredith was registered at Clinic 9, not because it was the closest or the best but because its intake coordinator, a weary man named Pell, had once handed her the renewaI form without asking her to describe her "relationship to the reflex," which was the standard intake question and which Meredith found so psychotically invasive that she had walked out of Clinics 1, 3, and 7 on the strength of it alone.

Her appointments were quarterly. They involved a nasal cavity scan, a mucosal sensitivity index, a brisk and completely pointless conversation with a technician about whether she had "considered her options" — the options being a suite of nano-suppression procedures that her particular anatomy rejected, as documented in her file, which the technician had apparently not read — and a signature on the renewal of her Exemption Status, which permitted her to exist in public spaces without nano-mucosal compliance for another ninety days, provided she carried the form on her person and produced it upon request.

She carried it. She produced it. She had produced it at grocery stores, at tram stations, at her place of employment (the Southwest District Archive of Material Culture, where she cataloged physical objects from the 20th and 21st centuries and where her supervisor, a kind woman named Doss, had once taken her aside and suggested, gently, that she might sneeze into a "privacy hood" while on the archive floor, as some of the interns had expressed "discomfort with unpredictable atmospheric events").

The privacy hood was a translucent bag, roughly the size and shape of a pillowcase, designed to be placed over the head during an "episode." It came in a discreet carrying case the color of medical putty. Meredith owned one. It was in a drawer in her apartment, beneath a broken tablet and a takeout menu from a restaurant that no longer existed. She had used it once, at a cousin's wedding, standing in a corner of the reception hall with a translucent bag over her head while the other guests glanced at her and didn't glance at her with the particular choreography that 2089 had developed for acknowledging a person's condition without acknowledging the person.

She had not used it since. Not out of pride. Not out of protest. Out of the specific, gritty, unlovely irritation of a woman who had been asked, repeatedly, by the entire apparatus of her civilization, to treat her own face as a malfunction, and who had — not heroically, not dramatically, but with the quiet, gritted stubbornness of someone who cannot stop being annoyed — declined.

## II.

The sneeze that changed the world happened on a Wednesday, at 2:17 in the afternoon, in the open-air atrium of the Southwest Civic Interchange, which was a transit hub connecting three tram lines and was, at that hour, populated by approximately three hundred people, none of whom were paying attention to Meredith Toyle, because nobody ever was.

She had been walking from the archive to Tram Line 6. She had been carrying a bag containing her lunch container and a 20th-century object she was taking home to examine — a small ceramic figurine of a cat with one raised paw, cataloged as "decorative, purpose uncertain, possibly devotional." The afternoon was bright. The atrium was vented with the standard municipal atmospheric filtration that kept public air at a regulated purity index of 99.97, which was, for most residents, indistinguishable from breathing nothing at all. For Meredith, whose nasal tissue was unmodified and therefore responsive to the things that nasal tissue has always been responsive to — particulate matter, thermal shifts, pollen from the decorative grasses that the Civic Beautification Bureau had recently installed along the atrium perimeter without, apparently, consulting anyone about allergens — the air was a minefield.

She felt it building from thirty meters away. The decorative grasses were a variety called *Paniculum viride*, engineered for visual appeal and low water consumption, and they produced a pollen so fine and so abundant that it registered on atmospheric sensors as a faint golden static. Meredith's nose located this pollen the way a dowsing rod locates water: instantly, involuntarily, with a deep and terrible specificity. The itch began in the left nostril. It spread to the right. It climbed to the bridge and then descended to the back of the palate, and by the time Meredith had set down her bag and reached for the fold of her sleeve — her usual suppression gesture, a discreet knuckle pressed to the philtrum — the sneeze was already past the point of negotiation.

What happened next took less than a second.

She sneezed. Not a Class 2 this time. A Class 4: bilateral, polyphasic, three distinct convulsions in rapid succession, each one driving a column of air through her unmodified nasal passage at velocities that would have, in a different context, impressed an aerodynamics engineer. Her eyes closed — they always do; it is one of the few involuntary reflexes that nano-suppression was designed to eliminate, the full-body capitulation of a sneeze, the way it seizes the diaphragm and the intercostals and the face all at once and briefly makes the body into a single, stupid instrument of expulsion. Her head pitched forward. The ceramic cat fell from her bag and broke on the tile into three clean pieces. The sound of the sneeze — and it was a *sound*, not a polite nasal whisper but a full, ragged, atavistic bark — crossed the atrium at the speed of sound, which is to say it was everywhere at once.

Three hundred people heard it. For a frozen half-second, the atrium achieved absolute stillness, the way a forest goes silent when something large moves through it.

Then everything resumed. People walked. Trams arrived. The moment closed over itself like water over a stone, and Meredith bent down to pick up the broken ceramic cat, and said "sorry" to nobody, and felt the hot, familiar blush climb her neck, and did not look up.

She did not, therefore, see the sky.

But the narrator did.

---

Fourteen kilometers above the Southwest Civic Interchange, in the upper mesosphere, in a stratum of atmosphere so thin that the word "air" was barely applicable, something shifted. It was not visible. It was not, strictly speaking, physical. It was a change in the resonance pattern of a structure that had been suspended in the mesosphere for approximately 300 years, dormant, silent, and so perfectly calibrated to the background radiation of Earth's upper atmosphere that no satellite survey, no climate monitoring array, no defense system had ever registered it as anything other than a minor density fluctuation, the kind of thing that gets filed in a database under "atmospheric noise" and never examined again.

The structure had been waiting. That is the simplest way to put it, though "waiting" implies consciousness, and the structure was not conscious. It was *conditional*. It had been placed in the mesosphere by entities who understood that the thing they were looking for — the specific thing, the precise biological event — might not happen for a very long time, and who had therefore built their detector to be patient in the way that only non-living systems can be patient: without suffering, without boredom, without the passage of time registering as anything other than a counter incrementing toward a threshold.

The threshold, as of 2:17 p.m. on a Wednesday in 2089, had been met.

The resonance pattern of an unmodified human sneeze — specifically the frequency generated by air passing over mucosal tissue that had never been restructured by nano-suppression, tissue that vibrated at the old frequencies, the mammalian frequencies, the ones that every human nose had produced for 300,000 years until sixty years ago when most of them stopped — matched, within a tolerance of 0.003 hertz, the activation signature that the structure had been built to detect.

There was no alarm. There was no beam of light. The atmospheric monitoring station in Reykjavik noted, at 2:17:04 p.m., a "transient mesospheric density anomaly, upper quadrant, non-recurring, classification: negligible." An intern flagged it, showed it to a supervisor, was told it was a sensor echo, and went to lunch.

Fourteen kilometers up, the structure began to unfold.

Meredith, on the ground, wiped her nose with the inside of her sleeve and went to catch her tram.

## III.

She was sitting on a bench in the small park adjacent to her apartment block, eating the remainder of her lunch and feeling sorry for the broken cat, when the first wall materialized.

It appeared roughly four meters to her left. "Appeared" is the wrong word — it *accrued*. First there was a faint shimmer that Meredith, glancing sideways, took for heat distortion. Then the shimmer took on texture: a pale, slightly opalescent surface that looked, in the late-afternoon light, like a cross between mother-of-pearl and the wall of a very expensive clinic. It was perhaps three meters tall. It hummed at a frequency that Meredith could feel in her molars.

She stared at it.

She looked around the park. Two other people were present — a jogger on the far path and an elderly man reading on a bench. Neither was looking at the wall. The wall, at that moment, was not particularly assertive. It was simply *there*, the way a wall sometimes is, except that thirty seconds ago it had not been.
