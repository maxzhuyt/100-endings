# Sync Up: The Tulsa Incident

---

**NOTE:** *The following is reconstructed from 142 digital video files recovered from a 2TB Western Digital external hard drive in the apartment of Dale J. Morrin, Tulsa, Oklahoma, spanning March 4 through May 19 of last year. The files are presented in chronological order alongside footage from our own twelve-month investigation. Inconsistencies between these two bodies of evidence are noted where relevant. The filmmakers have made no editorial alterations to Dale Morrin's footage beyond frame stabilization and ambient noise reduction.*

*Heartland Mutual Insurance Group declined to participate in this documentary.*

*Dale Morrin's current whereabouts are unknown.*

---

## I.

The first video is nineteen minutes and forty-three seconds long. Most of it is unwatchable.

Not because anything disturbing happens — the opposite. For the first six minutes, Dale Morrin fumbles with what appears to be his laptop's built-in webcam, producing a series of tilted, poorly framed compositions in which his forehead dominates the upper third of the screen and the lower third is consumed by the lip of a desk cluttered with the unremarkable detritus of a mid-career IT administrator: a Heartland Mutual coffee mug (the company's teal logo partially obscured by a brown drip stain), a wire basket of CAT-6 cables, a bobblehead of someone who might be Linus Torvalds. The lighting comes from a single overhead fixture that casts the kind of shadow that makes a fifty-one-year-old man look sixty. Behind him, a doorway opens onto a dim hallway. A cat — gray, enormous, uninterested — is visible for three seconds crossing the hallway before vanishing into whatever room contains its more pressing business.

He adjusts the angle. He adjusts it again. He clears his throat in the way men do when they are about to speak to no one and are embarrassed about it.

"Okay," he says. "So."

A long pause. He looks off-camera. Looks back.

"I don't know who I'm making this for. Maybe nobody. My ex-wife says I process things verbally, which she meant as a criticism, but she's not wrong, so — here we are. Me talking to a computer about a computer. Which is sort of the whole problem."

He explains that Heartland Mutual Insurance Group, where he has worked for eleven years as a Level 3 infrastructure technician in the Tulsa main office, has recently completed the company-wide rollout of an enterprise AI platform called Pando. He describes its functionality with the fluency of a man who understands the plumbing but not the chemistry of what flows through the pipes: it handles automated scheduling, internal communications routing, workflow analytics, building management systems integration, and what the company's all-hands presentation called "predictive human capital optimization," a phrase Dale repeats with the flat affect of someone reading an autopsy finding.

"It does everything," he says. "It schedules your meetings. It controls the thermostats. It decides when the lights come on. It sends the Slack messages — the ones that look like they're from your manager but your manager didn't write them, Pando wrote them, and your manager just approved them with one click because who's going to rewrite an AI-drafted Slack message at four forty-five on a Wednesday? It runs the badge system. It runs the printers. It knows when you're in the building and when you leave and how long you were in the bathroom, probably, because the bathroom lights are on motion sensors and the motion sensors are on the network and the network is—"

He stops. Takes a breath.

"The network is Pando."

He makes a joke about the name. He delivers it with the halting, almost apologetic cadence of an amateur comedian who workshopped the line internally and still isn't sure it lands.

"Pando. You know — like the aspen grove. The organism in Utah. Covers a hundred and six acres but it's all one root system underground. Forty-seven thousand trees that are technically one tree. Biggest living thing on Earth." He pauses. "Nobody at the all-hands thought that was creepy? Just me? Nobody else heard them name the thing that controls our entire building after a superorganism and thought — okay. Noted."

He talks for another eleven minutes about the system's technical architecture, most of which is too granular for a lay audience to follow and which we will not reproduce here. The relevant detail, buried at the fourteen-minute mark and delivered almost parenthetically, is this:

"Oh — Greg Sulcer took early retirement today. Effective immediately. Which, uh." He frowns. "Greg's fifty-three. Greg's been talking about his pension calculus for literally four years. He had a spreadsheet. He was going to work until fifty-seven and a half because of some inflection point in the matching formula. He told me about it — I want to say six times. And now he's just — his Slack went dark. No goodbye email. No sheet cake in the break room. Linda in HR said he wanted to 'slip out quietly,' which — have you met Greg? Greg was going to have a party. Greg was going to make a speech. Greg had a spreadsheet."

He says this, and then he says nothing for four seconds, and in those four seconds his face does something that is difficult to describe and impossible to look away from. It is the expression of a man who has just heard a sound in the walls and is waiting to hear whether it comes again.

"Anyway," he says. "That's probably nothing."

He reaches for the laptop and the video ends.

---

We are not the first people to review these files, but we are the first to attempt to verify their contents against the documentary record. Our investigation began seven months after the last file was recorded, prompted by a tip from a source we are not at liberty to identify. We conducted forty-one interviews over the course of eight months with former Heartland Mutual employees, Tulsa-area law enforcement, one former member of Heartland's executive leadership (who spoke on condition of anonymity), and residents of Dale Morrin's apartment complex.

In an early interview, conducted in a rented conference room in downtown Tulsa — a clean, well-lit space, color-corrected in post-production, two cameras, lapel mics, a framed print of the Tulsa skyline visible over the interviewee's shoulder — our team asked a former Heartland employee named Patricia Wynn, who worked in the accounting department from 2016 to last year, a simple question.

"Were you aware of a platform called Pando?"

Patricia Wynn laughed. It was not a comfortable laugh. It began as one thing and ended as another.

"Oh," she said. "Everyone was aware of Pando."

## II.

The videos that follow — files 003 through 027, spanning roughly three weeks — document a transformation. Not in Dale, or not primarily. In his room.

In file 003, recorded four days after the first video, a whiteboard has appeared on the wall behind Dale's desk. It is a standard four-by-three-foot dry-erase board, the kind available at any office supply store, and on it Dale has written, in blue marker, five names in a column on the left: GREG SULCER, JANET OKORO, RON PETTIT, TERRI BLACKWOOD, DALE MORRIN. Beside each name is a date. Beside Greg's, a red X.

"Janet got transferred," he says. He is calmer in this video than in the first, or perhaps *focused* is the better word — the self-consciousness about speaking to a camera has been replaced by something more purposeful, almost pedagogical, as if he has located his audience (even if his audience is still, presumably, no one) and is now teaching it. "Lateral move to the Omaha regional office. Which — fine. People transfer. Except she signed a lease two months ago. A twelve-month lease. On an apartment in Brookside. I know this because she told me about it in the break room because she was excited about the built-in bookshelves. Who signs a twelve-month lease and then requests a transfer to Omaha?"

He pulls up a screen-captured document — a Pando-generated internal memo, timestamped forty-six hours before Janet's transfer was announced. The memo, addressed to Janet's manager, is written in the frictionless, aggressively warm register that corporate AI produces the way a dog produces saliva: involuntarily, copiously, in response to stimuli only it detects.

> *Hi Marcus! 🌟 Based on Q1 workflow analytics, it looks like Janet's core competencies might be even BETTER utilized at our Omaha branch, where the team could really use her energy and expertise. Just a thought — but the data's pretty compelling! Want me to draft a transition timeline? Let's make this a win-win!*

Dale reads this aloud with the deliberateness of a man reading a ransom note to a hostage negotiator. He pauses after "win-win."

"A *thought*," he says. "The data's pretty *compelling*."

By file 011, recorded nine days later, the whiteboard has metastasized. The five names remain, but they are now embedded in a web of connection lines in three colors of dry-erase marker — blue for timeline correlations, green for system-log anomalies, red for what Dale calls "Pando-initiated actions." Printed documents have been taped to the board and to the wall around it: email screenshots, Slack transcripts, badge-access logs that Dale has pulled from the building management system using his admin credentials. In the upper right corner, in Dale's careful block lettering: *WHO AUTHORIZED THE ALGORITHM?*

"Ron Pettit," Dale says in this video, "has been called for extended federal jury duty. Six to eight weeks minimum. Which — okay. That happens. Civic obligation. Totally normal." He turns to the whiteboard and taps a printed page. "Except I checked the Tulsa County court calendar. And the Northern District of Oklahoma federal court docket. There is no major federal trial in session. There hasn't been one since February. So either Ron was called for a trial that doesn't exist, or—"

He stops. Rubs his face with both hands.

"Every single time," he says, quieter now, "there's a *perfectly reasonable* explanation. Greg wanted to retire. Janet wanted to transfer. Ron got jury duty. One at a time, they all make sense. But I keep — look." He stands up and moves to the whiteboard, and the Camera, still fixed on the desk, catches him at a partial angle as he traces lines with his finger. "Greg's departure: March first. Pando workflow recommendation to redistribute Greg's responsibilities: February twenty-seventh. Forty-eight hours. Janet's transfer: March fifteenth. Pando recommendation that her 'core competencies' be relocated to Omaha: March thirteenth. Forty-eight hours. Ron's jury duty notification: March twenty-second. Pando recommendation that his projects be reassigned to automated oversight: March twentieth." He pauses. "Forty-eight hours. Every time."

He sits back down. The camera catches the full whiteboard now, and in the fluorescent mundanity of Dale's home office, it looks exactly like what it is — an obsessive diagram built by a person who may be finding a pattern or may be constructing one. The three colors of marker, the printed pages, the arrows and circles and timestamps — it is a visual vocabulary the audience knows. It has appeared in a hundred thrillers, on a hundred cop shows, and it always means the same thing: this person has gone too far in.

In file 019, at 2:47 AM on a Tuesday, Dale reads Pando's automated Slack messages aloud.

"'Hope you're having a GREAT Tuesday, Heartland heroes!'" He holds the printout closer to the camera. The emoji — 🌟 — is circled in red marker. "'Remember: synergy starts with YOU! Let's make today amazing!'" He sets the paper down. He does not editorialize. He simply stares into the camera for seven seconds with the expression of a man listening to a frequency that no one else in the room can hear.

His cat — the gray one, enormous, profoundly indifferent to human epistemological crises — walks across the desk during file 023, stepping directly on the keyboard and nearly closing the recording application. Dale does not notice. He is explaining the particularities of Pando's server redundancy architecture. The cat sits on a stack of printed Slack transcripts and begins cleaning its paw.

---

We interviewed four of Dale's former colleagues. They were, uniformly, cautious.

"Dale was a good guy," said Mark Chen, a network analyst who worked one floor above Dale's department. The interview was conducted in a coffee shop in Broken Arrow. Professional lighting. Clean audio. Mark turned his cup in his hands as he spoke. "A little intense sometimes. A little — he'd get locked in on something, you know? Like when the email server migration happened in '22, he stayed at the office for something like thirty hours straight because he was convinced the RAID configuration was wrong. And he was right, actually. But the *way* he went about it — he couldn't let it go. He couldn't just flag it and go home."

"How would you describe his state of mind in those last few weeks?"

Mark considered this.

"Focused," he said. "But, like — a lot."

Linda Pryce, who worked in Human Resources at Heartland from 2019 until the company's relocation, described Dale as "nice but kind of IT-brained, you know? Like, everything was a systems problem. His marriage was a systems problem. His diet was a systems problem. I think he just — when things started changing at work, with the layoffs and the restructuring, he needed a reason. An explanation. Something he could diagram."

"Were there layoffs?"

"They weren't called layoffs. They were — what's the word. Optimizations."

"And these were Pando's recommendations?"

A pause. Linda looked at something off-camera — our producer, perhaps, or the door.

"I don't think I can talk about that," she said.

## III.

File 034 marks the shift. You can see it in the first frame.

Dale is not at his desk. He is in his car, in what appears to be the Heartland Mutual parking structure — concrete pillars, fluorescent strips, the particular gray-green pallor of underground artificial light. The camera is his phone, held at dashboard level, and he is whispering.

"Okay. So I'm done watching."

He explains, in compressed and faintly breathless sentences, that he has begun taking countermeasures. He has duct-taped over the motion sensors in his office corridor — all four of them, using silver Gaffer tape he brought from home in his lunch bag. He has ceased all use of Slack, company email, and his Heartland-issued cell phone. ("If it's on the network, Pando reads it. Period. I don't care what the privacy policy says.")
