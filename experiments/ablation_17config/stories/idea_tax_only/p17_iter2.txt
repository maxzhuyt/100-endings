# The Underliving

---

## I.

**March 29th, 9:47 p.m.**

The house is doing something wrong with sound.

That's not the right way to say it. Let me try again. Since Elliot died — since Elliot *drowned*, since the word "died" is the coward's version and I am trying not to be a coward even though I am one — since Elliot drowned, the house has become a series of noises that don't add up to anything. The faucet runs in the kitchen and it sounds like the faucet is running in the kitchen and that's all it sounds like. Before, it sounded like Mom was making pasta or filling the dog's bowl or soaking the burnt part off the skillet she always burns, and you could tell which one from your bedroom if you listened. Now it's just water. Just the sound of water. I am aware that I should not be writing about faucets.

Dad mowed the lawn today. He's been mowing the lawn every three days even though it's March and the grass hasn't started growing yet. He does the strip along the fence first, then the strip along the driveway, and then he goes back and does the strip along the fence again, overlapping it, pushing the mower over ground so short it's basically dirt. He did this for an hour and forty minutes. I timed it from Elliot's window. His window is the one that faces the yard, which is why I was at his window, which is also why I've been sleeping in his bed, which Mom hasn't said anything about, which is its own kind of horrible because Mom would have said something before, she would have come in and sat on the edge of the bed and said *Sweetheart, I know this is hard, but you need to sleep in your own room,* and the fact that she doesn't say it means she either doesn't notice or doesn't care or has lost the part of herself that would have noticed and cared, and I don't know which of those is worst.

Mom washes his cereal bowl every morning. The blue one with the chip on the rim that looks like a tiny mountain range, the one he picked out at the Goodwill because he said the chip made it interesting. She takes it out of the cabinet and washes it in the sink with soap and a sponge and sets it on the drying rack and that is the end of the ritual. She does not fill it with cereal. She does not set it on the table. She washes something that is already clean because she needs to perform the motion of caring for him and this is the motion her hands remember. I find this so unbearable that I have to write it in sentences that are too long or I will stop writing.

Margot stands in doorways. This is the thing Margot does. She stands in the doorway of the kitchen without entering the kitchen. She stands in the doorway of the living room without entering the living room. She stood in the doorway of Elliot's room this morning — I was in his bed, under his comforter that still smells like his shampoo, the green apple kind, though the smell is going, it's going a little more each day and I can feel it going the way you can feel a word you're forgetting — and Margot stood there with her hand on the frame and looked at me and didn't come in. She is seventeen and she used to take up every room she entered, she used to be the loudest frequency in any space, and now she is a girl who stands at thresholds. Grief is a bell that won't stop humming after you hit it. No. Grief is the hitting and the humming is what comes after and you can't tell when the hitting stopped and the humming started because they're the same vibration at different volumes. No. That's not it either.

I want to write about the root cellar.

I don't know why I want to write about the root cellar. It is the least interesting part of this house, which is a farmhouse, which is old, which has a root cellar because farmhouses have root cellars, and I have never once in my life had a thought or a feeling about it. It's below the kitchen. You get to it through a door next to the pantry that sticks when it's humid, which is always, because we live in the river valley and everything here is always a little bit damp. Stairs going down. Dirt floor. Shelves with jars on them, the jars older than anyone currently alive. The smell is specific and I want to get it right: it's packed earth, which smells the way cold wood sounds, which is — no, it's simpler than that. It smells like the inside of something. Like a body's inside. Like the air in there has been breathed already by the ground itself.

It was cold down there, when I went down today. Not house-cold. Not winter-cold. A different cold. An *alert* cold, like the air was paying attention. I stood at the bottom of the stairs and looked at the jars on the shelves and the hard dirt floor and the place where the foundation stones show through the wall like the house's ribs, and there was a door in the far wall.

I need to be precise about this. Precision is not my gift. My gift, if I have one, is overcomplication, is building fourteen-story metaphors on one-story feelings, and I know this about myself and I'm not going to do it here. There was a door. It was wooden. It was set into the far wall of the root cellar, between two shelf units, at a height that would require a grown person to duck. The wood was old but the hinges were not rusted. It had a handle — iron, curved, cold when I touched it, which I did, which I don't remember deciding to do. I touched the handle and the cold that came off it was not the temperature-cold of metal but something else, a cold that went up through my wrist and settled behind my sternum like a piece of ice with a pulse.

I have never noticed this door before. I have lived in this house for thirteen years and gone into the root cellar maybe a hundred times to get jars for Mom or to hide during games with Elliot, and I have never noticed this door, and I am not going to build a metaphor about what that means. It's there. I touched it. The cold felt like it was listening.

I came back upstairs. I'm in Elliot's bed. His pillow smells less like green apple tonight than it did last night. The bell is humming. Everything is the sound of water and nothing else. I'm going to go back down tomorrow.

Behind it there were stairs.

Not root cellar stairs. Not the rough-cut lumber and dirt-shelf steps that go from the kitchen to the cellar floor. These were — okay. They were stone. They were narrow, just wider than my shoulders, and they went down. They curved, slightly, like the inside of an ear, and they went down farther than any set of stairs beneath a farmhouse foundation has a right to go. I counted nineteen steps before I lost count because I started listening to something else.

The air warmed. That's important. I need to record that accurately. The first step was cellar-cold. By the seventh step the cold had thawed into something neutral. By the twelfth step the air was warm, not heated-warm, not furnace-warm, but *living-room-in-the-afternoon warm*, the warmth of a house that has people in it and a window letting in sun. His sneakers were by the chair.

I have to — I need to — his sneakers. The white ones. The Converse knockoffs from Target that he scuffed on the first day because he dragged his feet when he walked, because he had that walk, that shuffling boneless walk that drove Mom crazy, *pick up your feet, Elliot, you're going to trip* — and he always said he wouldn't and then he always did. The left one had a grass stain on the toe from the afternoon he tried to learn to kick a soccer ball and missed the ball entirely and kicked the lawn. The right one had a knot in the lace that he'd given up untying, so he just crushed the heel down when he put them on, and the heel was crushed, it was crushed in exactly the right way, the canvas folded over at exactly the angle his foot made when he shoved it in.

The couch had his dent. The specific depression in the left cushion where he sat sideways with his legs pulled up, because he never sat in a chair the way chairs were designed for, he draped himself over furniture like furniture had personally offended him and he was staging a protest. The dent was there. I could see the exact contour his body made — hip, thigh, the tucked angle of his knees.

I could hear him breathing.

Not loudly. Not dramatically. The small, slightly whistling intake he made through his nose because his septum was deviated, the doctor said, a tiny harmless deviation that made his breathing sound like a very quiet kettle that never quite reaches a boil. I used to hear it at night through the wall between our rooms. I used to fall asleep to it. I have not been able to fall asleep since he died because the wall is silent now and silence, it turns out, is not the absence of sound but the presence of absence, and

He was there.

He was on the couch. He was sitting sideways with his legs tucked up. He was holding a deck of cards. He was — this was March 7th. This was a specific Tuesday. He looked up at me and smiled.

It was his smile. ## III.

Her father found her at 6:15 p.m. The root cellar door was open. The kitchen light fell partway down the stairs and stopped.

He called her name twice. No answer. He went down.

She was sitting cross-legged on the packed-earth floor in the far corner of the cellar, just past the shelf units, in the dark. Her back was to the stone foundation wall. Her hands were open in her lap, palms up, fingers slightly curled, as though holding something light — a small animal, a folded piece of paper. She was speaking softly. Her tone was conversational, patient, the voice of someone demonstrating a task to a person who was not there.

"Lighter," she was saying. "Like you're barely even — yeah. Yeah, try again."

He said her name. She didn't look up. He crouched beside her and put his hand on her shoulder and she turned to him with an expression he would later describe to his wife as *annoyed*, though the word he almost used was *interrupted*, the particular blankness of a person pulled without warning from a vivid interior into a dull exterior.

He carried her upstairs. She was limp and warm, her body loose the way a sleeping child's body is loose, boneless, yielding, like a child pulled from a bath. She did not resist. She blinked in the kitchen light and looked at her hands and murmured something he didn't catch.

He set her in a chair at the kitchen table. She sat there. After a while, she went to her brother's room and closed the door.

That evening, the family sat in the living room: father on the couch with the television on but muted, Margot on the floor with a textbook open to a page she had not turned in forty minutes, the mother in the armchair by the window with a glass of water she was not drinking. The space they made was the space of four people with a fifth person's absence distributed evenly among them.

The mother said, to no one in particular, "Do you remember that play? The school thing. When he — he was in a costume, the costume with the — it was an animal. Some kind of animal."

Margot looked up. The father did not move.

"He had the ears on," the mother said. "The ears, they were — felt. Construction paper? He made them himself, I think. Or the teacher. Someone made ears." She paused. She touched the rim of the water glass. "What was the animal?"

No one answered.

"It was —" She stopped. She looked at her hand on the glass. The pause extended. It became something else — not a pause but a vacancy, a place where a thought had been standing a moment ago and now was not. Her brow creased slightly, the way a person's brow creases when they walk into a room and can't remember why.

"Anyway," she said. "It doesn't matter."

Margot went back to the textbook page she was not reading. The father stared at the silent television. The mother stood from the armchair and carried her glass to the kitchen and poured the water down the drain and set the glass in the sink and turned off the hallway light.

The hallway went dark. The house settled into its noises: the furnace, the pipes, the tick of the porch contracting in the cold. From behind the closed door of the boy's bedroom, nothing.
