# The Queen of Nothing

## I.

I have a room with a window that faces the ocean, which sounds grander than it is. The ocean off Coney Island in November is not the ocean of postcards. It is a flat gray thing that smells of diesel and brine, and it does nothing all day but repeat itself, which I suppose is why I find it tolerable company. I am seventy-three years old. I have a tin of butter cookies that a woman from the boardinghouse kitchen brings me on Tuesdays, and I have my cigarettes, and I have, apparently, you.

You want to know about Adelaide.

Everyone who comes wants to know about Adelaide, eventually, though they circle it first the way a man circles a puddle — they ask about Alexander, about the golden age, about the Persian drag and the trick cabinets and whether I ever saw Kellar drop his glass bowl. They want the spectacle. They want to hear about the days when a magician could fill the Academy of Music for a week and the gaslights would catch the silk scarves midair and turn them into something the audience would swear was actual fire. I can give you all of that. I have given it before. But what you are really here for, whether you know it yet or not, is the woman who kept performing after the man died, and what it did to her, and what it looked like from where I stood, which was close enough to touch her and too far away to stop anything.

Adelaide is dead. She has been dead since 1932. She died in an apartment on West 71st Street with almost no money and a closet full of equipment she could no longer lift, and the obituary in the *Times* was six lines long and misspelled Herrmann. I know because I cut it out and I have it still, in the drawer with the cookies, and if you want to see it you may, but I warn you it will tell you nothing. Six lines. She performed for thirty-six years after Alexander went into the ground and they gave her six lines and they got the name wrong and that, I think, tells you more about what happened than anything I could say, but I am going to say it anyway because I am the only one left who saw it and the dead are owed their autopsies.

I loved her. Let me say that now so it does not become the revelation. I loved her the way you love someone you work beside for three decades in the dark — not romantically, though I have been asked, and not maternally, though she was twelve years older than I was. I loved her the way a stagehand loves a performer she has watched every night for ten thousand nights: with total knowledge and no power whatsoever. I knew what she was doing to herself. I knew it for years. And I handed her the props and I set the wires and I watched from the wings and I did not stop her because she did not want to be stopped and because, God help me, it was beautiful.

You have your notebook open. Good. I will not repeat myself.

## II.

The first time I saw them perform together I was nineteen years old and I had been hired six days earlier to manage the backdrop rigging at the Academy of Music in New York, which in 1895 was still the kind of theater where the seats smelled of cigar smoke and rosewater in equal measure and the ushers wore gloves. I was not hired to work the Herrmann show specifically. I was hired generally, for the season, and I lugged sandbags and tied off fly lines for whoever came through — dramatic readers, musical acts, a man with trained dogs who urinated on the proscenium. Then the Herrmann company arrived, and my life became what it was.

Alexander was forty-two that year and looked like the Devil's personal attorney. That is not a metaphor I am reaching for — it is what every reviewer in the country said, and they were right. He had the goatee and the arched brows and the fingers, those fingers, which were longer than any fingers had a right to be and moved as though they had each been individually trained, which in a sense they had. He could palm a billiard ball in a hand that appeared to be empty and open. I saw him do it a hundred times and I never once saw the move. Offstage, those same hands were restless, fidgeting, always rolling a coin across the knuckles or shuffling a deck or adjusting his cuffs, as though stillness were a kind of suffocation. He was vain. He stank of pomade and he checked his reflection in every piece of glass he passed, including once, memorably, in the polished lid of a chafing dish at a supper after a show. He was also, when he wanted to be, the most charming man in any room he entered, and he wanted to be constantly.

Adelaide was thirty-two and she was nothing like him and that was the secret of the act.

Where Alexander was all flourish — the cape, the patter, the diabolical eyebrows — Adelaide was stillness. She stood where she was supposed to stand and she moved when she was supposed to move and she did both with a precision so absolute it looked, to the audience, like passivity. They thought she was the scenery. The beautiful woman who holds the box while the man does the magic. They were wrong. I knew they were wrong by my third night in the wings because by my third night I had begun to see what she was actually doing, and what she was actually doing was the entire act.

Let me tell you about the Strobeika Pass. I am going to describe it to you exactly once and I need you to remember it because it comes back later and when it comes back it will mean something different and if you do not remember the first version you will not feel the second one properly and I am too old and too tired to have carried this for forty years only to have it land wrong.

The Strobeika Pass was their signature vanish. It was not Alexander's invention — the principle was old, older than either of them — but they had rebuilt it from the ground so completely that no one who saw it ever connected it to its origins. Here is what the audience saw: Adelaide stood at center stage, inside a cabinet of dark wood and glass. Alexander circled the cabinet, tapping its sides — you could hear the solidity, the knock of his knuckles against real wood. He opened the glass door. Adelaide stood inside, hands at her sides, wearing a white gown with a train that pooled on the cabinet floor. Alexander closed the door. He made a gesture — a sweep of both hands, fingers spread, as though he were pulling a curtain of air across the cabinet. Then he opened the door again and Adelaide was gone. The gown was gone. The cabinet was empty. A beat of silence. Then Adelaide appeared at the back of the house, in the center aisle, wearing the same gown, as though she had simply decided to be elsewhere.

That is what the audience saw.

Here is what happened.

The cabinet had a false back, but not the kind you are imagining. It was not a hidden door. It was a panel that moved on oiled tracks, silently, triggered by a spring-loaded latch under the cabinet floor. When Alexander circled the cabinet and knocked, the knocking was not to demonstrate solidity — it was a rhythmic cue. Adelaide counted the knocks. On the fourth knock, she shifted her weight to her left foot, which depressed the latch. The panel began to slide. She had exactly three seconds to turn her body sideways, pull the train of the gown with her left hand so it would not catch, and step backward through the opening into a lightless channel behind the false back, which was eighteen inches wide. Eighteen inches. I measured it once. In that channel, in complete darkness, she stripped the white gown — it was constructed with a single concealed seam that let it fall open like a page — and she put on a second, identical gown that was hanging on a hook inside the channel. The first gown she folded and pressed into a cavity in the channel wall. Then she moved, in darkness, through a passage that ran beneath the stage, up a set of steps behind the rear wall of the theater, and into the aisle, where she emerged into the light as though materializing from the air itself.

The entire sequence, from the fourth knock to her appearance in the aisle, took eleven seconds.

Alexander's contribution to those eleven seconds was to stand in front of the cabinet and gesticulate.

I do not say this to diminish him. His gesticulation was essential — it held the eye, it created the theatrical frame within which the impossible could appear to occur. He was superb at it. But the mechanism of the trick, the physical danger of it, the precision required, the eighteen inches of darkness and the three-second window and the twin gowns and the silent pivot — that was Adelaide, every night, and she never missed.

But here is what I really need you to understand, and this is the part that is hard to say because it was not mechanical but human and I am better with gears than with people.

The cue. The knocking. Four knocks and Adelaide shifted her weight. That is the technical fact. But what I saw, watching from stage left with my hand on the fly line, was not a cue. It was a conversation. Alexander knocked and Adelaide *listened* — not with the fixed attention of a professional counting beats but with the specific quality of a woman who knows the man knocking so well that the rhythm of his knuckles on wood is as familiar as his breathing in the dark beside her. And on the fourth knock, when she shifted her weight, there was a moment — it lasted less than a second, it would never have been visible from the house, I am not even certain it was conscious — when she looked at him through the glass. Not at the audience. At him. And he, in the middle of his grand circling, looked back. Their eyes met through the glass door of the cabinet an instant before she vanished, and what passed between them in that instant was not a cue and not a performance and not anything that belonged to the audience at all. It was private. It was the look of two people who had done this together so many times that the trick had become a language and the language had become a skin and they were, in that moment, inside something no one else could enter.

Then the panel moved. And she was gone. And Alexander turned to the audience with his arms wide and his eyebrows raised and the audience gasped, and they were right to gasp, but they were gasping at the wrong miracle.

That is what she lost.

That is what I need you to understand she lost.

## III.

He died on a train. December 17, 1896. They were traveling from Rochester to Bradford, Pennsylvania, and he was in the dining car, and his heart stopped, and that was that. He was forty-three years old. I was not on the train. I had stayed behind in New York to oversee the crating of equipment for the next leg of the tour — we were supposed to go south after Bradford, into the Carolinas and then Atlanta. Someone sent a telegram to the theater office where I was sorting flash cotton into lots and I read it standing next to a crate stenciled HERRMANN THE GREAT in gold letters and I thought, absurdly, that someone should take a brush and change the lettering now, immediately, as though the crate did not know and should be told.

The funeral was large, theatrical, crowded with magicians. I will not describe it. Funerals are for the living and what the living did with Alexander's death was make speeches and jockey for his theater bookings and I was not feeling generous enough then to find that human rather than repulsive.

What I will describe is the Tuesday after the funeral.

I went to the brownstone on West 69th Street where Alexander and Adelaide had lived, to begin inventorying the equipment that was stored in the parlor-level rooms they had converted into a workshop. I expected to find Adelaide in mourning. I expected drawn curtains, black crepe, silence, the whole Victorian apparatus of visible grief, and I would not have been surprised to find her still in bed, because the man she had lived beside and performed beside and slept beside for twenty-one years had died four days earlier on a train between two towns she would never want to see again.

Instead I found her in the workshop with her sleeves rolled to the elbows, dismantling the false-bottomed trunk they used in the second act.

She had a screwdriver in one hand and a notebook in the other and she was making annotations in a hand so steady it frightened me. She looked up when I came in and said, "Edna, the spring mechanism on the Dobler box is inadequate for a solo presentation. The timing assumes a distraction at stage right that I will not have. I need you to find Müller and ask him if he can modify the release to a three-count instead of a five."

Those were the first words she said to me after her husband died. Not *thank you for coming.* Not *I don't know what to do.* A technical specification.

I said, "Adelaide —"

"The Kempelen cabinet is also wrong," she said. "The misdirection depends entirely on Alexander's patter during the load. I will need to replace the patter with a physical bit — a scarf routine, maybe, something the audience watches with their eyes, not their ears. Can you get me a bolt of white China silk by Thursday?"

"Adelaide," I said again.

She looked at me and her face was perfectly composed and perfectly dry and she said, "I have written to the managers at Keith's and at Tony Pastor's and at the Bijou in Philadelphia. I have proposed a twelve-week solo engagement beginning in March. I will need a new program. The billing will be *Adelaide Herrmann, Queen of Magic*. I need you to help me redesign every piece of equipment in this room for a single operator."

She turned back to the trunk and drove the screwdriver into a hinge.

I admired her for this. I want you to write that down. I stood in that room and I watched her work and I thought: *This is what strength looks like. This is a woman who will not be destroyed.* I admired the steadiness of her hands and the clarity of her planning and the way she had already, four days after his death, begun the architect's work of rebuilding the act around the hole where he had been.

That admiration is one of the things I have not forgiven myself for.

Because what I know now, and did not know then, is that there is a kind of industriousness that is not strength but panic. There is a way of moving fast that is not moving forward but moving *away* — away from the still room, away from the silence, away from the particular quality of air in a space where a body has recently stopped being expected. Adelaide redesigned seven major illusions in sixteen days. She slept, by my count, perhaps four hours a night. She ate when I put food in front of her and sometimes not then. She never mentioned Alexander — not his death, not his life, not his name. She referred to the act as it had been in the passive voice: "the misdirection *was* handled at stage right," "a second operator *was* used for the load." As though the second operator had been a mechanical device, now discontinued. As though she were revising a patent rather than amputating a marriage.

She began rehearsing the morning after the funeral. The morning after the funeral. That sentence should disturb you. If it does not, I have told it wrong.

## IV.

The first solo engagement was not Keith's or Pastor's. It was the Bijou Theatre in Philadelphia, because the Bijou's manager, a man named Gilmore who wore his ambition like a rash, saw the publicity value of a widow-magician and booked her for a Thursday-through-Saturday before anyone more cautious could make up their minds. March 11, 1897. I know the date the way I know my own birthday, though I celebrated neither.

The Bijou was not a grand theater, but it was a real one — twelve hundred seats, a proper fly system, decent wing space. I had been there twice before with the Herrmann company. Alexander had liked the Bijou because the acoustics were forgiving and the stage manager, a Swede named Lindqvist, ran the light cues without being asked twice. I remember Alexander standing in the Bijou's green room before a show in the spring of 1895, examining himself in the mirror — there was always a mirror — and complaining that the radiator made a ticking sound that disrupted his patter during the third illusion. "The audience will think the ticking is part of the effect," Adelaide had said from the costume trunk, where she was mending a torn seam. "They will think you have a clock hidden somewhere." "I do have a clock hidden somewhere," Alexander had said. "In the fourth illusion. Which is why I cannot have a competing clock in the third." And Adelaide had laughed, and the laugh had bounced off the green room's tin ceiling, and I had been crouched behind a flat sorting flash paper and I heard it, and I remember it, and that is the kind of thing I remember — not the performances but the laughter between the performances, the green rooms and the ironing boards and the small human sounds that stages were built to amplify and never did.
