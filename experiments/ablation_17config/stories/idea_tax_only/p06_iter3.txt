# The Minutes of All Things

## I.

The wall clock in Room 104-B of the Elsinore County Municipal Services Building had a hum. Not a tick — the mechanism was digital, or quartz, or whatever the county had purchased in bulk from an office-supply catalog in 2011 — but a hum, a low electrical drone at the precise frequency of a fluorescent ballast, which meant it harmonized with the overhead lights in a way that produced, if you sat in the room long enough, the aural equivalent of beige. Carmen Reyes had sat in the room long enough. She had sat in it for twenty-three years, or, to use the unit of measurement she preferred — having once calculated the figure during a recess on a drainage-impact appeal, and having immediately wished she hadn't — one thousand, one hundred and twenty-seven meetings. Give or take. She had stopped updating the count after the drainage recess. Some numbers, once known, reorganize the way you see your life, and Carmen had enough organizational problems.

She opened her laptop. The recording software loaded — a waveform flat-lined across the screen like a patient waiting to be resuscitated. Beside the laptop, a legal pad, college-ruled, the paper backup she maintained not because county policy required it but because she had once, in 2016, lost an entire session to a software crash and had to reconstruct the minutes of a forty-five-minute debate about a retaining-wall setback from memory. Her memory had been fine. Something else about the experience had not been fine — something about the act of reconstructing the official record of what had happened from the unofficial record inside her skull, and the way the two records had disagreed in small, persistent ways she could not resolve — and so now she kept the legal pad.

The chair called the meeting to order at 7:02 p.m. Two minutes late. Carmen noted the actual time in the legal pad and typed the scheduled time into the official document. This was not dishonesty. It was the first translation of the evening, the most innocuous one: the conversion of what *happened* into what had been *agreed to have happened*, which was the purpose of minutes, which was the purpose of Carmen.

"Good evening. This is the regular monthly meeting of the Elsinore County Zoning and Land Use Commission, held pursuant to Chapter 14, Section 3 of the county code. Let the record reflect that a quorum is present."

Carmen's fingers moved. They had been moving like this for so long that the movement had passed through skill, through habit, through reflex, and into something she could only describe as geological. Her hands were strata. The words passed through them the way water passes through limestone — slowly, chemically, leaving deposits.

*Meeting called to order at 7:00 p.m. by Chair Kaye. Quorum established.*

She watched the words appear on her screen with the mild, dissociated interest of a woman watching a spider work a web on the far side of a window. The words were correct. The words were always correct. The words bore approximately the same relationship to what was actually happening in Room 104-B as a topographic map bears to the experience of climbing a mountain — which is to say, they were useful, schematic, and almost perfectly drained of oxygen.

The chair moved to old business. Carmen's screen filled with references to prior actions: motion 2024-47, the Garner Street setback; resolution 2024-12, the amended noise threshold for mixed-use parcels along Route 9; the continued tabling of the Lakeview Estates drainage study, which had been continued and tabled so many times that Carmen had once, in a moment of private levity she would never share, assigned it a nickname in her legal pad: *Godot*.

Her fingers paused.

It was a small pause. An eighth-rest in the ongoing transcription. She was scanning the minutes from last month's meeting — she always pulled them up for reference during old business, a professional habit as automatic as breathing — and her eyes had snagged on a sentence in the record of the easement approval for the Mendez property on Caldwell Lane. The sentence read:

*Motion to approve a ten-foot utility easement along the eastern boundary of Lot 22, Block 7, Caldwell Lane, was carried unanimously, the ground having been opened and the terms set forth therein being found sufficient to bear what passes through.*

She had typed this sentence. She recognized her own phrasing, her own rhythm. But the clause at the end — *the ground having been opened and the terms set forth therein being found sufficient to bear what passes through* — was not language she would use. It was not language *anyone* would use in a zoning easement. It read like legal boilerplate that had been infected by something older, something that understood "easement" in its original sense: the easing of a passage, the granting of a right to cross from one territory to another, the opening of ground.

Carmen stared at the sentence. The fluorescent lights hummed overhead in their consensual key of institutional patience. The wall clock hummed with them. The two hums made a third hum, felt rather than heard, that lived somewhere in the lower register of the room itself, as though the building were digesting.

She corrected nothing.

She had learned not to correct these feelings. The feelings were not the problem. The feelings — the small snags, the syntactic wrongnesses, the moments when a word on her screen seemed to pulse with a valence it should not possess — had been arriving for years, maybe always, maybe since her first meeting in January 2002 when she was twenty-six and temping through an agency and the woman who had held the position before her had quit without notice, leaving behind a desk drawer full of legal pads covered in a handwriting that grew progressively less linear as the pages turned, the final page reading only, in letters pressed so hard the pen had torn through, *They will not stop deciding.* Carmen had thrown the pads away. She had taken the position full-time. She had learned the software, mastered the Robert's Rules, filed 1,127 sets of minutes with the county clerk, and built a career on the premise that the feelings — the snags, the wrongnesses, the creeping suspicion that the room was larger than its walls, that the air above the commissioners' heads occasionally thickened into something with texture and intent — were artifacts of repetition, floaters in the eye of someone who had stared at fluorescence too long.

She resumed typing.

*Chair Kaye directed the commission's attention to unresolved items from the September session. The Lakeview Estates drainage study remains tabled pending receipt of the revised environmental assessment from the county engineer's office.*

Godot, Carmen thought, and almost smiled. But the smile stalled. The air above the empty chair at the far end of the dais — Commissioner Phan's seat; Phan was on medical leave, gallbladder — shimmered faintly, as though heat were rising from the laminate surface, and Carmen saw, or thought she saw, or experienced the perceptual event she had long ago stopped trying to categorize as either seeing or thinking, a shape in the shimmer: a column, fluted, weight-bearing, rising from the folding table into a ceiling that was, for one-sixteenth of a second, not a ceiling but a vault, stone-groined and enormous, receding upward into a darkness that was not dark but *full*.

Then it was a drop ceiling. Then it was Room 104-B. Then the chair said, "Moving to new business," and Carmen's fingers resumed their geological work, and the clock hummed, and the lights hummed, and the room hummed with the particular frequency of a place where reality has been agreed upon so many times that the agreement itself has become structural, and load-bearing, and impossible to question without bringing the whole thing down.

## II.

Dolores Kaye arrived first, as she always did. Twelve minutes before the meeting, heels audible in the hallway — a measured, deliberate rhythm that Carmen had long ago identified as not walking but *approaching*. Dolores wore preparation the way other people wore perfume: it preceded her, announced her, and lingered in the room after she left. Tonight she carried a three-ring binder tabbed with twelve colored dividers for an agenda that contained six items. Carmen knew this because she could see the binder's thickness in relation to its owner's grip, and because she had spent twenty-three years watching people carry documents into Room 104-B, and the way a person carried a document told you everything about the distance between who they were and who they needed the document to prove they were.

Dolores had been, before this commission, a two-term congresswoman for the 14th District. "Had been" in the specific sense that involved a campaign-finance irregularity, a committee hearing, a resignation framed as a "return to community service," and a two-year gap in her résumé that she bridged by obtaining a certificate in municipal governance from the state extension program. She chaired the commission with the hyper-competence of a surgeon performing routine stitches — each procedural step executed with a precision wildly disproportionate to the wound. Carmen understood. The stitches were not for the wound. The stitches were for the surgeon's hands, which needed to remember they still knew how to heal something.

"Good evening, Carmen."

"Good evening, Chair Kaye."

This was the entirety of their personal interaction. It had been the entirety for three years. Neither woman varied the words. Carmen sometimes thought that if someone were to transcribe *their* exchange — the micro-meeting that occurred each night between the transcriber and the chair — the minutes would be the most accurate document she had ever produced: two women, quorum of mutual recognition established, no old business, no new business, adjourned.

Terrell Walker came in next, and the way he came in told you everything: sideways through the door, as though the door might close on him if he committed to a full frontal entry. He wore a name badge from the Elsinore County School District cafeteria — TERRELL, FOOD SERVICES — which he sometimes forgot to remove and sometimes, Carmen suspected, left on deliberately, a talisman of his real life pinned over the borrowed costume of civic responsibility. He had been appointed to the commission six months ago via the county's mandatory-service protocol, a process that shared its administrative DNA with jury duty and that Terrell treated with the same mix of baffled compliance and suppressed outrage. Carmen had watched him, at his first meeting, pull the summons letter from his jacket pocket and unfold it on the table as though checking the terms of a contract he had not agreed to. He still carried the letter. She'd seen its outline through the fabric.

"Evening," he said to the room, to no one, and took his seat with the posture of a man sitting in someone else's chair, which, in every sense Carmen could measure, he was. He asked good questions — practical, unornamental, drawn from the deep logic of a person who had spent years solving actual problems (how to feed four hundred children on a budget that presumed three hundred; how to route a lunch line through a cafeteria designed by someone who had never seen a child) — and these questions had a habit of landing in the exact procedural spot where they did the most structural damage. He did not know this. He thought he was asking about trash removal. He was asking about the nature of obligation.

Mika Torres arrived third, phone already raised, and Carmen recognized the gesture from a thousand prior observers but not a single prior commissioner: Mika was *documenting*. She was seventeen and should not have been here. She had applied for a summer internship in the county communications office — social media, event photography, the digital-infrastructure work that local governments perpetually needed and perpetually underfunded — and had, through a misrouted application and a human-resources error that no one had yet corrected because no one had yet noticed, been placed instead in the Zoning and Land Use Division, where she had been given a laminated badge, a folding chair, and instructions to "shadow the commission and take notes for the departmental archive." That had been four months ago. She was still here. She took notes on her phone with the fluency of a generation that experienced the world as content, but her notes — Carmen had glimpsed them once, over Mika's shoulder, during a recess — were not the disengaged jottings of a bored intern. They were ethnography. *DK taps binder when contradicted. TW touches letter in pocket when he agrees with something but won't say so. GD uses "frankly" before every statement that is not frank.* Carmen had read these notes with the complicated feeling of a veteran watching a recruit display, on her first day, an instinct that the veteran had spent decades training into herself.

General Arthur Clement Denny, United States Army, Retired, entered fourth, and the room contracted slightly, as rooms do when someone accustomed to commanding space crosses a threshold. He was seventy-one, compact, silver-haired, and held himself with the particular stillness of a man who understood that controlled immobility was a more effective display of force than movement. His path to the commission was the one Carmen found most grimly comic in a field of strong contenders: he had filed a noise complaint. The HVAC unit on his neighbor's roof was exceeding the decibel threshold laid out in Section 9.4 of the county noise ordinance, and General Denny had attended a public comment session to testify to this effect, and his testimony had been so detailed — he had produced sound-level readings, referenced the applicable code, cited three precedents from adjacent jurisdictions, and delivered the entire performance with the calm authority of a man briefing a joint staff — that the commission had appointed him to fill a vacancy the following month. He had been here for two years. He called agenda items "briefings." He referred to the meeting room as "the chamber." He treated every procedural question as a matter of strategic concern because, for General Denny, the world was composed entirely of zones — not in the municipal sense, but in the military one: areas to be secured, perimeters to be maintained, ground to be held or yielded according to calculations that ran ceaselessly behind his pale eyes.

"Chair Kaye," he said, and it was a greeting and a positional acknowledgment: I know where you are. I know where I am. We are adjacent. The negotiation is ongoing.

Sonja Bey arrived last, three minutes after the scheduled start, carrying the faint, warm scent of cumin and woodsmoke that Carmen had come to associate with the gap between Sonja's day job and her evening obligation. She owned and operated *Sonja's*, a food truck that served Yemeni-inflected street food from a converted step van parked, depending on the week, at one of seven rotating locations in the county, each of which required a permit, each of which expired, each of which she had renewed and re-renewed so many times that she had become, without intending to, the county's foremost civilian expert on mobile-vendor permitting law. It was this expertise that had landed her here. Her own permit appeal — she was contesting a denial for a permanent Saturday location at the Millrace Park farmers' market — had been "next on the docket" for so long that it had undergone a procedural metamorphosis: from agenda item to recurring old business to tabled matter to standing joke to, finally, a kind of background hum, like the clock, like the lights, like the room itself. When Commissioner Stein resigned to move to Arizona, Sonja's name had been put forward by Dolores, who had reasoned that no petitioner who had attended that many meetings could be less qualified than most appointees. This was correct. It was also, Carmen thought, a species of capture: Sonja had been absorbed into the machine she had come to petition, and now sat on the dais instead of before it, and her permit appeal was still next on the docket.

Sonja did not greet the room. She sat, opened a worn manila folder, uncapped a pen, and wrote something at the top of the first page. Carmen, from her angle, could just make it out: *Day 247.* Sonja was also counting.

The meeting began.

"Item one," Dolores said, her voice occupying the room with the practiced acoustic authority of someone who once addressed the House floor. "Fence-height exception, 414 Garner Street. The applicant requests a variance from the 42-inch maximum to allow a 60-inch privacy fence along the southern property line, citing documented harassment from the adjacent parcel. Staff report is in your packets."

Carmen typed.

General Denny opened his binder to the staff report. "I note the applicant's claim of harassment is unsubstantiated in the record."

"The police reports are attached as Exhibit C," Dolores said.

"Police reports document *calls*. They don't document *harassment*. The distinction is operative."

Terrell, who had been reading the staff report with the quietly focused expression of a man who did not take reading for granted, looked up. "The neighbor threw a dead cat over the fence."

A pause.

"Alleged," General Denny said.
