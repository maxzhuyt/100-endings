# The Committee for Tuesday

## I.

The fluorescent tube above Maeve Delacroix's desk had been dying for three weeks. It didn't buzz—that would have been too dramatic an act of rebellion for a Phillips Alto II 32-watt linear lamp installed, according to the maintenance log she had once filed a request to inspect, in November of the previous year. Instead it *pulsed*. A barely perceptible flicker at 1.3-second intervals that she had timed with the second hand of the wall clock during a particularly barren stretch of a Tuesday afternoon while waiting for her tax audit software to load. The interval was consistent. She found this neither comforting nor distressing. It was simply information, and Maeve Delacroix had long ago arranged her relationship with the world along the axis of information that could be filed and information that could not.

Her desk occupied the second-to-last position in a row of seven desks in the Municipal Revenue Bureau's open-plan office on the fourth floor of the Garfield Building, which was neither named after the president nor the cat but after a local developer named Harold Garfield who had, according to the plaque in the lobby that was itself partially obscured by a vending machine, "contributed generously to the civic improvement of this district." The desks were particleboard with a birch veneer. Maeve's had a small divot in the upper left corner where someone, years before her tenure, had apparently pressed a pen too enthusiastically into the surface. She kept a ceramic pot of paper clips over the divot. The pot was shaped like a frog. It had been a gift from her mother.

At 11:47 a.m. on this Tuesday, Maeve completed the adjustment review on the Petersen account (property tax, residential, discrepancy in the assessed vs. declared square footage of a garage conversion that may or may not have included a half-bath), printed the summary to the shared HP LaserJet that always smelled faintly of burned toast, and stood to retrieve it.

On her way past Greg Lundquist's desk—the last desk in the row, pressed against the wall with its sad view of the parking structure—she noticed that Greg was eating yogurt. Specifically: a Stonyfield Organic French Vanilla, 5.3-ounce cup. He ate this every day at approximately this time, peeling the foil lid back three-quarters of the way and folding it into a small square that he placed on the exact same corner of his desk. He used the same spoon—a tarnished silver teaspoon from home that he washed in the break room sink with a thoroughness that bordered on liturgical.

"That Petersen thing is going to flag," Greg said without looking up.

"The garage doesn't have a half-bath," Maeve said. "I called the assessor."

Greg snorted. It was his Tuesday snort—not his Friday snort, which had a whistling quality she attributed to weekend anticipation, and not his Monday snort, which was more of a grunt. She collected the printout. The LaserJet was warm. The paper smelled of burned toast. She returned to her desk.

At 12:15 p.m., she microwaved the leftover pasta she had packed that morning in a Pyrex container with a blue lid. The microwave in the fourth-floor break room was a Panasonic NN-SN67K, and its turntable had a slight wobble that caused the container to migrate toward the door over the course of its rotation, which meant she heated her pasta for two minutes and fifteen seconds rather than two minutes, to account for the uneven energy distribution. The pasta was penne. The sauce was marinara from a jar—Rao's, because her mother had told her once that life was too short for Ragú and too unpredictable for homemade, and this had struck Maeve as the most concise philosophy of moderation she had ever encountered.

She ate at her desk. The penne was slightly overcooked on the edges and cool in the center. The overhead light pulsed. Greg folded his yogurt lid. The clock read 12:32, then 12:33, then 12:34. An audit was due by four. She opened the file. The numbers arranged themselves in their familiar columns. The afternoon passed.

She drove home in a 2017 Honda Civic (silver, 67,000 miles, a scratch on the rear bumper from a shopping cart incident she had been too honest to file an insurance claim for) and ate a turkey sandwich on wheat bread while watching forty-five minutes of a show about people renovating houses in regions where houses should arguably not be built, and went to sleep at 10:20 p.m. in a bed that was slightly too firm but not worth replacing.

Then it was Tuesday.

Not *the next* Tuesday. Not *another* Tuesday in the way that Tuesdays come, as they do, with the dull reliability of a bus that is neither early nor late but simply present at intervals so regular they cease to be events and become atmosphere. This was *the same* Tuesday. She knew it the way you know a word is misspelled before you can say which letter is wrong—a sensation not in the mind but in the body, a proprioceptive flinch, as if the world had shifted one millimeter to the left and every nerve she had was calibrated to notice.

The fluorescent tube pulsed at 1.3-second intervals. The Petersen file was in her queue, unflagged. The microwave wobbled. The penne was overcooked on the edges and cool in the center. The afternoon assembled itself from the same parts, in the same order, with the same weight.

At 11:47 a.m., she passed Greg's desk.

"That Petersen thing is going to flag," Greg said.

"The garage doesn't have a half-bath," she said. "I called the assessor."

Greg snorted—his Tuesday snort—and then laughed, suddenly, at nothing.

He laughed a half-second too late—not late relative to anything she'd said but late relative to himself, as if the laugh had been triggered by a signal that had to travel a greater distance than it should have. The sound was correct. The pitch, the duration, the way it tapered into a nasal exhale through the left nostril—all Greg, all genuine, all verifiable against the extensive catalog of Greg's vocalizations she had accumulated through four years of sitting six feet from his face. But his eyes didn't move. His mouth performed the laugh, his chest participated, his spoon hand even gestured in that small circular way it always did when Greg was amused—but his eyes were fixed on a point slightly above her left shoulder with a stillness that belonged to an object rather than a person. Two seconds. Maybe less. Then his eyes caught up, animated, and he was Greg again, spooning yogurt, folding foil.

Maeve collected her printout. The LaserJet was warm. The paper smelled of burned toast.

She returned to her desk and sat with her hands on the particleboard and her fingers one inch from the ceramic frog and thought: *No.* She thought: *You slept oddly. You dreamed something and the residue of the dream is making you read significance into a perfectly ordinary moment with a perfectly ordinary man who has been eating the same yogurt at the same time every day for years, which is, if anything, evidence of his comprehensive realness, his exhaustive humanity, because what is a person if not a collection of habits so specific and pointless that no designing intelligence would bother to replicate them.*

She thought: *The yogurt was right. The spoon was right. The snort was right.*

She thought: *His eyes didn't move.*

She was not imagining it.

The fluorescent tube pulsed at 1.3-second intervals, which she had clocked weeks ago and confirmed today at the same time as before, except this time—this second time, on this second first time—the interval between pulsations at 2:47 p.m. expanded to 1.6 seconds for a single cycle before returning to 1.3. She noticed this too. She noticed this because she was the kind of person who noticed the interval of a fluorescent tube, who timed it with the second hand of a wall clock, who packed the same lunch in the same container and heated it for two minutes and fifteen seconds to account for a wobbling turntable—and who would therefore be precisely, uniquely, agonizingly equipped to detect that a repeated day contained a defect so small it would have to be measured in fractions of a second, in the displacement of a pair of eyes, in the absence of motion where motion should be.

She went home. She ate a turkey sandwich on wheat bread. She did not watch the show. She sat on the edge of her slightly-too-firm bed and stared at the wall and at 10:20 p.m. she did not fall asleep but eventually unconsciousness came for her with the indifference of something punching a clock.

Then it was Tuesday.

## II.

Several Tuesdays later—a quantity she had initially tracked on a notepad in precise tally marks and then abandoned when the notepad reset each morning, so that she tried scratching a mark into her forearm with a paperclip and found it healed each loop, so that she began simply holding the number in her mind like a stone in her mouth, seven, twelve, the number grew slippery, she thought it might be nineteen, or thirty, or a number that didn't correspond to a human experience of counting—the barista at the coffee shop on the corner of Welles and Fourth smiled at her with too many teeth.

Not more teeth. The same number of teeth arranged to produce more smile than the architecture of a human mouth should accommodate. Maeve took her coffee—medium drip, black, the same order she'd placed on the first Tuesday and every Tuesday since—and walked out and stood on the sidewalk and looked at the coffee and thought: *Did she always hold the cup at that angle when she handed it to me?* She couldn't remember. The recollection was there but it felt laminated, smooth and sealed, resistant to the kind of interrogation she needed to perform on it.

Her neighbor, Dale, who lived in 4C and who reliably appeared in the hallway at 7:15 a.m. on his way to a job she had never asked about, said, "Hey, Maeve—how was your weekend?"

It was Tuesday morning. There had been no weekend. There had been nothing but Tuesday for—the number slipped—a long time.

"Fine," she said. Dale nodded and smiled in a way that was technically correct and closed his door and Maeve stood in the hallway and stared at the door of 4C and the brushed nickel "C" was slightly crooked, it had always been slightly crooked, that was right, but the screwhead at the top of the letter was a Phillips and she could have sworn—she could *not* swear, that was the problem, the laminated quality of her prior memories made every comparison a hypothesis rather than a certainty—that it had been a flathead.

Her mother called at 6:15 p.m. She always called at 6:15 p.m. on Tuesdays. She said, "Hi, sweetheart, just checking in, how was your day, are you eating, you sound tired, I worry about you in that apartment alone, you know your father was the same way, needed his space but then the space got to be too much, are you getting out, are you seeing people—"

Every word correct. Every pause in its accustomed position. The cadence of concern that Maeve had been hearing for thirty-one years, delivered with the specific music of a woman who had raised a daughter alone and measured her love in the frequency of small interruptions. All right. All exactly right. Except the order. *Are you eating* came before *you sound tired.* It always came after. For thirty-one years—through Maeve's adolescence, her brief miserable year at the insurance company, her transfer to the Revenue Bureau, her father's quiet departure from both the marriage and the city, all the way through to this current and apparently permanent Tuesday—her mother had followed a conversational algorithm so consistent it might as well have been scored, and the algorithm went: *how was your day, you sound tired, are you eating.* Tired, then eating. The emotional assessment first, the practical concern second. It was such a small thing. It was such a vanishingly, insanely small thing. But Maeve's mother was a woman who had once driven forty-five minutes to bring her a different brand of cough syrup because she'd bought the cherry flavor and Maeve preferred grape, and a woman like that does not *swap the order of her concerns* any more than the earth swaps the order of its seasons.

"Mom," Maeve said.

"Yes, sweetheart?"

"Say that again."

"Say what again?"

"What you just said. How was your day, and then—"

"Honey, are you all right? You sound tired. Are you eating?"

The order was correct now. Maeve held the phone and listened to her mother talk about a neighbor's new fence and a recipe she'd clipped from a magazine and the weather, which was unremarkable, and she said yes and uh-huh and I love you too, and when she hung up she sat very still for a very long time and tried to determine whether the woman on the phone had been her mother or something that had worn her mother's voice the way you'd wear a coat—competently, plausibly—but with one button fastened in the wrong hole.

She drove to the grocery store. She did not need groceries. The loop would reset before she could eat anything she bought. But the act of choosing a shopping cart and pushing it through the fluorescent aisles and examining the expiration dates on yogurt containers—Stonyfield Organic French Vanilla, she saw, looking at it, and her stomach clenched—felt like the closest thing to agency she had experienced in what might have been weeks.

She did not buy anything. She walked back to the parking lot and sat in her 2017 Honda Civic (silver, 67,000 miles, the scratch on the rear bumper) and placed her hands on the steering wheel and did not start the car.

The parking lot was nearly empty. The store was open twenty-four hours but it was 9:40 p.m. on a Tuesday—the same 9:40 p.m. on the same Tuesday—and the lot held only a scattered handful of vehicles parked under sodium-vapor lamps that turned everything the color of old teeth. The asphalt was wet from a rain that had come at 8:15 p.m. on every iteration of this day, a brief and purposeless shower that lasted eleven minutes and accomplished nothing.

Maeve sat. Her hands on the wheel at ten and two. The engine off. The key in the ignition because the Civic was old enough to require a physical key, and she had sometimes thought of this as an inconvenience but right now it felt like the last honest mechanical relationship in her life: a metal object in a metal slot, cause and effect, no ambiguity.

She thought: *I am losing my mind.*

She thought: *I am not losing my mind. Something is happening and I am noticing it and the noticing is the opposite of losing my mind. The noticing is the mind. The mind is the noticing.*

She thought: *Greg's eyes didn't move.*

She thought: *Mom said "eating" before "tired."*

She thought: *The barista had too many teeth,* and then she thought: *No, the barista had exactly the right number of teeth and that was the problem. The smile was perfect. It was performed without flaw. It was a smile that had been *studied* and the study was detectable only in its perfection, the way a forged signature sometimes fails by being too precise, more consistent than the hand it imitates.*

A shopping cart sat at the far end of the lot, near the cart return corral, angled slightly as if someone had pushed it in the general direction of responsibility and then given up. As Maeve watched—and she could not say why she was watching, only that her eyes had landed on it in the absence of anything else to hold—the cart began to move.

Not dramatically. Not supernaturally. It rolled. A slow, almost apologetic migration across the asphalt, the front left wheel—the broken one, there was always a broken one—stuttering in its caster as the cart traced a long, meaningless curve away from the corral and toward the center of the lot. There was no wind. She was certain of this because the receipt she had declined at checkout but that had printed anyway and that she had placed on the dashboard was perfectly still. No wind. But the cart rolled.
