# Gerald Huang's Cul-de-Sac

## I.

The government van was the color of nothing. Not gray, not white, not silver — the color of a thing that had been designed by a committee whose single goal was for no one to ever describe it. It had no logo. It had no windows in the back. It came down Mockingbird Lane at four in the afternoon on the first Thursday of summer, and it parked in front of the Kessler house, which hadn't been the Kessler house for eleven months, not since Mrs. Kessler moved to Scottsdale and announced she was done with "weather states," and which was now just the beige house with the lawn that Mr. Pemberton called a "disgrace to the association."

I was sitting in my window. I should say that. The window is important.

It's the big one in my bedroom, the one that looks out over our side yard and directly onto the front walk of the beige house. My mother says I spend too much time there. She says it like it's an accusation, but she's never once told me what I should be spending that time doing instead, so I think it's more of an observation she hasn't finished thinking through. I've been watching the cul-de-sac from that window since I was old enough to kneel on a chair and press my face to the screen. Six houses in a circle, and mine has the best angle. I can see four front doors, three driveways, the communal mailbox station, and, if Mrs. Delgado leaves her kitchen blinds up, everything from her stove to her dining table. I'm not a spy. I'm a person who pays attention to what's happening within visible range, which, if you think about it, is what everyone's eyes are for.

Two men in plain clothes got out of the front of the van. They walked to the back and opened the double doors, and one of them said something I couldn't hear but that had the shape of a sentence meant to sound patient. Then Gerald Huang got out.

The first thing was size. He was enormous. Not tall-enormous, though he was tall — maybe six-two or six-three, which I can estimate because my father is five-ten and I have spent a lot of time calibrating people against my father. Gerald was six-two or six-three and then also *wide*, wide in a way that didn't look athletic or fat but structural, like someone had built a person out of load-bearing walls. His shoulders were the kind that made his government-issued polo shirt look like a punishment. His hands, even from my window, were visibly too big. Not cartoon big. Problem big. Big the way something is big when it was designed for a purpose that no longer applies.

He stood on the lawn — the disgrace lawn, the dead-grass lawn — and he looked at the house. He looked at it the way I imagined someone would look at a word in a language they'd studied but never spoken aloud. Like he understood it theoretically.

One of the plain-clothes men handed him a box. A single box. Not even a big box — the kind you'd get from a liquor store, the kind my mother uses when she's donating books to the library sale. He held it against his chest. The other plain-clothes man handed him a set of keys and said something else I couldn't hear, and Gerald nodded as though he were agreeing to terms, and then the two men got back in the color-of-nothing van and drove away and left him on the lawn.

Gerald stood there for approximately ninety seconds. I counted.

Then he walked to the mailbox.

It was the standard cul-de-sac model — powder-coated black metal, the kind with a little door and a red flag that every house on Mockingbird Lane has because the HOA mandates it. He set his box down on the walk. He reached for the mailbox door. He pinched the little handle between his thumb and one finger and pulled, and the door came off in his hand with a sound I could hear through the glass — a short, definitive *pop*, like a soda tab, but made of metal and finality.

He stared at it.

He held the small hinged door and looked at it the way you'd look at a phone that had just autocorrected your message into something unforgivable. Then he reached for the mailbox itself, maybe to test the remaining structure, and as his hand closed around the post the whole thing — box, post, concrete footing — leaned toward him like a drunk, and his grip reflexively tightened, and the post crumpled at its midsection like an aluminum can, and the mailbox that had survived eleven Midwestern winters and Mrs. Kessler's son backing his Civic into it twice simply folded in half and came away from the ground in Gerald Huang's hand.

He held it. He looked at it. He looked at the hole in the ground.

Then he picked up his single box, tucked the destroyed mailbox under his arm like a newspaper, walked to the front door, spent a surprisingly long time working the key, and went inside.

No one on the cul-de-sac came out. Mr. Pemberton's curtain twitched. The Delgados' wind chime moved, but that was the wind. The Okafor twins were at camp. The cul-de-sac held still, the way a pond holds still after something large has fallen into it.

From downstairs, my mother's voice: "Priya, I think the new neighbor's here. We should bring something over."

I stayed in the window.

## II.

What I knew about Gerald Huang, I knew the way you know anything real: in pieces, from sources of varying reliability, assembled over time into something that is probably close to the truth but is definitely shaped like me.

Source one: my parents, who whisper in the kitchen and who have never, in the eleven years I have been alive, understood that the kitchen vent connects to the hallway vent and that sound travels because it is sound and that is what sound does. From them I learned that the man next door was a "government situation," which is a phrase my father uses for anything that involves a federal agency and discomfort. My mother added that the real estate agent had called to "give them a heads-up," which is a phrase that means "warn" but in a voice that doesn't want to be blamed for anything later.

Source two: Mr. Pemberton, who told my father at the community mailbox station — which is different from the individual mailboxes and is where people go to collect packages and exchange theories about each other — that the man had been in a "facility" for "practically his whole life" and that in Mr. Pemberton's opinion the government had "no business putting something like that in a residential zone." Mr. Pemberton is the kind of person who uses the word *something* when he means *someone*, and I have been collecting evidence of this for three years.

Source three: the internet, which is where I went after the whisper-kitchen and the Pemberton-mailbox briefings, and which produced the following: Gerald Huang, born 1951, identified at age nineteen as possessing anomalous physical capabilities, enrolled — and that word, *enrolled*, carried a weight even the government press release couldn't hide — in a federal research program at a facility in New Mexico. Duration of enrollment: forty-three years. Date of program closure: two years ago. IQ assessed — and here the language of the government document did something I'd never seen bureaucratic language do, which was to basically throw up its hands — at "a level exceeding the viable parameters of standard psychometric evaluation," which I told my friend Asha translated roughly to "the kind of number that stops being a number and starts being a weather system."

Forty-three years. He went in when he was nineteen, which meant when he came out he was sixty-two. No children listed. No spouse. No emergency contact except a government department that had since been renamed. The phrase the relocation documents used was "reintegrated into civilian life," which I think about a lot, because it sounds like something you'd say about releasing a wolf into a petting zoo. As though the wolf is the problem. As though the petting zoo doesn't have its own rules, its own violence, its own fences designed to look like they aren't fences.

I found one photograph online. Congressional testimony from 1987, a hearing on the program. Gerald sat behind a table in an institutional room. He looked young. He looked enormous. He looked the way people look when they have been photographed without being asked and have no practice arranging their face. The expression was not anger, not fear, not blankness. It was the expression of a person who is being looked at and knows that being looked at is not the same as being seen.

What the cul-de-sac knew: a large man. Strange. Government. Possibly dangerous.

What I knew: all of that plus the part no one else seemed to think about, which was that forty-three years is a length of time so large that it contains entire lives. He went in before the internet. Before cell phones. Before the self-checkout lane, the smart doorbell, the neighborhood email chain. He came out into a world that had been rebuilt while he was inside, and no one had given him the manual, because there was no manual, because the rest of us had built it around ourselves one day at a time without noticing we were doing it, the way you don't notice your hair growing until someone shows you a photograph from three years ago.

Here is what the cul-de-sac did not know and what I discovered on the fourth night after Gerald moved in:

I couldn't sleep. I went to my window — and yes, I know I said the window was important, and this is one of the reasons — and I looked at Gerald's house. There was a light on in the second bedroom, the one that corresponds to my parents' office in our house, because six houses in a cul-de-sac means six variations on three floor plans, and this is the kind of information you acquire when you have lived somewhere your entire life and have a working memory.

His blinds were down but not closed, which is a mistake only someone who has never had blinds would make. Through the slats I could see the wall. And the wall was covered.

Equations. Or not equations — I'd taken enough math to recognize equations, and these were not like any math I'd encountered. They were symbols and diagrams and branching chains of logic that covered every visible inch of drywall like ivy, written in black marker, small and precise and absolutely dense. In the center was something that might have been a flowchart or might have been a map or might have been the kind of thing that doesn't have a name because only one person has ever thought it. Gerald stood in front of it in a white t-shirt, and I watched him lift his hand — slowly, with the exaggerated care of a man who has learned to be afraid of his own grip — and add three symbols to a chain.

Three symbols, and then he stood back and looked at what he'd done, and the looking lasted so long that I fell asleep in the window and woke up with the screen pattern pressed into my forehead and Gerald's light still on.

The next week, a letter appeared in the HOA's communal mailbox — hand-addressed, stamped with excessive postage, and written on plain white paper in a handwriting so small and tidy it looked like it had been produced by a very intelligent machine experiencing its first emotion. It was a request to replace the individual mailbox he had "inadvertently rendered non-operational." It included a detailed analysis of the metallurgical deficiencies of the standard mailbox model. It proposed three alternative designs with annotated diagrams. It closed with: "I appreciate your time and recognize that the mechanisms of communal governance require patience from all participants, a quality I am in the process of developing."

Mr. Pemberton photocopied it and brought it to my father and said, "You see what I mean."

My father read it and said, "This is actually very polite, Frank."

My mother read it and started making a second casserole.

I was the only person who noticed that the metallurgical analysis was correct, because I looked it up, and the standard HOA mailbox was, in fact, made of an alloy notably susceptible to fatigue stress. Gerald had identified a real problem. He had expressed it with perfect accuracy. And he had done so in a prose style that sounded like an alien's first attempt at human communication — technically flawless, emotionally indecipherable, and somehow still the most honest thing anyone on Mockingbird Lane had written all year.

## III.

The Kroger on Route 9 is the only grocery store within a five-mile radius that hasn't been replaced by a furniture warehouse or an urgent care clinic. It has automatic sliding doors, fluorescent lighting that makes everyone look like they've recently been exhumed, and seventeen aisles organized in a pattern that my father calls "deliberately confusing" and that I believe is just regular confusing because I don't think the Kroger corporation is interested in my father specifically.

I know exactly what happened on the day Gerald went to Kroger because I was there.

Not on purpose. My mother needed cilantro for the third casserole — the casserole campaign had escalated — and she brought me because my father was on a work call and she subscribes to the theory that children left alone will immediately start a fire. We were in produce when I saw him.

He was standing at the entrance, just inside the automatic doors. He had a list in his hand — I could see from three aisles away that it was long and precisely written, likely organized by some logic that made sense if you had never been in a Kroger and were working from first principles. He was wearing a button-down shirt, tucked in, with khaki pants and brand-new white sneakers that looked like they'd been selected from a catalog by someone who had typed "normal shoes" into a search engine. He was holding a shopping cart.

The shopping cart was already dented.

Not dramatically — one of the vertical bars on the child seat had a slight bend, the kind of damage that suggested Gerald had gripped it with the force required to steer a wheelbarrow full of concrete rather than a aluminum cart on plastic wheels. He was standing still, reading his list, and the automatic doors were going through an identity crisis behind him — open, closed, open, closed — because his body mass was large enough to keep triggering the sensor but he was standing just far enough inside that the doors couldn't decide if he was coming or going.

A woman with a toddler needed to get past him. She said, "Excuse me." Gerald moved, and in moving he angled the cart sideways, and the cart's front wheels caught the rubber floor mat and the back wheels locked, and Gerald corrected by pulling the cart toward himself, and the aluminum frame of the child seat folded inward with a crunch that was quiet enough to be ignorable but visible enough that the woman with the toddler gave him a look I have seen adults give each other and have catalogued as the "Oh So You're the Kind of Person Who" face.

Gerald's expression in response was one I had never seen on another human being. It was the face of someone who had just received data that confirmed a hypothesis he'd been desperately hoping was wrong.

He made it to the produce section. I watched from behind a display of bagged salad while my mother squeezed avocados and debated the existence of God with herself, which is what she does when none of the avocados are ripe. Gerald consulted his list. He reached for a bag of apples. He picked it up. The bag tore. Not because it was flimsy — it was a standard produce bag, as structurally competent as any produce bag — but because Gerald's fingers had closed around it with the precision of a man who understood, intellectually, that produce bags required gentle handling, and who had calibrated his grip to what he believed was gentle, and who was wrong.

Five Honeycrisp apples hit the floor and rolled in five directions. Gerald watched them go with the expression of a general watching a formation break.

He retrieved them. This took time, because bending down meant navigating the cart, and navigating the cart meant touching the cart, and touching the cart meant the cart sustained further structural commentary. By the time he had all five apples, the cart's handlebar had a visible thumbprint depression on each side.

Aisle six: canned goods. I had migrated, casually, with appropriate eleven-year-old plausible deniability, to the cereal aisle, which runs parallel and which, if you stand at the far end near the organic granola no one buys, offers a clear sightline through the shelving gap. Gerald was attempting to select a can of soup. He was reading the nutritional label with the focus of someone decoding a transmission. He had been reading it for over a minute. Behind him, a man with a cart waited. The man cleared his throat. Gerald did not move. The man said, "Hey, buddy."
