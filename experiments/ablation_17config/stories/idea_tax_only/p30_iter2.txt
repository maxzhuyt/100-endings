# The Trial of Pell Grindtooth

## I.

Oh, but you should have *seen* Vaelund that morning.

The sun came up the way it always did in the Third Erasure's twelfth year — which is to say sideways, through a scrim of trebuchet-flung livestock, because the Agricultural Redistribution Act of 3E-4 had mandated that all animal husbandry be conducted ballistically, and by dawn the sky over the Clattering Reach was reliably pocked with airborne sheep, their wool contrailing behind them like the tails of sad, bewildered comets. To the east, the city of Gantry Pivot was waking to its customary symphony of collapsing architecture: the facades of the merchant quarter, which were required by zoning ordinance to detach and reattach themselves no fewer than three times per sunrise, were completing their second rotation, and the merchants beneath them were performing the Merchant's Dodge (a kind of lateral waltz, taught in schools, tested annually, fatal to the inattentive) with the elegant, bruised proficiency of a people who had been dodging falling walls for eleven generations and had come to regard a morning without a concussion as evidence of spiritual laxity. To the west, in the Unsteady Fens, a postman named Greel was completing his appointed rounds while being pursued at a dead sprint by forty-six geese, which had been assigned to him by the Bureau of Postal Mayhem pursuant to the Postal Mayhem Act of 3E-12 (Amendment 7: "Regarding the Minimum Ferocity of Mail-Geese and the Conditions Under Which a Postman May Be Permitted to Weep"), and Greel was not weeping — he had used his weeping allotment on Tuesday — but he was bleeding freely from both shins, and his mailbag was on fire, and he was making excellent time, and this, in Vaelund, was called Thursday.

Now look south.

Past the last catapult silo. Past the farm where old Henk Brassfall grew exploding pumpkins (they detonated upon ripeness, naturally; the Consumable Volatility Standards Board permitted no other kind). Past the irrigation ditch that was, by law, dug in the shape of a question mark — no, not a question mark; a question mark *arguing with another question mark*, because the Ditch Ambiguity Mandate of 3E-9 had required all waterways to be "epistemologically uncertain in their trajectory," and the surveyor responsible for this particular ditch had taken the mandate to heart and also to drink.

Past all of that. There.

A fence. A straight fence. A fence that ran from one post to another post without wobbling, leaning, detaching, exploding, or questioning the fundamental nature of its own fencehood. A fence, in short, that *worked*.

Behind it: goats. Calm goats. Goats that were standing on the ground, on all four legs, not being launched anywhere, not wearing the mandated Goat Uncertainty Harnesses, not performing any of the seventeen Recognized Caprine Distress Postures. Just goats. Eating grass. Occasionally looking up with the placid, philosophical vacancy that is the goat's one contribution to the interior life of the universe.

And behind *them*, sitting at a wooden table outside a stone cottage whose roof — and I must be frank with you here, reader, because this detail is the one that first drew the attention of the authorities and I would be doing you a disservice to soften it — whose roof was *level*: a man.

He was eating porridge.

He was eating it from a bowl, with a spoon, in the usual direction. No one was throwing things at him. Nothing was on fire. The porridge was not explosive, sentient, or threatening legal action. He chewed, swallowed, and repeated the process with a regularity so mechanical, so devoid of incident, that a person watching from a distance might have mistaken him for a defective clock — a clock that had been built to do only one thing and, bewilderingly, *did it*.

This was Pell Grindtooth. He was forty-three years old. He was not tall. He was not short. He had the kind of face that sat on the front of his head and performed the standard functions of a face without editorializing. His hands were thick and calloused and did not shake. His eyes, beneath brows that neither bristled nor arched, held an expression that I can only describe as — and forgive me, for this is difficult — *content*.

I tell you, I did not know what to make of him.

None of us did.

He finished his porridge. He washed his bowl. He walked to the fence — the horrible, functional fence — and inspected a section where a post had begun to lean, perhaps a quarter of an inch, toward the east. He took a mallet from his belt and tapped the post back to vertical. One tap. The post held.

And that was when the window of his cottage exploded inward with a sound like a horse being fired from a cannon, because a horse *was* being fired from a cannon — no. Forgive me. Not a horse. A man. A very thin man in the blue sash of the Vaelund Bureau of Judicial Summons, seated in a regulation Trebuchet Delivery Cradle (Mark IV, with lumbar support), who sailed through the shattered glass, performed a triple somersault, struck the far wall hard enough to crack the plaster, and slid to the floor in a cascade of broken crockery, where he lay for a moment, twitching, before springing to his feet with the rubber-limbed resilience of a career civil servant and declaring, in a voice somewhat muffled by what appeared to be a piece of Pell's curtain stuck to his face:

"PELL GRINDTOOTH OF GRINDTOOTH FARM, CLATTERING REACH, YOU ARE HEREBY SUMMONED TO APPEAR BEFORE THE HIGH SLAPSTICK COURT OF VAELUND, TENTH CIRCUIT, THE HONORABLE JUDGE MARVEX VON CHANDELIER PRESIDING, TO ANSWER CHARGES AS ENUMERATED HEREIN, PURSUANT TO THE PUBLIC SPECTACLE CODE, TITLE 14, SECTION 9, SUBSECTION: OH DEAR."

He handed Pell a document. It was fourteen pages long. It was sealed with wax, counter-sealed with a smaller wax seal affixed to the first seal by a tiny wax chain, and counter-counter-sealed with a live beetle that had been trained to bite anyone who opened the document without first performing the Seal-Breaker's Jig (a brief, humiliating dance specified in Appendix 7F of the Summons Protocol).

Pell did not perform the jig.

The beetle bit him.

He opened the document anyway and read it standing in his kitchen, amid the glass and the plaster dust and the process server who was now stretching out his back with the practiced grimace of a man who had been trebucheted into nine kitchens that morning and had four more to go before lunch.

The charges were as follows:

---

**THE HIGH SLAPSTICK COURT OF VAELUND**
**Tenth Circuit — Division of Behavioral Irregularity**

**Case No. 3E-12-00457-GN**

*The People of Vaelund v. Pell Grindtooth*

**CHARGES:**

The defendant, PELL GRINDTOOTH, is hereby charged with **Grievous Normalcy** under the Public Spectacle Code, Title 14 ("On the Obligation of All Citizens to Be Entertaining"), in seven (7) counts, to wit:

**Count I:** On the 14th of Clamber, 3E-12, the defendant was observed *sitting without incident* on his front porch for a period of no less than forty-five (45) minutes, during which time nothing fell on him, tripped him, stung him, or detonated in his proximity, in direct contravention of the Minimum Mishap Requirement (Public Spectacle Code §14.2.1: "No citizen shall remain upright and undisturbed for a period exceeding six [6] minutes without at least one [1] qualifying incident, as defined in Appendix 12: The Hierarchy of Acceptable Calamities").

**Count II:** On the 3rd of Wobble, 3E-12, the defendant was observed *completing a task on the first attempt*, specifically the hanging of a door, which swung on its hinges correctly and without irony.

**Count III:** On the 19th of Pratfall, 3E-12, the defendant was observed *sleeping through the night in a bed that did not collapse*, fold itself into a suitcase, catch fire, or otherwise participate in the nocturnal renegotiation of domestic furniture mandated by the Household Entropy Standards Act of 3E-6.

**Count IV:** On multiple occasions spanning the full calendar year, the defendant was observed possessing *a roof that remains horizontal*, in violation of the Architectural Instability Code (§3.7: "All roofs shall express, through their angle, a minimum degree of existential doubt").

**Count V:** On the 8th of Tumble, 3E-12, the defendant was observed *reading a book without being interrupted by a falling object*, a state so irregular that the observing inspector was forced to sit down and was subsequently struck by a falling object herself, restoring local compliance but leaving the defendant's violation unmitigated.

**Count VI:** The defendant maintains a fence that is *straight*.

**Count VII:** The defendant maintains goats that are *calm*.

**RECOMMENDED SENTENCE:** To be determined by the Trial of Tumbles, as is the right and custom of all citizens of Vaelund, provided they survive the Bailiff's Mallet.

---

Pell read all fourteen pages.

He folded the summons in half, then in half again, and placed it in the breast pocket of his coat.

He looked at the process server, who was still in his kitchen, now eating a piece of bread he had found on the counter.

"When?" Pell said.

"Tomorrow. Ninth bell. Hall of Pratfalls, west entrance — the one with the rotating door. Mind the teeth on the second rotation; they added teeth."

"Alright," Pell said.

He finished the chores. He walked his goats to pasture. The goats went where he led them. The sun moved across the sky in the ordinary way. Nothing happened. Nothing happened. Nothing happened.

It was, I must confess, the most unsettling thing I had ever narrated.

---

## II.

The Hall of Pratfalls stood at the center of Gantry Pivot like a monument to the principle that no building should feel confident about its relationship with the ground.

Its foundation was mounted on eighty-four industrial shock absorbers, calibrated by the Bureau of Structural Comedy to produce a gentle, perpetual swaying that turned every hallway into a balancing act and every staircase into a negotiation. Its walls were paneled in polished crumblestone — a material quarried exclusively from the Sheering Cliffs, prized for its tendency to shed decorative chunks at dramatic moments, and by law the only permitted building material for any structure in which justice was administered, on the theory (enshrined in the Architectural Instability Code, §7.12) that "a court that cannot surprise its occupants cannot surprise the truth out of them." Its front doors were rigged with no fewer than sixteen spring-loaded mechanisms — bonking beams, dropping buckets, a boot on a pendulum arm that swung at shin height on a forty-second cycle — and the citizens of Gantry Pivot entered them every morning with the cheerful, crouching wariness of people for whom a trip to the courthouse was also, necessarily, a physical audition.

Pell arrived at eight-fifty. He wore the same coat. He had left his goats with Henk Brassfall next door, who had agreed to watch them on the condition that Pell accept, in return, a crate of pumpkins whose detonation schedule was "mostly predictable." Pell had accepted. He had placed the crate in his cellar. He had not seemed worried about it.

He entered through the west door and the rotating mechanism caught him on its second revolution, as promised. The teeth — new, brass, decorative in the way that a bear trap is decorative — bit into the doorframe as it spun, and Pell, who was not a fast man, caught the sleeve of his coat on one of them and was whipped halfway around before the fabric tore and released him, stumbling, into the lobby. A bucket of confetti (laced with thumbtacks, per the Lobby Festivity Standards) dropped on his head. A small hammer on an articulated arm swung from behind a pillar and struck him in the kneecap. He grunted. He limped forward. He did not stop.

The lobby was magnificent.

I need you to understand this: it was genuinely, heartbreakingly magnificent. The Hall of Pratfalls had been designed by Vaelund's greatest architects across four successive Erasures, each generation adding layers of booby-trapped grandeur to the last, until the building had become a kind of kinetic cathedral — a space where the ceiling was painted with scenes of famous judicial pratfalls (Justice Omber Yellowbristle's legendary triple-somersault verdict in *The Crown v. The Man Who Stood Too Still*, circa 2E-47, was depicted in gold leaf across the central dome), where the marble floors were waxed to a legally mandated coefficient of friction (0.03, per the Ambulatory Peril Standards, Appendix C: "On Slipperiness and the Appearance of Justice"), where every banister was hinged, every step was a different height, every chandelier was on a timer. It was a building that *believed* in something. That was the terrible thing about it. The chaos was not arbitrary. It was *sacred*.

Pell walked through it like a man walking through a rainstorm: he accepted what hit him, he adjusted, he kept moving. He did not dodge. He did not flinch. He did not perform. He was hit by things and he was hit by things and he continued to the end of the lobby, where a clerk behind a collapsing desk (it collapsed and reassembled itself every four minutes; the clerk typed through it without looking up) directed him to Courtroom 7, third floor, and warned him about the stairs.

The stairs were not worth describing. Suffice it to say that they moved, and some of the movements were lateral, and one of the steps was a trapdoor, and Pell, through no grace or skill but through the sheer blunt persistence of a man who has walked to pasture every morning for twenty years in mud and ice and wind, made it to the third floor with nothing worse than a bruised shin and a torn pocket.

Courtroom 7.

He pushed open the door (it was not rigged; the budget for door-rigging had been allocated entirely to the lobby, a fact that was the subject of ongoing interdepartmental litigation) and stepped inside.

A hand seized his elbow.

"You're Grindtooth? You're Grindtooth. Tell me you're Grindtooth because if you're not Grindtooth I have made a *catastrophic* error in seizing your elbow and my elbow-seizure insurance lapsed in Wobble and I cannot afford another —"

"I'm Grindtooth," Pell said.

"Oh thank the Tumbling Saints."

The hand belonged to a small, fast, bruise-mottled person whose left eye was swollen shut and whose right eye was compensating with a manic, searching intensity that made Pell feel, for the first time that morning, slightly uneasy. They wore the dented breastplate of a public defender — literally a breastplate, because the Trial of Tumbles did not distinguish between legal proceedings and physical combat, and a lawyer who could not absorb a mallet strike and argue through the pain was a lawyer who had not passed the bar (the bar was a literal bar, swung at the applicant's midsection during the oath of office). Their name tag, which was cracked and partially obscured by what appeared to be dried egg, read:

**Public Defender, Tenth Circuit**
**Specialization: Grievous Normalcy, Insufficient Pratfalls**
