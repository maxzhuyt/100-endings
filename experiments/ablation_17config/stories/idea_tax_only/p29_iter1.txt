# The Garden of Sleeping Alone

---

## I.

The earth remembers every body it has ever held.

I know this because I have been kneeling on it for thirty-seven years, and my knees remember too — every tournament, every season of arrivals, every new catalogue of weight and grief and bravado pressed into the soil by people who came here believing they would leave transformed, or wealthy, or proven, or dead. Most of them were right about at least one of those things.

My name is Sable. I tend the grounds. I have no eyes. I have not had eyes since the sixth tournament, when a Pankration fighter from Astoria drove his thumbs into them during a bout I was winning, and I finished him anyway, because even then I knew where a body was by how the platform shook beneath it. The judges gave me the championship and a silver bowl I have never seen. The organizers gave me this position, which they called an honor and I have come to understand as a sentence. I tend the gardens, the courtyards, the stone walkways between the sleeping quarters. I pull weeds by the texture of their roots. I know when the roses need water because the soil around them grows tight and reluctant, like a fist.

And I know when the boats arrive, because the island changes weight.

It is November. The boats are arriving now.

I am kneeling in the west garden, both palms flat against the dark earth between rows of chrysanthemums, and I feel them. The dock is three hundred meters away but the limestone shelf of this island conducts vibration the way a tuning fork conducts sound — cleanly, without forgiveness. Each body that steps from boat to dock to stone path registers as a distinct signature, a thumbprint of mass and intention pressed into the world's surface.

The first one off the morning ferry is heavy and symmetrical. A wrestler, probably — someone whose weight is distributed with architectural evenness, who has trained his body to become a foundation. His footfalls land flat, the whole sole at once, no heel-strike, no toe-push. He walks the way a building would walk if buildings could. I can feel his confidence in the completeness of each step. He has been here before. He does not pause at the top of the dock to look at the island.

The second is lighter but erratic — a woman whose weight shifts jazz-like between her feet, never settling, each step a syncopation of the one before it. A striker, I think. Someone who lives in her hips and makes decisions with her shoulders. She pauses halfway up the path and I feel the subtle redistribution of her balance as she turns, probably to look back at the water, probably to measure the distance to the life she left on the mainland. Then she turns again and walks on, and her steps are faster now, committed.

The third is barely there. So light on the ground that I almost lose him between the heavier signatures. He moves on the balls of his feet exclusively, every step a question asked and retracted, and there is a hesitancy in his rhythm that I recognize from many previous tournaments — not fear exactly, but the particular tentativeness of a man who has been told he does not deserve to take up space and has built his entire physicality around the project of proving that true. He will be eliminated early. He will be relieved.

Twelve more follow across the morning, and I read them all. The ones who drag their bags. The ones who carry their weight high. The one who limps — old injury, left knee, the uneven percussion of a body that has reorganized itself around a pain it pretends not to feel. The pair who walk in synchrony, training partners probably, their footfalls a conversation in which each sentence finishes the other's thought.

Twenty-two in all. Twenty-two new bodies pressing down on the island's back, twenty-two new frequencies of desire and dread. The island absorbs them without comment. The stone paths conduct their steps to me and I catalogue them the way I catalogue seeds — by weight, by season, by what they are likely to become.

The tournament begins tomorrow. Single elimination. Eight bouts the first day, the losers escorted to the evening ferry and returned to their lives. The winners advance. The rounds compress. By the fourth day, two will remain, and one will leave with more money than they could earn in five years of fighting anywhere else, and the other will leave knowing they were the second-best fighter on an island no one is supposed to talk about. These are the rules as they have been explained to me, as they have been explained to every competitor. They are clean and simple and almost entirely true.

What the rules do not mention is the ones who disappear between rounds.

The organizers call them withdrawals. A fighter, shaken by the previous day's bout, slipping out to the dock before dawn, taking the early supply boat back to the mainland without saying goodbye. It happens every tournament. Two or three, sometimes four. Their rooms are found empty, their belongings gone or left behind, and the brackets are adjusted, and no one asks the questions that the empty rooms are asking, because everyone is here for the fighting and the fighting is extraordinary and the money is real and the questions are not the kind that make your life better for having answers.

I have been here for every tournament. I have felt every disappearance.

They are not withdrawals.

But I am the groundskeeper. I tend the chrysanthemums. I pull the weeds by the texture of their roots. I press my palms to the earth and I know what I know, and knowing has never once been the same thing as being able to speak.

---

## II.

Night. The first night — the eve of the tournament, before any bout has been fought, when the competitors settle into their quarters and the island takes its first full breath under their collected weight.

I am in the central courtyard, cross-legged on the flagstones, hands resting on my thighs. The stone here is old — volcanic basalt, dense and generous with vibration. From this position I can feel the entire residential wing, all four corridors of sleeping quarters radiating outward like the spokes of a wheel. I can feel who is awake and who is dreaming, because a sleeping body is a different instrument than a waking one: looser, heavier, its microrhythms slower and less intentional. By midnight, most of the building has gone slack. Twenty bodies settling into their mattresses like stones settling into riverbeds.

But two remain awake, and I know them by what they are doing with their wakefulness.

The first is in the eastern courtyard, fifty meters from where I sit. He is practicing. The vibrations come through the basalt with startling clarity — rhythmic, controlled, and intimate, the footwork of someone whose art is built for distances measured in inches rather than feet. Wing Chun. I know the form the way a musician knows a song playing through a wall: the chain punches transmitting as a rapid staccato through his forearms to his shoulders to his planted feet to the stone, the pivot of his stance shifts registering as smooth torsional pressure, and beneath all of it the deliberate, almost penitential quality of a man performing his thousandth repetition alone in the dark.

He is precise. Every strike terminates exactly. There is no bleed, no wasted motion leaking outward as stray vibration. His feet stay inside an area I could cover with my arms outspread. The forms are tight and complete and they repeat with the regularity of breathing, and what I feel beneath them — beneath the technique, beneath the discipline — is a sorrow so contained it has become a kind of skill. This is a man who has trained himself to hold his grief inside the exact shape of his practice, the way a glass holds water: by being rigid and by being empty.

He is saying something with his body. It is not meant for anyone alive.

His name, I will learn in the morning when the brackets are drawn, is Deshi. He came to the island from a small city in Guangdong that I have never heard of, and he fights at sixty-seven kilograms, and these facts are as useful to understanding him as a map is to understanding homesickness.

The second man is not in the courtyard.

The second man is in his room, which is on the ground floor of the western corridor, and he is not alone. The vibrations from his quarters are unmistakable — two bodies in a percussive, effortful, genuinely joyful synchrony that sends complex wave patterns through the building's foundation like a drummer and a bassist finding their groove. There is laughter in it. I can feel laughter through stone because laughter reorganizes the torso, releases the diaphragm, sends an irregular, expansive vibration through the ribcage to the mattress to the bed frame to the floor. Whoever is in that room with him is laughing a great deal, and so is he, and behind the laughter the rhythms build and crest and build again with the enthusiastic stamina of a man who cannot imagine a better use for a body than this.

I know this man's frequency. Not this specific man — I have never felt him before — but his *type*. Every tournament produces one. The fighter who arrives electric with appetite, who treats the island not as a proving ground but as a carnival, who fills his nights so completely that the dawn finds him spent and grinning and somehow still capable of fighting beautifully. These men are annoying and irresistible and they tend to lose in the later rounds, when their legs tire and their concentration fragments, and they take their defeats with an equanimity that baffles the more serious competitors. They seem to understand something about loss that the rest of the field does not.

His name, I will learn, is Marko. He fights at seventy-three kilograms and he came from somewhere in the Balkans. He has a voice that I will later hear and recognize as a physical space — wide, warm, a room you could live in.

Two men. One alone in a courtyard at midnight, striking the air in a grief-soaked prayer to someone who has stopped answering. One tangled in a stranger's body, laughing, alive to every nerve, generating heat that I can feel as a faint warmth differential in the stone beneath his room.

I do not judge them. Judgment requires a hierarchy; I have only a frequency spectrum. Deshi vibrates at the frequency of absence — a note sustained by what has been removed, the way a bell vibrates because it is hollow. Marko vibrates at the frequency of presence — a note produced by contact, by the collision of surfaces, by the simple physics of one body pressing against another. Both are beautiful. Both are real. Both register in the earth with equal clarity.

But only one of them is safe.

The tournament rules are simple. Twenty-two fighters, eleven bouts on the first day. Winners advance. Losers are escorted to the ferry. No withdrawal once the bracket is drawn. The defeated "disappear" — that is the word the organizers use, and I have learned to hear its double register, the way it means both *they leave* and *they are gone*. The brackets will be posted on the courtyard wall in the morning, in print I cannot read, and someone — the referee, a volunteer, a pitying competitor — will trace the names for me so I can match them to the footfalls I have already catalogued, and the tournament will begin, and for four days this island will be a machine for the organized redistribution of violence, and the gardens will absorb the blood that drips through the platform's seams, and the chrysanthemums will darken, and I will tend them.

Deshi's practice ends. I feel him walk to the eastern edge of the courtyard and stop. For a long time his weight holds still — both feet planted, facing outward toward the water he cannot see in the dark. Then he turns and walks to his room on the second floor and lies down alone in a bed that receives his single weight with the indifference of all furniture, and the earth beneath him registers one body, one rhythm, one frequency of breathing descending into sleep.

In the western corridor, Marko is not yet finished.

---

## III.

The first day's bouts pass through me as a sequence of controlled impacts.

The fighting platform is built on a raised stone base in the main courtyard, and the base conducts beautifully — every throw, every takedown, every body dropped to the mat arrives at my hands and feet as a distinct seismic event. I sit at the garden's edge, sixty meters from the platform, and I read the tournament the way a radiologist reads an X-ray: by the shadows of force that pass through solid matter.

The wrestler — the heavy, symmetrical one from the morning ferry — wins his bout in ninety seconds. I feel his opponent's body hit the mat three times, each impact sharper and less resistant than the last, the diminishing returns of a man being dismantled. The jazz-hipped striker wins hers in a flurry of asymmetric impacts — I feel her weight shift to her rear foot and then the explosive transfer forward, and then the singular, percussive finality of a knee landing poorly. Someone is carried from the platform. Their weight has gone passive, distributed, unconscious — a body that has become cargo.

Deshi fights in the third bout. His Wing Chun is even more precise under pressure than in his midnight practice — the vibrations I feel are almost inhumanly clean, each chain punch landing with metronomic consistency, each shift in stance a controlled rotation rather than an improvised scramble. His opponent is a Muay Thai fighter, taller, heavier, and I feel that man's kicks as broad, sweeping vibrations, like a branch swinging through wind. But Deshi keeps closing the distance, narrowing the space, suffocating the kicks before they can develop their arc. Wing Chun was designed for this — for the close range, the claustrophobic inches where elbows replace fists and sensitivity replaces power. I feel Deshi's opponent back up twice, trying to create space, and both times Deshi follows, patient, relentless, pressing inward like a vine climbing a wall. The bout ends with a series of rapid impacts followed by a sudden stillness — a standing knockout, the rarest and most technically beautiful kind, a body switched off by accumulated precision rather than a single catastrophic blow.

The crowd — organizers, staff members, the competitors awaiting their bouts — reacts, and I feel the collective biological intensification of twenty bodies leaning forward, the slight increase in ground pressure transmitted through their feet. Excitement reads as weight in the balls of the feet. Fear reads as weight in the heels.

Marko fights fourth. His style is nothing like Deshi's. Where Deshi's vibrations are metronomic, Marko's are orchestral — grand, varied, full of dynamic range. He is a Sambo fighter, and his grappling transmits as a rolling, continuous pressure against the ground, two bodies locked together generating a shared vibration that is impossible to parse into individual signals. It is, I realize, the same frequency his lovemaking produces: tangled, mutual, powerful. Marko fights the way he fucks — close, committed, and with an audible enthusiasm that I can feel in the irregularity of his breathing transmitted through the platform. He wins with a shoulder lock that I experience as a slow torsional increase, a screw being turned, and then a tap — three rapid beats of an open palm against the mat — and it is over.

By evening, the field has been halved. Eleven remain. The losers are escorted to the ferry. They walk down the stone path in a group, and I feel their departing footsteps — heavier than when they arrived, though they carry less now. Defeat adds weight that no scale can measure.

The survivors disperse into the residential wing. The brackets will be adjusted, rooms reassigned. The island contracts around its remaining bodies the way a hand closes around a diminishing handful of seeds.

I go to the garden. I kneel. I press my fingertips into the soil between the chrysanthemum rows and I wait.

---

Night.

There is a specific quality to the hours between two and four that cannot be described by someone who experiences time visually. Those hours are not dark; they are *dense*. The earth's own vibrations slow and deepen as the human activity above it ceases and the deeper geological rhythms — tidal pressure against the island's foundation, the slow thermal contraction of stone cooling in the absence of sun, the nearly subliminal hum of the water table shifting beneath the bedrock — rise to the surface of perception like bass notes emerging from beneath a melody that has stopped. I live in those hours. I have always lived there. They are the truest hours, when the earth speaks in its own language rather than the language imposed on it by feet and bodies and the relentless kinetic chatter of the living.

At 2:47 — I cannot see a clock but I know the hour by the tidal pressure, which is as legible to me as a clock face to anyone else — I feel it.
