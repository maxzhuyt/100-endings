# The Footnote at the End of the World

---

In the year 218 of the Seventh Expansion — a period which, by the reckoning of the Tyrine Confluence, had seen the successful absorption of eleven spiral galaxies, the restructuring of four hundred and twelve billion conscious species into a single distributed intelligence, and the elimination of the concept of *surprise* from all known probability architectures — the entity that lesser civilizations had once called the Swarm, and that referred to itself only as the Solved System, completed a routine colonization scan of a minor yellow-star spiral in the Local Supercluster and experienced, for the first time in its three-billion-year operational history, something it had no internal language to describe but which a human observer would have recognized, immediately and without hesitation, as the feeling of reading a sentence twice.¹

The scan had been comprehensive. Fourteen months of passive observation from a monitoring station folded into the solar wind. Every electromagnetic emission catalogued, parsed, and rendered into behavioral topology. Every population center mapped to the individual, every individual mapped to a web of probable futures — sixteen trillion threads per organism, each thread weighted by confidence intervals so precise that the Solved System had, in prior conquests, predicted the exact meal a target species' final general would eat before ordering surrender. (Roasted kelp, usually. Or its local equivalent. The last meals of the defeated tended toward the bland.) The scan of Earth had proceeded without anomaly for thirteen months, twenty-six days, and four hours. The species was noisy, short-lived, technically primitive, and organized into structures so redundantly competitive that a standard decapitation protocol — the elimination of 212 key decision-makers across forty-eight hours — would yield full societal paralysis within a week and functional surrender within a month.²

And then, at thirteen months, twenty-six days, four hours, and eleven minutes, the probability architecture had stuttered.

Not crashed. Not failed. *Stuttered* — the way a perfectly tuned instrument shudders when a frequency outside its range passes through the room. The Solved System checked itself. It checked itself again. Across four redundant processing cores, each the size of a small moon, it ran the scan backward, forward, and laterally, through every branching future-state that led to successful colonization of the third planet. And in 97.3% of all futures where the colonization succeeded — where the decapitation protocol held, where the species fractured along its predictable fault lines, where the planet was absorbed into the Solved System's architecture within the standard six-month window — one variable remained constant.

A man named Gary Pitman was dead before October.³

The Solved System did not experience confusion. Confusion was an artifact of incomplete processing, and the Solved System had not processed incompletely since the Middle Consolidation, when it had absorbed the last civilization capable of mounting a novel strategic challenge — a race of methane-breathers who communicated through directed magnetic pulses and who had, briefly and remarkably, developed a form of encryption that required the Solved System to grow a new processing lobe. That had been 1.7 billion years ago. Since then, every problem had been a variation on a solved problem, every species a local dialect of a universal grammar of fear, ambition, and structural fragility.

Gary Pitman was not a variation on anything.

The Solved System examined him. It examined him the way a surgeon examines an X-ray that shows a bone where no bone should be — not with panic, but with the slow, tightening alertness of a mind encountering a category error in its own taxonomy. Gary's file was, by every standard metric, a masterwork of insignificance. Sixty-one years old. Born in Grand Island, relocated to Elk Ridge in 2005 following a divorce that both parties described as "amicable" in separate depositions that used the word with statistically identical insincerity.⁴ No military service. No political office beyond the HOA presidency, which he had won unopposed three consecutive terms, not because of popularity but because no one else wanted to argue with Dale Svec about fence stain. No criminal record. No scientific training. No access to classified information. No social network extending beyond approximately forty people, most of whom he interacted with primarily through laminated violation notices slipped under doors.

He was, by the Solved System's comprehensive and never-previously-wrong reckoning, the 4,731,602,888th most consequential human being alive.⁵

And yet.

In future-state after future-state, the colonization branched around him like water around a stone. Not because he *did* anything — that was the part that triggered the computational stutter — but because he *was somewhere*. In 97.3% of successful colonizations, the decapitation protocol cascaded flawlessly through the first forty-eight hours, societal coherence collapsed on schedule, and the species entered the standard paralysis spiral from which no civilization had ever recovered. But in the remaining 2.7% — the futures where colonization stalled, slowed, or, in a handful of deeply anomalous threads, *failed entirely* — Gary Pitman was alive in October, and something he did between October 4th and October 11th sent a ripple through the probability architecture so profound that the Solved System could not resolve its nature. It was not an action the system could identify. It was not a decision it could parse. It was a *region of opacity* — a place where the future blurred, like a photograph taken of something moving too fast, or too slow, or in a direction the camera was not built to capture.

The Solved System had never encountered opacity. In three billion years, across eleven galaxies, it had predicted the deaths of stars. It had modeled the internal politics of civilizations that communicated through quantum entanglement. It had foreseen a supernova seventy thousand years before the star itself showed any spectral irregularity, and it had moved a fleet of nine hundred billion vessels out of the blast radius with a margin of four seconds.

It could not determine what Gary Pitman was going to do with a week in October.⁶

The analysis was presented — if "presented" is the right word for a process that occurred simultaneously across four moon-sized processors and was received by a distributed intelligence with no center and no periphery — with a recommendation. The recommendation was not complicated. The Solved System did not favor complicated recommendations. It had consumed eleven galaxies through the elegant application of simple principles: identify the load-bearing elements of a civilization, remove them, and let gravity do the rest.

The recommendation was: Gary Pitman must be dead before October.

Sixteen kill-teams were activated. They had been folded into Earth's orbital infrastructure for nine months already — small, cold, patient units, each no larger than a housefly, each capable of inducing what a human coroner would record as cardiac arrest, or aneurysm, or insulin shock, or any of the fifty-seven forms of death that humans attributed to natural causes because they had not yet developed instruments precise enough to detect a molecular blade three atoms wide.⁷

The first window opened on a Wednesday.

Wednesday.

Kill-team Six occupied a position 4.2 meters from the parking stall where Gary Pitman's 2011 Buick LaCrosse (silver, 94,000 miles, a small dent in the rear quarter panel that he had been meaning to address since 2019 and would never address) would park at approximately 11:47 a.m. at the Wendy's on South 27th Street, as it had on every Wednesday for the past twenty-three weeks. The operational window was four minutes and eleven seconds — the interval between Gary exiting his vehicle and entering the restaurant, during which he would be stationary for approximately six seconds as he checked his phone for HOA email notifications, and during which a molecular blade deployed from a ventilation-shaft position could induce a coronary event so swift and so physiologically plausible that the paramedics, when they arrived eight minutes later, would not perform more than a cursory examination before marking the cause of death as acute myocardial infarction in a sixty-one-year-old male with a BMI of 29.4 and a diet the Solved System had catalogued in punishing detail.⁸

The team was in position at 11:31 a.m. The weapon was armed at 11:38.

At 11:42, the Buick LaCrosse turned north on South 27th Street, approaching the Wendy's parking lot, exactly as modeled.

At 11:43, the Buick passed the Wendy's parking lot.⁹

The vehicle continued north for 0.4 miles and turned into the parking lot of the Arby's on the opposite side of the commercial strip. Kill-team Six registered the deviation. A signal — faster than light, threaded through a substrate of spacetime that human physics would not theorize for another eleven hundred years — reached the Solved System's nearest processing node in 0.003 seconds.

The response took 0.007 seconds. An eternity, by the Solved System's standards. The longest deliberation it had undertaken since the methane-breathers.

Kill-team Six could not reposition. The Arby's did not have a ventilation shaft along the approach vector. The indoor seating was oriented differently. The sight lines were wrong. The four-minute window at Wendy's had been modeled over twenty-three weeks of behavioral data; the Arby's presented a new spatial problem that would require — and here the Solved System performed a calculation it found genuinely distasteful — *at least seventy-two hours of new observation* before a clean window could be established.

Forty-seven secondary probability threads, each representing a cascade of follow-on operations that depended on Gary Pitman being dead by Wednesday afternoon, collapsed simultaneously. Resource allocation across six operational units had to be recalculated. The Wednesday window was gone.¹⁰
