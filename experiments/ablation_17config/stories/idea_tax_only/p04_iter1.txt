# The Sixth Friend

---

## I.

The letters arrived on the same Tuesday in September, singled out from the ordinary mail by their anonymity—no return address, no postmark anyone could trace, the envelopes a heavy cream stock that belonged to no one's handwriting because the address labels had been typed on machines that no longer exist. Inside each was a single sheet bearing a date three weeks hence, the name of the village (Ault, population 412, though it had been 430 when they were young), and one line: *The water still remembers the Bravery Club.*

No one alive should have known that name.

Martin Kaur, who lived now in Edinburgh in a flat overlooking a cemetery and who sold marine insurance for a firm whose name he could never say at parties without apology, read the letter standing at his kitchen counter at six in the morning and set it facedown on the granite as though it were hot. He left it there. He went to work. He came home. He turned it over and read it again. He did not call anyone. He booked a car.

Catherine Welles, née Morrow, read hers in the mudroom of a house in Norwich while her youngest screamed about a shoe, and she felt something close over her chest like a hand. She put the letter in her coat pocket. She carried it for four days. On the fifth day she arranged for her mother to take the children, and when her husband asked why, she said, I need a weekend, and he, who had learned not to press the silences that sometimes dropped across her face like shutters, said, All right. She drove south. She almost turned back outside of Lewes. She almost turned back again at Newhaven. She almost turned back a third time at the unmarked road that split from the A road and descended through beechwoods toward the coast, because the smell of the trees was the same, exactly the same, as though twenty-three years were a door and she was opening it.

She did not turn back.

Niall Fenton received his letter in a rented room in Montpellier where he had gone to finish a book that he would not finish, and he laughed when he read it—a short, hard sound that startled the woman sleeping in his bed—and then he sat on the balcony in his underwear and smoked and watched the Place de la Comédie fill with early-morning joggers, and he thought: No. Absolutely not. He was on a plane the next week.

Priya Chandrasekaran, who was a GP in Wolverhampton and who kept an orderly life and who had not thought about Ault in years, or so she told herself—Priya, who still could not swim, who had never learned, who at forty-one still chose hotels without pools—opened her letter at her desk between patients and felt nothing at all for a long time, and then felt everything. She told her practice she had a family matter. She took the train.

And David Ó Ceallaigh, who had been among them the youngest and who was now, at thirty-nine, a man of such careful mildness that his colleagues at the Dublin planning office sometimes forgot he was in the room—David read his letter, folded it into precise quarters, and placed it in the breast pocket of his shirt, where it stayed, transferring from shirt to shirt each morning like a talisman, until the appointed day, when he rose before dawn and drove to the ferry and crossed to Wales and drove south through a country he had not visited since he was sixteen, arriving at Ault in the late afternoon with the sun directly behind him so that his shadow entered the village before he did.

They came by different roads. None of them wrote to the others. None of them called. This was the first remarkable thing, though it would not seem remarkable until later, when they were sitting together and the silences had grown long enough to walk around in: that five adults, sober and employed and in most cases responsible for the lives of children, had obeyed an anonymous summons without question. Without telling one another. Without demanding explanation.

People who have truly let go of a thing do not travel four hundred miles on its account.

The village was nearly unchanged, which is the particular cruelty of small places—they remain, while the people who were formed there molt and scatter and become unrecognizable to themselves. The pub still had the green door. The church still had the crooked steeple that Martin, at nine, had insisted was falling. The single shop still sold ice lollies from the same chest freezer, its hum audible from the pavement. The lane down to the water still bent left at the same oak, and the oak still bore, if you looked, the faint scarring of initials cut with a penknife by children whose hands were now the hands of actuaries and doctors and people who had not finished their books.

The lake was still there.

It sat in its bowl of hills, green-black and quiet, reflecting a sky that on this particular afternoon was the color of tin. It was not a large lake. You could swim across it in twenty minutes if you were strong, or in twelve if you were fifteen and fearless and showing off, or in no minutes at all if you were fifteen and not as strong as you believed, and the water was colder than it looked, and your friends were on the far shore, cheering.

The five arrived within hours of one another, and each, before seeking shelter or food or the others, went first to look at the water.

---

## II.

The cottage at the lake's eastern edge had a gate that needed paint, and Ellen opened it that morning with the heel of her hand, the way she always did, leaning her weight into the wood until the latch gave. The garden was coming along. She had planted marigolds along the front path in April and they had taken fiercely, almost violently orange against the grey stone, and the roses along the south wall were in their second bloom—roses that seemed, in certain light, to glow as though generating their own warmth. They had never troubled her with thorns. She was lucky that way. Some people got roses that fought back. Hers were gentle, yielding under the shears like soft wood, and she moved along the row now in the mid-morning sun, cutting back the leggy canes, gathering the clippings in a basket she'd owned so long she could not remember acquiring it.

Her husband, Thomas, stood at the fence with his tea, watching the road. He did this most mornings—not from anxiety, she thought, but from a kind of contented vigilance, the way a man watches a landscape he has chosen. He was lean and quiet and exact with his kindness, the sort of man who noticed when the firewood was low and replenished it before she could ask, who left small flowers—daisies, buttercups, the ordinary beauties—on the windowsill above the kitchen sink. She had loved him for what felt like always. She could not quite remember meeting him, which she attributed to the gentle erosion of familiarity: when you have lived with someone long enough, the origin story becomes apocrypha.

"Someone's coming today," Thomas said. "I can feel it."

"You always feel it."

"And someone always comes." He smiled at her, and the morning light, passing through the steam of his tea and through the fingers of his hand where they curled around the mug, made a brief prismatic pattern on the gatepost—the light moving through the gaps as though his grip were looser than it appeared, or as though his hand were not entirely there.

"Mrs. Calloway's girl is having the baby soon," Ellen said, not looking up from the roses. "She'll want the blanket I promised."

"You've time."

"I always have time." She said this without irony. She felt it. Time moved in the cottage like honey, slow and golden, each day much like the one before but in a way that felt like sufficiency rather than sameness. She could not recall the last time she had been bored. She could not recall the last time she had been afraid. She baked bread on Wednesdays and Saturdays, and the bread rose without yeast, which she had never questioned, because it had always risen this way, because it had never occurred to her that bread required anything beyond flour and water and the willingness of her hands.

The neighbor, old Rhys, passed on the lane with his dog and raised a hand. "Morning, Ellen. Morning, Tom."

"Morning, Rhys."

"Garden's looking grand."

"Thank you."

He moved on. The dog sniffed the gate. Thomas watched the road.

Ellen carried her basket inside and set it by the door and washed her hands in the kitchen sink, looking out through the window at the lake, which was flat and still and held the sky on its surface like a coin. She had always lived here, or felt that she had. The village knew her. She knew its rhythms—the way the light came over the eastern hills in summer like a slow brass note, the way the church bell rang on Sundays and the sound scattered across the water and returned as echo. She had a life. It was small and warm and daily. She dried her hands on the towel hanging from the oven door and put the kettle on and stood for a moment in the kitchen that smelled of rosemary and old wood, and she was happy, and the narrator, who has been watching her with an attention that might seem disproportionate to so ordinary a morning, pauses here—in the kitchen, in the light, in the smell of herbs and the sound of water beginning to heat—because this is the last moment in which everything she has will be hers without question, and it is worth it, it is necessary, to let the warmth of it linger.

## III.

They found each other by accident and inevitability—Martin at the pub, Catherine arriving just as he was ordering, the recognition cutting through two decades of changed bodies and different hair and the particular mask of adulthood, which is just the face of a child wearing exhaustion. Catherine said, "God, Martin," and Martin said, "You got one too," and they sat at a corner table with their drinks and the years between them and said nothing useful for a long time while the pub filled around them with people who were not their childhood.

Niall appeared in the doorway like a projection—thinner than they remembered, tanned, wearing the expression of a man who had prepared six different attitudes and was cycling through them too quickly for any to land. "Well," he said. "Shit." He sat down. He ordered wine. He grimaced at the wine. He said, "I wasn't going to come," and no one believed him, and he knew they didn't, and the knowing settled between them like a third drink no one had ordered.

Priya arrived by taxi, which in Ault meant Rhys's son-in-law with a Toyota, and she stood outside the pub for four minutes before entering—Martin's watch, Martin's count, because he had seen her through the window and said nothing, sensing her deliberation, respecting it because Martin had always been the one who understood what it cost certain people to walk into certain rooms. When she came in, she looked at the three of them and said, "Where's David?" with a voice that meant: I will deal with the emotions later; first, the headcount.

David arrived last. He had the look of a man who had been standing outside a different door. His coat was wet. It was not raining. He sat down and folded his hands on the table and said, "I went to the lake first."

No one asked him why. They all had.

The evening gathered around them. They ordered food they didn't taste. They performed the rituals of reunion—the metadata of lives lived elsewhere. Martin's insurance work (boring, stable, the kind of career that functions as an apology to cautious parents). Catherine's children (three, the youngest still in nappies, the eldest already displaying the terrifying agency of a person who will one day leave). Niall's book (unfinished, always unfinished, the permanent alibi of a man who fears completion). Priya's practice (overcrowded, underfunded, the satisfactions of it difficult to separate from the fatigue). David's planning office (an irony he acknowledged: "I approve other people's blueprints for a living"). They spoke in the present tense. They spoke fluently. They spoke the way people speak when they are building a dam with their mouths.

It was Catherine who said, "The letter."

The table changed. Not visibly—the glasses didn't move, the plates kept their positions—but the electromagnetic field of five bodies shifted. Spines straightened. Hands went to laps.

"Who sent it?" Catherine asked.

"I assumed one of you," Martin said.

"Not me," said Niall. Priya shook her head. David looked at his folded hands.

"Then who?"

"Someone who knew about the Bravery Club."

"No one knew about the Bravery Club. That was the entire point of the Bravery Club."

"The six of us knew."

"Five," Catherine said, and the word landed on the table like a stone on glass, and the cracks spread outward and no one moved.

"Five," she said again, more quietly. "Five of us knew."

The silence that followed was not empty. It was full of a name that no one said. It sat among them, occupying the space that a sixth chair might have held—the space that was, in fact, occupied by a sixth chair, pushed slightly back from the table, because the pub had set six places and no one could say who had requested it.

They spoke around the name. They circled it the way the lane circled the oak.

"That summer," said Niall.

"What happened," said Priya.

"The day at the lake," said Martin.

David said nothing. He was looking at the sixth chair.

It was Niall who breached the membrane first, not from courage but from the wine and the particular impatience of people who write—or who mean to write—and who experience silence as a dare. "She would be our age now," he said. "She'd be thirty-nine, forty. She'd have a life somewhere. And every year, every single year since I was sixteen, I've thought about what that life would look like, and I've—" He stopped. He drank. "I've got forty pages on it. That's the book. That's the book I can't finish."

"You were writing about *her*?" Catherine's voice had an edge like a letter opener.

"I was writing about all of us."

"You don't get to write about all of us."

"It doesn't matter," Priya said, cutting between them with the flat, diagnostic calm she used on patients who were escalating. "The question is who sent the letter and why we're here."

"We're here because we came," David said. His first sentence in twenty minutes. They looked at him. "We're here because we read a sentence on a piece of paper and we packed our bags. That's the answer and the question."

"She was always the bravest of us," Catherine said, speaking into her glass. "She would have known why."

And inside Martin's memory—a room no one else could enter, a room with different wallpaper—the girl called Ellen was not brave. She was brittle. She stood at the edge of things and laughed too loudly and volunteered for dares not from courage but from the sick terror of being left behind. He remembered her face at the top of the quarry wall the summer they were twelve, the way her lips went white and her eyes went wide and she jumped not like a person leaping but like a person falling who had decided to call it flight.

He did not say this.

Inside Priya's memory—the same summer, the same girl, but seen from a body that had been shorter and shyer and conscious always of its difference—Ellen was a wall. Ellen took up space. Ellen spoke first and loudest and chose the game and assigned the roles, and if you were Priya, you were always the one who had to stay behind and watch the bags. Ellen's bravery was really a kind of tyranny, and Priya had feared her the way small animals fear weather—not personally, but as a condition of the world.

She did not say this either.
