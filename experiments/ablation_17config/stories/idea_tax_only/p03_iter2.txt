# The Elk Ridge Couponing Club

## I.

The potato salad had been acquired for eleven cents. This was mentioned three times during the first hour of the potluck — twice by Linda Carzoli, who had made it, and once by the handwritten card she'd propped against the bowl, which read, in a cursive as careful as calligraphy: *Yukon Gold Potato Salad — Retail Value $14.87 — Final Out-of-Pocket: $0.11.* Nobody at the Elk Ridge Community Center considered this a strange thing to write on a table card at a memorial. It was, in the language of the group, a tribute. You honored the dead the way the dead would have wanted, and Brenda Kasprzak would have wanted to know your cost basis.

The surviving members of the Elk Ridge Competitive Couponing Club had reserved the community center's Rosewood Room — the good one, with the functioning outlet near the buffet table and the windows that overlooked the elementary school parking lot rather than the dumpster corral behind the Rite Aid. June Pelletier had booked it four weeks in advance using a rewards credit she'd accrued from six consecutive months of volunteering for the township's recycling education program, which she had joined solely to accrue this credit, and which she would quit the following Tuesday, and which would send her three politely confused emails asking if she needed bereavement time. She did not respond to any of them. She was not, at that point, thinking about recycling.

Present in the Rosewood Room that Saturday in October were the following:

June Pelletier, sixty-one, who had brought a baked ziti assembled entirely from components sourced across four stores and two manufacturers' rebate cycles, total outlay $0.43, and who sat nearest the door because she had sat nearest the door at every group function since 2016, when an incident at a Kroger — which will be described later, or possibly never — taught her the value of knowing where the exit was.

Linda Carzoli, fifty-seven, contributor of the potato salad and also a crockpot of Swedish meatballs ($0.22 all in, excluding the electric cost of the crockpot, which Linda did not track and which Brenda, when she was alive, had considered a philosophical failing). Linda was wearing a black blouse she had purchased at full retail price from Macy's in 2011 for the funeral of her first husband, and she would wear it only two more times: once at the memorial described here, and once on a day still fourteen months away that none of them could yet see coming, though the narrator can, and elects, for the moment, not to dwell on it.

Soo-Yun Park, forty-four, the youngest member of the club, who had joined in 2019 after witnessing June execute a quintuple-stack at a Target in Haddonfield that saved $212 on a single transaction and who had described the experience, later, as "like watching someone play chess against a computer and win." Soo-Yun had brought a fruit salad. The fruit was in season and had been free after a combination of a store coupon, a manufacturer's coupon, and a digital rebate, and the small container of whipped topping had cost nine cents. She was the only member of the group who would, in the end, escape what was coming fully intact, though the word "intact" will require some qualification, and "escape" is doing work in that sentence that it perhaps should not be asked to do.

Claudette Morris, sixty-eight, the club's eldest, seated in the corner, saying very little, eating the meatballs with the slow mechanical focus of someone who has decided that chewing is a form of not-crying. Claudette had once saved $947 in a single shopping trip at a now-defunct Safeway. The receipt was forty-one inches long. She'd had it framed. The frame — along with the receipt, along with much else — would be confiscated by the task force in March, and Claudette would sit in her kitchen the evening after the confiscation and eat an entire sleeve of Ritz crackers purchased, for the first and only time in her adult life, without a coupon, and the checkout clerk who rang them up at full price would later say that the woman had seemed "totally fine, just regular," because that is what devastation looks like when it happens to people whom the world has decided are not the type to experience it.

And Patty Melinka, who arrived eleven minutes late, who came through the Rosewood Room's double doors carrying a vegetable tray.

The tray was from ShopRite. It was the premade kind — the circular plastic clamshell with the ranch dressing cup in the center, the baby carrots and celery sticks and broccoli florets arranged in their fixed plastic compartments like the face of a grocery-store clock. It was the kind of thing that existed primarily to be brought to gatherings by people who had not planned ahead, and it had been purchased, as the bright yellow sticker on its upper-left lid made clear to anyone within four feet, for $8.99, which was its full and unreduced retail price.

The room registered the sticker. The room processed the sticker. The room, collectively and without a single word or glance that could be described as unkind, forgave the sticker.

This was noted. This was never spoken of again. In a group that cataloged everything — savings percentages, coupon expiration dates, the exact per-unit cost of every conceivable household product in a seventy-mile radius — the forgiveness of Patty Melinka's $8.99 vegetable tray was the one data point that was never entered into the record, because to enter it would have required acknowledging that Patty had failed at the foundational act of the group, and to acknowledge this at the memorial for the woman who had taught Patty everything she almost knew would have been a cruelty, and the Elk Ridge Competitive Couponing Club, whatever else it was and whatever it would become and whatever the task force's incident reports would later claim about its "organized and deliberately adversarial posture toward lawful retail operations" — the club was not cruel.

Patty set the tray down between the potato salad and a bowl of seven-layer dip (Linda again; $0.31) and took her seat, and nobody mentioned the sticker, not then and not later, not even in the months after when the group fractured and things were said that could not be taken back and loyalties were tested by the specific kind of pressure that loyalty was never designed to hold. The $8.99 sticker was a sealed room. They all lived in the house of that mercy.

June stood, eventually. She smoothed her napkin. She said, "I'm not going to make a speech because Brenda hated speeches," which was true only in the narrow sense that Brenda hated other people's speeches. Brenda had loved her own speeches and given them freely and at length, often from the community center parking lot while the group stretched, often about the moral architecture of the coupon stack, often continuing well after the stretching was over and the minivans had begun to pull out. But no one corrected June, because correcting June at a memorial would have been inappropriate, and because everyone understood that "Brenda hated speeches" was not a factual claim but a structural one: it meant *I do not have the words, and I am asking you not to need them from me.*

"She was the best of us," June said instead, which was factually accurate and also sufficient, and she sat down, and Claudette ate another meatball, and Soo-Yun put her hand on Linda's forearm, and Patty stared at the center of the vegetable tray where the ranch dressing sat undisturbed in its small plastic moat, and outside the community center the October light came through the windows at the angle that made the elementary school parking lot look briefly like something from a painting — all amber and long shadow — and across town, in a rented office suite above a dry cleaner, a man named Ted Devlin was typing Patty Melinka's name into a database for the first time, though she would not learn this for another five weeks, and by then the database would contain photographs.

## II.

To understand how they loved each other, it is necessary to go back approximately six years and to the parking lot.

The parking lot of the Elk Ridge Community Center measured roughly two hundred by one hundred and forty feet and was surfaced in asphalt that had last been sealed in 2009, meaning that by the time the couponing club began using it as a staging area in the spring of 2017, it had developed the kind of cracks and undulations that gave it the topography of a relief map. Brenda Kasprzak had mapped these imperfections. She knew which sections pooled water after rain and which areas received morning shade from the building. She had, in a binder separate from but adjacent to her legendary coupon binder, a laminated diagram of the parking lot with annotations indicating optimal van-positioning for various retail targets based on time of day, traffic patterns, and highway-ramp proximity. She updated this diagram seasonally.

The stretching began at 7:15 a.m. This was non-negotiable, even though the stores they targeted did not open until 8:00, because Brenda believed that the body and the mind required a period of synchronized preparation before "engagement," and because the twenty-minute drive to the nearest viable coupon-stacking store — a Wegmans, seventeen miles east on Route 38 — needed to be preceded by what she called Stage One Readiness, which sounded like something from a missile-launch protocol, and which consisted, in practice, of four middle-aged-to-elderly women in the parking lot of a community center doing quad stretches against the bumper of a Chrysler Pacifica.

The stretches were Brenda's own invention. They were not based on any known physical therapy or athletic warm-up regimen. They were, if examined by a kinesiologist, probably inadvisable. There was one that involved rotating the wrists in alternating circles while rising on the toes, which Brenda claimed improved "coupon-handling dexterity" and which Claudette maintained had cured her sciatica, though Claudette's sciatica had never been formally diagnosed and seemed to appear and disappear based entirely on whether she was winning an argument. There was another that required squeezing an imaginary grapefruit between the shoulder blades, which targeted, according to Brenda, the specific muscle group responsible for "aisle endurance" — the ability to remain standing and focused during extended shopping sessions that could last ninety minutes or more and involve navigating a cart through displays, end-caps, and other shoppers while simultaneously consulting a coupon binder, a store-loyalty app, and one's own memory of that week's circular.

Patty could never remember the order. Every Thursday morning — this was before the stretching moved to Saturday to avoid a scheduling conflict with the community center's Zumba for Seniors class, a feud that the narrator will not detail but that involved a formal complaint to the township and the phrase "we were here first" deployed by both parties with equal conviction and equal factual dubiousness — Patty would arrive at the parking lot already flustered, already holding her keys in one hand and a coffee in the other, already somehow behind despite the fact that the sequence never changed. Brenda would begin the wrist rotations and Patty would begin the shoulder squeezes. Brenda would move to the quad stretch and Patty would attempt the wrist rotations and spill coffee on her sleeve. The group tolerated this. The group absorbed it. Patty's wrongness was a metronome they set their rightness against, and this sounds unkind, but it was not experienced as unkind, because Patty knew — Patty had always known — and she stayed, and they wanted her to stay, and the reason they wanted her to stay was not, as an outsider might assume, that they needed someone to feel superior to, but that they needed someone who loved the ritual more than the performance. Patty was terrible at couponing. Patty would have couponed until she died. These two facts lived in the same body and the group understood, without ever articulating it, that this was what devotion looked like when it was not dressed up in competence.

After the stretching came the driving. After the driving came the shopping. But between the stretching and the driving, on Thursdays, there were the lamination parties.

They were held at Brenda's house, a split-level on Fenwick Lane with a finished basement that smelled like toner and adhesive and the particular variety of boxed wine that Brenda purchased in bulk from a distributor she had cultivated a relationship with over the course of three years, during which she had never paid more than $2.40 per box and had, on one occasion that became legend, acquired eleven boxes for free after finding a misprint in a holiday circular that offered a percentage discount equal to the alcohol content of the purchased product. The distributor had honored the misprint. The distributor, the narrator can report, is no longer in business, for reasons that are only tangentially related to Brenda Kasprzak but that she would have taken pride in nonetheless.

The basement contained a folding table, eight chairs, an assortment of scissors in a ceramic mug shaped like a rooster, and Sheila.

Sheila was a GBC HeatSeal H535 Turbo industrial laminator, manufactured in 2004, acquired by Brenda at a school-district surplus auction in 2015 for $35, and named by Brenda during the car ride home on the grounds that "she deserves a name — she's going to outlive all of us." This was, in the end, technically accurate, though not in the way Brenda had meant it. Sheila was dove-grey and weighed forty-two pounds and had a warm-up cycle of exactly ninety seconds that produced a smell that none of the club members could precisely describe but that all of them associated, instantly and permanently, with Thursday nights. It was the smell of heated adhesive, and of the particular brand of laminating pouch that Brenda bought in cases of five hundred from an office-supply wholesaler, and of the boxed wine, and of the basement itself — its cool concrete and old carpet — and it is the kind of smell that only becomes important after you cannot encounter it anymore, when your brain, desperate and rummaging, tries to reconstruct it from its components and finds that it cannot, because the smell was not the sum of its parts but the artifact of a specific configuration of objects and people in a specific room at a specific time that will not come again.

On Thursdays, they laminated.

The process was this: each member would arrive with their week's coupons, clipped from newspapers, printed from websites, or acquired through the group's network of sources — a network that included, at various points, a mail carrier in the next township who saved inserts from undeliverable catalogues, a woman named Diane who worked at a printing company and had access to proof copies of Sunday circulars before they were distributed, and a teenager at the Elk Ridge Public Library who had, for reasons never examined, begun leaving stacks of printed coupons in a specific library book (*The Joy of Cooking*, 1997 edition, large print) that Brenda checked out and returned on a biweekly cycle. The coupons would be sorted by store, by product category, and by expiration date. Then they would be fed through Sheila.

The lamination was not strictly necessary. A coupon did not need to be laminated to function. But Brenda had discovered, early in the club's history, that a laminated coupon conveyed a specific psychological signal to cashiers: it communicated preparation, legitimacy, and a degree of organizational commitment that discouraged scrutiny. A laminated coupon did not look like something a person had torn from a newspaper in the parking lot. It looked like a credential. It looked, Brenda said, "like it had been to law school." And so on Thursdays the coupons were fed through Sheila, and the pouches sealed with a faint crinkling sound, and the finished products were trimmed with scissors and filed into binders that each member maintained according to her own system — systems that varied and that generated, among the members, the kind of low-grade perpetual disagreement that is the actual substrate of friendship.
