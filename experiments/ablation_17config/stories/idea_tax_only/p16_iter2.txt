# The Reverend's Account

---

## I.

I will tell it wrong. I am telling you now so that when I tell it wrong you will not think I have done it on purpose, though I may have done it on purpose, though purpose is a word I have no right to anymore, not since — but that is the middle and I mean to start at the start which was either a field in Virginia where the guns ate the sky or a silver mine in Absalom, New Mexico Territory, where Sweetmouth Pruitt held my daughter by the wrist and said *Reverend, I have been keeping your accounts,* or it was neither, or it was the kitchen, my mother's kitchen, where the flour hung in the air before it fell and I learned that things could be *stopped* if you thought about them the right way, only I did not learn that in the kitchen, I learned it in the war, only I learned it in the kitchen too because the mind does not — the mind is not a *sequence,* it is —

Let me begin again.

My name is not important. They call me Reverend because I attended the seminary at Lynchburg for two years before the conscription, which is to say I am as much a reverend as a man who once held a hammer is a carpenter. I am fifty-one years old or forty-seven years old. The discrepancy is not vanity. I have genuinely lost four years. They may be in Virginia. They may be in the mine with Pruitt and the women. They may be in a room I rented in Santa Fe where I woke up and the plaster on the ceiling was cracked in a pattern that looked exactly like the road from Cold Harbor to my mother's house, and I lay there staring at it until a Mexican woman came in and told me I had not moved in six days and did I want broth, and I said yes and the bowl came across the room to my hand without her carrying it and she screamed and left and I drank the broth and the cracks spread while I drank and I thought: this is what I do to rooms. This is what I do to everything I am inside of.

There. That is the whole story. I could stop.

I should tell you about the siege. The siege is in 1887 and it is the present, except when I am telling you about it the siege will occasionally become 1864 because the sounds are the same. Pruitt's pistol going off inside the mine shaft sounds precisely like a Napoleon twelve-pounder firing canister at three hundred yards, which is to say it sounds like the world tearing along a seam, and when I hear it I am not merely *reminded* of the field outside Cold Harbor, I am *there,* the red dirt is under me and the seminary boy next to me, Aldiss, is missing everything below his sternum and asking me if I have seen his Testament, he left it in his Testament the letter from his — he means he left in his *letter* the — no, he means the Testament is the letter, the letter is the Testament, everything is everything else, and the canister comes over the redoubt and I think about my mother folding dough, about the flour suspended, and the metal stops.

That was 1864.

But also that was the mine. Pruitt's mine. Last week or last month.

You see the problem.

I had a daughter. I have a daughter. She is in the mine or she is in Santa Fe or she is nowhere because she was never — no. She is real. Her name is Constance and she arranges things in rows when she is frightened, stones or buttons or spent cartridge casings, and she is twelve or she is nine, and she is in the mine with her mother, who is my wife, who was my wife, who left because — but that is Beat Three, that is the gap, and I am getting ahead or behind, I cannot tell the difference between those two directions anymore, they are the same hallway and I am walking it and the walls are cracking as I walk.

I will tell you what happened.

I will tell you three times because once is a lie and twice is an argument and three times is the closest I can come to whatever this is. Not truth. Truth is for men whose minds move in one direction. My mind moves in all directions at once, like shrapnel, like the flour, like the canister shot that hung in the air over Cold Harbor and waited for me to tell it what to do.

The siege. Pruitt. My daughter. My wife. My mother, who is dead and was in the mine. I will explain that. I will fail to explain that. We will begin.

---

## II.

This is how you stop a bullet.

You will need: one body, preferably your own. One field, preferably under fire. A memory of something white — flour, snow, the inside of a seashell, anything pale and particulate that once hung in the air before you while a woman you trusted stood nearby. A complete and credible fear of death. This is essential. The fear must be total. You cannot do this if any part of you believes you will survive. The mind only reaches out when it has nowhere else to go. Like a rat in a burning barn finding a hole it has walked past a thousand times. Fear is not the obstacle. Fear is the door.

Step one. You will be lying flat. There will be a boy beside you who is dying. He will be talking about something that does not matter — a letter, a Testament, a girl in Farmville who said she would wait. You have been awake for three days. Your teeth hum. There is a taste in your mouth like you have been chewing on a lead ball, which you may have been; we did that, in the trenches, to stay awake, and I believe now that the lead was part of it, part of the door, the slow poisoning of certain structures in the brain that normally prevent a man from — but I speculate. I was not a doctor. I was a seminary student who had read too much Greek and not enough Gospel and who could not, at the moment the canister shot broke over our position, remember a single prayer.

Step two. The canister arrives. It is not a single projectile. You must understand this. It is a tin can packed with iron balls, dozens of them, and when the can ruptures they spread in a cone, and the cone was aimed at us, at me and Aldiss and four other men whose names I will not say because I stopped the metal for myself and did not stop it for them, and they died, and their dying was the sound of — but you don't need that sound. You need the next part.

Step three. You think of something white. I thought of flour. My mother's kitchen, February, the window light making the flour a slow country in the air between her hands and the board. The flour did not fall. That is the memory. Not that it was beautiful. Not that I was safe. Only that the flour *did not fall.* It hung. And I held that image the way you hold a breath — not with your lungs but with the locked place behind your lungs, the place where your body meets whatever your body is inside of — and the iron balls hung.

I am not speaking in metaphor.

They hung in the air approximately nine feet in front of our position. Forty-three iron balls, each roughly one inch in diameter. They hung the way the flour had hung. They waited. Aldiss was still talking about his letter. The four men to my right were still dying from the previous volley, the one I had not stopped because I had not yet learned the recipe. The balls were so close I could see the casting marks on them, the little seams where the mold had joined. One of them was slightly ovoid, more egg than sphere. I remember that. I remember that better than I remember my daughter's face, which tells you something about what this did to me, what this does, what the recipe costs.

Step four. You let them fall. Not forward. Down. You take the image of the flour and you turn it, the way you would turn a picture on a wall, and the flour falls, and the iron falls, straight down, and the balls drop into the dirt at the lip of the trench and sit there like a handful of marbles a child has abandoned.

Step five. There is no step five. You have stopped the bullets. You will vomit. You will not remember the next two hours. When you come back to yourself you will be in a field hospital and a doctor will be telling you that you have suffered a concussion, though no shell landed near you, and that the men beside you died of canister wounds except Aldiss, who died of his prior injuries, and that you are lucky, and that the Lord was watching over you.

You do not correct him. You do not say: I was watching over me. You do not say: I reached out with something that is not my hand and I held forty-three iron balls in the air and I could have pushed them back, I *felt* that I could have pushed them back into the mouths of the men who fired them, and I chose not to, and that choice is the only moral thing I have ever done with this and I have spent twenty-three years unsure whether I made it or whether I simply lacked the strength.

You do not say any of this.

You learn to shoe horses. You learn to dig latrines. You learn the recipe, and you use it four more times before Appomattox — a timber that was falling, a wagon wheel that was rolling toward a lieutenant's legs, a shell fragment the size of a dinner plate spinning toward a drummer boy's neck — and each time it is the same: the flour, the white suspension, the locked place behind the lungs, and the vomiting after, and the lost hours, and the slow return to a world where things fall when they are supposed to fall and you are merely a man who was in a war and survived it.

You go to the seminary. You try to finish. You sit in a pew and the hymnals on the shelf in front of you begin to tremble and you leave. You go west. Everyone is going west. You tell yourself it is a new beginning. You do not tell yourself that you are a loaded weapon traveling through a populated country without a safety, because you do not yet know this about yourself, or you know it and you are telling this part of the story in the voice of a man who did not know because it is easier, because the recipe is not just for bullets, the recipe is for *narrative,* you lay the events flat and you tell them in order and they sound like things that happened to you rather than things you did, and the flour hangs, and you do not let it fall, not yet.

This is how you stop a bullet.

You cannot stop what happens after.

---

## III.

Between Cold Harbor and the mine I lived — I must have lived — in sequence, day by day, the way other men do. But I cannot give you the sequence. I can give you the shards. I will lay them out like Constance lays out her stones, in rows, by color, and you may arrange them as you wish, because I no longer can.

Here is a shard: Baxter Springs, Kansas, 1871 or 1873. A church. Not mine. I was passing through. The preacher was a Methodist with a voice like a saw hitting a nail, high and shrieking, and I sat in the back pew and thought about nothing, about silence, about the flour, and every window in the building blew outward simultaneously. Not inward. Outward. As though the church itself had exhaled. No one was cut. The glass flew *away* from the congregation, into the yard, where it caught the light and lay there like a field of jewels, and the Methodist stopped preaching and everyone turned to look and I was already walking out the back, already on the road, already adding Baxter Springs to the list of towns where the recipe had worked without my asking it to.

Pruitt was in Baxter Springs. I think. A man in a cream-colored hat was watching from across the street when I left the church, and he was smiling the way a man smiles when he has been proven right about something, and that was Pruitt, or that was a man in a hat, or that was no one and I have put Pruitt there now, after the fact, the way you hang a painting over a stain on the wall. I do not know. I am telling you I do not know.

Here is a shard: a woman named Eleanor. My wife. I loved her in the way that men with my condition love, which is to say utterly and at a great distance even when she was beside me. She had hands that were always warm. She put her hands on my face once when I had a fever and I felt the recipe start and I shut it down, I *swallowed* it, I held it in the locked place, and nothing happened, nothing broke, and she said, "You're shaking," and I said, "It's the fever," and it was not the fever.

We married in Dodge City. We moved to a house outside of town, a small house, pine boards and a dirt floor, and for a while things did not move unless I asked them to. Constance was born in that house. The midwife commented that the birth was unusually easy, that Eleanor's body seemed to know what to do, and I wondered then and wonder now whether I helped, whether the recipe reached out while I was pacing in the other room and *eased* something, aligned something, and if so whether that was a gift or a violation, and I have never told Eleanor this because how would you say it: *I may have rearranged the interior of your body while you were in labor and I am not sure whether I had the right.*

Here is a shard: the night the bedroom wall cracked. Constance was three or five. I was dreaming about the field — I always dream about the field — and in the dream the canister shot was not iron balls but teeth, hundreds of human teeth, and I stopped them the way I always stop them, with the flour, with the white, and in the waking world the wall above our bed split from floor to ceiling and the plaster dust fell on Eleanor's face and she woke up screaming and I told her it was the house settling and she believed me or she pretended to believe me and the crack stayed and widened over the following weeks and I patched it and it widened again and she stopped sleeping in the bed and began sleeping in Constance's room and I did not blame her and I do not blame her and the wall kept cracking.

Here is a shard that contradicts the previous shard: Pruitt came to the house. I was away — preaching, or what I called preaching, which was mostly standing in front of small groups of frontier people and saying things about God that I could not verify and collecting enough coin to eat. Pruitt came and sat in my kitchen and spoke to Eleanor in his soft dragstore voice and offered her things. A house in town. Money. An arrangement. He was laying the groundwork because he had been following me since Baxter Springs or since Virginia or since always, because Pruitt is the kind of man who collects debts the universe didn't authorize, who sees a man with a gift and decides the gift must have a *price* and *he* is the one to extract it. He told Eleanor I was dangerous. He told Eleanor about the church windows. He showed her a ledger — he always has a ledger, Pruitt, he keeps accounts of everything, every cracked wall and shattered window and unexplained detonation, all of it tallied in neat columns with dates and locations and estimated damages in dollars and cents — and Eleanor read the ledger and when I came home she was different. Not afraid. She had always been a little afraid. But now the fear had a *shape.* It had entries and sums. Pruitt had given her fear an arithmetic.

Or: there was no ledger. There was no visit. Eleanor's fear accumulated the way the cracks accumulated, slowly, along existing fault lines, and one morning she took Constance and went to her sister's in Las Cruces and left a note that said, "I cannot live inside your dreams anymore," and that was all, and there was no Pruitt, and the cracking was mine.

Both of these are true. I need you to understand that I am not choosing between them for the purpose of drama. I am telling you that I possess both memories with equal clarity and neither one dissolves when I hold it up to the light. I remember Pruitt in my kitchen. I remember coming home to the empty house. These two memories occupy the same space in my mind the way two objects cannot occupy the same space in the world, and yet they do, and the contradiction is not a literary device, it is the sound my mind makes, it is the cracking.
