# This American Strife: A Thanksgiving Inquiry

---

## Act One: Prologue, or How I Learned to Stop Worrying and Narrate Everything

Here is what you need to know about Margot Olssen-Crane, disgraced audio journalist, newly freelance, formerly of the public-radio ecosystem where she was, for eleven years, a mid-tier but reliably competent producer of the kind of segment that makes people in Volvos feel something in traffic: she has a microphone, a rental car with Nebraska plates, and a project.

The project is this: a documentary audio diary — let's call it a piece, let's call it long-form, let's use the vocabulary of the medium that recently and with minimal ceremony declined to renew her contract — investigating the contemporary American Thanksgiving from the inside. Embedded reporting. Participatory. The premise writes itself, or rather, it writes the grant application, which in the attenuated economy of public media is functionally the same thing: reporter returns to the familial table after an absence of some duration and, microphone in hand, anatomizes the rituals, tensions, and performative togetherness of the national holiday most likely to require a follow-up appointment with a therapist.

It is, she — I — will be the first to admit, not exactly war correspondence. It is not the segment on water-table contamination in the Ogallala aquifer region that earned a Peabody nomination in 2019, nor the six-part investigation into meatpacking-plant labor violations that earned both a Third Coast Award and, eventually, a formal complaint from the Kansas Livestock Association, which is, if you are scoring at home, a more powerful entity than it sounds. It is not even the ill-conceived but audiologically gorgeous piece on cicada emergence patterns in southern Illinois that my former executive producer once described, not without admiration, as "the most beautiful twelve minutes of radio no human person will ever voluntarily listen to." It is smaller than all of that. It is a Thanksgiving piece. It is a going-home piece. It is, in the parlance of the profession, a moments piece — the kind of segment where the reporter's voice goes soft and the ambient sound does most of the work and the listener is meant to feel, in the eighty-seven seconds between sponsor acknowledgments, that something has been shared.

But — and this is the crucial structural element, the thing that elevates it from seasonal programming to genuine inquiry — the reporter in question has not been to this particular table in nine years, and the family in question has, in the interim, apparently transformed their three-hundred-and-twenty-acre soybean-and-corn operation outside Platte City, Nebraska, into a couples' intimacy retreat called — and I have confirmed the spelling on this, I have checked the website, which features a stock photograph of two people holding hands in a field of wheat that is absolutely not a crop grown in Dawson County — *The Threshing Floor*.

So there is that.

I am Margot. I am the reporter. I am also, for purposes of the inquiry, the subject, though the line between those two categories is one I have, historically, been very good at maintaining, which is to say I maintain it the way a surgeon maintains the sterile field — automatically, because the alternative is contamination, because the alternative is everything going septic and someone dying on the table, and in this metaphor the someone is my ability to function in a room full of people who share my last name.

The drive from Lincoln takes three hours. I left nine years ago.

I have the rental, the microphone, and forty-seven hours of blank recording capacity on the Zoom H6, which is more than enough for a weekend, which is more than enough for anything.

## Act Two: Cartography of the Changed Country

The farmhouse was white clapboard with green shutters. Is. *Is* white clapboard with green shutters — the verb tense catching like a hangnail on the present, because the house I left is the house I'm pulling up to, except it isn't, and the difference is legible from approximately a quarter mile out, which is where the gravel drive meets the county road and where there is now a sign.

The sign is hand-painted, tasteful in the way that implies someone watched a YouTube tutorial on the subject, and it reads:

**THE THRESHING FLOOR**
*A Space for Couples to Do the Tender Work*
*Private Retreat • Workshops • Seasonal Gatherings*
*Jim & Linda Olssen-Crane, Facilitators*

*Jim & Linda Olssen-Crane, Facilitators.* My parents. Facilitators. My father, who once communicated marital displeasure exclusively through the angle at which he held the Omaha World-Herald at breakfast — fifteen degrees from vertical meaning mild irritation, horizontal meaning someone should check the gun safe — is a facilitator. My mother, who for the first forty-eight years of her life expressed emotional vulnerability only through the medium of casserole — a tuna-noodle indicating concern, a king-ranch-chicken indicating love, a cold-pasta-salad indicating a disappointment so vast and architectural it could not be spoken aloud — is a facilitator.

I pull up the drive and perform my first field recording: the tires on gravel, identical to the tires-on-gravel of every arrival and departure in this driveway for the past thirty-one years of my life, except that now the gravel borders a small decorative garden featuring a sculpture of two abstract human figures leaning into each other in a way that suggests either mutual support or a shared inner-ear condition.

The house: same bones, same porch, same slope of roof against the flatness of everything. But. The porch now holds two Adirondack chairs arranged at the precise angle of intentional conversation. The kitchen garden has been expanded and formalized into something with flagstone paths. There is a hand-lettered chalkboard by the front door reading *Today's Intention: Receive Without Rehearsing*. And on the back forty — visible from the driveway, visible from space, probably — there is a yurt.

It is a large yurt. It is, I will learn, a thirty-foot-diameter Pacific Yurt with insulated walls and a wood-burning stove, and it is where the group sessions happen, and I am asked to tell you all of this very slowly so that you can metabolize it at the pace I was unable to: there is a therapeutic yurt on the land where I learned to drive a tractor.

I step out of the rental. The air smells the same — that specific Nebraska autumn smell of cold dirt and corn stubble and distance, the olfactory equivalent of being told to calm down. I shoulder the field bag. I press record.

My mother comes out of the house.

She is — and here the journalistic inventory stalls for a half-beat, because the woman on the porch is and is not the woman of the mental photographs I have carried in the way one carries expired identification, as proof of a self that has technically been superseded — she is sixty-three, and she is wearing linen. My mother is wearing linen. Wide-legged linen pants in a color I believe the catalogs call "oat," and a tunic-type shirt, and her hair is longer than I have ever seen it and streaked with a gray she has not, apparently, bothered to correct, and the overall effect is of someone who has achieved either profound inner peace or a subscription to an expensive clothing brand marketed to women who have achieved profound inner peace, and from the driveway I cannot determine which.

"Margot," she says. Not a question. A recognition. She says it the way you say the name of a place you are visiting for the second time — with the pleasure of reconfirmation.

She comes down the porch steps and she hugs me and the hug lasts three seconds longer than any prior hug in our shared history, which I know because I am, God help me, counting.

"Hi, Mom."

"Your brother's here. He brought Tomas and Wren."

"Tomas and Wren."

"You'll love them."

This is delivered with a confidence I am not equipped to parse. My brother, Graham, two years younger, a large-animal veterinarian in Kearney, was, at the time of my departure, a man whose emotional range spanned the territory between "fine" and "pretty good" and who conducted his romantic life with the discretion of someone in witness protection. Tomas and Wren. Plural.

"He has," I say, selecting each word with the care of someone defusing ordnance, "two partners."

"He has two partners," my mother confirms, as if I have correctly identified a species of bird. "Tomas is the farrier. Wren does something with data. They're wonderful. Come inside."

I follow her. In the kitchen — the kitchen where the 4-H calendar hung for the entirety of my childhood, where the only posted text was a faded prayer card Scotch-taped to the cabinet above the stove reading *Lord, bless this food and the hands that prepared it* — there is a schedule. It is printed on cream cardstock in a font that suggests a graduate degree in graphic design, and it reads:

**THANKSGIVING RETREAT WEEKEND**
**The Threshing Floor — November 2024**

*Thursday*
7:00 AM — Silent Gratitude Walk (meet at yurt)
9:00 AM — Couples' Breathwork: "Arriving in the Body Together"
11:00 AM — Kitchen as Sacred Space: Preparing the Meal with Intention
2:00 PM — The Feast
4:00 PM — Fireside Sharing Circle
7:00 PM — Restorative Partnered Yoga (yurt, bring blankets)

*Friday*
7:00 AM — Journaling & Reflection
9:00 AM — Workshop: "The Myth of Self-Sufficiency"
11:00 AM — Free time / Nature immersion
2:00 PM — Closing Circle & Intention Setting

Where the prayer card used to be, there is now a small framed print reading: *The wound is the place where the light enters you — Rumi*.

I stand in my mother's kitchen and I note these things for the record. I note them thoroughly, comprehensively, with the full taxonomic attention of the trained observer. The tantric breathing schedule. The Rumi. The linen. The yurt. I note them because that is what I am here to do, and because the alternative to noting them is feeling them, and I have forty-seven hours of blank tape and there is absolutely no reason to waste any of it on silence.

---

The paying guests arrive in two cars within twenty minutes of each other, like birds of the same anxious species finding the same wire. There are six of them. Three couples.

Dennis and Maura, mid-sixties, from Papillion. He is a retired electrician who sits with his knees spread and his hands on his thighs like a man waiting for a diagnosis. She has the particular hairstyle of a woman who has maintained the same hairstyle since 1994, not out of indifference but out of a loyalty to the self she was then that she cannot fully explain. They are here because, Maura says when I introduce myself, "we've been married forty-one years and we realized last spring we've never actually had a conversation." She says this with a laugh that is either brave or insane. Dennis nods.

Craig and Bev, early fifties, from Hastings. He coaches high-school wrestling and has the compressed energy of a man who has been asked to sit in a circle and share his feelings about it. She teaches kindergarten and has the compressed energy of a woman who has spent twenty-six years loving a man who communicates primarily through referee signals.

And — this noted for its sociological interest, this noted for the segment, this absolutely not noted because it produces an unnameable sensation in the lower register of my chest — Hector and James, early forties, from Lincoln. Married seven years. Hector is an ER nurse. James manages a bookstore. They are here because, James says with a directness that makes me grip my field recorder a little tighter, "we lost a pregnancy in August, a surrogate, and we have not been able to touch each other since, and someone told us about this place, and we figured what the hell."

He says "what the hell" and Hector takes his hand, and the taking of the hand is so practiced and so effortful — practiced because it is effortful, effortful because it still matters enough to practice — that I feel the Zoom H6 in my bag like a tumor and I press RECORD through the fabric because that is what I do, that is the work, that is the only sanctioned reason for me to be standing in this room paying this much attention to the way a man's thumb moves across another man's knuckle.

My father appears from the direction of the back forty. He is wearing Carhartts, as he has always worn Carhartts, but over the Carhartts he is wearing a fleece vest embroidered with *The Threshing Floor* and a small logo of two hands cupping a seedling, and this detail almost finishes me right there in the mudroom but I hold the line.

"Margot," he says. Same voice. Same face. The same deeply Nebraskan face I carry in the bones of my own face, the face of people who were shaped by wind and the absence of mountains, who evolved a countenance specifically designed to reveal nothing to the horizon. Except that his eyes are — not different. *Unlocked*. As if someone found the key to a room in him that I always assumed was just wall.

"Hi, Dad."

"You're recording?"

"I'm always recording."

"Good," he says. "You were always the one who listened."

He says this and goes to wash his hands for dinner, and I am left in the mudroom with my microphone and the fact of it, which I file under background sound, ambient texture, the undifferentiated noise floor of a weekend in Nebraska, and I do not — I do not — give it its own track.

## Act Three: Field Work, or The Apparatus and Its Deceits

There is a methodology to what I do. I want to be clear about this. The methodology is the thing itself — is the product, the craft, the reason the work has value beyond its content. Anyone can shove a microphone at a person and capture speech. The journalist's task is architecture: where to place the device so that the room forgets it, what angle yields the voice at its least performed, how to arrange the silence around a statement so that the statement arrives in the listener's ear with the weight of something discovered rather than something said.

I know how to do this. I have done this in slaughterhouses, Senate offices, hospital wards, and one unforgettable afternoon in a Topeka karaoke bar where a woman sang "Angel of the Morning" with such devastating sincerity that two sound engineers cried during the mix. I am good at this the way a card counter is good at blackjack: not through talent, which is cheap, but through a disciplined refusal to be distracted by the game's emotional noise.

So: the devices. Thursday morning, before the Silent Gratitude Walk, while the house sleeps and the paying couples dream their paying-couple dreams, I place microphones.

One Zoom H1n, set to auto-record at volume threshold, in the kitchen, wedged between the ceramic rooster cookie jar that has occupied the same counter space since the Reagan administration and a new addition — a small wooden bowl of affirmation cards reading things like *I am worthy of the love I seek* and *My vulnerability is my strength*, which is the kind of sentence I used to hear in therapy before I stopped going to therapy, before the therapist said the thing about the microphone being a wall, before I told her that walls are load-bearing and that's the point.

One H1n in the living room, behind the bookshelf, which now holds — alongside the old Larry McMurtry paperbacks and the Willa Cather my mother kept for decoration — titles including *Mating in Captivity*, *The Body Keeps the Score*, and a book called *Passionate Marriage* whose cover features a photograph of a sunset over wheat that is, again, not a crop grown in Dawson County.

One H1n in the yurt. This one is the most aspirational placement — the yurt is the ceremonial center, the holy of holies, the place where the actual therapeutic work occurs — and I tuck it behind a stack of Mexican blankets on a shelf near the wood stove, angling the capsule toward the circle of floor cushions where, presumably, people will shortly sit and expose the most calcified parts of themselves to one another while a retired soybean farmer and his wife in linen facilitate.

The verb "facilitate" — I keep coming back to it. To facilitate: from the Latin *facilis*, easy.
