# The One Square Mile God Finished

---

I am already inside the piano when I will tell you this.

My name is Lenora Sayles and I have tuned keyboards in the territories and in the state for forty-one years, and before you ask, no, I was not born blind, I went blind at seven from a fever that took my sight and gave me, in its place, an ear so particular that I can tell you the age of a piano string by the way it holds its overtones, the way a widow holds her mouth at a funeral — tight at first, then loose, then not at all. I have my tools on the felt. I have my tuning lever and my mutes and my temperament strip, and I am working on this upright, which is a Kimball from perhaps 1902, which has not been tuned in a period I would estimate at six years by the dropped pitch of its middle register, and which sits in a building I have been told will be rubble by Tuesday.

No one requested this tuning. Let me say that clearly so you understand the terms. The church that owned this building dissolved its congregation. The county that owns this lot has sold it to a man who sells gravel. I came because the instrument is here and it is out of tune, and because I have spent my life entering rooms that did not ask for me and leaving them closer to true.

I am going to say the name of a man.

I am going to say it into the open cavity of this piano, where the soundboard will receive it the way a lake receives a stone — not by holding it forever but by carrying it outward in every direction until the motion is too thin to measure. I am going to say how he lived. I am going to do it in this room, before this room ceases to exist, because the room is the last space I know of that his body touched that has not yet been renamed, repurposed, or paved.

His name was Jubilee Meeks.

He could not write it. I will come to that.

---

I was not there when he arrived. I was a girl in Muskogee, already sightless, already learning pitch by laying my cheekbone against the casings of any piano that would let me near. But I have heard the arrival told so many times, by so many mouths, in so many rooms where I sat working with my back to the teller, that the sounds have settled in me the way sediment settles in a glass — not as memory but as geology. I know what I was told. I know the weight of what I was told. And the ear does not lie the way the eye lies, because the ear cannot be shown only what power wants it to see. The ear receives everything in the room. It has no lid.

He came from the south. That is the direction people named when they told it, and I believe them, because the people who came from the south in those years had a particular gravity in their footfall — they struck the ground harder than those who came from the east, as though they were still testing whether the earth beneath them was real, whether it would hold, whether it belonged to the earth at all or was merely a painted scrim that would be pulled away, revealing the ordinary hell behind it. That is a sound I know. I have tuned pianos in the homes of dozens of men and women who walked that way, and I have never confused it with any other gait.

The horse was overloaded. Everyone agreed on this. You could hear it in the animal's breathing, which carried a rattle that meant the rib cage was compressed by the load — not dangerously, but enough that the lungs could not fill fully, and each exhale came with a faint whistle, like wind through a cracked door. The load was iron. Farrier's tools. Hammers — a driving hammer and a rounding hammer, distinguishable by mass, the first making a lower thud against the horse's flank with each step, the second lighter, sharper, knocking against what I was told was a portable anvil strapped with rope to the animal's left side. Tongs. A rasp. A box of nails that rang faintly, like a toy piano, with every hoof-strike on the ungrassed track.

And the hooves themselves were bare. Unshod. Do you hear the irony that makes? A farrier riding an unshod horse into the territories. The sound of those hooves told you the soil — a chalky, calcium-rich dirt, pale and powdery, the residue of an ancient seabed, and I know this because I have been told that the unshod hoof on that soil made a dry, almost calcium-bright sound, a flat muted report with no ring, no resonance, the kind of dead acoustic you get from striking a surface that absorbs rather than reflects. That soil was good for grass. Good for nothing else, unless you were the kind of person for whom *nothing else* was already more than you had ever been given, in which case it was the world.

He dismounted. He did not speak. Not a word, and every account agrees on this silence. The first sound Jubilee Meeks made in the territory that would become his was the sound of a metal stake being driven into red earth.

I know that sound. Rather — I know its family. I have spent my life listening to metal under tension, metal being struck, metal entering and altering the material it meets. A tuning pin enters a pin block and the sound it makes tells you the density of the wood, the moisture content, the years ahead the instrument will hold its pitch. A stake entering soil is the same diagnostic. The ring, the resistance, the depth of the bite. I was told his stake sank easily for the first four inches, then caught on somethingite orite — someite or stone — and the ring changed from dull to bright, and he adjusted his angle, and drove it past the obstruction, and the final strikes were the deep, settled sound of metal that has found its hold and will not come out without considerable persuasion.

That was the first sound that mattered in this story. A man who could not write his own name, who understood — with the certainty of someone who had been owned, who had been owned the way a tool is owned, used and set down and picked up again at another's pleasure — that the only ground you possess is the ground you mark before someone marks it for you.

He marked it with a hammer. Not a pen.

I want you to hold that distinction in your ear. Hammer, not pen. The whole story turns on it.

---

Now I will tell you about the writing of the laws, and you must understand that what I am telling you is the story of a transformation more violent and more beautiful than anything I have heard in forty-one years of listening to metal and wood try to become music.

Within two years he had drawn families to him. How, I cannot say exactly, though I suspect it was the same way a struck tuning fork draws sympathetic vibration from a string across the room — not by persuasion but by frequency. He was operating at a pitch that other people recognized. Freedpeople. Exodusters out of Kansas and Mississippi and Tennessee. Creek freedmen. Seminole freedmen with their own claims tangled in a different knot of law and blood and broken paper. They came, and when enough of them had come, the settlement needed what every gathering of humans needs the moment it exceeds the number that can be governed by a single man's voice carrying across a single room: it needed law.

Jubilee could not read. Jubilee could not write. I say this not to diminish him. I say it because it is the central fact of his power and the central fact of his destruction. He carried his law in his body the way I carry pitch in mine — not as something learned from a page but as something *felt*, tested against experience, refined by every injustice that had ever been applied to his person, the way a blade is refined by the stone that grinds it. His sense of what was fair did not come from Blackstone or the Constitution. It came from having lived inside a system of absolute unfairness and having listened — year after year, in a condition where listening was the only freedom available — to every sound that system made. He knew what injustice sounded like. He knew its tempo, its rhetoric, its false harmonics. And he simply — I believe this, I have always believed this — he simply reversed it. Every law he conceived was the negative image of a cruelty he had survived. Where he had been denied water, he legislated water rights with a precision that made territorial judges blink. Where he had seen animals beaten past use, he made the treatment of horses a matter of public law, enforceable, punishable. Where he had been forbidden to read, he required — *required* — that every household in his township teach one other household their letters. This was not charity. It was not uplift. It was architecture. He was building the inverse of the place he had escaped from, and he was building it by sound, by voice, by the spoken word that carried from his mouth to the ear of Parthenia Sills.

Parthenia. The schoolteacher. She arrived with three primers and a bottle of ink and a penmanship so careful that I was later told you could feel the letters with your fingertips, raised slightly from the page by the pressure of her nib — and I did feel them, once, years later, on a document that survived what followed, and them letters were as legible to my fingers as Braille, each one formed with the deliberate pressure of a woman who understood that the marks she was making had to carry the full weight of another person's mind.

I was told what the dictation sessions sounded like. Jubilee pacing the porch of the building he used as a courthouse, which was also the building where he shod horses, so the porch boards had the particular springy give of wood that has absorbed oil and iron filings, and his boots on those boards made a metered sound, almost iambic, a walking rhythm that was also a thinking rhythm. He spoke in long stretches. Unpunctuated — or rather, punctuated only by breath, and his breath was punctuation enough. He would speak a full provision of law in a single exhale, and Parthenia's pen would begin scratching the instant he began, and the two sounds — his voice and her nib — ran parallel, like two horses pulling the same wagon, and when one got ahead of the other there was a small disturbance in the rhythm, a syncopation, and Jubilee would slow, or Parthenia would say *wait*, and the rhythm would re-establish itself.

Sometimes she stopped him for a different reason. He would use a word that was not, strictly speaking, a word. Or rather, it was a word he had made, because the situation he was legislating had no English term that fit. I was told he had a law concerning what happened when two families both needed a well dug but could afford only one. The provision required — and I am told the word he used was something like *co-holden*, not beholden but not merely cooperative, a word that meant *you are held together by the thing you share, and the sharing is the holding*. Parthenia asked him how to spell it. He could not tell her. She asked him to say it again, and he said it three times, each time slightly different, the word reshaping itself in his mouth the way hot iron reshapes under the hammer — and she wrote down her best approximation, and that approximation became the law.

I think about that. I think about it when I am tuning. I think about the moment a sound becomes a mark on a page, and what is lost in the passage. A vibration in the air is sovereign. It belongs to the body that made it and to the room that holds it and to the ear that receives it, and it answers to no authority but physics. But a mark on a page — a mark on a page can be read by anyone. Moved by anyone. Seized. Burned. Overwritten by a clerk with a different pen and a different idea of who deserves the benefit of the law. Jubilee's voice, in his body, in the air of his porch, was untouchable. No power on earth could have made him unthink his laws. But the moment Parthenia's nib touched paper, the laws left his body and entered a medium that was subject to every power on earth.

The tools of liberation and the tools of destruction are the same tools.

I know this. Every tuner knows it. The lever that brings a string to pitch is the same lever that can wind it past its breaking point.

He had a law that said no man could hold more land than he could walk the perimeter of before noon. Think of that. Think of the body it centers — the human body, walking, as the measure of what a person may claim. Not the surveyor's chain. Not the railroad's grant. Not the speculator's plat map filed in a distant office. Your legs. The sun. That is the jurisprudence of a man who has been made into property and has decided, in the aftermath of that making, that property must be scaled to the body, or it becomes the instrument of another making.

The laws were written down. Parthenia's pages accumulated. And I find I need to pause and tune this A above middle C, which has drifted flat in a way that suggests the pin is loose in its hole, the wood having shrunk around it through years of dryness, years of no one in this room breathing the moisture that a congregation breathes, that keeps the air human and the wood supple and the pins tight.

There.

---

I knew the sound of the township at its best. I will tell that now, and I will tell it from my own ear, not from anyone else's account, because by the late 1890s I was making my circuit through the territories — Boley, Langston, Taft, Red Bird, and the township Jubilee had built, which had a name, which I will say when I am ready, which I am holding for now the way you hold a note you are not yet ready to resolve.

I tuned pianos. That was my route. I traveled by wagon with a man named Curtis who drove for me and who said, on average, eleven words a day, which I appreciated because it meant the acoustic space around me was clean, and when I entered a town I could hear the town instead of hearing Curtis. He would bring me to the instrument and leave, and I would work, and while I worked, the town played itself for me.

Jubilee's township on a Saturday. Let me say what I heard.

I could separate nine gaits on the main street without effort. Men in brogans — the heavy, flat, whole-footed strike of men who had labored since they were old enough to lift. Women in laced boots — a sharper, more vertical sound, the heel landing first, the sole following in a controlled roll that spoke of posture held upright under the weight of whatever they were carrying, and they were always carrying something: water, children, opinion. Children barefoot, the slap of bare sole on packed earth, arrhythmic and fast, a sound like applause from a scattered audience. Old Deacon Samuels, who had one leg and moved on a crutch so well-fitted to his body that the crutch-tip and his remaining foot struck the ground in a dotted rhythm, long-short long-short, as steady as a metronome, and I used it to calibrate my tuning when I was working near a window. A dog — I believe the same dog each time, a large one, heavy-chested — whose claws on the porch boards made a particular scrabbling that told me its nails were overgrown and the boards were smooth from traffic. The sound of the water pump behind the general store, which squealed on the third stroke of every pumping cycle because a bearing was dry, and which was never fixed in all the years I visited because, I believe, the squeal had become a landmark, a sound people navigated by the way sighted people navigate by a steeple.

And under all of it — through all of it — the ring of Jubilee's anvil.

He was still shoeing horses. Let me say that twice, because it is the thing that should be understood above all other things about this man. He was lawmaker and judge. He had built the codes that governed water and debt and domestic quarrel and the treatment of animals and the spacing of fences and the conduct of elections and the education of children. He had settled more disputes than any magistrate in the territory, and he had done it with a precision that left both sides feeling that something exact had been accomplished, even if neither side was entirely satisfied, which — I have come to understand — is the sound of justice, the same way a well-tempered scale is not *pure*, not free of all friction, but is distributed in its impurities so evenly that every key is equally, tolerably, beautifully imperfect. And still he shod horses. He refused to be a man who governed without working. His hands stayed in iron. His back stayed bent over the hoof. And the sound of his hammer on his anvil was, for me, the base frequency of the town — the fundamental tone on which every other sound was built, so that if you removed it, all the harmonics would collapse, and what you would have left is not a chord but noise.
