# The Cartographer Who Walked Backward

## Being the Official Chronicle and Evidentiary Record of the Inquiry into the Conduct, Commission, and Disposition of Eluin nin Vassar, Senior Cartographer of the Royal Guild of Memory and Measure, Compiled by Order of the Seventh Judiciary Panel of His Majesty's Court at Threnne

---

### Preamble and Jurisdictional Authority

**§1.** This chronicle is compiled under the authority of Judiciary Writ 1172-A, which grants the Seventh Panel full investigatory power over matters pertaining to the so-called Pallor Crisis of the Western Reaches, inclusive of all persons conscripted into the Crown's identification and containment efforts during the period spanning the 14th of Ashmonth through the 3rd of Midwinter, Year of the Closed Gate.

**§2.** The undersigned keeper of this record is the Court Scribe, Third Appointment, whose name and personal particulars are immaterial to these proceedings and whose function is solely to transcribe, organize, and preserve testimony for the use of the Panel and its successors. The scribe is an instrument. A tool of notation. There is no reason a tool should need to state, at the outset, that it is broken.

**§3.** The subject of this chronicle is Eluin nin Vassar, age thirty-one at the time of her conscription, unmarried, no surviving kin excepting a maternal aunt in the Northern Reach Province whose correspondence was intercepted and is entered separately as Exhibit 14-C. Senior Cartographer, which is the Guild's formal designation for one who has mastered the discipline of *lucid survey*, commonly known — inaccurately, reductively, as though the word could hold what she did — as "memory-walking."

The court's preferred terminology for her condition, latterly, was *sensory displacement disorder.* I will use this terminology where required, because I have been told to, and I am a tool of notation. But I will note, once, for whatever record outlives this one: her hands, when she held a mapping stylus, were the steadiest things in any room she entered. I transcribed for her over the span of three years, and I never once saw those hands shake until the week before the end.

This is a chronicle. It is not a confession.

I will try very hard to remember that.

### Part the First: Demonstration Before the Guild — 7th of Ashmonth

**§4.** Twelve days before the first Pallor reports reached the capital, Eluin nin Vassar performed a standard recovery walk before an assembly of the Cartographic Guild and three representatives of the Crown's Survey Office. The purpose was evidentiary: to recover the precise location of a boundary marker along the Thessenwald border, said marker having been destroyed in the flooding of the previous autumn and its coordinates disputed between two provincial governors who had been threatening armed survey for the better part of a year.

The assembly convened in the Lower Cartography Hall, a room I have transcribed in before: stone walls, high clerestory windows, a faint smell of linseed and old vellum that settles into one's clothing and stays for days. Twenty-three persons present, seated in the observation gallery. I was stationed at the recording desk, six paces from the survey platform, as protocol requires.

**§5.** The trigger object presented to Eluin was a sample of river clay taken from the Thessenwald border region — a specific alluvial composite, I am told, with a mineral signature unique to a three-mile stretch of the southern fork. It was placed in a shallow ceramic dish. Eluin held the dish in both hands. She closed her eyes. She inhaled.

What happened next, I have transcribed eleven times across three years of service, and I have never once found a way to render it accurately. The court should understand that my transcription of Eluin's walks is, by necessity, a reduction — the way a map is a reduction of the territory it represents. I record what she says aloud. I cannot record what she *becomes.*

**§6.** Her breathing changed first. It slowed and deepened, and there was a quality to it — I do not have the technical vocabulary, though the Guild physicians surely do — as though her lungs were drawing in a different air than the rest of us were breathing. Her pupils dilated. I know this because I was watching her face with the attention the court may choose to call professional diligence.

Then she spoke.

What follows is the verbatim transcription of her vocal report during the walk. Notations in brackets are my observations of her physical state. I have been trained to maintain both streams simultaneously. It is, I was told during my apprenticeship, like writing with both hands. It is nothing like that. It is like trying to take notes on the ocean while drowning in it.

**§7.** The walk lasted four minutes and twelve seconds by my count. Eluin opened her eyes. She placed the dish of clay carefully on the survey platform.

What the assembly saw: a successful recovery. Applause — restrained, professional, but genuine. The Guild Master nodded. The Crown representatives exchanged satisfied looks. The boundary dispute could be resolved. Coordinates had been obtained. The mechanism had functioned.

What I saw, because I was six paces away and it is my function to observe:

The moment before Eluin's eyes opened, her face wore an expression I will not describe as *pain*, because pain implies a single, locatable sensation, and this was something wider and less navigable. It was the face of a person who has been *in a place* and is being *pulled out of it* — not willingly, not cleanly, but as a hook pulls a fish from water, which is to say: from one medium of being into another that is technically survivable but fundamentally alien.

Her eyes opened. She looked at the assembly and, for a span of approximately three seconds — I counted; counting is what I do when I am frightened — she did not appear to recognize the room. Her gaze moved across the gallery with the particular quality of someone trying to identify a sound they cannot locate. Then her focus returned. She oriented. She smiled, which was the smile she used for the Guild and the court, which was not the same smile she used when she was actually—

This is a chronicle. I will confine myself to observable phenomena.

She blinked. She touched the edge of the survey platform with two fingers, a light, confirmatory touch, the way one touches a wall in a dark corridor. Then she said, aloud but softly, not directed at the assembly, almost to herself:

"I'm back. I can still smell — yes. The linseed. The old vellum. I'm here."

And then, lower still, not in her public voice but in the voice I had come to recognize from three years of working in close quarters, the voice she used when she was verifying her own existence:

"Bread. I can still—"

She stopped. She straightened her shoulders. She turned to the Guild Master and delivered her formal report.

I transcribed it. I am a tool of notation.

**§8.** The assembly dispersed. The Crown representatives departed. The hall emptied. I was collating my transcription pages when Eluin returned — I had not realized she had left — to retrieve a scarf she had forgotten on the survey platform. She paused by my desk. She smelled of the river clay, faintly, as though the walk had deposited a sediment of elsewhere on her skin.

"Did you get all of it?" she asked.

"Every word."

She nodded. Then: "The part at the end. The part about the smell."

"I included it."

She looked at me — and the court may record this or not, it happened regardless — with an expression that I can only, uselessly, inadequately describe as *gratitude for being witnessed.* As though the most important thing about the walk was not the coordinates but the fact that someone had been watching her come back.

"Thank you," she said. And left.

The Panel may note that this exchange is immaterial to the jurisdictional inquiry and should have been excluded from the official record. The Panel may note whatever it wishes. I am the one holding the pen.

### Part the Second: The Onset of the Pallor Crisis — 14th through 22nd of Ashmonth

**§9.** What follows is a condensed summary of the incident reports received by the Seventh Judiciary Panel during the week of the 14th. I have organized them chronologically. I have tried to organize them chronologically. In retrospect there is something about the Pallor that resists chronology, as though the events themselves had been smoothed of their sequence, sanded into a single featureless surface.

**Incident Report 7, Filed 15th of Ashmonth, Threadneedle Ward.** Witness: Maren Goss, baker's wife, 46 years. Deposition excerpt: "He came home at the usual hour. He washed his hands at the basin the way he always does, left hand first. He asked after the children. He knew their names. He sat in his chair and he ate his supper and he knew which was his cup and which was mine, and I keep moving them, Magistrate, I keep switching them to test him, and he always knows. He always knows. But I am telling you he is not my husband. His hands are the same and his voice is the same and his body is the same body I have slept beside for twenty-two years but he *smells of nothing.* Not like he's been scrubbed clean. Not like absence. Like the *idea* of absence. Like the air around him has been — I don't have the word. I don't have the word for what he is."

**Incident Report 11, Filed 17th of Ashmonth, Coppergate District.** Witness: Lenn Aldrath, clerk, 29 years. Deposition excerpt: "My mother has called me by the wrong name my whole life — she calls me Bevvith, which was my uncle's name, and then corrects herself, and I have never once minded. It's the correction that I loved. The little shake of her head and the *oh, I mean Lenn, you know I mean Lenn.* She came to see me on the 16th. She called me Lenn. First try. Every time. She didn't shake her head once. She didn't correct herself because there was nothing *to* correct. She was — she was performing my mother from a perfect copy of the text, but she had not understood that the errors were the things I loved most."

**Incident Report 14, Filed 18th of Ashmonth, Judiciary Quarter.** Witness: Ceyl Ashward, age 16, daughter of Magistrate Pol Ashward. Deposition excerpt: [Witness was distressed. Transcription reflects pauses.] "I hugged him. I always — when he comes home from — I hugged him. And he hugged me back and his arms were the right arms and his coat was the right coat and he said *there's my girl* the way he always — but. [Pause.] You know when you walk into a room where someone has been cooking, and you can't see the food, but you know what it is because the *air* tells you? My father's coat always smelled of pipe tobacco and the leather of his chair and something else, something under those, something that was just — him. Just the smell of my father. And when I hugged him his coat smelled of nothing. Not the nothing of a clean coat. The nothing of a — of a — [Pause. Witness is crying.] — of a place where a person should be."

**§10.** The pattern was established by the 19th. Forty-seven reports in eight wards. The replacements — the court had not yet coined the term "Pallor duplicates," which would come later, along with the formal epidemiological designation, as though naming the thing could shrink it to the size of a policy problem — were appearing without observable mechanism. No one witnessed the replacement itself. Citizens went to sleep genuine and woke up — or appeared to wake up — *performed.* Or they left a room and returned subtly, catastrophically different. Or they didn't change at all and their families were wrong, were hysterical, were suffering from the general anxiety that attends times of crisis. This was the official position of the Crown for nearly three days: that the reports reflected only the public's well-documented tendency toward pattern-finding in the absence of real threat.

The official position shifted on the 21st, when the Royal Physician examined eleven suspected duplicates and reported his findings in a sealed memorandum I am not authorized to reproduce in full but which contained the following sentence, since entered into public evidence:

*"The subjects are, by every metric available to medical science, indistinguishable from their originals. Blood, bone, tissue, reflex. But when I listen with a stethoscope to the space between heartbeats, there is a quality — I cannot name it — a quality of* silence *that I have never before encountered in a living body."*

**§11.** On the 22nd of Ashmonth, the Crown issued Conscription Order 54-W, directing the Cartographic Guild to make available "all qualified practitioners of lucid survey" for the purpose of "identifying, through whatever mechanisms the Guild's discipline affords, those citizens who have been subjected to anomalous replacement, commonly styled the 'Pallor.'"

All qualified practitioners. There were, at the time, three living cartographers certified in lucid survey. One was seventy-four and long retired to the coast. One had suffered a stroke the previous winter and could no longer sustain a walk beyond thirty seconds. The third was Eluin nin Vassar.

I was present when the order was read to her. I was always present. That was my function.

She was standing by the window of the Lower Cartography Hall, holding a cup of tea that had gone cold — she was always letting her tea go cold, always intending to drink it and then forgetting because something had caught her attention, a thought or a shadow or the way the dust moved in a column of light, and the cup would sit in her hand growing cool while her mind went wherever her mind went when she—
