# Quedarse

---

## I.

Here is the first postcard. Hold it by the edges, please, the way I showed you.

The front is a photograph from the 1970s — a saguaro cactus against a gradient sky, the colors oversaturated in that particular way of cheap lithography, the green too hopeful, the sunset too committed to its own pinks. In the lower-left corner, italicized: *Greetings from the Sonoran Corridor.* There is a small coffee ring overlapping the cactus's left arm. Someone used this postcard as a coaster before someone else used it as a letter.

Turn it over.

The handwriting is pressed deep into the card stock, blue ballpoint bearing down hard enough to emboss. A left-handed writer — you can tell from the smearing, the way the heel of the hand dragged through the still-wet ink of each previous word, turning the sentences into small ruined rivers running east.

> *I loaded the car this morning. The urn doesn't fit in the cup holder, which you would have found funny — you would have said something about how even in death you can't be convenient. I buckled it into the passenger seat. I used the belt extender from when your mother visited. I'm on the 10 now. Your route is taped to the dashboard. Your handwriting looks like you're still alive, which is the cruelest thing about handwriting. I don't know who I'm writing this to. I bought the postcard at the gas station where I filled up. The woman at the register said "have a good trip, honey" and I almost told her everything.*

There is no address on the right side. No stamp. She wrote it and kept it, the way you keep a breath you were going to release but didn't.

Now, here — this goes beside it. The gas station receipt. You can see the total ($38.47, regular unleaded, one bottled water, one postcard — $0.99) and on the back, in a different hand, a man's hand, architect-precise block letters that don't match the postcard's urgent scrawl:

> **ROUTE: 10W → exit 246 → Old Hwy 80 → Swan Motel (they'll have the room) → Chapel Fuel, mi. 12 past the motel, turn at the blue mailbox → Twin Rivers Bridge (no exit marked, look for the cottonwood split by lightning) → Las Ofrendas, end of the road**

Each stop has a small symbol beside it — a circle with a line through it, like a zero negated, or a planet with a horizon. I have seen this symbol before, on other documents, in other hands, across what I can only describe as a span of time that would make you uncomfortable if I named it. I won't name it yet.

Beside the receipt, lay this: a AAA Southwestern States road map, edition 2006, which means it was already seventeen years out of date when she folded it into the glove box. Page 74, the southern Arizona section. Someone — the same block-letter hand — has circled five locations in yellow highlighter. The highlighter has bled through to page 73 (eastern New Mexico), creating pale haloes around towns that have nothing to do with the route, ghost-marks from the pressure of the pen on the other side. This is how traces work. You press hard enough into one thing and you leave a mark on the thing beneath it, the thing you didn't know was there.

The map is annotated. Beside the first circled stop: *She'll know when she sees it.* Beside the second: *Tell her to check the guest book, third page from the back.* Beside the third: *She'll hear the rivers before she sees them.*

Beside the last stop, the one labeled Las Ofrendas, he wrote only: *Quedarse.*

I should tell you what that word means, but not yet.

One more thing from the box. The guest book. It is a cloth-covered journal, the kind sold at tourist shops, its cover printed with howling coyotes and crescent moons. The first entry is dated eleven years before Marisol's trip. The last is dated three days before I sat down to organize these materials for you. Between those dates, the book has been handled by at least forty people, based on the variation in handwriting, the different inks, the occasional smudge of sunscreen or lipstick or motor oil on the pages. Most entries are banal — *Great trip! Kids loved the pool!* — but seven of them are not. You will know the seven when you reach them. They are the ones written by women who were not writing about vacations. They are the ones that sound like prayers. They are the ones where the handwriting changes mid-sentence, as if the writer suddenly remembered something she hadn't known she'd forgotten.

I have arranged the materials for you in the order Marisol encountered them, which is not the order in which they were created, which is not the order in which they matter. Chronology is a convenience for people who believe time moves in one direction. I have not believed this for a very long while.

My hands are steady. I have done this before.

Let us begin.

---

## II.

I need to tell you something before we go further, and I need you to hold it loosely, the way you'd hold a bird that landed on your hand — firm enough that it doesn't startle away, gentle enough that you don't feel its heartbeat stop.

I have watched this journey before.

Not metaphorically. Not in the way a scholar watches a historical pattern recur, noting the rhymes between empire and empire, flood and flood. I mean I have stood — in whatever way I stand, which is not the way you stand, which is more like the way light stands in a window, which is to say *I was there and the room was changed by my presence but no one looked at me directly* — I have stood in the room when the woman who was not yet called Marisol packed a bag for the road. I have stood in the room when the man who was not yet called Tomás said to her, *I found a route. You'll love it. Every stop is shaped like something impossible.* I have heard him say this, or versions of this, in languages that would fill a catalog you'd need a separate grant to index.

*What I am telling you is that they have done this before.*

Not the road trip. The road trip is new each time—the car is different, the highway is different, the century is different, the name of the desert is different. What is not different is the essential gesture: *one of them dies, and the other one drives*.

I have the evidence. You are holding some of it. The rest is in boxes I will open for you as we go, because I have learned — across the iterations, across what I suppose you would call my professional experience — that too much evidence at once produces not belief but suspicion. You would put the documents down. You would say, *This is a very elaborate fiction.* And you would leave, and I would be alone again, and the collecting would have been for nothing.

So let me ask you something instead.

Have you ever read a love letter that wasn't addressed to you and felt, with strange certainty, that it was? Not that you wished it were — that it *was*. That the person who wrote *I would find you in any life, I would know your mouth in any language* was not being rhetorical, was not deploying the inflated promises that lovers deploy, but was in fact describing a logistics problem they had solved.

If you have felt this, stay.

If you haven't — perhaps you will.

I am not a storyteller. I need you to understand this. I am a curator of materials that accumulated in my care across a period I will not specify because the number would make you unkind to me, and I cannot bear unkindness today, I am very tired. What I can tell you is that the documents are real. The paper is real. The ink is real. The love is so real it has worn grooves into the geography the way a river wears grooves into stone — slowly, over eons, until the landscape has no choice but to accommodate its direction.

Marisol is the latest name. Tomás is the latest name. The love is the oldest thing I've ever held, and I have held — but no. We are going in order. I promised you order.

What is in front of you: one woman's road trip. What is behind it: every other time. What I need from you: the willingness to hold both.

Here. Take the map. I've marked where we are.

---

## III.

The Swan Motel sits fourteen miles from the highway on a two-lane blacktop with no center line, the asphalt so sun-bleached it is almost the same color as the desert on either side. You could miss it. People do. But Marisol did not miss it, because the directions said *turn at the wooden swan* and there was the wooden swan — ten feet tall, planted in the dirt median like a ship's figurehead run aground, its paint peeling in strips that did in fact look like feathers releasing from a body in the process of becoming something else. One eye had fallen off. The other stared at the road with the fixed, manic optimism of a thing that had been built to astonish and now astonished only by its refusal to collapse.

The motel behind it was shaped like the swan's bigger, stranger sister. Two wings of rooms extending from a central lobby, the roofline curved to suggest plumage. The neck of the building was the entrance archway — a long, arched corridor you drove through to reach the parking lot, stucco sculpted into the suggestion of feathers, the beak forming a canopy above the front office door. It had been white once. It was the color of old teeth now. The neon sign on the beak said **VA A CY**, the other letters dark, which meant there was either a vacancy or the sign was a command: *go to CY*, whoever CY was, whatever CY knew.

Marisol parked and sat in the car for four minutes. I know this because the dashboard clock is visible in a photograph she took — a photograph of the steering wheel and, beyond it, through the windshield, the motel office door with its brass swan knocker. 4:47 PM in the first photo, 4:51 PM in the second. In the intervening four minutes she did not check her phone, did not adjust the rearview, did not speak to the urn. She sat. Her hands were on the wheel. She was gathering something — not courage, exactly. Marisol was not short on courage. She was gathering *willingness*. There is a difference. Courage pushes you forward. Willingness lets the forward come to you.

At 4:51 she took the second photograph and got out of the car.

The woman at the desk was old enough that her age had become architectural — not a number but a *quality*, the way a canyon is not a measurement but a condition. She gave Marisol room 7 without being asked. She said: "Your husband called ahead." Marisol said: "My husband is dead." The woman looked at her with the particular patience of someone who has heard this before and considers it, while sad, irrelevant to the reservation. She slid the key across the counter. A real key, brass, heavy, on a plastic fob shaped like a swan.

Room 7 was wood-paneled, the grain so dark and knotted it looked like contour maps of mountains that existed only inside the trees they'd been cut from. The carpet was rust-colored and smelled of lavender and age — not decay, but the specific sweetness of a room that has been cleaned ten thousand times with the same product by the same hands. The bed was covered in a white chenille spread with a swan embroidered in the center, its neck forming a curve that Marisol stared at for a long time because it was the exact curve of the ceramic urn sitting in her car. She retrieved it. She set it on the right side of the bed, which was his side, which had been his side in two apartments and one house across eleven years of marriage.

She did not cry. You might expect her to cry. She had done her crying in the car between the funeral home and the on-ramp, a burst so violent she had to pull over, so total that afterward she felt not emptied but *recalibrated*, as though her body had needed to discharge something before it could function at the frequency required for the road. Now she was past crying. She was in the territory beyond crying, which is a quiet, wide-open space, not unlike the desert, where you can see a very long way and nothing is hidden and everything looks like it might be sacred.

She found the guest book in the nightstand drawer, beneath a Gideon Bible (King James, its spine cracked at Ecclesiastes, a passage underlined in pencil: *What has been will be again, what has been done will be done again; there is nothing new under the sun*) and a takeout menu for a Chinese restaurant called Lucky Dragon that, based on the menu's area code, was in a town forty miles away from anything.

The guest book was small, leather-bound, specific to this room. On the inside cover, handwritten in fountain pen: *Room 7 — for those who return.* Not *for returning guests*. For those who *return*. The distinction mattered, though Marisol could not yet have said why.

She turned to the third page from the back, as the map annotations instructed. Here is what she found there. I have reproduced it exactly, including the places where the ballpoint skipped and the ink went thin, which I've marked with brackets:

> *Novem[ber] 14, 1953*
>
> *Ele me trouxe aqui na primeira noite. O pescoço do cisne fazia sombra na cama e eu disse que era como dormir dentro de um conto. Ele disse: é um conto. Nós somos o conto. Eu não entendi naquela noite. Agora eu entendo. Chegamos a um lugar pela primeira vez e sabemos onde fica o interruptor de luz. Sabemos qual degrau range. Sabemos a curva exata que o pescoço faz na parede, porque a vimos antes, em outro corpo, em outro século, e o reconhecimento não é lembrança — é o osso sabendo o caminho de casa.*
>
> *Ele morreu na terça-feira. Estou voltando pelos lugares por onde passamos. Estou tentando entender por que o caminho parece mais longo sem ele e mais curto sem ele ao mesmo tempo.*
>
> *Se você está lendo isto, e se meu rosto parece com o seu, não tenha medo. Já fizemos isto antes. Faremos de novo. O amor é o único documento que sobrevive à tradução.*

Marisol does not speak Portuguese. She took two years of high school Spanish, enough to order food in restaurants and misunderstand song lyrics. She has never studied Portuguese — not in this life, not in any course catalog she could produce.

She read the entry without difficulty. Not translated it — *read* it, the way you read your own language, the meaning arriving before the mechanism, the understanding preceding the decoding. She read it the way you read a sign in a dream: you don't know the language, but you know what it says, because in the dream you are the language.

I will translate it for you, because you are not Marisol, and you have not yet earned the fluency that comes from repetition across lifetimes:

> *He brought me here on the first night. The swan's neck cast a shadow on the bed and I said it was like sleeping inside a story. He said: it is a story. We are the story. I didn't understand that night. I understand now. We arrive at a place for the first time and know where the light switch is. We know which step creaks. We know the exact curve the neck makes on the wall, because we have seen it before, in another body, in another century, and the recognition is not memory — it is the bone knowing its way home.*
>
> *He died on Tuesday. I am retracing the places we went together. I am trying to understand why the road feels longer without him and shorter without him at the same time.*
>
> *If you are reading this, and if my face looks like yours, don't be afraid. We have done this before. We will do it again. Love is the only document that survives translation.*
