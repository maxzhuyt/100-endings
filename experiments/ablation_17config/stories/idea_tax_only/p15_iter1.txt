# The Surrender Index

---

The word made flesh, she thinks, and almost laughs—almost, but the position won't allow it, the muscles of her abdomen engaged in the specific work of kneeling upright on the hardwood in the way he has explained is correct, which is to say the way that looks like nothing, like resting, though every fiber from hip to sternum is a drawn bow, and the thought completes itself without her permission: the word made flesh and *dwelt among us,* only dwelling is wrong, the Greek is *eskēnōsen,* tabernacled, pitched its tent, which implies the temporary, the provisional, a God who does not buy but *rents*—and the radiator in the corner clanks its one bronchial note as if to underscore the joke she hasn't made aloud because she has not been asked to speak, and his hands when they come to rest on the crown of her head smell like Dial soap, that orange institutional bar she associates with the dispensers in rest-stop bathrooms off I-95, not incense, not chrism, nothing anointing about it, just the alkaline honesty of a man who washed his hands before this the way you'd wash before a meal, and her knees are finding the grain of the floor, and she is thinking about kenosis.

She is always thinking about kenosis.

*Kenosis:* self-emptying. Philippians 2:7, *heauton ekenōsen*, he emptied himself, the great christological problem that ate three years of her life and produced a monograph Cambridge published in a gray cover she thought looked like a headstone and David Bentley Hart called "the most sophisticated contribution to the discourse in a generation" which she keeps in the second drawer of her office desk facedown not from modesty but because her own author photo, taken at thirty-four, has an expression of such composed certitude that she cannot look at it from *here,* from *this*, from the floor of a rent-controlled one-bedroom on West 106th Street with her hands palm-up on her thighs—a detail he specified, the palms, their upward orientation, and she had felt the rightness of it before she'd felt the absurdity: *stigmata position,* she'd thought the first time, *without the wounds, or rather the wounds are the point, the willingness toward the wound*—

His thumb traces the part in her hair, and the Song of Solomon surfaces like a body from deep water: *Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth, for thy love is better than wine.* She has not thought about the Song of Solomon in any devotional capacity since she was fourteen and Sister Alice at Our Lady of Mercy read it to their confirmation class in a voice carefully emptied of all inflection, as though the text were Levitical tax code, and Katherine Beecham two chairs over had gone red from sternum to scalp, and she herself had felt—what? Not arousal. Recognition. The recognition that language could do *that,* could make the body a site of address, and she had wanted—even then she had wanted—to528

His hand tightens. Not in her hair. On the back of her neck. The specific grip.

Everything else stops.

---

She does not stop, is the thing. She has never once been able to stop. The mind528—her particular mind, with its most-cited-article-in-the-Journal-of-Feminist-Theology mind, its keynote-at-the-AAR mind, its peer-review-seven-manuscripts-in-a-single-semester mind—does not go quiet when the body is handled. It goes *symphonic.* The hand on her neck and she is producing a treatise no one will read, a live annotation of her own submission, the text and the commentary running simultaneously like the Talmud's architecture of margin and center and she has never once in her life been the center, she is *always* the margin, the gloss, the voice that speaks about the thing rather than the thing—

Except here.

His other hand. The instruction, which is not spoken but applied, a downward pressure with geometric clarity: *lower.*

And lower she goes, and the floor meets her forehead, and the thought that arrives is: *This is the only prayer that has ever worked.*

---

It is Tuesday. It is, specifically, the third Tuesday, which means: the department colloquium at 4:00, the graduate seminar on Mudflower to Mujerista at 6:30, and between them the forty-minute window in which she will eat twelve almonds at her desk and answer the email from the tenure committee she has been avoiding since Friday. The colloquium is Ben Alderman on negative theology and climate grief, which will be adequate and earnest and precisely fifteen minutes too long, and she will sit in the second row because the front row implies endorsement she does not feel and the back row implies the contempt she does feel but cannot display, because Ben is coming up for tenure and she is on the committee and her face is, she has been told, "illegible in a way that makes junior colleagues spiral," which Marcus said at a cocktail party as though it were a charming observation and not a tiny indictment—

She is selecting the right scarf. This is not vanity. There are four bruises on her clavicle that are five days old and have ripened to the particular yellow-green of a Granny Smith apple, and the selection of the scarf involves a practical calculation she performs with the same quiet efficiency with which she calculates the modal shift between Rahnerian and Balthasarian soteriology: the silk one covers more but draws attention; the wool one is seasonally appropriate but sits too low; the gray cashmere is correct, is always correct, has become the Tuesday garment, and she wonders briefly if anyone has noticed that she wears it only on Tuesdays, and then she thinks: *No. People who study God for a living are not observant about scarves. This is, in its way, a foundational claim of the entire discipline.*

In the elevator she reviews her notes for the seminar. Ada María Isasi-Díaz. *Lo cotidiano*—the everyday as a site of theological knowledge. She will ask her students to consider whose *cotidiano* is legible as theology and whose is legible as pathology. She will mean it. She will also mean something else by it that she cannot say, a private text running beneath the pedagogical one, and the elevator opens onto the fifth floor and she is, immediately and completely, Dr. Miriam Loukas, Associate Professor of Constructive Theology, the woman whose work on kenosis and gendered power has been translated into nine languages, the woman who once made Kathryn Tanner pause for eleven seconds at a conference before conceding the point—eleven seconds she counted, she *counted them*—the woman whose graduate students refer to her, she knows, as "the Oracle," which is half affection and half terror and which pleases her in a complicated way she has not entirely theorized, though she has tried, though she always tries—

"Miriam! Did you see the announcement?"

Sandra Koh-Alvarez, patristics, holding a printout in the manner of a person who has been waiting in the hallway for the specific purpose of ambush.

"Which announcement."

"The Wainwright Chair. The shortlist. Don't tell me you didn't—the *shortlist*, Miriam."

And the floor tilts, very slightly, the way it does in dreams where you are in a building that is also a ship, and she says: "I haven't checked my email today," which is a lie of such precision that it functions as a truth, because she has checked her email eleven times today but not the email from the Provost's office because she has known since Friday what it would say and knowing a thing and *reading* a thing are categorically different, the difference between the doctrine and the sacrament, the map and the territory, and Sandra is still talking—

"—you and Okafor and, I think, possibly David Kim but I heard that might be a courtesy inclusion—"

"Sandra."

"Yes?"

"Thank you. I'll look at it properly after the colloquium."

She says this in her Oracle voice, the voice that makes graduate students revise their chapters and junior colleagues reconsider their premises, and Sandra retreats, and Miriam walks into her office and closes the door and stands very still with her hand on the back of her own neck where his hand was forty hours ago and she thinks: *the Wainwright Chair in Feminist Theology and Ethics, named for Dorothy Wainwright, endowed in 1987, the year I was born, which means it has existed for exactly as long as I have and has been waiting for me or I for it and this is the kind of magical thinking I teach my students to identify and dismantle—*

She sits. She does not open the email.

She opens instead, without deciding to, the browser window she keeps in a private tab behind two layers of departmental portal, and there he is: a screenshot of yesterday's EWTN appearance, the segment on liturgical renewal and the crisis of masculine formation. Father Gregory Alain Morse, S.J. The Roman collar. The jaw that looks carved for a reliquary. The hands, which on television gesture with the controlled authority of a man who has studied homiletics at the Roman rig and which in the apartment do—

She closes the tab.

His television voice: considered, mellifluous, with the slight Bostonian flatness he has never fully exorcised, speaking about "the recovery of authentic masculine spiritual leadership" to a nodding host, and she could annotate every sentence he produces on that show with a counter-text drawn from his actual behavior, the *actual* liturgy he performs on his knees with his face in—no. She will not do this here. The office is the office. The apartment is the apartment. The membrane holds. The membrane has held for nineteen months. The membrane is the *condition of possibility* for both lives, as Kant would say, except Kant would not say this, Kant would say nothing about this, Kant would leave the room—

"Surrender," says the student.

She has lost the transition. She is in the seminar now—it is 6:47 P.M. and she is in Mujerista Theology and Amy Park-Whitfield has said "surrender" in the context of a discussion about mystical experience and Mechthild of Magdeburg, and the word detonates in Miriam's chest like a flashbang, white-hot and concussive, and she can feel the floor of the apartment under her knees, can smell the Dial soap, can hear the specific register of his voice when he says *Stay*—

"—whether Mechthild's language of surrender to the divine beloved can be recuperated by feminist theology or whether it's irreparably contaminated by patriarchal—"

"Is anything irreparably contaminated?" Miriam hears herself say, and it is the Oracle voice, and her students are writing it down, and she continues: "Or is that the wrong question? What if contamination is the condition of all language about God? What if the erotic metaphor isn't a *layer* we can strip away from the mystical to get at some pure experience underneath—what if the erotic *is* the epistemic mode, and the question is not whether to use it but whether we're honest about what it costs?"

Amy Park-Whitfield's pen stops.

"What does it cost?" Amy asks.

And Miriam Loukas, who knows exactly what it costs, who could *itemize* what it costs—the scarf, the private browser, the forty-seven-minute crosstown cab ride she takes instead of the subway because the subway goes past his stop and she cannot be seen at his stop, the particular loneliness of being known completely by a person whose worldview you have built your career opposing—says: "That's your paper topic. Twelve pages. The Tuesday after Thanksgiving."

The class laughs.

She is very good at this.

His apartment is not what you'd expect, which is itself something she would say, the academic tic of exploding assumptions, though in this case the assumption is worth exploding because a Jesuit media personality who appears on EWTN to discuss the crisis of masculinity should not live in a one-bedroom walk-up with a radiator that sounds like a dying animal and a kitchen whose countertop is the particular shade of harvest gold that locates it precisely in 1974 and a bookshelf that contains, in no discernible order, Balthasar and Patricia Highsmith and three volumes of Cook's Illustrated and the Collected Poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins with a cracked spine and a water stain on the cover that she once traced with her finger while he watched and he said, "That was from a bathtub. Novitiate. I was reading 'The Windhover' in the bath and the book fell in and I thought: *this is a sign from God that pleasure and damage are the same event,*" and she had laughed—she laughs here, in the apartment, in a way she does not laugh anywhere else, a laugh that starts in the diaphragm and is not curated—and he had said, "That was the last uncomplicated theological thought I ever had."

The apartment smells like coffee and radiator dust and, very faintly, the sandalwood soap that is different from the Dial soap; the Dial is what he uses *before,* a deliberate stripping, an ablution, she understands this, the way the hands that touch her are first made *secular,* emptied of the sacristy—his own kenosis, she thinks, and the thought makes her furious because it is too good, too neat, it makes what they do sound like something you could present at a conference panel, and you cannot present this at a conference panel, you cannot present any of this at any panel, you cannot—

"Apron," he says.

She puts on the apron.

It is not a costume. That is the thing she needs someone to understand, the thing she has tried and failed to explain even to herself in the privacy of her own theoretical apparatus: the apron is a $12 cotton apron from a restaurant supply store on Bowery and it is white and it ties in the back and when she puts it on she does not become someone else. She becomes *more precisely herself.* The way a word becomes more itself when you italicize it. The apron is the italics.

She ties it. He watches. The watching is part of it—not voyeuristic, not lascivious, but *attentive,* the quality of attention a priest gives to the elements during consecration, and she knows this comparison is volatile, she knows it, she has published three articles that could be read as preemptive arguments against exactly this kind of sacramental slippage and she does not care, or rather she cares with a ferocity that makes the caring itself into a form of arousal—

The choreography: she washes the dishes that are already clean. She washes them again. This is the first movement. The water must be very hot. The washing must be thorough and slow. He sits at the kitchen table with his breviary open—he actually reads it, actually prays the Hours while she washes clean dishes, and once she turned from the sink and his lips were moving and she thought she might die, not metaphorically, literally die, that her heart might stop from the collision of the mundane and the sacred and the erotic, the three-body problem that governed this apartment like a physics no one had published—

*Foucault,* she thinks, reaching. Foucault on disciplinary power, on the production of docile bodies through repeated practice. Yes: the dishwashing is disciplinary, it produces a particular body, her body, a body that is—but no. Foucault's docile body is produced *without* its consent, that's the point, the subject is constituted by the power that traverses it, and this is not that, she *chose* this, she *chooses* it, every Tuesday and every other Friday she makes a forty-seven-minute cab ride and she *chooses*—but doesn't Foucault also say that the choice is itself produced by the power it appears to resist? Doesn't her own monograph on kenosis argue, in chapter four, that— She scrubs the plate. The plate does not need scrubbing. She scrubs it.
