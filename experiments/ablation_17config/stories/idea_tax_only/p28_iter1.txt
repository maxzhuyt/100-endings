# The Directive

## I.

The call came at 2:47 p.m. on a Tuesday, which Dara would later think was right, because that was the kind of time things like this happened—not at midnight, not at dawn, but at 2:47 on a Tuesday when you were comparing two translations of a Tajik water-rights agreement and deciding whether *обязательство* in context meant "obligation" or "commitment" and whether the difference mattered enough to flag.

It mattered. It always mattered. That was the whole point.

The phone on her desk didn't ring. Kevin Marsh appeared in the doorway of the translators' bullpen on the third subfloor of the State Department annex—not the building tourists photographed, but the one behind it and to the left, with windows only on the top two floors, so that working in the lower levels felt like being stored. Kevin was a staff assistant to someone whose title Dara had memorized and immediately forgotten, and he had the specific blankness of expression that she had learned, in eleven years of federal service, meant someone above him had said *now*.

"Kessler. Conference Four. Bring your badge."

She saved the document. She locked her workstation. She took her badge from the lanyard around her neck and held it in her hand because Conference Four required a prox scan and then a PIN and then a second prox scan, and she'd learned not to fumble. The hallway was the temperature it always was. The carpet was the carpet.

Conference Four was a room she'd been in twice, both times for post-incident reviews that hadn't concerned her directly. It had a long table, eight chairs, a screen on one wall, and the particular acoustic deadness of a space that had been swept for devices so many times the walls seemed to have given up on transmitting sound altogether.

Deputy Undersecretary Ellen Marsh—no relation to Kevin, Dara had once confirmed—was seated at the midpoint of the table with a manila folder, a hard-shell diplomatic pouch the color of a bruise, and a paper cup of coffee that she was not drinking. Dara had met her once, at a reception for the Central Asia desk that Dara had attended, stood near the shrimp for eleven minutes, and left. Marsh had been pointed out to her by a colleague. *That's Marsh. She was Bishkek before.* Meaning: she had served in the embassy in Kyrgyzstan, meaning she was relevant, meaning Dara should know her face.

Beside Marsh sat a man Dara didn't recognize: mid-fifties, gray suit, no tie, the particular stillness of someone who was used to being in rooms where other people did the talking. He was not introduced.

"Sit down, Dara. This is time-sensitive."

She sat.

"You have active TS/SCI clearance, regional authorization for Central Asia, and you speak Kyrgyz and Russian. Is that correct?"

"Kyrgyz, Russian, Tajik, and functional Uzbek."

"Good. You're aware of the situation in Bishkek."

Dara was aware. Everyone on the Central Asia desk was aware, the way you are aware of a sound that starts in one room and moves to the next: the coup attempt ten days ago, the military factions, the communications blackout that had dropped like a curtain over the entire northern half of the country. She had translated some of the early signals intercepts before the intercepts stopped.

"The digital infrastructure in the north is essentially gone," Marsh said. "No satellite uplinks, no functioning cell towers in the corridor between the Uzbek border and Bishkek, no encrypted cable capability at the consulate. We've attempted three burst transmissions. None confirmed received."

"We need a courier," the unnamed man said. It was the only thing he said during the entire briefing.

Marsh opened the manila folder. Inside was a single-page document: a chain-of-custody form, already filled in except for the signature line. The diplomatic pouch sat on the table like a small, hard-sided piece of luggage, the kind that handcuffed to your wrist in movies but in practice simply had a tamper-evident seal and a serial number.

"Inside that pouch is a presidential directive classified at the TS level. It needs to reach Consul Brenda Okafor-Wells at the U.S. consulate in Bishkek. The consulate has thirty-seven staff, including twelve nonessential personnel who were supposed to rotate out the week the coup started. The directive authorizes the consul to begin evacuation protocols. It needs to be in her hands within forty-eight hours for the protocols to be actionable within the current operational window."

Dara looked at the pouch. It had the standard State Department seal, unbroken, with a red tamper strip.

"The route is Tashkent to the border at Dostyk, then overland to Bishkek. Approximately three hundred forty kilometers, most of it on the M39 and the secondary highways. The embassy in Tashkent has arranged local transport and driver from the border. You'll carry a diplomatic passport and standard credentials. The road situation is—" Marsh paused, selected a word, "—variable. But the factions in the area have generally respected diplomatic vehicles. Generally."

"You have the language skills and the regional clearance," Marsh added. "And you've done field courier work before. Islamabad, 2019."

That was true. A pouch to the consulate in Peshawar, a four-hour drive on a road that turned out to be fine. She'd listened to a podcast on the way back.

"Any questions?"

Dara had questions. She was a translator; she lived in the gap between what was said and what was meant, and she could feel that gap in this room the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm. The unnamed man. The coffee Marsh wasn't drinking. The fact that this briefing was happening in Conference Four and not in a regular office.

But the questions she had were not the kind you asked. They were the kind you noted and filed and carried with you the way you carried everything in this work: privately, without weight, as part of the job.

"When do I leave?"

"There's a car waiting to take you to Andrews. You'll fly military transport to Tashkent, arriving approximately oh-six-hundred local. Embassy staff will brief you on current road conditions and hand off the driver. Chain of custody transfers to you the moment you sign that form."

Dara picked up the pen. It was a government pen, the kind that wrote a little too thick and smelled faintly of the plastic cup it had lived in. She signed on the line.

The pouch was lighter than she expected. It always was. She'd carried pouches before—the Islamabad run, a few domestic transfers during her first year—and the physical weight never matched the bureaucratic weight. This one felt like a hardcover book. She did not think about what was inside it, the same way she did not think about the content of the documents she translated until she was actively translating them. You didn't front-run the text. You waited for it to arrive in your hands in the proper sequence, and then you rendered it faithfully. That was the job. The entire job.

She went to her apartment. She packed the go-bag she kept in the closet by the door—the one she'd packed and repacked after Islamabad, keeping it updated the way the department handbook recommended: clothes for three days, toiletries, a photocopy of her credentials, two protein bars, a phone charger she wouldn't need where she was going. She changed into flat boots and a jacket that didn't wrinkle. She checked the stove. She checked the lock.

The car to Andrews was a black Suburban driven by a man who did not give his name and did not play the radio. Washington moved past the windows in its usual way—the Memorial, the bridge, the highway—and Dara watched it without seeing it, the way you watch a screensaver. She held the pouch on her lap. The tamper strip was intact. The serial number was a string that meant nothing to her.

She was a translator. She carried meaning from one place to another without altering it. That was what she was doing now.

On the plane—a C-17 with cargo netting and fold-down seats and the smell of hydraulic fluid—she slept for four hours, the pouch wedged between her hip and the fuselage, and did not dream.

## II.

The driver's name was Azamat. He was waiting at the embassy motor pool in Tashkent with a white Toyota Land Cruiser that had seen service in the way that old dogs have seen service—everything functional, nothing pretty. There were dust-yellowed U.N. decals on the doors that someone had half-scraped off, leaving the ghost of blue letters. The embassy logistics officer, a woman named Priya who looked like she hadn't slept in several days, handed Dara a laminated card with radio frequencies, none of which, Priya said, were likely to work past the border.

"The M39 is open to Dostyk. After that"—Priya did something with her mouth that wasn't quite a smile—"it depends on the day."

Azamat was perhaps forty, perhaps fifty. He had the face of someone who had spent most of his life outdoors and had made a private, permanent accommodation with this fact. He wore a canvas jacket, clean but shapeless, and drove with one hand on the wheel and one hand resting on the gear shift in a way that suggested he and the Land Cruiser had long since stopped being separate entities. He spoke Russian to Priya, Uzbek to the gate guard, and to Dara, after they pulled out of the compound and onto the road south toward the border, he spoke English.

"You have been to Kyrgyzstan before?"

"No. I studied the language at Georgetown, and then a summer program in Bishkek. But that was six years ago."

"Six years," he said, as if measuring something. "Things are different now."

"I know."

"You know what they tell you. On the road you will know something else."

He said this without emphasis, the way someone might observe that it was Tuesday or that the fuel gauge read three-quarters. Then he turned on the radio, got static, turned it off, and they drove.

The Uzbek side of the border was organized in the manner of borders that have been argued over for decades: a concrete building, flags, a boom gate, soldiers who were bored in the specific way of soldiers who have been given a post and insufficient instructions. Dara's diplomatic passport moved her through in fourteen minutes. Azamat's paperwork, which involved a separate window and a conversation she could hear but not see, took thirty-one. When he came back to the Land Cruiser he said nothing about it. They crossed.

The road on the Kyrgyz side was the same road and was a different road. The pavement held for the first ten kilometers—cracked but present, a surface you could call a highway. Then the cracks deepened into gaps, and the gaps into stretches of gravel, and the gravel into dirt that had been churned by vehicles heavier than any civilian truck. Dara felt the change in her spine before she saw it with her eyes. The Land Cruiser began to rock in a low, persistent way, like a boat in a chop.

The landscape was brown and gold and enormous. Late-autumn steppe giving way to the first folds of highland, the Tien Shan visible to the south as a line of white against a sky so blue it looked synthetic. It was beautiful. It was also, Dara noticed, empty. The few farmsteads they passed showed signs of habitation—a tethered goat, smoke from a stovepipe—but no people in view. The road itself was deserted except for a fuel truck heading the opposite direction and, once, a white SUV with no markings that passed them at high speed and disappeared.

The checkpoint appeared around a bend, where the road narrowed between two low hills. It was not a permanent installation. It was three vehicles—a BMP armored carrier with one working headlight and two pickup trucks with machine guns mounted in the beds—parked across the road in a loose V, with concertina wire filling the gaps. A fire was burning in a cut-down oil drum. Six soldiers, maybe seven. They wore government uniforms, or parts of government uniforms: the jackets were standard Kyrgyz military issue, but the boots were mismatched, the helmets inconsistent, and one of the soldiers—the youngest, who couldn't have been older than nineteen—wore a black wool cap instead of a helmet and had an AK-74 slung across his chest with a piece of nylon cord where the strap should have been.

Azamat slowed the Land Cruiser to a crawl. He said, "Government troops. Loyal to Mamatov. For now, Mamatov is the one with the western roads."

A soldier approached the driver's window. Azamat lowered it. Cold air came in, carrying diesel exhaust and woodsmoke and something else—a sweetish, wrong smell that Dara could not immediately place and chose not to investigate. Azamat spoke to the soldier in Kyrgyz.

Dara listened.

This was what she did. This was, if she was honest, the thing she did better than almost anything else—listened not just to words but to the architecture underneath words: the register, the dialect, the social positioning embedded in verb forms and modes of address. The soldier spoke a southern Kyrgyz dialect, which meant he was far from home; the Mamatov government's base was in the north. His verb forms were clipped in a way that wasn't natural to the dialect—he'd learned to talk this way recently, from someone above him, a commander. Military shorthand. He called Azamat *ake*, a respectful term for an older man, which meant he was either polite or uncertain of his authority or both.

Azamat handed over papers. The soldier looked at them, looked at Dara, and said something she caught at a slant.

"He wants the diplomatic passport," Azamat said, unnecessarily. Dara was already reaching for it.

The soldier took it to the BMP, where another man—older, bearded, a scar or a burn on the left side of his neck—sat in the open rear hatch with a radio handset. The bearded man looked at the passport. He picked up the handset and spoke into it. He waited. He spoke again. He waited. He put the handset down and said something to the younger soldier that Dara heard clearly because the wind died for a moment and the distance between the BMP and the Land Cruiser was only about fifteen meters and her ear, her trained and calibrated ear, caught it whole:

*Connection is down again. I don't know. She says American. The passport looks real. What do you want me to do, hold every foreigner until the radio works? Let them through. If Asan's people pick them up at Kara-Balta, that's Asan's problem.*

Asan. Dara filed the name. The briefing in Washington had described two principal factions: Mamatov's government loyalists and a coalition led by a General Dzhunusov. There had been no mention of anyone named Asan. This did not, in itself, mean anything—the briefing had been prepared four days ago, and coups were not static. But she noticed it the way she noticed everything on this road: without comment, as a point of data that had not yet earned the weight of meaning.

The younger soldier brought the passport back. He handed it through the window with both hands, a gesture of respect that looked strange coming from a man with a rifle. He said something to Azamat. Azamat nodded. The soldier waved, and another soldier pulled one of the pickup trucks back far enough to let the Land Cruiser through.

As they pulled away, Dara watched the checkpoint recede in the side mirror. The youngest soldier, the one with the wool cap, was warming his hands over the oil-drum fire. He looked up and watched the Land Cruiser until it rounded the next bend.

"How far to the consulate?" Dara asked.

"Perhaps two hundred seventy kilometers. Depending on the road."

"And the road depends on what?"

"On who holds it."

A pause. The Land Cruiser rocked through a pothole that Dara felt in her teeth.

"The next fifty kilometers are contested," Azamat said. He said this the way someone reports weather—not the weather of a particular dramatic storm, but the regular weather, the kind that simply is, the kind you drive through because the road goes through it and you are on the road.

The pouch sat on the back seat behind Dara. She could feel it the way you feel something you are not looking at: its weight, its sealed edges, its serial number, the red tamper strip that proved it had not been opened.

She watched the road. The mountains grew. The sky was the same blue that it had been all day, vast and indifferent and so clear that the snow on the distant peaks looked close enough to touch.
