# FLESH SEMESTER

## or, The Testimony of Braxley Fontaine-Rutherford III and Others, Regarding the Events Aboard the Shuttle *Perihelion*, En Route to Europa Influencer Immersion Camp, July 2091

---

## I.

Here is what I want you to understand about me before anything else: I was the only person on that shuttle who had packed sensibly.

Seven carry-ons crammed with ring lights and mood-reactive bodysuits and subscription-tier skincare that required its own climate-controlled pouch, and then there was my bag — one regulation duffel, soft-sided, containing four days of clothes that could survive a Europa thermal swing, a toothbrush, a physical book (Middlemarch, since you'll ask, and yes I was aware of what that signaled, and yes that was the point), and exactly one pair of backup shoes. I mention this not because it matters — it doesn't, obviously now it doesn't — but because the way a person packs tells you everything about the distance between who they think they are and who they'll become when the overhead bins open at the other end.

The shuttle was a Kepler-class short-haul, seats for twelve but only eight of us aboard because the Immersion Camp had oversold and then under-enrolled in the way everything in 2091 oversells and under-enrolls. You could feel the empty seats. They had a quality. Four absences shaped like people, still holding the impressions of bodies that had decided at the last minute not to come, and I remember — I'm certain I remember — thinking that the empty seats looked almost relieved.

The seven other passengers, then. I'll list them as I understood them in those first hours, which is to say: incorrectly.

**Margaux Kil-Prescott**, seat 2A, brown-eyed, already streaming before the airlock sealed, narrating her departure from the Bay Area Skyport in the breathy second person she used for her 1.3 million followers ("and you're stepping onto the shuttle, and you're finding your seat, and you're so ready for this—"). She had the particular beauty of someone who has been optimized since birth, every feature legal and paid for but arranged in a combination that still somehow read as effortless. I disliked her immediately, which I mention because I want to be honest, or because I want you to think I'm honest, and I'm no longer able to tell whether those are different things.

**Caspian Yoon**, seat 3B, our only passenger who had previously been to Europa and would not stop mentioning it. He wore the look of someone who found the rest of us unserious. His jawline was either natural or the work of a surgeon so expensive that the difference had ceased to be meaningful. He'd brought a guitar. An actual acoustic guitar, in a hard case, on an interplanetary shuttle. I thought this was the most ostentatious thing I had ever seen. I was wrong. It was the second most ostentatious thing. The most ostentatious thing was what came later, but we'll get there.

**Dove Bridenthaal**, seat 1A, closest to the cockpit, small and watchful and wearing a sweater that seemed engineered to communicate harmlessness. She had green eyes. She had brown eyes. She had eyes that I am now certain changed color between the first time I looked at her and the second, though I would not have said so then; then I would have said I wasn't paying attention, that the lighting in the Kepler-class shuttles does something strange to pigmentation, that I had simply looked too quickly. I want to be clear: she had green eyes, and then she had brown eyes, and both of those things are true, and I am reporting this not as metaphor.

**Theo Liang-Bauer**, seat 4A, loud, already opening a bag of synth-jerky with the deliberation of someone who wants witnesses to their snacking. He was the one who connected to the shuttle's sound system without asking and began playing a playlist titled "SLAUGHTER VIBES (chill)" which contained, as far as I could tell, exclusively early-century pop remixed over low-frequency hums that the shuttle's sub-bass speakers turned into something you felt in your sternum.

**Petra Okafor-Winslow**, seat 2B, directly across the aisle from Margaux, whom she pretended not to recognize despite the fact that they had publicly feuded for six weeks over a collaborative content deal that had collapsed in a way that generated more engagement than the collaboration itself would have. Petra was beautiful in a different register than Margaux — sharper, more architectural, the kind of face that made you feel you were standing too close to a building designed by someone famous. She was reading something on her lens display, and when I caught a reflection of it in the window I could see it was a compatibility report from the shuttle's onboard AI. I remember she looked annoyed. I remember she looked frightened. I remember both of these.

**Sable Voss**, seat 3A, who told us within twenty minutes of departure that she was "post-content" and had come to Europa Camp purely for the experience, and who was, I would later learn, running four separate account-growth automation bots from a partitioned zone in her neural lace. She had a laugh that sounded like glass being dropped from a specific height — not shattering height, but the height at which it hits carpet and makes the sound of something that *almost* broke. She sat next to Caspian. They did not know each other. They would be dead within — but I'm getting ahead of myself. I am getting ahead of myself.

**Wren Achebe-Castellano**, seat 4B, next to Theo, and the one I found hardest to categorize and therefore the one I spent the least time looking at, which I now understand as the most important failure of my perception. Wren was quiet. Not Dove's watchful quiet — something more opaque, more restful, as though silence were not a strategy but a natural condition. They wore clothes that fit. They did not stream. They did not perform irritation or irony or ease. They seemed to be simply sitting in a seat on a shuttle going to Europa, and the simplicity of this was so alien to the rest of us that it functioned as a kind of camouflage. I wrote them off. You'll notice that I've listed them last.

So. Eight teenagers in a Kepler-class shuttle. Seventeen hours to Europa. A playlist nobody voted for. Synth-jerky. Margaux streaming. Caspian holding his guitar case like a baby he was pretending not to love.

And the algorithm. I should tell you about the algorithm.

The shuttle's AI ran a standard Kepler social-integration suite, which among other things included a "compatibility matrix" that the manufacturer marketed as a community-building tool and everyone understood to be a hookup engine. Within forty minutes of departure it had already pushed match notifications to everyone's lace, rating our pairwise romantic compatibility on a scale of 0.00 to 1.00, adjusting in real time based on biometric data, conversational analysis, and a proprietary model trained on — I was told, I don't remember by whom — eleven billion relationship trajectories. The notification that appeared on my lace said:

> **BRAXLEY + CASPIAN: 0.72**
> **BRAXLEY + WREN: 0.91**
> **BRAXLEY + DOVE: 0.88**

I dismissed it the way you dismiss the seatbelt demonstration. You know it's there. You know it's technically relevant to your survival. You look out the window.

Theo's playlist shifted to something slower. Margaux aimed her lens at the viewport, where Europa was not yet visible but the darkness had begun to take on the faintly bluish cast that told you Jupiter was nearby, exerting its gravity on the quality of the light in ways that were probably metaphorical and possibly not.

"This is nice," Dove said, to no one, and for a moment it was.

I'm telling you this because I want you to have the shuttle as it was. Its particular smell — the recycled air had a sweet, plasticine quality, like unwrapping something new. The sound of Sable's laugh. The way Caspian looked at the viewport with the expression of someone returning to a place where something had once happened to him.

I want you to have it because in a moment I will lose control of the telling.

I was never supposed to lose control of the telling. I was, in my own understanding, the narrator. The one who stood slightly apart, who noticed while others experienced, who would organize the events into comprehensible sequence afterward. That is how I understood my function on the shuttle. That is how I have always understood my function everywhere. I am the one who tells the story.

I need you to know this before the others start talking.

**Recorded 72 hours post-incident, Europan Surface Authority Intake Facility**
**Interviewer: ████████████**

—

Q: Tell us about the first three hours of the trip.

A: I was streaming. Obviously. I had a creator obligation, legally binding, to produce a minimum of forty minutes of transit content for my Europa Camp brand series. So I was in my seat — 2A — running my usual format, soft narration, ambient shuttle vibes, you know the format. And the thing you need to understand is that I was looking at everyone through my lens display, like actually *looking*, because the algorithm was doing its thing and I was tracking the compatibility scores to see if there was a narrative angle. You know? Like, shuttle romance, confined space, Europa at the end of the rainbow, that's content.

Q: And where was Braxley Fontaine-Rutherford during this period?

A: In the rear cabin. The whole time. I didn't see her in the main compartment until at least hour two. She was back there alone, which I remember thinking was weird because no one goes to the rear cabin unless they're getting sick or they're avoiding someone, and we were forty minutes into a seventeen-hour flight, so what was she avoiding? I have footage. I have forty minutes of continuous footage and she is not in a single frame until —

Q: We'll review the footage separately. Continue.

A: The compatibility scores were fluctuating. Like, more than normal. Petra and I were matched at 0.94, which — we literally had a cease-and-desist between our management teams, so that felt algorithmic, like the AI was trying to generate drama for engagement because it was pulling from our interaction history, and I thought, fine, that's annoying but that's what algorithms do. But then I got a push notification that said I was 0.97 compatible with someone called PASSENGER_NULL, and when I tried to open the profile it was just — it was a slot. An empty seat. Seat 7A. The algorithm was matching me with nobody. With the *shape* of nobody.

Q: When did you first notice something wrong?

A: Define wrong. Define *notice*. I was streaming. Everything was content. You learn not to distinguish between wrong and interesting, because interesting performs and wrong performs and the only thing that doesn't perform is boring, and nothing on that shuttle was ever boring, and isn't that — I mean, looking back, isn't that the thing that should have told us?

## III.

The thing with the tentacles happened at hour four and it happened to Theo and it happened fast enough that fast is not really the word. The word would need to contain the idea of *already.* It happened in the already. As if it had happened before anyone saw it happen and what we witnessed was the playback.

Here is Margaux's version:

> Theo was standing by the snack bay. He was reaching for a hydration pouch. Something came through the floor — through the *floor*, through the reinforced hull plating — and it was red and wet and articulated like fingers, if fingers were six feet long and edged with something that caught the light the way light is caught by things that can open you. It opened him. It opened him from the sternum to the navel in a motion that looked — and I know this is insane, I know — *rehearsed*. Like it had practiced on him before, in some other iteration, and had perfected its angle of entry. He didn't scream. His mouth was open but what came out wasn't sound, it was — the tentacle was in his throat by then.
