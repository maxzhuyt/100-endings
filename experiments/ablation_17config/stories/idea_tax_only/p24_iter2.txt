# The Ground Not Ground Anymore

---

## I.

By April the Kivalik school district had lost the following to subsidence: one fuel storage shed (Lot 14, condemned October), the northwest corner of the post office (structural crack measured at four inches and widening, no repair scheduled), eleven feet of Kuuġvik Road past the turnoff to Mary Toolik's, and the gym floor of Kivalik K-12 Consolidated, which buckled on February ninth during a junior varsity basketball game, the court splitting along a diagonal from the free-throw line to the southeast bleachers with a sound that Superintendent Darren Okakok would later describe in his report to the state as "consistent with prior observed settling events." The game was Panthers vs. Nanooks. The score was 34–31. Nobody was hurt. The gym was closed pending assessment. The assessment was not funded. The gym stayed closed.

At the April board meeting, held in the cafeteria because of the gym, Okakok read the updated timeline. The thermal monitoring wells behind the school had recorded a mean annual ground temperature of negative 0.3 degrees Celsius, up from negative 1.7 eight years earlier. The active layer—the seasonal thaw zone above the permafrost—had deepened by fourteen centimeters in two years. A geotechnical report, commissioned the previous spring and delivered seven months late, recommended "relocation of critical infrastructure within a 3-to-5-year planning horizon." The board voted 4-1 to table discussion of the report until the fall meeting. The dissenting vote was Albert Apassingok, who said nothing during the discussion and simply raised his hand when it was time.

The minutes noted the vote and moved on to bus maintenance.

What the minutes did not note, because minutes do not hold this kind of information: that Agnes Nayupak's yard had become a pond, a gray shallow thing that smelled of methane and old rot, and that her grandchildren skateboarded around its edges on plywood because Agnes refused to leave and also refused to pretend the yard was a yard; that the cemetery road was impassable in anything less than an ATV after June because the culvert had dropped eighteen inches into a thermokarst that opened the previous August, quietly, overnight, the ground just deciding to stop being ground; that Toolik's cache—the underground food storage her grandmother had dug in 1974, permafrost-cooled, insulated by forty years of frozen earth—had thawed in September and she'd lost four hundred pounds of muktuk and dried fish, a winter's worth, and that the insurance claim form had a field for "cause of loss" and the options were fire, flood, theft, windstorm, vandalism, other, and she'd circled other and written in careful block letters THE GROUND and the claim had been denied.

The children of Kivalik knew the terrain. They knew it the way you know the face of someone who is dying slowly—not with alarm, not anymore, but with a daily, metabolic attention to what had changed since yesterday. Ruthie Apassingok, fifteen, could walk the half-mile from her grandfather's house to the school and tell you which sections of boardwalk had new give, which ones would hold another winter, and which ones you crossed quickly, on the outer edge, where the pilings still bit into something solid. She could do this in the dark. She had been doing it in the dark since November, when the light went and didn't come back for weeks and you still had to get to school.

There was no memorial for any of it. No moment of silence. The earth was swallowing the town one lot, one road, one floor at a time, and the town was filling out the paperwork. The children grew up inside this and did not find it strange because it was not strange. It was Tuesday. It was the walk to school. It was the particular way your foot learned to read a surface and your body learned to carry its weight on the balls of your feet and your brain learned to hold, always, a low-level map of what was solid and what was pretending.

The Kivalik cross-country team trained on this terrain because there was no other terrain. They ran on ground that was a question. They had been running on it all their lives.

---

## II.

Ruthie's left knee was a liar.

It would swell in the night after a training run—a thick, hot puffiness that she could feel before she opened her eyes in the morning, the joint rendering its opinion of what she'd asked it to do. She'd swing her legs over the bed and test it: weight, quarter-weight, half, full. Some mornings it held. Some mornings it spoke to her in a dialect of clicks and heat that meant she would be wrapping it before she brushed her teeth. The wrap was an ACE bandage—not even the good kind, the real athletic tape Coach Pruitt occasionally brought back from Anchorage, but one of four ACE bandages she rotated and washed and hung from the towel rack in the bathroom, a system her Auntie Bea had taught her the spring she turned twelve and started outrunning the ache.

"You wrap from below," Bea had said, showing her, the bandage moving in precise diagonals across the joint. "Below the knee. Tight but not so tight you lose your toes. You're giving it something to push against. Like a wall."

This was how Auntie Bea talked about bodies—structurally, as though they were buildings that needed shoring. Bea had worked twenty years at the clinic before the clinic lost its only physician's assistant and became a place where you could get your blood pressure read and not much else. She knew the bodies of the village the way Superintendent Okakok knew the infrastructure: by what was failing, by what had been deferred.

Ruthie's lungs were wet paper. This was not metaphor. She'd had pneumonia at eleven—the real kind, the kind that fills you up, that turns breathing into a negotiation between what your body wants and what your body can actually do. They'd medevacked her to Anchorage, three days in Providence, antibiotics by IV, and when she came back her lungs were technically clear but never the same. There was a capacity she'd lost, a depth of breath that she could feel the absence of the way you feel a missing tooth. She compensated. She'd learned to run in the shallows of her breathing, to meter out air in small, precise allotments, to never go to the well because the well wasn't as deep as it used to be. Coach Pruitt called it her "ceiling"—the heart rate above which Ruthie's breathing went ragged and her pace cratered. She trained below the ceiling. She lived below the ceiling. Her running was not about speed. It was about the disciplined management of insufficiency.

Coach Pruitt had tried to fix her hitch—the slight rightward lean, the way her left hip dropped a centimeter with each stride to take weight off the knee, a compensation so ingrained it had become form. He'd shown her drills, filmed her on his phone, drawn stick figures on the whiteboard in his math classroom that doubled as the team's meeting room. Ruthie had tried. The corrected stride felt like a lie, like walking on ground you knew was hollow and pretending it was solid. She'd gone back to the hitch within a week. Pruitt stopped mentioning it.

Her feet were her best instruments. She could feel, through the thin soles of her trainers—Asics, three years old, bought used from a cousin in Nome—the particular quality of ground: its density, its moisture, the micro-signatures of what was solid versus what was only frozen, what was dirt versus what was ice pretending to be dirt. This was not mystical. It was proprioceptive, the same way a carpenter reads wood grain by running a thumb across it. She had been walking on permafrost since she could walk. Her feet had learned its language. They knew when the ground was lying.

The team was six runners. Five would score.

Thomas Oomittuk, sixteen, trained by running his trapline—nine miles of rough trail through scrub willow and tussock tundra, checking snares and conibears, which meant he ran with a pack and often with a dead hare or marten swinging against his back, which meant his stride had an asymmetry that drove Pruitt to quiet despair but which also meant he could run nine miles over broken ground carrying weight without slowing down, which was, in a cross-country race, not nothing.

Winnie Simmonds, fifteen, had the best VO2 max on the team and possibly in the district, though no one had tested it properly because the testing equipment was in Anchorage and the funding for testing was in Anchorage and everything was in Anchorage. What Winnie had was a set of lungs she'd built hauling water from the washeteria to her grandmother's house three times a week since she was nine—five-gallon jerry cans, two at a time, a quarter mile uphill—and a resting heart rate of forty-four, which Coach Pruitt had measured with two fingers on her wrist and a wall clock because that was the equipment available.

Derek James, seventeen, quiet, big for a runner, slow-twitch, the kind of kid who would finish twentieth in a 5K and feel nothing about it either way, but who could run all day without breaking, who had a mechanical consistency that made him the team's anchor, the one who would never win and never fall apart.

Janey Kavairlook, fourteen, the youngest, fast in bursts, panicky in crowds, who ran with her elbows out and her chin tucked like she was expecting a fight, who could cover a quarter mile faster than anyone on the team and who could not, yet, cover three miles without surging and dying and surging again, a pattern Pruitt was trying to train out of her with the patience of a man who also taught pre-algebra to twelve-year-olds.

Eddie Siku, sixteen, Ruthie's cousin, the most naturally talented runner on the team, fluid and easy, the kind of stride that coaches in Anchorage would have built a program around, but who had missed three weeks in September with a bone bruise in his right foot from stepping through a section of boardwalk that should have been replaced in July and wasn't, because the budget for boardwalk replacement had been reallocated to shore up the school's north foundation. Eddie ran now with a tenderness in that foot, a flinch so small only Ruthie could see it, a hesitation at the moment of contact, his body asking the ground a question before committing its weight.

These were the instruments. These were the tools.

Pruitt drove them in the school's surviving bus—a 2004 Blue Bird with 220,000 miles and a heater that worked in the way that certain promises work, intermittently and with conditions—and he coached them and he taught them math and he filled out the entry forms and the travel requisitions and the parental consent forms (four of which came back signed by grandparents or aunties, because parents were in Anchorage for work or in Fairbanks for treatment or otherwise not where the forms were) and he paid for gas-station sandwiches out of his own pocket when the per diem was late, which was always. He had coached cross-country at Kivalik for eleven years. His teams had never placed higher than ninth at the state meet. He did not expect to place higher than ninth at this state meet. He expected his kids to run the race, to finish, to not get hurt. The bar was not low. The bar was *precise*.

They were going to the state championship. They were going to run on ground that they did not know, in a landscape that had not yet learned to dissolve. This was, for all of them, the farthest they had ever been from home.

## III.

The course was in the Matanuska-Susitna Valley, ninety miles north of Anchorage, routed through a broad glacial basin where the Chugach front range gave way to boreal lowland—black spruce and muskeg and gravel flats, the kind of sub-Arctic terrain the village kids recognized the way you recognize a dialect of your own language, similar enough to read, different enough to keep you alert. The race organizers had flagged the course with orange ribbons tied to willow stakes. The route ran five kilometers through the valley, climbing slightly to a ridgeline, then dropping through a section of tussock and scree before looping back to the start-finish near the parking area. It was a hard course. It was meant to be hard. The state meet was held here because the terrain was real—not a manicured park loop but actual backcountry, the kind of ground that tested something other than fitness.

They arrived the night before. Pruitt parked the bus at the far end of a gravel lot already filling with vehicles—vans with school logos, SUVs with bike racks and roof boxes, a charter bus from one of the Anchorage schools that was nicer than anything Kivalik owned for any purpose. The village team climbed out into the September cold, breath visible, mountains filling the southern horizon with a scale that made Ruthie's chest tighten—not from her lungs but from the sudden unfamiliarity of mountains that were not her mountains, ground that was not her ground.

The other teams were already there. Ruthie saw them from the bus steps and her body registered the information before her mind organized it: the matching uniforms. Not just same-color-shirts matching, but *systems*—compression tights, moisture-wicking tops with reflective piping, team jackets, the school name arced across the back in the kind of lettering that someone had designed. The Kivalik team wore navy-blue T-shirts that said KIVALIK XC in iron-on letters that Pruitt's wife had applied in their kitchen. Two of the shirts had started to peel. Eddie's said KIVAL C.

The shoes.

Ruthie noticed the shoes first because Ruthie always noticed the shoes. The Anchorage teams wore Nikes with visible air units, or the new Hokas with the maximal cushion, or Saucony Endorphins, race-day shoes, shoes that cost what Ruthie's grandmother spent on heating fuel in a month. Ruthie wore her Asics. Thomas wore Salomons he'd bought at a Salvation Army in Nome, trail shoes, good ones actually, though the tread was half gone. Winnie wore Brooks that Pruitt had gotten from some charity clearinghouse, the right size but the wrong width, so she laced them loose and wore two pairs of socks. Janey wore basketball shoes. Coach Pruitt had tried to explain to Janey about basketball shoes—the ankle support that restricted stride, the sole that gripped pavement but slid on mud—and Janey had said These are my shoes and that was that.

They set up near the bus. No tent. No canopy. Pruitt spread a tarp on the gravel and the team sat on it and ate gas-station sandwiches—ham and cheese in plastic triangles, bought at the Tesoro in Wasilla—and drank water from bottles they'd refilled at the same Tesoro. Across the lot, the South Anchorage team had a pop-up tent with the school wolverine logo on the sidewalls. Inside the tent: a folding table, a cooler of Gatorade, energy gels in a cardboard box, a portable speaker playing something low. Their coach—a woman in performance fleece, a GPS watch on each wrist, a lanyard with credentials and a whistle—was talking to her runners about course strategy, her voice carrying in the clear air, something about "negative splits on the backside" and "staying in zone two through the first kilometer."

The Kivalik team's race strategy was: go out together, run your pace, don't die.

A girl from the Anchorage team walked over. She was tall, lean, the kind of runner whose body was a purpose-built thing—everything spare, everything aimed. She wore compression sleeves on both calves and shoes that looked like they had never touched mud.

"Hey," she said to Winnie, who was closest. "Are you guys Kivalik?"

"Yeah."
