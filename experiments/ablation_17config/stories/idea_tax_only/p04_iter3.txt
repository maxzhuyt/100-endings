# The Fifth Inn

## I.

The radiator in the east corridor had been singing since October, a quarter-tone sharp, and Marguerite pressed her palm against it every morning the way you press a hand to a chest to feel if the breathing has changed. Warm still, and the note — that thin, reedy F-sharp that was also, had always been, the first struck tone of the bell that called travelers in from the coastal road when fog made the lanterns useless — held for a moment against her skin before she pulled away and wiped the rust-colored condensation on her trousers.

The wallpaper in the second-floor hall was peeling again above the chair rail, a slow-motion unfurling that exposed the prior layer underneath. She caught the edge with her thumbnail, and the paper came away in a strip like birch bark, revealing not the expected 1970s florals but a pale green that she did not remember anyone choosing, a green that had no decade, the color of the stone in the vaulted passage between the east and west wings where the Heralds once stood in their cloaks of felt and river-water, waiting to announce each guest by the name they had not yet earned. She let the strip of wallpaper drop. It drifted to the floor. She stepped over the spot where the hallway had begun to bow — a soft place in the subfloor, a yielding, three inches lower than it should have been, causing the floorboard to sigh under her weight the way a drawbridge sighs on its chains when it lowers over dark water and the reflected moon fractures but does not drown.

Downstairs: Cass.

Cass had been at the breakfast table since six, which Marguerite knew because Cass was at the breakfast table by six every morning and had been since the winter she'd arrived clutching a bus ticket and a paper bag with two tangerines and announced to the empty lobby, "I'll need a room on the ground floor because my knees are political prisoners and refuse to climb." She was eighty-one now, or eighty-three. She played solitaire with a deck so old the queens had become featureless. She laid the cards out in a pattern that was not any solitaire game Marguerite had seen diagrammed in any book, a radiating spiral, seven arms, and when another resident wandered in for coffee Cass would study the spread and then say something like, "Sit next to the tall one with the ears at dinner," and the resident, baffled, would obey, and by the end of the month there would be hand-holding in the garden. Thirty-six years she'd watched Cass do this. Thirty-six years the Oracle of Pairings had sat at the center of the inn's morning and laid her spread on the table's water-ringed oak, the same table that in the great hall of the palace was a single slab of ash wood blessed by the first keeper, and the cards she dealt were not cards but tessellations of longing, and the pattern they described was the secret architecture of who would love whom, and Cass would look up and say, "More coffee, Marguerite, this one's complicated," swatting at the three of clubs, which had a crease through its middle and which, held to the light, showed the faint mapwork of a country that did not exist and never needed to.

"More coffee, Marguerite."

"You've had four cups."

"I've had three, and the third was decaf, which doesn't count morally."

Marguerite poured. The kitchen smelled like the pilot light that wouldn't stay lit and the lavender that grew wild against the west wall and pushed its scent through the cracks in the window frame. She poured and set the pot back on the burner and leaned against the counter and looked at Cass's spread and felt what she always felt, which was the world doubling, the way a struck bell contains both the note and the silence the note displaces.

The phone rang.

She knew before she picked it up. She'd known for months, the way you know a season is ending — not from any single sign but from the accumulation of small dimnesses, as though each day were a room with one fewer lamp. The building inspector had come in August. The structural engineer in September. The county's letter in October, which she'd left unopened on the front desk for eleven days, then opened with a butter knife at midnight, reading it once and then placing it facedown, as though it might revise itself in the dark.

But this was not a letter. This was a voice — a woman at the county assessor's office, efficient and not unkind, who said the words "condemned" and "structural compromise" and "timeline" and finally "Tuesday."

"Tuesday," Marguerite repeated.

"The demolition contractor will need access to the full building. He'll do a survey first. Two days, maybe three. Then we'll schedule the—"

"Tuesday."

"Ma'am, this has been communicated in writing on several—"

"I know."

She hung up. The receiver settled into its cradle with a click that was also, in the hall beyond the kitchen door, the sound of a great iron latch falling into place, the outermost gate of the palace swinging shut against a wind that had no name, only a direction, which was inward, always inward, toward the center of something that would not hold.

Cass looked up from her spread.

"Trouble?"

Marguerite said, "Tuesday."

Cass turned a card. Studied it. Turned it back.

"Well," she said. "That's not enough time for the six of hearts to do what it needs to do, but has that ever stopped anyone?"

## II.

Sol arrived on Tuesday in a truck the color of a headache. White, institutional, with the county seal on the door and rust eating the wheel wells. Marguerite watched from the front window as they climbed out — mid-thirties, short-haired, wearing steel-toed boots and a canvas jacket and carrying a clipboard with such evident reluctance that the clipboard seemed to be leading them rather than the other way around. A hard hat sat on the passenger seat. They looked at it, decided against it, and walked up the gravel path.

Marguerite met them at the door.

"Ms. Delaune?"

"Marguerite."

"Sol Marin. County sent me to do the pre-demolition survey. I'll need to access every room, check the load-bearing walls, test for asbestos in the older sections. Shouldn't take more than—"

"Would you like coffee?"

Sol blinked. "I — sure. Thank you."

The door opened, and the inn received them. This is what Marguerite saw: a contractor stepping into a foyer with a cracked tile floor and a ceiling fixture missing two of its five bulbs, wiping their boots on a mat that said WELCOME in letters so faded only the W and the E survived. This is what else was happening: the drawbridge shuddered. Something in the hinge-work of the palace groaned, not in protest but in recognition, the way a body groans when a hand it has been waiting for finally touches it. In Cass's breakfast room, the cards slid — without being touched, without wind, without excuse — into a new configuration, and Cass stared at the pattern and said nothing, which was itself unprecedented. And in the east wing, where the corridor ran the length of the building and terminated in a window that faced the sea, a door appeared. Not opened. *Appeared.* Flush with the wall, made of wood that smelled like pencil shavings, with a handle shaped like a question that expected no answer.

"The kitchen's this way," Marguerite said.

She gave Sol coffee and watched them take their first sip and fail to suppress a wince.

"Too strong?"

"No, it's — it's fine."

"It's terrible."

"It is genuinely terrible. What do you use, a ore refinery?"

"My husband used to make the coffee. He died in 2005 and I apparently learned nothing from him in forty-one years of marriage."

Sol looked at her. Set the cup down. Picked up the clipboard.

"I should start with the basement."

"There is no basement."

"The file says—"

"The file is wrong. There was a cellar, but it flooded in 1987 and we sealed it. You can access it from the outside if you need to, but I wouldn't recommend it. The stairs are — suggestive."

"Suggestive?"

"They suggest you will fall."

A small sound from Sol that might have been a laugh. They made a note on the clipboard and began.

Marguerite did not follow. She had no reason to follow. She went to the front desk and began sorting receipts that did not need sorting and listened to Sol's footsteps move through the ground floor — dining room, sitting room, the old parlor that had been converted to a television room in the '90s and still smelled faintly of the conversion's failure. She heard them open doors, knock on walls, make the small investigative sounds of a person trying to understand what was behind what they could see.

Then the footsteps stopped.

Marguerite waited. The silence extended. She put down the receipts and walked toward the parlor and found Sol standing in the doorway of the linen closet, staring at nothing — at shelves of towels and a broken ironing board and a bag of rags — but their hand was moving. They had produced, from the inside pocket of the canvas jacket, a book. Not the clipboard. A sketchbook, pocket-sized, spiral-bound, soft-covered, the kind sold at art supply stores for seven dollars. And they were drawing.

Not a floor plan. Not a measurement diagram. Not anything that belonged to the clipboard's purpose.

A room. A room with a vaulted ceiling and seven tall windows arranged in an arc, each window showing a different phase of light — dawn, morning, noon, afternoon, dusk, evening, and one that showed no light at all but was somehow the brightest. A round tower room, drawn with the precise, unsentimental hand of someone who understood load-bearing walls and asbestos and structural compromise, and who, understanding all of that, was drawing a room that could not exist.

Sol noticed her. The sketchbook snapped shut.

"Sorry. Habit."

"What was that?"

"Nothing. Just — I draw sometimes. Between measurements. Keeps my hand busy." They slid the book back into the jacket. "The joists in this section are shot. You can feel it in the floor — there's a sponginess. When was the last time anyone inspected the—"

"The room you were drawing."

Sol's hand went to the jacket pocket. Stopped. Retreated.

"It's nothing. Something I do. An old — it's nothing."

Marguerite looked at the linen closet. At its shelves of fraying towels and its one bare bulb. At the thing she had always known was also there — the alcove of the seventh threshold, where the Keeper of Unspoken Invitations once stood and decided which arriving strangers carried in their suitcases a loneliness shapely enough to fit another person's loneliness, and who would touch the lintel above the door so that the room beyond would arrange itself in the precise geometry of welcome, a geometry that had seven windows and light that moved like a question through each one.

She looked away.

"The joists," she said. "You were saying."

## III.

Beau had lived in Room 6 for eleven years, though he would tell you it was twelve because he counted the three weeks he'd spent as a guest before admitting he wasn't leaving. He was seventy-nine. He had sung with a touring opera company in the 1970s and '80s — never the lead, always the second or third baritone, the one who sang the father or the priest or the comic servant — and his voice had gone to gravel sometime around the millennium, but the gravel had its own music, and when he spoke, which was constantly, the dining room arranged itself around his sound.

He was the inn's self-appointed social director. He organized the Tuesday cribbage tournament, the Thursday film nights, the Sunday walks along the seawall that he called "the Promenade of Inadvertent Confessions" because something about the combination of salt air and Beau's refusal to tolerate silence produced, in even the most reticent guests, admissions of loneliness so raw and specific that the only possible response was to introduce them to another guest whose loneliness was of a complementary shape. He had once, during a cribbage game, noticed a retired widower named Paul counting points with a hand that trembled not from age but from the effort of not reaching across the table toward the woman sitting opposite him, and Beau had said loudly, "Marguerite, this man's pegging is a disgrace, give him Helen as a partner before he embarrasses us all," and Paul and Helen had been married within the year, and at their wedding Paul's hand did not tremble at all.

Beau's daughter arrived on a Thursday. Her name was Claire. She was forty-seven, kind, organized, and visibly exhausted by a worry she'd been carrying so long it had become structural, part of her posture. She found Marguerite in the kitchen.

"He can't stay."

"I know."

"The facility is ten minutes from our house. The grandchildren can visit every day. He'll have his own room — a nice room, with a garden view — and there's a music program, and they do outings—"

"Claire."

"He won't listen to me."

"He's listening. He's just —" Marguerite searched for the word. "He's mourning in advance."

Claire's composure buckled for a moment, then rebuilt itself. "Can you help me pack?"

Beau did not want to go. He said this simply, standing in Room 6 with his hands at his sides, surrounded by eleven years of accumulation — the framed playbills, the cribbage board with its ivory pegs, the collection of hats he'd bought at thrift stores and worn to breakfast with ceremonial gravity, the photograph of his wife, dead thirty years, in which she was laughing at something outside the frame.

"I don't want to go, Marguerite."

"I know, Beau."

"This room is the right size. I've spent my whole life in rooms that were too big or too small and this one is exactly the right size. Do you know how long it took me to find a room that was the right size?"

"You told me the first night. You said, 'This room has clearly been waiting for me, the acoustics respect a baritone.'"

"And I was right. Listen." He hummed — a low note, just a breath with a shape — and the room took it and gave it back warmed, the way a cupped hand warms a candle flame.

He packed a single suitcase. Three bow ties — his favorite, the one with the gold thread, the one he'd worn when he introduced Paul to Helen, and a third whose significance he did not explain. Two playbills. The photograph of his wife. A book of crossword puzzles half-completed in pencil. No socks. No undershirts. Claire added these while he wasn't looking, and he didn't check.

Marguerite walked him to the car. The gravel crunched under his good shoes, the ones he refused to call dress shoes because, he said, "all my shoes are dressed, I'm not an animal." The air was salt and diesel and the lavender that would not stop growing. Claire opened the rear door. Beau stood beside it.

"I'm going now," he said.

"Yes."

"You should charge more for Room 6. It's undervalued."

"Beau."

"And tell Cass her solitaire game is a con and everyone knows it but we're too polite to say."

"I'll tell her."
