# Dead Letters

## I.

There are places the government keeps for the things it cannot deliver, and this story begins in one of them.

The United States Postal Service Dead Letter Office in Topeka, Kansas, occupied the second floor of a building that had once been, in sequence, a hat factory, a recruiting station for a war no one in Topeka could remember clearly, and a storage facility for decommissioned voting machines. The machines were still there when the postal service moved in, stacked in the basement like the skeletons of small iron democracies, and no one had come to claim them, which was, if you thought about this sort of thing—and Meredith Plank did not, or had not, or would not until the morning that everything changed without appearing to change at all—a species of irony native to the building itself: a dead letter office built on top of dead ballots.

Meredith had worked there for eleven years. She was forty-three. She wore her hair in a manner that communicated not style but the absence of time spent considering style—pinned, with two brown clips that she bought in packs of twelve from a drugstore on Quincy Street. She owned four work blouses. They were not the same blouse in four colors; they were four different blouses that had, through years of institutional laundering, arrived at a kind of convergence, a washed-out quantum state between cream and gray that the eye could not resolve into either. Her desk was the third from the left in a row of nine. The desks were oak, which sounds distinguished until you saw them: they were the kind of oak that had absorbed so many decades of bureaucratic misery that the wood itself seemed to have developed a policy of non-engagement with the world. Nothing bounced off those desks. Nothing gleamed. Light hit them and simply stopped.

The work was this: bins arrived. The bins were gray plastic, the size and shape of infant coffins, though no one in the office had ever made this comparison aloud, and if you had suggested it to Meredith Plank on any morning before March 7th, 1974, she would have looked at you with the flat, courteous incomprehension of someone who had been raised to consider metaphor a kind of indulgence. The bins contained mail. The mail was dead—which is to say, undeliverable: wrong addresses, insufficient postage, illegible handwriting, houses that had burned, people who had moved, people who had died, people who had never existed, people who had existed so briefly or so marginally that the postal system, which could find almost anyone, could not find them. These letters had traveled as far as they could on their own volition and had come to rest here, in Topeka, in a building that smelled of dissolved ink and the particular brand of floor wax the federal government purchased in tanker quantities. This is where language went when it could not reach its destination. This is where longing became material and then, very shortly, became trash.

The procedure was threefold and federally mandated. First, open. The letter opener was not a letter opener but a thin steel blade bolted to the edge of the desk, over which the envelope was drawn in a motion that resembled, if you watched from a certain angle—and the narrator confesses to watching from that angle now—a hand passing over a throat. Second, catalog. The contents were recorded in a logbook: sender (if identifiable), addressee (if legible), contents (if classifiable), value (if monetary—checks were voided and forwarded to Treasury; cash above five dollars was forwarded to Treasury; cash below five dollars was placed in a manila envelope and forwarded to Treasury; Treasury, one imagines, was drowning in manila envelopes full of crumpled ones). Third, dispose. The opened, cataloged, valueless letter was placed in a second bin, identical to the first, and wheeled to a room at the end of the hall where a man named Gus fed paper into a machine that was technically called a document destroyer but which everyone in the office called, with the grim fondness of people who spend their lives among euphemisms, the mouth.

On the morning of March 7th, 1974, Meredith Plank was performing step one—draw, slit, open—on a letter from the third bin of the day. The envelope was a No. 10, standard business size, white once but now the yellow of old piano keys. It was addressed, in a hand that leaned hard to the right as if blown by a wind that existed only on the page, to:

*Mr. Everett Driscoll*
*14 Seward Lane*
*Pennville, Indiana 47369*

The return address read:

*Sister, from Sister*
*You know where*

The postmark was August 1961. The stamp was a four-cent Lincoln, purple, slightly torn.

Inside was a single sheet of paper, college-ruled, torn from a spiral notebook, the perforated edge still fringed with tiny paper tabs. The handwriting was the same wind-leaned script, and it said this:

*Everett I am going to say this the one time. I forgive you. I woke up this morning and the house was quiet and the dog was on the porch and the coffee was the way it always is and I looked at the yard and there was nothing different about any of it except I was not angry anymore. It was not a decision. I think it was more like how a fever breaks. You did what you did and I have carried it the way I have carried it and now I am setting it down. Not because you deserve it. I make no ruling on what you deserve. I am setting it down because it is heavy and I am sixty-one years old and I would like my arms back for whatever is left. I will not write again. I would say God bless you but I do not know that he does.*

*From your sister.*

The letter had never arrived. Everett Driscoll of 14 Seward Lane, Pennville, Indiana, had—according to the postal routing codes that Meredith could read as fluently as a conductor reads a score—moved without leaving a forwarding address sometime before September 1961. The letter had chased him. It had been routed to three successive possible addresses, each one generating a new yellow forwarding sticker, each sticker a small flag of institutional effort, until the system had exhausted its capacity for mercy and sent the letter here. To Topeka. To the bins. To Meredith.

She read it twice. Then she set it down on her desk and placed both hands flat on either side of it, the way a person might brace themselves against a table during an earthquake, except there was no earthquake. The office was quiet. Janet Cooke, at the fourth desk from the left, was blowing on a cup of coffee. The radiator under the east window was ticking. Somewhere in the basement, one of the dead voting machines shifted with a sound like a mechanical sigh.

What happened to Meredith Plank in this moment was not a breakdown, not a vision, not the sort of thing that could be narrated with a crack of light or a swelling of music. Had you been watching—and no one was—you would have seen only a woman sitting very still at a desk with her hands flat on oak, her face composed, her breathing even. You would not have called for help. You would not have noticed anything at all.

What happened was closer to arithmetic. A sum that had been running in the back of her mind for eleven years—some quiet, ceaseless tabulation of envelopes slit and pages cataloged and messages fed to the mouth—reached its total. The total was: enough. Not in the sense of outrage, not in the sense of protest. In the sense of a thermometer reaching a specific temperature and a substance changing state. Ice to water. Water to steam. Meredith to whatever Meredith was now.

Every undeliverable letter was a small death. That was the sum. Every message that reached Topeka instead of its destination was a voice that had spoken into a room and found it empty, a hand that had reached across distance and closed on nothing. And for eleven years, she had been the last person to hear those voices before they were destroyed—the priest at a confessional where absolution was a steel-bladed desk fixture and penance was the mouth—and she had done nothing. She had cataloged them. She had filed them. She had been, she understood now with the calm of someone who has simply finished a long calculation, complicit in the silence.

She was the only resurrection available.

She began to sort. Not for disposal. For delivery. She pulled the bin toward her and removed each envelope, not slitting but *reading*—return address, destination, postmark, the weight and texture of the paper—and she placed each one in a stack on her desk with a care that Janet Cooke, had she looked up from her coffee, might have described as religious, though Janet Cooke would not have meant anything by the word beyond *excessive*.

Meredith Plank counted. She counted that day, and she counted the next morning when she arrived at the office two hours before anyone else and went through bins that had not yet been assigned to her, and she counted that afternoon, working through lunch, working past five, working until the janitor came through with his mop and his radio and did not ask why she was still there because janitors have learned that the questions you ask of the federal government are never the questions the federal government answers. She counted for two and a half days, touching every piece of mail that the Topeka Dead Letter Office held in its possession, and when she was finished—standing in the archive room among opened bins with her hair coming loose from its clips and her cream-gray blouse untucked and her fingertips black with old ink—the count was:

Two thousand, three hundred and eleven.

She did not choose this number. She discovered it. And having discovered it, she wrote it in pencil on the inside of her left wrist, the way a nurse notes a patient's temperature, and she went home and she found a box and she came back and she began to pack.

## II.

The car was a 1966 Plymouth Valiant, four-door, the color of a headache. It had 94,000 miles on it and a passenger-side window that would descend but not ascend without manual intervention, which meant pushing it up with both palms from the outside, leaning into the car like a woman trying to resuscitate a reluctant animal. The rear bumper was attached on the left side by faith and on the right side by a coat hanger that a mechanic in a Texaco station had bent into place three Octobers ago and that had held, green and rigid, through two winters, which is to say: the car was exactly the car you would expect to find in the parking lot of a federal building in Topeka, Kansas, in 1974, and that is why it matters. Meredith Plank's vehicle was not mythic. It was a Valiant. The name was aspirational.

She loaded it on the night of March 9th, a Saturday, after her last shift of counting. She had acquired, from the supply closet at the end of the hallway, eleven cardboard boxes of the kind used to archive Form 3849s—Delivery Attempt Notices, which seemed correct to her in a way she did not examine. Each box held approximately two hundred and ten letters. She had organized them by region: the first four boxes, which went into the trunk, were everything east of the Mississippi, a country of letters addressed to porches in Virginia and apartments in the Bronx and a P.O. box in Ocala, Florida, that she suspected no longer existed. The next five boxes, which filled the back seat, were the middle—Missouri through Montana, Texas through the Dakotas, the wide gut of the nation where addresses were often rural routes and the handwriting tended to be larger, as if the openness of the land had entered the hand. The last two boxes went on the passenger seat and the passenger-side floor. These were the western letters—California, Oregon, Washington, Nevada, a single letter addressed to a mining concern in Coeur d'Alene, Idaho, that had been postmarked 1958.

The rear suspension of the Valiant compressed two inches under the weight. This is a fact that the narrative records because Meredith noticed it, bending down to look at the wheel wells the way a sailor checks a waterline. Two inches. The car was seaworthy. She straightened, pushed the trunk closed with both hands until the latch caught, and stood in the parking lot.

It was eleven at night. The parking lot was lit by two sodium lamps that gave everything the color of urine samples, and the building behind her was dark except for the single window on the second floor where the janitor's radio was playing something with horns. The air smelled of cold and of the Kansas particular, which is the smell of distance—not flowers, not trees, not industry, but the skin-scent of miles of uninterrupted flatness, which arrives as a kind of clean vacancy, as if the air has traveled so far to reach you that it has forgotten what it was carrying.

Meredith did not go back inside. She did not leave a note on her desk. She did not call her supervisor, Harold Fenn, at his home number, which she had memorized eleven years ago and never used. She did not call anyone. She walked around the car, opened the driver's side door, sat down, adjusted the rearview mirror—which had drifted, as if the car itself had been looking somewhere else while it waited—and started the engine.

In the house she did not return to—a one-story bungalow on Fillmore Street with a detached garage and a yard that she had mowed every Saturday until this one—there were signs of a life that fit more than one person, though the narrative will not enter that house and will not confirm what those signs imply. There was a second toothbrush in the ceramic holder by the bathroom sink. The refrigerator held a quantity of food calibrated for more than solitary consumption: a gallon of milk rather than a half, two kinds of lunch meat, a bag of oranges. On the nightstand on the left side of the bed—not Meredith's side; she slept on the right, near the window—there was a library book, overdue, face down, its spine cracked at page 140, a novel about the sea. These objects will wait. The milk will expire. The book will accrue its fines. The toothbrush will stand in its holder like a small, stiff flag planted on an abandoned claim.

Meredith Plank drove west out of Topeka on U.S. 24 and did not look at her house as she passed the turn that would have taken her to it. At the edge of the city, where the last subdivision surrendered to the fields and the streetlights ended with the abruptness of a sentence that has decided it has said enough, the Valiant entered the dark.

Now the narrator asks the reader to rise. Come up. Leave the car's interior with its vinyl smell and its dashboard lights and its eleven boxes humming with undelivered language, and lift—above the roof, above the road, above the last lights of Topeka—until you can see what the night sees: a small car, headache-colored, moving west on a two-lane highway through a blackness so complete that the headlights seem not to illuminate the road but to *invent* it, pulling the asphalt into existence a hundred yards at a time. Behind the car, the city is an orange glow, a half-shut eye. Ahead, nothing. Or rather: everything—the whole dark body of the country, as heavy and patient as a sleeping dog—but visible as nothing, because the Great Plains at midnight are the closest America comes to the experience of deep ocean, and a car on that road is a creature of bioluminescence, making what tiny light it can, moving through the black enormity by its own small insistence that motion is possible, that destinations exist, that the road goes somewhere because someone once believed it should.
