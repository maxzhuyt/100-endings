# The Ballad of Dusty Marvell, As Best I Can Remember It

---

## I.

Whoever the hell finds this journal, I need you to understand three things before we go any further.

First: my name is Hen Pool and I am a midwife, which is to say I have pulled more screaming life into this world than anyone in Doña Ana County and been paid for approximately a third of it. I have delivered breech babies by candlelight. I have delivered twins in a rainstorm in a sodhouse where the roof was coming down in clumps and the mother was fourteen and the father was nowhere because fathers are almost always nowhere, which is the first thing you learn in my trade and the last thing you forget.

Second: I am a liar. I am a liar the way some women are left-handed — it is simply the orientation of my nature, and I came by it young enough that I cannot tell you where honest leaves off and the rest begins. I have lied to priests. I have lied to lawmen. I have lied in three languages, one of which I do not actually speak, which I suppose is a fourth lie stacked on whatever I was lying about in the first place. I tell you this not as a boast but as a courtesy. You should know what you are holding.

Third: Dusty Marvell is dead.

She has been dead for seven months. She died on the ninth of March, 1893, which was a Thursday, which was a cold day for the territory, and she died — 

She died and I am in this cell and the trial is in six days and this journal is the only paper they will give me because the lawyer, that wretched gap-toothed boy with the new collar, thought it would be *therapeutic* for me to write down my *feelings*. He actually said the word feelings. He said it the way you'd say *carbuncle* or *diphtheria* — like it was a medical condition he'd read about in a book and was now diagnosing from a safe and Christian distance.

So here are my feelings: I want whiskey, which they will not give me. I want a cigarette, which they will not give me. I want to stop dreaming about Dusty's hands, which were the most capable hands I have ever seen on a human body, man or woman, and which I will never see again because she is dead, which I have mentioned, which I will not stop mentioning, because if I say it enough times in enough ways perhaps it will finally lose its teeth and become just another fact I can set down and walk past.

It has not lost its teeth yet.

I do not expect you — whoever you are, wherever you are, whenever you find this moldering little book in whatever corner of whatever ruin — to believe what follows. I do not expect you to like me. I don't even expect you to read to the end. But I am going to tell it anyway, because there is nothing else to do in here but count the cracks in the adobe and listen to Deputy Boggs pick his teeth, and because Dusty Marvell lived, she *lived*, and the world should have at least one record of that fact even if the record is composed by a liar in a jail cell who can't be trusted to get the weather right.

Don't get comfortable.

This is not that kind of story.

---

## II.

I met Dusty Marvell in — 

The year was 1873. Probably. It may have been 1874 because I remember the Garza store had burned already and some people say the Garza store burned in January of '74, but other people say late '73, and I was not in the habit of writing down dates at that age because I did not yet own a calendar or a reason to care what day it was. Days were just weather then. You got up and the weather was a thing that happened to you and then it was dark and you went to sleep and the next weather started.

I was living outside the settlement of Los Huesos, which was not a town so much as a rumor that had accumulated some buildings. There was the Garza store before it burned. There was a cantina that served mescal that tasted like it had been strained through a saddle blanket. There was a post office that was really just a crate inside the cantina. There were perhaps forty people, depending on the season, roughly half of whom owed me money for delivering their children and roughly a quarter of whom were those children. I lived in a canvas-and-timber structure I will generously call a tent, and I owned: one mule, one set of midwifery tools wrapped in oilcloth, one copy of a anatomy text I had stolen from a dead surgeon's field kit during the war — not the fighting part of the war, the messy afterward part — a wool blanket, a coffeepot, and one good knife.

I need you to understand about the knife. It was not fancy. It was a skinning knife with a walnut handle that had cracked once and been bound back together with copper wire. My mother had used it and her mother had used it and I could not tell you the providence before that. It was not, by any empirical assessment, a valuable knife. It was not sharp enough. The balance was wrong. But it was mine, mine in the way that a thing can be yours when you have almost nothing — not a possession but an organ. An external heart. I would sooner have surrendered a finger.

This is how I met Dusty:

I was asleep and then I was awake and there was a shape in my tent and the shape had my knife. I could see it — the copper wire on the handle catching what little moonlight came through the canvas. The shape was moving toward the flap. I said, or rather screamed, something that was probably not a word, and I launched myself at the shape and we went down together in a tangle of limbs and blanket and the tent half-collapsed on us so that we were thrashing there in the dirt like two cats in a sack. I got a fistful of hair. The shape got an elbow into my stomach. I bit something — an arm, I think — and the shape said, very clearly, very calmly, in a voice like smoke over gravel: "You bite hard for someone so skinny."

I said — and this I remember exactly, because it was the first honest thing I said to her — "Give me back my knife, you [piece of shit], or I will bite a lot harder."

She laughed. She was lying under me in a collapsed tent with my teeth marks on her forearm and she *laughed*. And then she said: "I'll trade you. The knife for a meal. I haven't eaten in two days."

So I fed her.

No.

That is not right either. Or it is right but it is not *true*, which is a different thing, a thing I have never been able to explain to anyone who has not lived it. You can get every fact correct and still lie with the shape of them, the order, the weight you give each one. A midwife knows this. A birth told as *the baby came and the mother wept with joy* is a different creature than the same birth told as *there was a lot of blood and the cord was wrapped and I thought for a full minute we were going to lose them both and then we didn't*, even though both versions happened, both are true, both are the same twelve hours in the same room.

What is true, in every version, in every way I have tried to tell this to myself in the years since:

She was half-Apache on her mother's side and something else on her father's — Mexican, maybe, or some mix she never pinned down and did not care to investigate because her father was a gap in her history she kept sealed shut, the way you'd board up a well that had gone poisonous.

She was broke the way only people who understand money can be broke — not ignorant of it but emptied of it, scraped out, the way a mine is broke after you have taken everything that glitters and left nothing but the shaft.

She was smart the way a river is fast. You did not experience her intelligence as a quality she possessed. You experienced it as a current, and you were either swimming with it or you were drowning.

She took my knife. That part is true in every version. She took it and I followed her and I have spent twenty years trying to decide if I ever got it back or if I just stopped noticing it was gone because the hand that held it had become more interesting than the knife itself.

She looked at me — this is the last thing I will say about the meeting because I am aware I am stalling — she looked at me the way I have seen certain women look at a locked door. Like I was the answer to a question she hadn't finished asking. Like she was still constructing the question. Like the answer had shown up early and she was annoyed and curious in equal measure.

I did not know what to do with that look.

I should tell you: I still don't.

## III.

The first job we ever did together was the Peralta claim, and it was so stupid it should not have worked, and it worked beautifully, and I have never been happier in my life before or since, and if that is a pathetic thing to say about a land fraud I committed at the age of — at whatever age I was — then I invite you to go to hell because you did not feel what I felt and you do not get to judge the venue where I felt it.

Here is how it went.

There was a man named Horace Blunt. There is always a man named something like Horace Blunt. He had come out from St. Louis with capital and very clean boots and a conviction that the New Mexico Territory existed specifically for the purpose of making him rich. He wanted land. He wanted a great deal of it. He particularly wanted a parcel along the river south of Los Huesos that was, at the time, being used by a handful of families — Mexican families who had been there long enough that the dirt knew their names — but Horace Blunt did not recognize the claims of people who could not produce the right papers in the right language in the right office, which is to say Horace Blunt did not recognize the claims of people who were not Horace Blunt.

Dusty heard about this in the cantina. I don't know who told her. People told Dusty things. She had that quality — you wanted to offer her information the way you'd offer a pretty woman a drink, just to see if she'd take it, just to be part of whatever she was going to do next.

She came to me with the bones of it and I fleshed it out. That was how we worked, from the very beginning. She was the architect. I was the — what is the word — the contractor. The one who knew where the nails went.

The plan: we would sell Horace Blunt a land grant. Not the actual land grant, which did not exist in any form he could use. A *better* land grant. One we would make ourselves. Dusty had a hand like a printer's — she could reproduce any signature, any seal, any official flourish, after seeing it once. I was the one who knew what the documents should *say*, because I could read English, Spanish, and enough legal Latin to fake the rest, and because I understood — here is where the midwifery comes in, oddly — I understood what people needed to see in order to believe something was alive. A birth certificate and a land grant are not so different. Both are pieces of paper that insist a thing is real. The baby does not need the paper. The land does not need the paper. But the world will not acknowledge either without it.

We forged a grant. We aged it with coffee and careful tearing. We invented a genealogy for the grant — a chain of title going back to the Spanish crown, complete with a family named de Peralta that had never existed outside of Dusty's handwriting and my imagination. We decided I would present it because I was white and plain-faced and had the kind of trustworthy forgettable look that authority relies on, and because Dusty — Dusty *could not* present it, for reasons that had nothing to do with ability and everything to do with the particular ugliness of who is believed and who is not in a Territory that was three different countries' stolen property depending on which century you held up to the light.

This is something I need you to understand. I need you to understand it now because it will matter later and I will not be in the mood to explain it later: Dusty was better than me at everything. *Everything*. The forgery, the reading of men, the strategy, the nerve. She was better and she could not walk into Horace Blunt's office and be believed because she was a brown woman with cheekbones that told the truth her mouth never would about where she came from. So I walked in instead. I was the face. She was the mind. And this arrangement — which we never discussed, which we did not need to discuss, which was as obvious and as rotten as the weather — this arrangement was the first brick in a road I am still walking and it led here, to this cell, to this page, to the fact that she is dead and I am the one with a pencil.

But I was telling you about being happy.

Horace Blunt bought the grant. He paid in cash and land scrip and the deed to a small warehouse near the river, which Dusty later sold to a man from Tucson for twice what it was worth. The families along the river kept their homes — we made sure, or Dusty made sure, which I suppose is the same thing except it isn't, except it should be. Horace Blunt did not discover the fraud until months later in a territorial office in Santa Fe, at which point he was too embarrassed to pursue it, because admitting you were swindled by two women in Los Huesos was a social death no amount of St. Louis capital could resurrect you from.

We celebrated. Dusty bought mescal — the good kind, from below the border, not the saddle-blanket variety — and we sat outside my tent and drank it and the stars were obscene, as they always are in that part of the country, and she told me about a con she'd seen as a child, a man in Tucson who had sold the same horse to three different buyers in the same afternoon by simply never letting any two of them see the same side of the animal. She told it with her hands, acting out the parts, and the firelight was on her face and her mouth was loose from the mescal and I thought—

She told the story and I laughed and we drank more and I remember the specific quality of the happiness. It was not soft. It was not warm. It was *bright*, it was the happiness of having found the one other person in the world who spoke your language, the language under language, the one that is not taught but *recognized*, and I had spent my whole life up to that point certain that I was the only native speaker and here she was, fluent, and what I felt was not — it was not what you might think, it was not that simple, and even if it was I do not have the vocabulary for it, this language was not built to hold what I — 

It doesn't matter what I thought.

We were partners. That is the word I will use. We were partners and we were brilliant at it and the world was going to regret letting us find each other.

## IV.

I do not have the patience or the paper to tell you everything we did in the years that followed, so here is the brief accounting, the way you'd read a ledger if the ledger was written by a woman who was there for all of it and is now trying to fit a decade into a few pages because the alternative is remembering each moment individually and that is a thing I cannot do from this cell without losing what is left of my composure.

*1875, spring:* Dusty acquires forty head of cattle through means I choose not to detail except to say that the previous owner was a man who had himself acquired them through means I choose not to detail, and so on, back through a chain of theft so long that the cows themselves probably could not have identified their original provenance. We drive them south and sell them to a broker who does not ask questions because he has arranged his life specifically around the not-asking of questions.

*1875, fall:* I deliver the Sandoval twins.
