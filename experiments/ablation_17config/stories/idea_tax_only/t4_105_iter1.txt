# The Last Epidemiologist Standing

---

## I.

The subcommittee chamber could seat three hundred. Maren counted eleven people, including herself.

Senator Wynn from Idaho had arrived fourteen minutes late and was reading something on his phone below the dais with the posture of a man who believed himself unobserved. A junior staffer for the ranking member sat in the second row with a legal pad on which she had written the date and nothing else. The camera operator from C-SPAN 3 had set up his equipment with professional efficiency and then leaned against the east wall and begun scrolling. Two other staffers whose principals had not appeared sat at opposite ends of the front bench. One was eating a protein bar, slowly, tearing small pieces from it and placing them in his mouth with the self-consciousness of someone who knew he shouldn't be eating but had calculated that no one important was watching. He was correct.

Maren's binder was three inches thick. She had organized it into seven tabbed sections: epidemiological timeline, case clustering data, transmission pathway analysis, feedlot sampling results (2019–2026), mortality tables, interagency correspondence, and a final section she had labeled simply "Recommendations." She had printed everything on acid-free paper because she had once been told that congressional testimony could be archived, and she had thought, in some part of herself that still operated on an older model of how things worked, that archival permanence mattered. That what was preserved would eventually be consulted.

"Dr. Hubble, you may proceed with your prepared statement," said the chairwoman, Senator Alice Parr from Connecticut, who was the only member of the subcommittee physically present and who had told Maren in the hallway beforehand, touching her elbow with the practiced warmth of someone who touches many elbows, that she thought this hearing was "so important."

"Thank you, Chairwoman Parr."

Maren adjusted the microphone. Her voice was hoarse. It had been hoarse for three weeks, a low-grade laryngitis she could not shake, and she had started carrying a thermos of hot water with lemon and honey that she sipped between sentences when she practiced reading the statement aloud in her apartment, standing behind the kitchen counter because the counter was roughly the height of the witness table and she had wanted to practice the physicality of it, the posture of testimony.

She read.

"In September 2019, during routine surveillance of bovine tissue samples collected from a USDA-inspected feedlot in Weld County, Colorado, I identified an atypical prion variant exhibiting a hemorrhagic presentation inconsistent with any previously classified transmissible spongiform encephalopathy. Over the subsequent eight years, I documented the geographic spread of this variant across feedlot operations in fourteen states, its probable zoonotic transmission pathway through contaminated beef products, and its correlation with a cluster pattern of rapidly progressive neurological disease in human populations that has, to date, resulted in an estimated eleven thousand four hundred confirmed deaths and an epidemiologically modeled toll approximately three to four times that figure."

She paused. She took a sip from her thermos.

Senator Wynn looked up from his phone, briefly, the way a person looks up when a truck passes outside a window. Then he looked back down.

Maren continued. She read for nineteen minutes. She presented the data with the precision of someone who had been presenting this data for eight years, which is to say without emphasis, without rhetorical acceleration, without any of the narrative strategies that might have made the numbers land as something other than numbers. She had learned, at some point she could no longer identify, that emphasis did not help. That raising her voice did not help. That the data either mattered to the person hearing it or it did not, and that the determining variable was never the quality of the presentation. She had given this presentation — this exact data set, in earlier and less complete forms, but structurally the same presentation, the same argument, the same conclusion — in rooms like this one, and in rooms much smaller, and in rooms with no windows, and on conference calls where she could hear someone typing, and in a Zoom meeting during which one participant's child appeared on screen and everyone spent four minutes being charmed while Maren's slides waited, and in emails that received auto-replies, and in reports that were filed in systems that were later migrated to other systems, and she had given it with passion and she had given it without passion and it had not mattered which.

"In conclusion," she said, "the data supports an immediate federal response including but not limited to a targeted recall of beef products from implicated supply chains, mandatory prion screening protocols at USDA-inspected processing facilities, and the establishment of a dedicated interagency task force with the authority to coordinate between the CDC, USDA, FDA, and state-level public health departments. I have included in my written testimony a detailed implementation framework for each of these recommendations. I am happy to answer any questions."

The chairwoman thanked her. Senator Wynn did not look up. The staffer with the legal pad wrote something — Maren could not see what, but the motion of the pen was brief, a word or two, not a sentence. The protein bar was finished. Its wrapper was being folded into smaller and smaller squares.

"I do have a few questions," said Chairwoman Parr, and she asked three questions that were thoughtful and well-informed and that Maren answered thoroughly and that would appear in a transcript that would be uploaded to a government website that would be visited by Maren, by Maren's graduate advisor who had been following her work and who sent her an email after that read simply, *I'm proud of you, kid, for whatever that's worth*, by a journalist in Minneapolis who was working on a long-form piece that would be published and read by approximately nineteen thousand people, and by essentially no one else.

The hearing adjourned at 3:47 PM. It had lasted thirty-one minutes. It had been scheduled for two hours.

Maren closed her binder. She capped her thermos. She stood, and her knees ached the way they had been aching for months, a deep-bone soreness she attributed to stress and her doctor attributed to early-onset arthritis and neither of them connected to the fact that she was forty-one years old and had been carrying something heavy for eight years and the weight of it had settled, as weight does, into the joints.

She walked out of the chamber and into the hallway, which was marble and enormous and empty in the way that only government buildings can be empty — populated by architecture that was designed for significance, vaulted ceilings and carved stone and the specific quality of silence that exists in rooms built to house voices that mattered.

Her heels echoed. She did not look back.

---

The fluorescent lights in the CDC's Division of High-Consequence Pathogens and Pathology satellite office in Fort Collins, Colorado, operated at a color temperature of 4100 Kelvin, which is called "cool white" and which, in practice, makes human skin look faintly grey and makes the hours between 2:00 and 4:00 PM feel identical to the hours between 2:00 and 4:00 AM.

It was a Thursday in September 2019. Maren Hubble was thirty-three years old. She was sitting at her bench — the third bench from the left in a shared laboratory space that also housed two other investigators, one of whom was on leave and the other of whom was at a conference in Atlanta — looking through a microscope at a tissue section from a bovine brain.

The tissue had arrived in a cooler with eleven other samples from a USDA-inspected feedlot in Weld County. The samples had been collected as part of a passive surveillance program that Maren administered, which involved, primarily, the processing of tissue samples that arrived in coolers, the maintenance of a database that had been built in FileMaker Pro in 2011 and migrated to a newer platform in 2017 with partial data loss, and the filing of quarterly reports that were sent to her supervisor in Atlanta and that her supervisor read, or at least acknowledged receiving, which was not the same thing but which was the available metric.

She adjusted the focus. She was looking at the characteristic spongiform vacuolation of a transmissible spongiform encephalopathy — the tiny holes in the brain tissue that gave the disease family its name, the look of a sponge, the look of something that had been eaten from inside. This was not unusual. BSE-related prion samples passed through the lab on a semi-regular basis, remnants of a surveillance architecture built in the early 2000s during the last significant public concern about mad cow disease, a concern that had since receded into the cultural background noise the way concerns do, not because they are resolved but because attention is a resource and resources are allocated by mechanisms that have nothing to do with the persistence of a threat.

But the vacuolation pattern was wrong.

Not dramatically wrong. Not wrong in the way that would make a person gasp or call a colleague over. Wrong in the way that a particular note in a piece of music can be wrong — technically a valid note, within the range of the instrument, but not the note that belonged in that position if the piece was what you thought it was.

The holes were distributed in the thalamus, which was expected. But they were also present, densely, in the vascular endothelium. And the tissue around the vascular clusters showed something Maren had not seen in a prion sample before: petechial hemorrhaging. Tiny bleeds. As if the misfolded proteins were not merely destroying neural tissue but were attacking the blood vessels themselves, or — and this was the thought that made her sit back from the microscope and stare at the wall for a moment — as if the prion variant had a vascular tropism that the known BSE strains did not.

She looked at the sample label. WC-0919-07. Weld County, September 2019, sample seven of twelve.

She pulled the next slide. WC-0919-08. Same feedlot, different animal. She looked.

The same pattern. The vascular hemorrhaging. The endothelial clustering.

She pulled 09. Same.

She sat with this for six minutes. She did not time herself; the six minutes was reconstructed later from the laboratory's keycard access log, which showed that she did not leave the building or swipe into any other area during that interval, and from her own email records, which showed a gap between her last routine message (a reply to a departmental query about supply orders, sent at 2:11 PM) and the next email she composed (sent at 2:17 PM, to her supervisor in Atlanta, Dr. James Okoro, subject line: "Atypical prion morphology in Weld County bovine samples — requesting guidance").

The email was 340 words. It described the vacuolation pattern, the vascular involvement, and the hemorrhagic presentation. It noted that the findings were preliminary and that she wanted to run confirmatory staining and a Western blot before drawing conclusions. It asked whether Dr. Okoro thought the samples warranted flagging to the interagency liaison channel. It was written in the careful, hedged, institutionally fluent language of a field investigator who understood that the first communication about an anomaly set the rhetorical tone for every subsequent communication, and that if you sounded alarmed before the data justified alarm, you would be remembered as the person who sounded alarmed, and that memory would adhere to every piece of data you produced afterward like a dye that would not wash out.

She proofread the email twice. She sent it.

Dr. Okoro replied four days later. "Interesting. Keep me posted. Let's discuss at next month's team call."

Maren returned to her bench. She pulled sample WC-0919-10.

Same pattern.

She began a new file in her database. She labeled it with the systematic neutrality that was the only language the institution could metabolize: "Atypical TSE Variant — Bovine — Hemorrhagic Presentation — Preliminary Surveillance Finding."

She typed her initial observations. She saved the file. The fluorescent lights hummed at 4100 Kelvin. Outside, the Colorado sun was setting over the Front Range, turning the sky a color that no one in the building could see because the windows in the laboratory faced the parking lot.

## II.

The meeting was held on February 11, 2020, in Conference Room 3B of the USDA's Jamie L. Whitten Building in Washington, D.C. The room had a long oval table, seating for twenty, and a speakerphone in the center of the table shaped like a flattened black starfish. The speakerphone was not used. Everyone was present in person except for Maren, who participated via video link from Fort Collins, her face occupying a forty-inch screen mounted on the east wall at a height that required everyone at the table to look slightly upward to see her, which most of them did not do.

The meeting had been convened by the Office of the Surgeon General in response to a routine interdepartmental flag — Maren's data having traveled upward through Dr. Okoro to the DHHS liaison channel with the slow, frictionless momentum of a piece of paper dropped into a still pond, sinking at a rate determined not by urgency but by density. The meeting's official purpose, as stated in the calendar invitation sent by a scheduling coordinator named Denise, was to "discuss preliminary findings regarding atypical BSE-adjacent prion activity in domestic cattle populations and assess interagency communication protocols."

Seven people sat at the table. Maren was the eighth, on screen.

Dr. Nathan Tull, Deputy to the Surgeon General, chaired the meeting. He was fifty-six, silver-haired, with the particular physical gravitas of a man who had spent decades in rooms where physical gravitas was a form of currency. He opened with a summary of the agenda that lasted four minutes and contained no information that was not already in the calendar invitation.

"Let's start with an overview of the findings," he said. "Dr.—" He glanced at his notes. "Hubble. You have the floor."

"Thank you, Dr. Tull."

Maren shared her screen. Her first slide was titled "Atypical Hemorrhagic Prion Variant: Preliminary Findings from Bovine Surveillance, 2019–2020." She had designed the slides herself, in PowerPoint, using the CDC's standard template. The font was Calibri. The graphs were in three colors: blue, red, and grey.

She spoke for eleven minutes. She described the anomalous vacuolation pattern. She presented microscopy images. She showed a map of the three states — Colorado, Nebraska, and Kansas — where she had now confirmed the variant in feedlot samples. She displayed a graph showing the frequency of the variant's appearance in her surveillance samples over the preceding five months: a line that rose, not steeply, but with a consistency that, to an epidemiologist, read as a signature. She explained, carefully, that the hemorrhagic presentation was unlike any classified TSE and that the vascular tropism suggested a potential — she used the word "potential" twice in one sentence, a prophylactic repetition — for a transmission pathway and clinical profile that existing surveillance protocols were not designed to detect.

"To be clear," she said, "I am not, at this time, asserting a zoonotic risk. I am noting that the characteristics of this variant fall outside the parameters of our current screening framework, and I am recommending that we expand the surveillance sample to determine whether this is a localized finding or something more geographically distributed."

She stopped sharing her screen. Her face reappeared on the monitor, slightly pixelated, looking up and to the left, which was where her camera was relative to the video window — a misalignment that made her appear, to the people in the room, as if she were not looking at them. Which, in the ways that mattered, they were not looking at her.

Dr. Tull nodded. "Thank you, Dr. Hubble. Very thorough."

He turned to the table. "Rick, your thoughts?"

He was a veterinarian by training who had moved into administration fifteen years earlier and who spoke with the measured cadence of someone who had learned that the speed at which you talked in interagency meetings was inversely correlated with the seriousness with which you were taken.
