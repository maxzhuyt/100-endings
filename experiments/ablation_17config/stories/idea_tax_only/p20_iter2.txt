# The Undertow

## I.

We were nine, and the sea took us both on the same wave.

I need you to see it the way I saw it then, before I knew what I know now—before the knowing got into everything like salt into wood and warped it all past closing. I need you to see two girls on the breakwater in October, the granite dark with spray, their shoes left in a pile because they'd been daring each other to go further out, one more rock, one more, the way children do when they are trying to build a country between them and have not yet learned that countries require blood.

Maren went first. She always went first. She had that way about her even then—not bravery exactly, but a kind of porousness, as though the boundary between herself and the world were thinner than it should have been and things simply *flowed into her* before she could decide whether to resist. She stepped onto the farthest rock, the one the fishermen called the Pulpit, and she turned back to me with her arms out and her hair mad with wind, and she was laughing—I have never in my life heard anyone laugh the way Maren laughed on that rock, with her whole throat open like a bell—and then the swell came over the Pulpit and she was gone.

I went in after her. I did not decide to go in after her. My body went in after her the way water goes toward low ground, because she was where the gravity was. The cold was total. It was not like being cold; it was like learning that warmth had always been a temporary condition and this—this airless black compression—was the native state of things. My lungs clenched like fists. I could not see. I could not find which direction held the sky. I kicked and reached and I could feel the current pulling me somewhere that was not a direction at all, somewhere that was *down* in a way that had nothing to do with the ocean floor, and I thought with the very clear, very small voice that children use when they understand something enormous: *Oh, I am going to die now, and I have not yet done a single thing.*

Then a hand.

I have told myself for twenty-three years that it was Maren's hand. She told herself for twenty-three years that it was mine. We surfaced within seconds of each other, retching, dragging ourselves onto the rocks with our palms torn open against the barnacles, and we held each other and shook. Her blood went into my cuts and mine into hers and we did not notice because we were too busy being alive, too busy pressing our faces together and screaming and sobbing and saying each other's names over and over like an incantation against the dark we'd just escaped.

The village called it a miracle. Father Emory said a prayer of thanks at evening service.

I tell it to you now and my hands are steady, you'll notice. Steady and cold. Because I no longer believe in the miracle. I believe something was in the water with us—beneath us—and I believe it held us both and considered us and chose to send us back. Not out of mercy. The way I understand it now, and I have had a long time to understand it, mercy would have been letting us drown.

What brought us back was something else.

Patience, maybe. Appetite.

The opening of a very long mouth.

## II.

You have to understand how good it was, or the rest means nothing.

After the drowning we were not two girls anymore. We were a single organism with two bodies and one circulatory system made of loyalty. The village understood this instinctively, the way a village understands which fields drain well and which hold water—they simply observed the landscape and adjusted. Our mothers stopped asking "Is Maren coming?" or "Will Sable be there?" The question was always *where are they*, plural, a compound noun, the way you'd ask after a pair of shoes or a set of hands.

We had a language. I need to describe it precisely because precision is the only weapon I have left against what happened to it. Three taps on the wrist meant *I'm here*. Two taps meant *look*. One long press of a thumb into the soft part of the palm meant *I need to leave*, and the other would make an excuse and follow. We had a whistle—a four-note phrase Maren invented, the interval of a falling minor third followed by two quick rising tones—that we could thread through crowd noise, through fog, through the clatter of the fish market on Saturday mornings when the whole harbor smelled of brine and diesel and everyone was shouting prices. I could find her across the village with that whistle. She could find me in the dark.

Her house kept a pillow for me in the cedar chest at the foot of her bed. We had slept in the same room so many times that my body knew the sounds of her family's house the way it knew my own: the way the third step from the top popped when the temperature dropped, the particular frequency of her father's snoring through the wall, the tick of the kitchen clock that was always four minutes fast. Her house. My pillow. The way she smelled in sleep—warm bread and salt and something green, like moss after rain, a smell I have never encountered in any other living person, a smell I would know in total blindness, in total deafness, in any condition short of death and possibly beyond it.

On the dock after school she braided my hair. My hair is thick and fights back against structure, and Maren was the only person who had the patience for it—she'd sit behind me with her knees bracketing my hips and her fingers working through the tangles, not gently, she was never gentle, but with a kind of focused competence that felt like being taken seriously. She braided while I read aloud to her. We went through entire books that way. I can still feel the pull of her hands in my scalp when I read certain sentences, as though the words and the touch were recorded on the same track and cannot be played separately.

We had an oath. I will not tell you the words. I will tell you that we spoke it standing knee-deep in the shallows at the cove north of the village on the night of Maren's eleventh birthday, the water so cold it burned, the stars out in that dense and staggering way they only appear over the Atlantic when there is no moon and the village has gone to bed and you are young enough to believe the sky is showing itself to you specifically because you asked. We pushed our cut palms together—our old scars from the barnacles had healed badly and opened easily—and let the blood run down into the water and said the words, and I believed them. God help me, I believe them still. That is the part I cannot get clean.

The village, meanwhile, had its own rhythms, and I gave them no more thought than I gave the tides. I noticed, of course—you could not grow up in Carra Mór and fail to notice—that the fishermen spoke to the water before casting their nets. Not prayers exactly. Muttered phrases, almost sheepish, the way you'd apologize to someone you'd bumped in a narrow corridor. I noticed that the older women, the ones who had outlived their husbands, kept shallow dishes of salt on their windowsills, refreshing them each morning, and that if a child knocked one over the reaction was not annoyance but something colder, something closer to the way you'd respond to a child playing with a loaded gun. I noticed that no one in Carra Mór swam for pleasure. The children were not forbidden from the water—nothing so dramatic—but the water was treated the way you might treat a large and unpredictable dog in the room. You did not turn your back on it. You did not shout near it. You certainly did not enter it without purpose.

I see all of this now as though someone has taken a painting I thought I knew and adjusted the lighting, and in the new light every shadow reveals a figure that was always there. But I was a child, and then I was a girl, and the salt on the sills was just the village being the village, and the fishermen's whispers were just the sea being the sea, and the only thing I understood as sacred and whole and real was the girl beside me whose hand was always exactly where mine reached for it.

Maren. Maren, Maren, Maren.

I am saying her name too many times. I know that. I am building a reliquary and you can see me doing it. But I need you to hold these things—the whistle, the pillow, the braid, the oath, the smell of her in sleep—I need you to hold them the way I held them, in the center of your chest, treated as precious and unbreakable, *because they are about to break and you have to feel it when they do*.

## III.

It started—or rather, I started *admitting* it started—in the January of our eighteenth year.

We had stayed. Neither of us had talked about leaving, though most of the young people in Carra Mór treated the village as a thing to be escaped. Maren worked at her father's chandlery. I kept books for the co-op. We rented the upstairs of the Donnelly house together, two rooms with a shared wall so thin we could tap messages through the plaster, our old language of knocks adapted to a new medium. I fell asleep most nights to the sound of her breathing through the wall, and sometimes, when the wind was bad and the building groaned, she'd come in and slide under my blanket and press her forehead to my shoulder blade and we'd lie like that, fitted, and the world outside was nothing.

The first thing I noticed was the cold.

Maren had always run warm. It was one of the small, physical facts that constituted my knowledge of her—the way she generated heat in sleep, the way her hand in mine was always the warmer hand, the way she could walk to the harbor in January in a thin pullover and not shiver. But that winter her skin changed. Not all at once. Not dramatically enough to make a case of it. Just—her hand, when I reached for it, was cool. And then cooler. And then, one night in February when she crawled into my bed during a storm, her forearm against my stomach was so cold it woke me, a cold that did not feel like the absence of warmth but like the *presence* of something else, a cold with weight and texture, the cold of deep water.

I pulled the blanket tighter around her. She murmured something I didn't catch. I pressed my face into her hair.

There was salt in it.

It was crusted fine along her hairline, the way it gets when you've been swimming and let your hair dry in the wind. But it was February, and it was three in the morning, and she had not been near the water. I knew this because I knew the sounds of her world—I would have heard the front door, the stairs, the particular creak of the gate. She had been in her bed. The salt should not have been there.

I licked my thumb and wiped it away. I did not ask.

In March, the pauses began. We'd be walking to the chandlery, or sitting across from each other at breakfast, and Maren would stop mid-sentence—not lose her train of thought, nothing so ordinary—she would *stop*, her chin lifting slightly, her eyes going to a middle distance that seemed to have depth, as though she were looking not at something far away but at something very close that occupied a frequency I couldn't perceive. Her head would tilt. Five degrees, maybe seven. Toward the harbor. Always toward the harbor. And her lips would part just barely, the way they did when she was about to say something she'd been composing in silence, and then she'd blink and come back and finish her sentence as though nothing had happened.

"You went away for a second," I said once.

"Did I?" She smiled. "Just thinking."

"About what?"

"I don't remember. Isn't that funny?"

I let it go. Of course I let it go. What was I going to say—*your pauses frighten me*? *Your skin is the wrong temperature*? *I found salt in your hair on a night you didn't leave*? These are not things you say to the person you have built your entire architecture around. These are things you fold into the structure and plaster over, because the alternative is to admit the foundation is unsound, and if the foundation is unsound then nothing—nothing—

I let it go.

The night I should not have let go was in April.

I woke at what felt like the floor of the night—that dead hour between three and four when the body's processes slow toward their minimum and the world outside the window is so dark it seems to generate darkness, to push it into the room like breath. Maren was beside me. She was lying on her back, and her eyes were open.

"Mare?" I whispered.

She did not respond. Her eyes were open and fixed on the ceiling and they were *wet*—not with tears but with a sheen, a gloss, the way eyes look when they have been open too long and the body has stopped blinking. Her lips were moving. Barely. The smallest possible articulatory movements—tongue and teeth and the shaping of the mouth—and from her throat came a sound, a sound I have spent years trying to describe, and the closest I can come is this: it sounded like water moving over stones in a streambed, but shaped. Organized. Patterned in a way that was unmistakably linguistic. There were repetitions. There was something that might have been syntax. It rose and fell with the rhythm of breakers on a shore.

I lay beside her and did not move and did not breathe and listened to my best friend speak the ocean in her sleep.

It lasted perhaps ninety seconds. Then her eyes closed, and her breathing evened, and the room was just a room again.

In the morning she remembered nothing. She ate her toast. She braided my hair on the step outside the chandlery. She whistled our four-note phrase when she saw me across the market at noon.

I said nothing.

I said nothing because I loved her, and because love, I have learned, is the most effective form of silence, and because silence is the thing that moves through the dark unnoticed, and because the dark was already inside the house, already inside the bed, already inside the breath of the person beside me, and I chose not to see it, and not seeing it let it in further, and letting it in further meant that by the time I finally opened my eyes there was nothing left to see but the shape of what my silence had allowed.

I was so careful with her.

I was so careful, and my care was the corridor through which the thing walked.

## IV.

Bridget Ó Ceallaigh was eighty-one years old and had buried two husbands and a son, all three taken by the water, and she frightened me even before she had reason to. She was the kind of old woman the village treated with a deference that had nothing to do with affection—people stepped aside for her not out of courtesy but out of the same instinct that makes you step aside for a dog you're not certain about. She knew things. Everyone agreed she knew things. No one asked her what they were.

It happened at the Saturday market in May.

Maren and I were at the stall where Bridget sold smoked mackerel wrapped in newspaper and small jars of pickled samphire that she sealed with white wax. I was picking through the samphire, looking for the jars where the stalks were still bright green. Maren was beside me, her hand resting lightly on the small of my back the way it always did when we stood near each other, her thumb tracing absent patterns through the fabric of my coat.

Bridget looked up from making change for another customer. Her eyes found Maren. And her hand, the one holding the coins, clenched shut.

The color left her face in a way I had never seen color leave a face—not a gradual paling but a *departure*, as though the blood had been summoned elsewhere by a higher authority. She took a step backward. Her shoulder blades hit the board behind her stall and the jars of samphire shuddered on their shelf.
