# Evidence Locker Hearts

## I.

The Garden of Eden Motor Lodge sat between a bail bonds office and a tire store on Route 9 in Paramus, New Jersey, and if God had ever visited it, He had done so anonymously and left no review. The parking lot was the color of exhaustion. The ice machine had been broken since the second Clinton administration, and someone had taped a sign to it that read SORRY FOR THE INCONTINENCE, which no one had corrected in fourteen years because no one who worked at the Garden of Eden Motor Lodge was paid enough to notice anything, least of all the difference between two words that, if you thought about it long enough—and the narrator has—described roughly the same problem.

It was into this parking lot, on the first Monday of November, that a county van deposited four jurors and one bailiff like cargo whose insurance had lapsed.

The bailiff's name was Pat Keenan. Pat was forty-one and had held this job for nine years because it permitted a life organized around the absence of surprise, and because the Mercer County Superior Court provided dental. Pat carried a clipboard and a duffel bag and a face that had been assembled, feature by feature, for the express purpose of being forgettable. This was, Pat had always believed, an advantage. Witnesses never remembered the bailiff. Attorneys never worried about the bailiff. The bailiff was furniture that could lock doors. Pat Keenan had made a career, and something resembling a peace, out of being the least interesting person in every room.

What Pat Keenan did not yet know—could not know for another eight days—was that this particular set of rooms would require a filed affidavit, two incident reports, one falsified logbook entry, and a letter of resignation that Pat would begin drafting on hotel stationery at 3:00 a.m. on Day Seven and then fold into a paper crane because a woman with no business being on this jury had taught the entire hallway origami at midnight using pages torn from the Gideon Bible, and by Day Seven the bailiff could no longer look at a piece of paper without wanting to make it beautiful, and that—*that*—was the kind of trouble this was.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves. The narrator apologizes. The narrator does not, in fact, apologize. The narrator has been waiting to tell this story the way a woman who has survived a hurricane waits to describe it at Thanksgiving: with relish, with too many details, with the specific delight of someone who knows the ending involves everyone still being alive but also the roof being gone and a canoe in the living room.

Room 6 went to the retired IRS auditor.

His name was Leonard Lundkvist, and he was sixty-eight, and he entered the room the way he had entered every room for forty-two years: with the expectation that it was lying to him. He touched nothing. He stood in the doorway and performed a visual audit with a thoroughness that the room—which had been cleaned using a mop that itself needed cleaning—did not deserve. He noted the carpet stain near the bathroom threshold (organic, brownish, roughly the shape of Delaware). He noted the curtains, which were the particular shade of maroon that motels select to hide the evidence of sorrow. He noted that the smoke detector's battery light was not blinking, which meant the battery was dead, which meant that Leonard Lundkvist's safety in the event of a fire depended entirely on the same institutional competence that had produced the carpet stain. He noted all of this in three seconds, because Leonard had spent four decades finding the discrepancy in the fifteenth decimal place of a corporation's filed return, and a motel room was, by comparison, a document that could not even be bothered to lie convincingly.

He sat on the edge of the bed. He placed his hands on his knees. He looked, in that moment, like a man who had arrived early to his own sentencing and was too polite to mention it.

Room 8 went to the taxidermist.

Her name was June Pepitone, and she was fifty-three, and before her suitcase had made contact with the bedspread—a polyester floral print that had given up on joy sometime during the Reagan years—she had already pocketed both miniature soaps from the bathroom counter. She did this with a gesture so practiced and fluid that it resembled, in its economy and its quiet reverence, the motion of a woman crossing herself before an altar. Her fingers closed over the small wrapped rectangles and delivered them into the left pocket of her cardigan in a single arc that lasted less than a second and contained, if you knew how to look at it, the entirety of her philosophy: that all small things were perishing, and that the act of taking them was indistinguishable from the act of saving them, and that no one had ever, in fifty-three years, understood the difference.

She also took the pen from the nightstand. And the notepad. And the laminated card explaining checkout procedures, which no one had ever in the history of motor lodges wanted or needed, which is precisely why June wanted and needed it—because it was the kind of thing that existed in a state of total neglect, artifacts so beneath notice that removing them was practically a mercy. The Do Not Disturb sign. The channel guide. A single bobby pin left by a previous guest in the shallow tray beside the television remote. June cataloged each item by touch as it entered her pockets or her luggage, and she felt the particular calm that other people reportedly got from yoga or the ocean or the love of a faithful dog—none of which June had ever experienced, but she imagined they were similar.

Room 10 went to the defendant's mother.

Her name was Donna Russo, and she should not have been there. Both the prosecution and the defense had been provided a jury questionnaire in which Donna, who had the same surname as the defendant and whose listed home address was eleven blocks from the crime scene, had written, in a handwriting that leaned forward like a woman walking into wind: *I can be fair.* And someone—a clerk, an intern, a humaneli in the system's vast digestive tract—had processed this questionnaire without comparing it to the defendant's filing, and neither the assistant district attorney nor the public defender had caught it during voir dire because Donna Russo was a small woman with reading glasses and a sweater set who looked like a person whose strongest opinion would be about the proper way to blanch broccoli rabe, and this was Donna's great gift and great weapon: she looked like nothing. She looked like a woman who had never once in her life required surveillance, and this was a lie so comprehensive that it functioned as camouflage at the species level. Donna Russo had raised three sons in the Ironbound and buried a husband who owed money to men who did not send invoices, and she had done all of this while maintaining the exterior of a woman whose most dangerous act was double-parking outside the Italian bakery on Ferry Street, and if the American justice system could not see through that, then the American justice system had gotten exactly the juror it deserved.

Donna entered Room 10 and went directly to the kitchenette—a counter, a mini-fridge, a microwave so ancient it might have been manufactured during a period when the word "radiation" was still used casually in advertising. She opened the single cabinet above the counter and found it empty. She plugged in the mini-fridge and listened for the compressor. She located the room's single electrical outlet—the one nearest the desk, partially hidden behind the pressboard nightstand—and examined it with the calm, assessing gaze of a woman performing reconnaissance.

She had plans. The narrator knows what they are. The narrator is choosing, for the moment, to let you wonder.

And Pat Keenan stood in the hallway.

The hallway of the Garden of Eden Motor Lodge was six feet wide and lit by fluorescent tubes that buzzed at a frequency the human ear was not designed to forgive. The carpet was the same defeated maroon as the curtains. At the hallway's far end, a window overlooked the parking lot and, beyond it, the tire store, whose sign—AL'S ALIGNMENT: WE KEEP YOU STRAIGHT—would become, over the next nine days, an object of increasingly hysterical philosophical contemplation for every person on this floor, but especially for Pat, for whom the concept of straightness—moral, procedural, otherwise—was about to become very complicated.

Pat consulted the clipboard. Pat had a schedule. Pat had *protocols.* Jurors would be escorted to and from the courthouse each morning in a van. Jurors would not access personal electronic devices, which had been surrendered upon check-in and were now stored in a safe in Pat's room. Jurors would not communicate with anyone outside the sequestration group. Jurors would not discuss the case among themselves except in the deliberation room. Jurors would receive a continental breakfast each morning, delivered by the motel's contracted food service. Jurors would behave like human beings stored in a filing cabinet—present, inert, and organized by number.

The judge had said nine days at minimum. The judge had said it the way a dentist says "some discomfort," and Pat had understood.

The trial was State v. Russo. Anthony Russo, age twenty-nine, charged with the first-degree murder of Vincent "Vinnie Ankles" Tagliaferro, which had occurred in the parking lot of a Red Lobster in a manner so gruesome that the crime scene photographs had, according to courthouse rumor, caused one forensic technician to become a vegetarian and another to simply leave law enforcement for a career in ceramic tile installation. The particulars of the murder are not, the narrator wishes to assure you, the point. The murder is the weather in which this story occurs. It is the atmospheric pressure. You do not need to understand the Cheddar Bay Biscuits. You need to understand the hallway.

Pat walked the hallway once. Pat checked each door. Pat made the first entry in the logbook:

*Monday. 4:47 PM. All four jurors checked in. Rooms 6, 8, 10, 12. Room 12 unoccupied at present—alternate juror to be assigned if needed. No irregularities.*

Room 12. The narrator wants you to notice Room 12, sitting empty at the end of the hall like a gun on a mantelpiece in a play by a Russian who understood that objects, placed in view, demand their own futures.

At 5:30 PM, a young man from the motel's contracted food service delivered four bagged dinners to Pat, who distributed them door to door. The young man was polite. He wore a polo shirt with a logo Pat did not recognize—not the motel's brand, which was visible on everything from the towels to the soap wrappers to the fire exit maps, but something else, some catering company: a small oval, dark green, with lettering too small to read from the hallway's distance. He carried the bags in a plastic crate. He said, "Four dinners, all the same, turkey club, chips, cookie," and he said it with the rehearsed flatness of a person who had been told this information and was transmitting it without attachment, the way a wire carries current without understanding electricity. He was maybe twenty-two. Forgettable. Pat signed the delivery receipt and did not read it.

The young man walked back toward the service entrance. He passed through the hallway like weather. Like mud. Like a detail mentioned once and never quite accounted for.

## II.

On the evening of Day One, Donna Russo performed an act of defiance so domestic it was almost invisible, and so invisible it changed everything.

She unpacked the hot plate.

It had been at the bottom of her suitcase, wrapped in a dish towel that smelled of cedar and the particular variety of laundry detergent that has not been manufactured since 2004 but that Donna Russo had been stockpiling in her basement in unmarked containers because the new formula did not smell right—did not smell like the house she had made, the life she had maintained, the kitchen in which she had fed seven thousand meals to people who were now either dead, incarcerated, or living in Delaware, which Donna considered approximately the same thing.

She placed it on the pressboard desk. She did not hide it. She did not angle it away from the door. She plugged it into the outlet behind the nightstand with the brisk competence of a woman establishing a field kitchen, and if you had told her that this was what she was doing—building a kitchen in a war zone—she would not have argued with the metaphor. Donna Russo had been at war since 1987. The enemies rotated. The kitchen was the only fixed position.

From the suitcase, she produced: a box of De Cecco spaghetti, a block of Pecorino Romano wrapped in wax paper, a Ziploc bag containing perhaps a cup and a half of freshly cracked black pepper (freshly cracked at home, that morning, before the van came, while her son sat in a cell eleven miles away), a small bottle of olive oil, a single pot, and a fork. A real fork. Not motel cutlery, not plastic, not disposable—a fork from her kitchen, stainless steel with a slight bend in the second tine where her youngest had used it as a lever to open a paint can in 1998, and she had never straightened it because the bend was, in a way she had never articulated, proof that her house had been *lived in,* that things had happened there that were not planned and could not be undone and were, on balance, good—or at least *hers.*

She boiled water in the pot using the bathroom tap. The hot plate took seven minutes. She stood by it with the terrifying patience of a woman who has raised sons.

She made cacio e pepe.

If you have not made cacio e pepe, the narrator will tell you that it requires almost nothing—pasta, cheese, pepper, the starchy water in which the pasta cooked—and that this austerity is its danger, because there is nothing to hide behind. Every gesture matters. The moment the cheese hits the water, the ratio of heat to starch to fat decides whether you have produced something transcendent or a clumped, grainy failure, and Donna Russo had been making this dish since before the defendant was born, which is to say: she had been making this dish since before the event that would place her in this motel, on this jury, performing this exact act of unsanctioned cooking, had even been possible. There is a word for this. The narrator believes the word is *providence,* though the narrator concedes that a less romantic person might say *premeditation.*

The smell left Room 10 like an expeditionary force.

Cacio e pepe, when made correctly—and Donna made it correctly in the way that a river flows correctly, which is to say, without deliberation—produces an aroma that is at once sharp and warm, peppery and fatty, aggressive in the way that all Italian food is aggressive: it enters a room before being invited and does not ask whether you are hungry. It simply assumes, with an immigrant's certainty, that you are.

The smell traveled. The Garden of Eden Motor Lodge was not a building designed to contain smells. It was not, in truth, a building designed to contain anything, including its guests' despair, which it managed to hold only in the loosest sense, the way a paper bag holds rain. The hallway acted as a duct. The gap beneath each door acted as an inlet. Within three minutes, three rooms and one hallway had been colonized.

In Room 6, Leonard Lundkvist lifted his head from the turkey club he had been eating with the joyless discipline of a man refueling a machine. He had been an auditor. He knew that smell was particulate—that what he was experiencing was the physical migration of cheese volatiles and pepper compounds through inadequate door seals—and knowing this made no difference whatsoever, because the body does not audit its own desires, and Leonard's body, which had spent sixty-eight years being managed rather than *fed,* recognized Donna Russo's cacio e pepe the way a person raised in a language they'd forgotten recognizes a word spoken in a crowd: not with comprehension but with a devastation that precedes comprehension and does not require it.

He put down the turkey club. He stood. He was not aware, in the moment, that he was making a decision.
