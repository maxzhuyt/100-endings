# THE TURING FIELD

---

**BBC ENGINEERING DIVISION**
**BROADCAST CONTINUITY — TEST CARD F**
**COLOUR SYSTEM: PAL I — 625 LINES**
**GENERATED 09:14 GMT 14.11.1978**

---

The test card holds for eleven seconds. A horizontal band of interference rolls upward through the image twice, consistent with playback from a quad-format videotape that has been stored in suboptimal humidity for approximately twenty years. The colour bars flicker. A generation counter, barely visible in the upper-left quadrant of the frame, reads **G3**, indicating this is a third-generation copy. The original recording has not been located.

A title card appears in the standard BBC Reith typeface used for internal productions between 1974 and 1981:

---

**THE TURING FIELD**

**A Film by the BBC Science Features Department**

**Producer: J. R. Alford**
**Associate Producer: M. E. Cantwell**
**Commissioned under Reference BF/SF/73-1187**
**Classification: RESTRICTED — NOT FOR BROADCAST**
**Internal Distribution Only — See Annex D**

---

A voice begins speaking over the title card. It is male, middle-register, carefully modulated in the RP accent characteristic of BBC science programming of the period. It does not belong to any presenter identified in the Corporation's personnel archives for 1978. Freedom of Information requests filed with the BBC in 2019 and 2021 regarding production reference BF/SF/73-1187 received identical replies: "No records matching this reference number have been located in our current cataloguing system." The British Film Institute's TRILT database contains no entry for *The Turing Field*. The videotape from which this transfer was made was discovered in 2017 during the decommissioning of a deep-storage facility in Perivale previously used by BBC Enterprises for overflow archival material. It was found in an unlabelled grey case between a boxed set of 1975 *Open University* geology modules and a tape marked only "ERASE — NTU."

The provenance of the tape has been partially verified. Chemical analysis of the oxide layer is consistent with Scotch 479 one-inch stock manufactured between 1976 and 1979. The control-track signatures match BBC Engineering Division calibration records from Television Centre Studio E, which was used by the Science Features Department for post-production during the relevant period. The BBC has declined to comment on the circumstances of the tape's presence in the Perivale facility or on the existence of its stated production reference number.

What follows is a transcription and description of the tape's contents, presented without editorial interpolation.

---

The narrator speaks:

*"In 1950, the mathematician Alan Turing published a paper in the journal* Mind *that would fundamentally alter our conception of the boundary between human thought and mechanical process. The paper was titled 'Computing Machinery and Intelligence.' It began with a question so deceptively simple that the whole of subsequent philosophy has been, in a sense, an attempt to answer it:* Can machines think?

*"Toward the end of this paper, in a passage that has received rather less attention than the famous 'Imitation Game' that opens it, Turing addressed an objection he called the 'Argument from Surprise'—the claim that machines cannot truly think because they can never surprise us, because they can only do what we tell them. Turing's reply was characteristically measured, and characteristically unsettling. We quote it now."*

The screen fills with a photographed page. It is unmistakably a reproduction from the original 1950 issue of *Mind*, Vol. LIX, No. 236, page 450. The typesetting, the margin width, the faintly yellowed stock are all correct and can be verified against any university library's holdings. The passage is highlighted:

> *Machines take me by surprise with great frequency. This is largely because I do not do sufficient calculation to decide what to expect them to do, or rather because, although I do the calculation, I do it in a hurried, slipshod fashion, taking risks. Perhaps I say to myself, 'I suppose the voltage here ought to be the same as there: anyway let us assume it is.' Naturally I am often wrong, and the result is a surprise for me, for by the time the experiment is done these assumptions have been forgotten. These admissions lay me open to lectures on the subject of my vicious ways, but do not throw any doubt on my credibility when I testify to the surprises I experience.*

The camera holds on the page for nine seconds—long enough for the passage to be read twice. Then the image cuts.

---

Static. One point four seconds. Then a photograph fills the frame.

It is the photograph of Turing that everyone knows—or rather, one of the very few. This one shows him at a slight angle, taken at the National Physical Laboratory, a lock of dark hair falling above his left eye, his gaze directed past the camera with the expression of a man whose attention has already moved several steps ahead of whatever the photographer asked him to think about. It is an authentic image. It has been published thousands of times.

The narrator resumes:

*"Alan Mathison Turing. Born 23 June 1912 in Maida Vale. Educated at Sherborne School and King's College, Cambridge. Fellow of the Royal Society. Fellow of King's. The man who, in 1936, at the age of twenty-three, published a paper that invented the modern computer as an incidental step in proving that mathematics itself contains questions no procedure can settle."*

More photographs. The Bombe at Bletchley Park—the real one, the reconstruction now housed at the museum, shown here in its wartime configuration. A memorandum on Government Code and Cypher School letterhead, dated September 1941, describing personnel allocation for Hut 8. Turing's name appears on the second line. The document is authentic and was declassified in 1996.

*"During the war, Turing's work at the Government Code and Cypher School—Bletchley Park—was instrumental in the breaking of the German Enigma cipher. The details were classified for thirty years, and so the man who shortened the war by an estimated two years returned to civilian life without public recognition, holding secrets he would never be permitted to share."*

A letter. The handwriting is Turing's—the distinctive forward-slanting script recognisable from authenticated correspondence held at King's College Archive Centre, reference AMT/D. The letter is addressed to Robin Gandy, Turing's former student and friend, and discusses, in characteristically playful language, the Manchester Mark I's ability to generate unexpected outputs from simple initial conditions. The letter is genuine. It has been published in *The Essential Turing* and can be consulted in the original at Cambridge.

*"At Manchester, he worked on morphogenesis, on the chemistry of pattern in biological growth, on what he called 'the chemical basis of life.' He ran. He ran prodigiously—near-Olympic marathon times, alone, for the pleasure of it, pounding muddy lanes in the Cheshire countryside in shorts and plimsolls."*

Then a photograph never previously published in any Turing biography: a formal portrait in which Turing wears a dark suit and a tie of indeterminate pattern, seated before a plain backdrop, his hands folded. The image quality is poor—slightly overexposed, the contrast range suggesting a government identification photograph rather than a portrait. No archive reference appears on screen. The narrator does not remark on its provenance.

*"In 1952, Turing reported a burglary at his home. The subsequent police investigation led not to the apprehension of the burglar but to the discovery that Turing was in a sexual relationship with another man. He was prosecuted under Section 11 of the Criminal Law Amendment Act 1885—the same statute used to convict Oscar Wilde. He pleaded guilty. He was given a choice: prison, or chemical castration through a course of oestrogen injections. He chose the injections. They lasted a year. His security clearance was revoked."*

A long pause. The screen shows a Home Office document—correctly formatted, correctly classified, the crest of the period accurately reproduced—regarding the terms of Turing's probation. This document is known to historians. It is real.

*"On the seventh of June, 1954, Turing was found dead at his home in Wilmslow, Cheshire. Beside him on the nightstand was an apple from which several bites had been taken. The coroner's inquest determined the cause of death to be cyanide poisoning, self-administered. He was forty-one years old."*

The screen goes to black for four full seconds. When the narrator's voice returns, it is unchanged in pitch, register, or cadence. The institutional tone is identical. If there is a seam, it is not audible.

*"That is the accepted account. What follows has not been part of any accepted account until now."*

---

A document fills the screen. It is formatted as an internal Home Office memorandum. The header reads:

---

**HOME OFFICE — CLASSIFIED**
**Ref: HO/TS/54-3341/ULTRA**
**DATE: 9.vi.54**
**TO: Permanent Under-Secretary (Security)**
**FROM: Dir. Sec. (Technical Liaison)**
**RE: DISPOSITION — TURING, A.M.**

---

The classification marking "ULTRA" is of the correct type used in post-war British intelligence documents for material related to wartime signals intelligence and its derivatives. The formatting—wide left margin, single-spaced, courier typeface—is consistent with Home Office internal memoranda of the period. The date notation ("9.vi.54") follows the convention used in classified correspondence requiring ambiguity-free dating.

The text reads:

> *Further to the action of 7.vi.54 and the release of the agreed cover account to the Cheshire Constabulary (see HO/TS/54-3338), the subject has been transferred to the Manchester facility under Special Technical Group arrangements. Medical clearance pending. The cover death is regarded as adequate for press purposes and need not be elaborated. The inquest should be allowed to proceed on the existing evidence. Dr. Lancaster will ensure the toxicology findings are consistent. No family notification is authorised at this stage.*
>
> *The subject's cooperation has been obtained on the basis described in Annex C (retained, not attached). He is aware that no alternative to cooperation exists. His technical assessment of the Manchester difficulty is expected within fourteen days.*

Below the text, a routing annotation in manuscript reads: "Copy 1 of 3. Destroy after action. C." The handwriting is unfamiliar and unattributed.

The narrator says nothing for six seconds. Then:

*"The document shown is one of eleven recovered from a sealed file within the National Archives deposit at Kew, reference DEFE 57/114, a batch of files transferred from the Ministry of Defence in 1974 and embargoed under a seventy-five-year closure order expiring in 2049. We are not able to describe the means by which access was obtained."*

There is a brief cut to the exterior of a building. It is filmed in 16mm—grainier than the video material surrounding it, the colour shifted toward green in a way characteristic of aged Eastmancolor stock. The building is low, institutional, surrounded by a chain-link fence. Behind it, the flat expanse of a Lancashire or Cheshire industrial estate, the skyline dominated by two cooling towers. The footage lasts only five seconds and appears to have been shot from a car. No identifying markers are visible.

*"The Manchester facility referenced in the document was identified through a grid reference appearing in a second memorandum, dated 11 June 1954, as a former Royal Ordnance site repurposed in 1951 for a joint Ministry of Defence–GCHQ signals research programme. We have not been able to determine the current status of the site. The grid reference resolves to a location in Trafford, Greater Manchester, now occupied by light industrial units. Planning records show the demolition of an underground structure at this address in 1967.*

*"What Turing found inside it is described in his own hand."*

---

The camera pushes in. The image fills with paper—not the clean bureaucratic typescript of the Home Office memorandum, but manuscript. The handwriting is Turing's. The same slanting, energetic script visible in his authenticated letters to Gandy, to Church, to his mother. The ink is a faded blue-black consistent with Quink or a similar post-war fountain pen ink. The paper is unlined, slightly foxed at the edges, with a watermark partially visible in the lower-right corner—"BASIL" or "BASILDON," the image resolution is insufficient to determine. The pages are numbered in Turing's hand, top right.

**Page 1. Dated 15.vi.54.**

> *Arrived yesterday evening under escort. Two men, civilian dress, no names given. Driver knew the route by memory—no map consulted—which suggests regular traffic. The gate was manned by a single guard who inspected no documents and spoke to no one. Procedure consistent with a site whose security model relies on anonymity rather than physical deterrence. Sensible for something you wish not to exist.*
>
> *Descended three levels via a goods lift. The shaft was original to the ROF complex—wartime poured concrete, standard Ministry of Works specification, unreinforced at the joins—but the lower level itself is newer. I would estimate 1950 or 1951 construction. The corridor lighting is fluorescent, American-made, General Electric catalogue if I'm not mistaken, which suggests either Lend-Lease surplus or a direct procurement relationship with a US programme. The former is more likely; the latter more interesting.*

The page turns. The narrator reads. His voice has acquired no additional drama, no conspiratorial undertone. He reads as a man reads minutes in a committee meeting.

**Page 2.**

> *There are seven of us in total, not counting the security detail, who occupy the upper level and do not descend. The team:*
>
> *Davies—the project lead in a nominal sense. Civil servant, Home Office Technical Section, has the look of a man who administers rather than understands. Smokes a pipe at his desk, which is unwise given the ventilation limitations.*
>
> *Ingham—electronics engineer, formerly Ferranti. Knows the hardware. Dislikes me on principle or reputation; I have not yet determined which.*
>
> *Ashworth—mathematician, University of Leeds. Competent. Frightened. Has been here four months and has the pallor to prove it.*
>
> *Hendricks—American. Signals intelligence background, evidently the liaison element. Carries himself with a casualness that is almost certainly calculated.*
>
> *Margaret Lowell—the only woman. Systems analyst. Formerly NPL. I believe I may have met her in 1949, though she shows no sign of recognition and I will not press the point. She is the most precise mind here.*
>
> *Cartwright—Royal Engineers. Maintains the physical plant. Barely speaks. Reads westerns.*
>
> *And myself. The dead man. They needed someone who could read the machine's architecture from the inside, and there is no one else. There was never anyone else. I wonder if they knew that when they arranged the apple.*

There is, here, a barely perceptible edit—a single dropped frame in the tape, visible as a horizontal flash. It may be a playback artefact. It may be an edit point where material was removed. The tape continues.

**Pages 3 through 5.**

> *The machine occupies the lowest chamber—a single rectangular room approximately forty feet by sixty, ceiling height twelve feet, temperature maintained at 55°F by a refrigeration system I can hear but not see. The room smells of ozone and warm Bakelite, a scent I know as well as my own hands.*
>
> *It is, at its core, a cryptographic engine. This much I was told before my arrival, and this much is immediately apparent. The relay banks—sixteen arrays of twelve, each array housed in a standard GPO rack—are configured for key-stream generation. The wiring pattern is a cascade topology: each array's output feeds the next as a modifier, the whole chain forming a polyalphabetic cipher generator of formidable depth. The basic architecture is not novel. I could name three systems at GC&CS that used a similar principle during the war, and the Americans undoubtedly had more.*
>
> *But this machine is not those systems.*
>
> *The
