# The Ninth Summit

## I.

The rope communicated in a language older than Russian. Two tugs: *I am anchored, you may proceed.* One tug: *stop.* Three tugs, sharp and descending: *trouble.* No tugs at all, and the wet fiber simply hanging slack in the white nothing between two bodies: *I have made a decision about my life that does not include you.*

Yevgenia climbed third on the line. She always climbed third. The cartographer belonged in the middle, where she could see forward and back, where the rope told her stories from both directions. Ahead of her: two men. Behind her: four. Seven total, roped at twelve-meter intervals through a couloir that had no name on any chart she'd drawn because no chart had ever been asked to hold this part of the mountain. The Soviets did not name what they intended to conquer. They numbered it, requisitioned it, and sent seven people up its southeast face under an order of radio silence so absolute it extended, by some unspoken consensus, to the human voice itself.

No one had spoken since Camp Three. That was eleven hours ago. Eleven hours is not a long time to go without speaking if you are sleeping, reading, sitting in a cinema. It is a very long time if you are roped to six other bodies on a forty-degree ice face at seven thousand meters, where of every three breaths only one reaches the bottom of each lung, where the cold is not a temperature but a pressure, a thing that leans on you like a drunk companion who will not let go.

The man at the front of the rope climbed with his left hand slightly forward of his right, always, as though reaching for a door that kept receding. He set his ice axe with a strange precision—two taps to test, then the full swing—and the rhythm of it came down the rope to Yevgenia's harness in a code she could read with her hips: *tap-tap-THUNK, pause, step, tap-tap-THUNK.* This was Devlin. Lieutenant Devlin, A.K., Hero of the Soviet Union, Order of the Red Banner, who had held a machine-gun position outside Stalingrad for nine hours after the rest of his platoon had been reduced to a problem for the burial detail. This was—said the expedition pamphlet, the one she'd helped typeset at the Ministry office in Moscow, the one with the photographs in which every man stood identically, shoulders back, chin raised, as though they'd all been carved from the same civic monument—"a symbol of the indomitable will of the Soviet fighting man, whose bravery at the Barrikady factory district inspired a generation of defenders." On the mountain he was a pair of hands that could not properly close. The frostbite was old, from before, and he gripped the axe with his palms rather than his fingers, a technique Yevgenia had seen in no manual and which should not have worked at all.

Behind Devlin, climbing second, was the one who always looked back. Every third step he would pause and turn his head, not fully, just enough for the bulge of his oxygen mask to swing toward Yevgenia, and in the beginning she thought he was checking on her, being courteous or protective or one of the other small gallantries men performed when they wanted to be seen performing them. But his eyes never settled on her. They swept the slope behind her, past her, as though cataloguing the positions of the four men at her back. This was Gretchko. Captain Gretchko, P.S., Hero of the Soviet Union, Order of Suvorov Second Class, who had led a tank company through the northern shoulder of the Kursk Salient in July of 1943 and was described in the pamphlet as "an exemplar of armored daring whose tactical innovations contributed materially to the destruction of the fascist offensive capability." On the mountain he was a man who could not stop counting the people behind him.

Third: Yevgenia.

She did not know what her body said to the men on the rope. She could not step outside herself and read her own signature the way she read theirs. She knew only what the pamphlet said about her, and what the pamphlet said was very little, because the pamphlet had been uneasy about her from the start. "Y.D. Menshova, senior cartographic technician attached to the expedition in an advisory capacity, whose mapping expertise in the Tian Shan range has proven invaluable to Soviet geographic science." No Hero of the Soviet Union. No Order of anything. She was the only woman and the only civilian, and the pamphlet handled this the way the Ministry handled all its embarrassments: with a passive construction and a quick change of subject.

Behind her, at positions four through seven, the rope darkened with the weight of four more men whose bodies she knew better than their names.

Fourth: the one who clipped carabiners with two fingers—index and thumb only, the other three curled against his palm as if protecting something. Lebedev. Major Lebedev, R.V. The pamphlet called him a Hero of the Defense of Moscow. He had done something with artillery in December 1941 that involved repositioning twelve guns across a frozen river in four hours. On the mountain his economy of motion was so extreme he seemed to climb inside a space only slightly larger than his own body, as though the air itself had been rationed and he was determined to use no more than his share.

Fifth: the one who hummed. Not audibly—not a violation of the silence—but Yevgenia could feel it in the rope, a faint vibration that was not the wind, not the creak of ice, but something alive and rhythmic, a pulse traveling through the cord that connected them. Sorokin. Lieutenant Colonel Sorokin, M.G. The pamphlet mentioned Sevastopol. Beyond this Yevgenia knew nearly nothing, and the rope told her only that he was steady, that his pace never varied, that the humming—if it was humming—never stopped.

Sixth: the heavy one. Not fat—there was no fat left on any of them—but dense, wide-strided, a man who moved through the mountain the way a plow moves through a field, applying force in one direction without apparent interest in grace or efficiency. Tarkovsky. Senior Lieutenant Tarkovsky, O.I. He had done something at Königsberg. The pamphlet did not say what, exactly. It called him "a participant in the storming of the fortress city," which was a phrase Yevgenia recognized from her years of typesetting: it meant whatever had happened was not suitable for a pamphlet.

Seventh, at the end of the rope, in the position most vulnerable to rockfall and least likely to arrest a fall by anyone else: the one who climbed as though apologizing. Each placement of his axe was tentative, almost interrogatory, as though he were asking the ice for permission. Each step was followed by a microscopic hesitation—a pause so brief it might have been balance, might have been doubt. Volkov. Sergeant Volkov, E.N. The pamphlet called him "a twice-decorated veteran of the Vistula-Oder Offensive whose personal courage in the crossing of the Oder River contributed to the liberation of the fraternal Polish nation." On the mountain he was the youngest of them by fifteen years and the only one who still flinched at the sound of falling rock.

Seven people. One rope. The mountain above them, white and grey and without opinion.

Yevgenia kept her eyes on the ice ahead and recorded everything. This was what cartographers did. They recorded what was there and said nothing about what it meant, and the map was useful precisely because it had no feelings about the territory. She'd mapped seventeen peaks in the Tian Shan. She'd drawn the contour lines of places where men had died, and the contour lines had not mourned them. She climbed now the way she drew: observing, noting, placing one mark after another in a line that would eventually describe a shape she did not yet understand.

The wind dropped. The rope went taut, then slack, then taut. Above her, Devlin's axe rang against something harder than ice.

*Tap-tap-THUNK.*

The mountain said nothing. The mountain, of course, had not been placed under any order of silence. It simply had nothing to say.

## II.

They felt it before they heard it, and they heard it before they saw it, and when they saw it there was nothing to see—only a new smoothness in the valley below, a white correction, as though the mountain had looked down at its own base and decided to erase a draft.

The vibration came through Yevgenia's crampons first, then through her knees, then into the rope, which hummed—a different hum from Sorokin's, lower, geological. Devlin stopped. The rope stacked up behind him as each man halted in sequence, and for a moment they stood on the face like seven notes on a staff, evenly spaced, all facing down.

Base camp had been in the valley at four thousand meters. Three tents: one for sleeping, one for communications, one for the supplies they would need for the descent. The radio—the radio they were forbidden from using, the radio whose frequencies were monitored by a signals unit in Almaty that was itself monitored by a different signals unit in Moscow—had been in the communications tent, along with the codebook, the backup batteries, and a photograph of the General Secretary that regulations required in any temporary Soviet installation, even those pitched on glacial moraine at the edge of the known world.

All of it was now under nine meters of snow. Perhaps twelve. The deposit was smooth and pale and new, and from their elevation it looked like a fresh bedsheet pulled over something that had stopped moving.

Yevgenia watched the six men watching the grave of their retreat. No one signaled. No one moved. The rope hung between them in shallow catenary curves, describing in the language of physics what none of them were willing to say with their hands: *we are slack. We are waiting. The tension has gone out of us.*

Lebedev, at position four, lowered himself into a crouch. Yevgenia thought he was buckling. Then he unlaced his left boot with those two precise fingers, upended it, and tapped a small stone out onto the snow. The stone rolled a few centimeters and stopped. Lebedev examined the inside of the boot, replaced his foot, laced it, and stood up. The whole operation took perhaps forty seconds. His face, or what was visible of it between the balaclava and goggles, displayed no expression whatsoever. He had, Yevgenia thought, been waiting to do this. He had been climbing with a stone in his boot and had chosen this moment—the moment of their entombment—to fix it. As though the small discomfort had become irrelevant only now, when the large facts had settled.

Gretchko, at position two, turned to face the slope above. He raised his left hand and made the signal for *continue.* Not the signal for *assess.* Not *regroup,* not *descend to evaluate,* not the complex two-handed semaphore for *emergency requiring deviation from mission plan.* Just: *continue.* The hand went up, the fingers extended, the palm rotated once toward the summit, and that was all.

No one signaled for descent.

Yevgenia noted this in the place where she kept her map-notes: a mental register of facts too precise for interpretation. She noted that no one looked at anyone else for consultation. She noted that no one made the signal for *count off,* which was the protocol when a team lost contact with its base. She noted that Volkov, at position seven, who should have been the most alarmed—he was the youngest, the least experienced at altitude, the one whose boot-placements still carried the tentativeness of a man not fully committed to the surface he occupied—was standing with his axe planted and his face lifted slightly toward the summit ridge as though the avalanche had not interrupted him but merely confirmed something he'd already decided.

The base camp was gone. The radio was buried. The descent route was now a problem that would require two additional days of traversal through avalanche debris at an altitude where each hour subtracted something measurable from the body's capacity to function. The rational response—the response mandated by every mountaineering protocol written by every Soviet, Swiss, British, and American authority—was immediate retreat by an alternate route, while the team still had the caloric reserves to descend.

What happened instead was that Devlin set his axe into the ice above him—*tap-tap-THUNK*—and began climbing.

The rope went taut at Gretchko's harness. Gretchko followed. The rope went taut at Yevgenia's harness, and she followed too, because she was the cartographer and the cartographer went where the line was, and because the feeling that moved through her hands when the rope pulled was not dread but a sensation she would spend many hours, later, trying to name—a feeling like the moment when a door that has been locked for a long time is not opened but removed entirely from its frame, hinges and all, so that the doorway becomes simply a space you were always meant to walk through.

Behind her, one by one, the rope went taut at each man's harness, and each man followed.

Nobody said anything. Nobody could say anything. But even if the silence order had been lifted that instant—even if a voice from Almaty had crackled through a radio that was now under four vertical meters of compacted snow and said, *Expedition Seven, you are authorized to speak freely*—Yevgenia understood, with the wordless certainty of a body reading a rope, that they would have climbed in silence anyway.

Some decisions do not require language. Some decisions were made so long ago that language was only ever keeping up appearances, and now that the appearances were buried under nine meters of avalanche deposit in a valley that would not be visible by reconnaissance aircraft for another six months, there was nothing left to pretend.

They climbed. The mountain received them. Below, the valley held its new white surface with the composure of a clerk who has stamped a file and moved on to the next.

## III.

They reached the fixed lines at noon, or at what Yevgenia's altimeter watch—the only instrument still working, because she wore it against her wrist where the skin kept it warm—indicated was noon. Time at altitude was a conjecture. The sun moved but refused to set, circling the horizon like a dog examining a place to lie down and never quite committing. Day was a word that had lost its antonym.

The anchor station was a titanium piton driven into a slab of granite that jutted from the ice face like a shoulder shrugging off a coat. The piton was Soviet, standard-issue, placed three weeks ago by the advance team that had come up to establish the route. A rope should have been hanging from it—thirty meters of ten-millimeter kernmantle, doubled back through a rappel ring—dropping down to the next anchor in a series of fixed lines that would carry them across the traverse to the upper snowfield.

The rope was there. The anchor was there. But the rope was short. Three meters of it hung from the ring, ending in a clean edge that did not fray, did not taper, did not show the long threads and splayed fibers of a rope that had been eaten by weather or abraded by rock. The cut was surgical. Perpendicular to the lay of the strands. Made by a blade that had been very sharp and applied from a single direction with a single motion.

Yevgenia looked at the cut. She looked at it the way she looked at the intersection of a contour line and a drainage feature on a half-finished map: not with emotion, but with the particular discomfort of a technician who has found data that does not fit the model. Weather didn't cut rope. Rockfall crushed or frayed. Ice crystal abrasion produced a tapered, brushed-out failure. The only thing that produced a cut this clean was a knife.

She reached forward and touched the end. The strands were cold and stiff but the cut was recent—within the last forty-eight hours, she estimated, because the sheath had not yet begun to absorb moisture at the exposed core. It was fresh.

She turned.

Six men stood on the face behind and below her, arrayed in a descending line, each clipped to the ice by his own anchor.
