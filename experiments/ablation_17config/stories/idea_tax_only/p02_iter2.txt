# The Verdict

---

The prosecutor was saying *systematic* when it stopped.

I mean everything stopped. Not the way a power outage stops a room, where the hum you didn't know you were hearing suddenly isn't there and you notice. This was different. This was the fluorescent buzz of Courtroom 4B, the asthmatic wheeze of the HVAC unit above the jury box, Judge Alderman's chair creak, the bailiff's perpetual sinus whistle, the low idling hum of El Paso itself — eight hundred thousand people generating the white noise of civilization on the other side of the blast-resistant windows — all of it, every frequency, excised at once, like someone had lifted the needle off the record of the world.

My fingers were still on the keys. They are always on the keys.

AUSA Patricia Leigh had been mid-syllable, her right hand raised at approximately forty-five degrees from the lectern, index finger extended toward the defendant, her mouth shaped around the second vowel of *systematic* — a word she had used fourteen times across the six-week trial, always with that same upward emphasis on the third syllable, always directed at the man in the navy suit who sat motionless at the defense table. She had been saying: "What the defendant oversaw was not an enterprise — it was a systematic—"

And then.

I noticed the silence first. Then the light, or rather its quality — the shift from the 4100-Kelvin commercial fluorescent tubes the General Services Administration installs in every federal courtroom to something that had no Kelvin rating at all, something that did not come from above. It came from below. It came from what we were standing on.

I am a certified court reporter for the Western District of Texas. I have held this certification for twenty-three years. In that time I have transcribed 1,147 proceedings, including 312 felony trials, 89 capital sentencings, and one congressional hearing that was classified above my clearance before the transcript was sealed. My function is to record. Not to interpret. Not to react. The American in Hemingway's story says things and the girl says things and the narrator does not tell you who is right. That is my profession made into literature.

I record.

So: the surface beneath our feet was not a floor. It was a membrane, mildly concave, possessing a slight give under the heel — approximately the resistance of a wrestling mat or a well-kept fairway after rain — and it was alive. I use the word with full awareness that it invites skepticism. But there is no other word that applies. Beneath its uppermost layer, which was smooth and faintly warm and the color of wet pearl, vascular structures pulsed in rhythms I could see: bioluminescent channels, violet-blue to amber, branching and reconnecting in patterns that suggested circulation or communication or both. The light they produced was the only light. It rose through the membrane the way a candle rises through cupped hands, giving our three bodies a faint underlighting that eliminated shadows altogether and made us look, to each other, like projections of ourselves.

I looked up, or rather outward, because above us there was no ceiling and no sky. There was a space. It was vast beyond useful estimation — I am not a man who deploys words like *infinite* — but the horizon, insofar as a horizon existed, curved upward at its terminus in a way that contradicted every spatial orientation my inner ear has been calibrated to since birth. The geometry was concave. We appeared to be standing on the inner surface of something.

There was no wind. There was no scent. The temperature was approximately 72 degrees Fahrenheit. I note these absences because the human sensorium, when deprived of expected input, does not go quiet. It screams in the negative. The silence had a texture. It was not the absence of sound. It was the presence of something that had chosen not to speak.

AUSA Leigh still had her hand raised. Herrera was standing — he had been standing for the verdict — and his attorney was not with us, which meant that whatever had happened had selected. His hands were at his sides. His face was composed. I had watched that face across six weeks of testimony. I had watched it during descriptions of mass graves in the Juárez Valley, during wiretap playback of his voice authorizing the murder of a rival's eleven-year-old daughter, during the presentation of financial records documenting the laundering of approximately $2.3 billion through seventeen shell corporations. His face had remained, throughout, precisely as it was now: still, observant, and infinitely patient, like a man watching a weather system he knows will pass.

The prosecutor lowered her hand.

The membrane beneath our feet brightened.

It was subtle — the kind of shift you register peripherally before you can name it, the way you know a room has gotten warmer before you see the thermostat. But it brightened. The vascular patterns directly under the place where we stood pulsed once, twice, in rapid succession, and the blue-violet hue shifted toward amber, and the membrane itself became fractionally more translucent. I could see deeper into the structure below. Layers. Branching networks. What might have been, at tremendous depth, a faint and sourceless movement.

This brightening had begun at the moment AUSA Leigh lowered her hand. Or more precisely — and precision is my trade — at the moment the last audible fragment of her sentence stopped reverberating in whatever medium this space used in place of air. The word *systematic,* half-completed, had been the last human utterance. The membrane had responded to it. Or responded to its cessation. I was not yet certain which.

I recorded this observation on my stenotype, translating it into the machine shorthand that has been my language for longer than English has. My hands had not stopped. They never stop. I have reported proceedings during a 5.2 earthquake in Los Angeles (United States v. Markham, 2014), during a power outage that lasted forty-one minutes (In re: Consolidated Petroleum Partners, 2009), and during the medical emergency in which Judge Geraldine Foster suffered a cardiac event on the bench and was dead for ninety seconds before the paramedics restored sinus rhythm. My hands did not stop for any of these. They did not stop now.

Something about that fact — my fingers still moving, the machine still accepting input, the tape still advancing through mechanism — was the only continuous thread between where we had been and where we were. The courtroom was gone. Everything was gone. But the record continued.

---

Eleven seconds of silence. I counted.

Then the prosecutor spoke.

"What happened." It was not a question the way she said it. Her voice was the voice I had transcribed across 1,247 pages of trial record: controlled, mezzo-soprano, capable of projecting to the back wall of a courtroom without appearing to raise its volume by a single decibel. She had won the Solicitor General's award twice. She had argued before the Ninth Circuit eleven times and the Supreme Court once. She had, in the opinion of every defense attorney I had ever heard discuss her, no discernible emotional range beneath the professional register. This was not true. I had heard the microvariations. The slight flattening of vowels when evidence had been excluded that she believed was critical. The almost imperceptible acceleration of cadence when she was approaching a point she found personally, not merely legally, compelling. The single time, during closing arguments, when she had paused for two point three seconds — I timed it — before saying the name of a seventeen-year-old witness who had been murdered before he could testify, and in that pause I heard something I had no professional obligation to name and did not.

"What happened," she said again. Then she turned, and the turn itself was information: she turned first toward the space where Judge Alderman's bench should have been. Finding nothing, she swept her gaze through the full three-sixty. She was, I understood, doing what she always did. She was taking inventory of the room. Identifying exits. Noting the positions of all parties. Establishing the record in her mind before establishing it aloud.

Her gaze settled on Herrera.

"Mr. Herrera." She used the name she used in court, though his legal name for purposes of the indictment was Defendant Herrera-Garza, Alejandro Luis, and she typically used the full designation. The abbreviation was involuntary. A concession to disorientation. "Mr. Herrera, you will remain where I can see you. As an officer of the Department of Justice, I am informing you that you are still in federal custody, that the proceedings of the United States versus Herrera-Garza are still in effect, and that any attempt to—"

The ground lit up.

Not the gentle brightening I had noted before. This was violent — a flare, a detonation of light from directly beneath her feet that turned the membrane from pearl-opacity to near-transparency in the span of a breath. AUSA Leigh stumbled backward and I saw her face do something I had never seen it do in twenty-three years of observing faces professionally: it opened. All the musculature of restraint released at once and what was beneath was not fear, exactly, but the rawness of a person confronted with the total failure of a system they believed was coextensive with reality.

Because through the membrane — through what had been solid ground and was now the clarity of deep water — we could see down.

Below us, fathoms below, an immensity. Neural structures — I use the word because no other word approaches accuracy — luminous, branching, interconnected across distances that made the word *vast* feel parochial. Gold and violet and a color I do not have a name for, something between green and the idea of green, threaded through tissue that was not rock and not flesh and not anything I could analogize to an earthly substance. It was alive the way a forest is alive: at every scale simultaneously, each part communicating with every other part through pathways too numerous to count and too purposeful to be random. And within those neural pathways, at depths that should have been beyond visual resolution but were not — as though the medium itself functioned as a lens — there were shapes. Not quite buildings. Not quite organs. Structures that possessed the intentionality of architecture and the inevitability of anatomy, as though someone had designed a city and then the city had begun to breathe.

AUSA Leigh stopped speaking.

The ground re-solidified. Over perhaps three seconds, the transparency reversed itself, opacity returning from the edges inward like ice forming on a pond, and the membrane was again a pearl-smooth surface you could stand on without vertigo.

The neural abyss vanished. But we had seen it.

In the silence, Herrera laughed.

I have transcribed thousands of laughs. I have rendered them in the shorthand notation as [LAUGHTER], sometimes with qualifiers the judge has requested: [LAUGHTER — NERVOUS], [LAUGHTER — DERISIVE], [LAUGHTER — SUSTAINED, PROMPTING ADMONITION FROM THE COURT]. I have developed, over two decades, an amateur taxonomy of courtroom laughs. The nervous laugh of the defendant's family. The performative laugh of the attorney who wants the jury to find a witness incredible. The single, involuntary exhalation of the juror who has heard something so awful that laughter is the only neurological response that prevents screaming.

Herrera's laugh was none of these.

It was short — one syllable, expelled from somewhere lower than the chest. It was involuntary. And it contained, in its timbre, a quality I will attempt to describe because to leave it unrecorded would be a failure of my function: it was the sound of a man who has just seen the bars of his cage and the bars of his jailer's cage revealed to be the same bars.

"Looks like the ground doesn't like your court," he said.

I recorded the line. I recorded all of it. My fingers had not stopped.

---

Time passed. I know this because my watch continued to function — a Casio F-91W, digital, purchased for $12.99 at a Walmart in Las Cruces, the same model I have worn for fifteen years because it is accurate to within fifteen seconds per month, because its battery lasts seven years, and because no one has ever looked at a court reporter's wrist, which means I have never needed it to communicate anything other than time. The watch read 2:47 PM when we arrived, or I first looked at it, and it read 5:13 PM when the prosecutor completed what I will describe as her first survey. Two hours and twenty-six minutes.

The number felt honest and irrelevant simultaneously. There was no sun. The light from below did not change in intensity or hue. Nothing marked the passage of time except the watch and our bodies — thirst, which was mild; hunger, which was absent; fatigue, which was present but muted, as though the air itself, if it was air, provided some caloric substitute. I noted these details the way I note a judge's instructions to the jury: completely, sequentially, without assessment of their implications.

AUSA Patricia Leigh adapted.

Of course she did. I had watched this woman take a case that the Bureau had declared unprosecutable — seventeen witnesses dead or recanted, a chain of evidence compromised by a corrupt agent inside the DEA, a defendant with four prior acquittals and the legal resources of a mid-sized corporation — and, over fourteen months, reconstruct it from its foundations. I had watched her read six thousand pages of intercepted communications, learn enough forensic accounting to cross-examine a partner at Deloitte, and master the geographic and familial structure of a narcotics organization that spanned three Mexican states. She did not crumble. She was not built for crumbling. She was built for the application of organized intelligence to resistant problems, and this — whatever this was — was a resistant problem.

She walked the terrain.

She did it carefully, the way she did everything: systematically, with apparent calm, covering distance in a grid pattern that I recognized from the prosecution's own exhibits — the search methodology the FBI had used to process the mass grave site outside Juárez. She walked and she looked down and, when the membrane was translucent enough, she catalogued what lay beneath. She spoke aloud as she did this. Not to us. To herself, or perhaps to the habit of constructing arguments in real time, which for her was less a professional technique than a mode of cognition.

"The luminescent channels appear to follow a branching pattern consistent with biological neural networks — dendrite analog structures, possible axonal bundles at depth. The membrane responds to pressure — slight depression under full body weight, approximately two to three centimeters of give, elastic recovery within one second of step release. Temperature constant. Humidity constant or absent. Horizon curvature suggests a concave interior surface with a radius I can't estimate without instruments but which is — large. Very large."

She paused. She was standing perhaps forty meters from where Herrera and I remained. The ground beneath her held firm.

"There are regional variations. Here—" she knelt, pressed her palm flat — "the bioluminescence is denser. Amber-dominant. The neural structures below are closer to the surface. Over there—" gesturing toward an area to what I will call the north, though no compass direction applied — "the membrane is thicker, more opaque. Fewer visible structures. Possibly a difference in function. Cortical versus subcortical, if the analogy holds."

She stood and turned to face us, and her expression was one I had seen before: the expression she wore when she was about to present a theory of the case.

"We were taken. The mechanism is unknown, but the selectivity — three individuals from a room containing approximately forty — suggests intention. If there is intention, there is logic. If there is logic, it can be discerned. If it can be discerned, it can be navigated."

She paused, and I watched her construct the next sentence before she spoke it, the way I had watched her construct thousands of sentences: with the visible architecture of someone who understands that sequence is argument.

"The ground reacted when I spoke. Specifically, when I invoked jurisdiction. When I asserted custodial authority. That's data. It tells us the medium is responsive to some category of speech or thought, which means there are rules, and rules can be mapped."

She walked back toward us. The ground held. Her footsteps produced no sound.

"We map the rules," she said. "We find the pattern. We find the way back."
