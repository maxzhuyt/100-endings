# The Anatomy of Luck

---

## I.

The first thing I see is his hand—the right one—resting on the blanket like something that has been put down and forgotten. The knuckles are swollen into knobs. The skin has gone translucent in patches, showing the architecture beneath, the metacarpals pressing up against the surface the way rebar shows through old concrete. He is forty-seven years old. The hand is seventy, eighty. The hand has already left him.

There is a banner taped to the wall across from his bed, the kind of thing someone brings to a hospital to cheer a room the way you'd throw a cloth over stained furniture. It is gold and green—Clement State colors—and it reads *2011 NCAA FINAL FOUR* in block lettering that has faded to a shade of accusation. Beneath the banner, on the sill, someone has propped a framed photograph: the team at the buzzer, bodies mid-leap, Darius at the center with his arms raised, his face cracked open with the most unguarded joy I have ever seen on another human being. The photograph is from March 31, 2011. Darius in the photograph is twenty-two and infinite. Darius in the bed is forty-seven and finite, and the distance between those two men is the distance this story covers, and I have been walking it for thirteen years, and I am not yet at the end.

I pull the chair to his bedside. The vinyl squeaks. The heart monitor throws its green line across the screen in a rhythm I know better than my own: I have been reading this man's cardiac signature since we were fifteen and I pressed two fingers to his carotid in the locker room of Franklin Magnet High School after he ran sixteen wind sprints and wouldn't stop until someone stopped him. The rhythm now is wrong. It's not the wrongness of disease, exactly—though there is that. It is a wrongness of *sequence*. The beats come in clusters, three quick then a pause, then one long suspicious thud, as though his heart is trying to play a song it only partially remembers, a song in a time signature that doesn't exist.

"You're early," he says. His eyes are closed.

I look at my watch. I am not early. I am four minutes late, same as always, because I still stop at the vending machine in the lobby and stand in front of it without buying anything, using the time to practice the face I will wear into this room. The face of a friend and not the face of an athletic trainer who can read in the angle of Darius's jaw, in the gray crepey folds of his neck, in the faint convexity where the temporal bone is now too prominent beneath the skin, exactly how much further he has traveled since my last visit.

"Four minutes late, actually," I say.

"No," he says, still with his eyes closed. "Yesterday you came at two-twelve. Today you came at two-eight. You're early."

I did not visit yesterday. It is Tuesday. I visit Tuesdays and Saturdays. My last visit was Saturday. And yet I know—in the way I have come to know things about Darius, which is reluctantly and too late—that he is not confused. He is simply not in the same Tuesday I am.

His eyes open, and they track to a point above my left shoulder and hold there, following something across the room. I don't turn. There is nothing there. There has never been anything there, or rather: there is nothing there *now*, in the version of the room that I can access.

"Mallory will come in soon with the new IV bag," he says. "She'll drop the clamp. It'll be loud."

I write this down in the pocket notebook I carry, the one his neurologist doesn't know about, the one his cardiologist would not understand, the one that has become the record of what I should have started recording in 2011 and didn't. Thirty seconds later the door opens and a nurse named Mallory comes in carrying an IV bag. She reaches for the rolling clamp and it slips from her fingers and strikes the steel IV pole with a bright clatter, and Darius does not flinch, because Darius flinched twenty seconds before it happened, when his eyes were still closed.

I write that down too.

---

The notebook has 340 pages. I am on page 274. When it is full, I do not know what I will do—start another one, probably, or stop visiting, which is the same as stopping breathing and therefore not an option. The notebook is the third iteration: the first was a yellow legal pad I started in the spring of 2024 when the diagnosis came—*early-onset systemic degeneration*, they called it, *of unknown etiology*, which is medicine's way of saying *we see that he's dying and we can tell you the names of the organs that are failing but we cannot tell you why a forty-seven-year-old man has the vasculature of a chain-smoking octogenarian and the bone density of a woman twenty years post-menopause and the telomere length of someone who has simply run out of time*. The second was a composition book I filled over the summer, cataloging his physical presentation alongside everything I could find in the public record about the 2011 season: box scores, injury reports, game film, the ESPN retrospective that aired on the tenth anniversary and called it "the most improbable tournament run in modern history." The third—this pocket notebook—is the one where I am trying to do what I should have done thirteen years ago, which is to read the body of my closest friend with the same clinical precision I bring to every other athlete I have ever trained, and to follow the data where it leads, even when it leads somewhere that the language of sports medicine cannot reach.

Ask me about a stranger's rotator cuff, and I will tell you the truth within thirty seconds. Ask me about Darius Yoon's systematic self-immolation across four games in March 2011, and I will tell you that I saw everything and understood nothing, and that the understanding, when it came, came too late and too completely, and that it is the worst thing I know.

## II.

We met because our mothers worked the same shift at the Seabrook Linen mill on the north side of Redhaven, Connecticut—a city of 80,000 people with a minor-league baseball team and a Chrysler plant and the kind of public school system that produces either tradespeople or soldiers, depending on the economy. My mother was from Bridgeport. His mother was from Incheon. They spoke to each other in a pidgin of broken English, shared cigarettes, and swapped children on alternating Saturdays: Mrs. Yoon took me for piano lessons I was terrible at; my mother took Darius for errands that always ended at the Dairy Queen on Willow Street, which she knew he loved and would never ask for.

I have a memory of Darius at nine years old, standing in my mother's kitchen, eating a vanilla cone with a concentration that amounted to reverence. He was small for his age. His ears stuck out. His wrists were so thin that when he held the cone with both hands, you could see the ulnar styloid process on each side—those small bony knobs—pushing against the skin like doorknobs in a paper bag. I didn't know what the ulnar styloid process was called. I was nine. But I remember the wrists. I remember all of his body, from the beginning, the way some people remember the words to songs: not because I tried, but because the information entered me and never left.

He played basketball with a fury that had no relationship to his talent. That is not a cruelty—it is the most important thing about him. There are athletes whose bodies are arguments for predestination: the high school quarterback with the arm that throws the ball on a rope, the sprinter with the femur-to-tibia ratio you cannot train into existence. Darius was not one of them. He was five-eleven with a vertical leap of twenty-four inches—respectable, not remarkable. His shooting mechanics were unorthodox: a release point slightly behind the ear, a guide hand that drifted, a follow-through that broke to the right at the wrist in a way that any shooting coach would have corrected and that Darius, through ten thousand hours of repetition, had turned into a reliable if aesthetically heretical jumper. He was quick but not fast. He saw the floor well. He was, by any honest evaluation, a Division III talent playing Division I basketball through sheer, lunatic refusal to accept the verdict of his own physical dimensions.

When he was cut from the Franklin Magnet varsity as a sophomore, he came to my house and sat on the back steps and didn't speak for two hours. I sat with him. I had just started volunteering in the school's athletic training room—taping ankles, filling ice bags, learning the names of things—and I understood, even then, that I would never be on the court myself, that my body was a spectator's body, built for watching and measuring and cataloging but not for the luminous, self-forgetting act of play. I don't think I resented this. I think I was grateful for it, because it gave me an excuse to look at Darius the way I needed to look at him, which was closely.

He made the team the next year. He made it by outworking every player in the program, including the seniors, including the kid who'd go on to play at UConn. The coach, a man named Wyckoff who later sold insurance, told me once that Darius was the hardest-working player he'd ever had and that it would never be enough. "The kid's ceiling is the gym," he said. "He'll be the best player in the building and the building will always be too small."

No scholarship offers. A walk-on spot at Clement State, a mid-major program in the Colonial Athletic Association that had not been to the NCAA tournament since 1994 and whose gymnasium seated 4,200 and was usually a third full. I enrolled there too. I was in the kinesiology program by then, already building the skill set that would become my career, and when I applied for the student athletic trainer position, I told the head trainer, Maurice Pugh, that I wanted to work with basketball. Maurice asked me if I had experience. I said I'd been studying one player for seven years.

Those early years at Clement State are the years I return to when I want to remember Darius without the stain of 2011, though even that is complicated, because the body I memorized in those years—the body I loved in the way a trainer loves, which is to say, through the disciplined accumulation of its data—was the same body that would later burn itself up, and every clean memory is also a baseline measurement against which I now chart the deviation.

I would watch him from the training room doorway during practice. I was supposed to be watching everyone—monitoring for compensatory movement patterns, for the slight hitch in a stride that signals a stress reaction, for the asymmetric landing that precedes an ACL tear—but my eye always went to Darius first, and it stayed there longest, and it knew things about him that it did not know about anyone else. I knew that when he was tired, his left foot began to supinate slightly on his plant step. I knew that his hip flexors tightened before his hamstrings did, which meant his fatigue pattern started in the core and migrated outward, which meant that when he looked fresh to a coach, I could see, in the almost imperceptible forward tilt of his pelvis, that he was already twenty minutes past his optimal recovery point. I knew the sound of his breathing at different effort levels: the easy nasal rhythm of warm-ups, the controlled oral draw of half-court sets, the ragged, wet sound of the fourth quarter when he had given everything and was now giving something beneath everything, some reserve that existed past physiology, in a territory I could map but never name.

He sat with me in the training room after practices, icing his knees on the metal table while I filled out charts. We talked about nothing. We talked about our mothers. We talked about the Chrysler plant closing and what that would do to Redhaven whenever we graduated. We did not talk about what we were, which was two people from a place that produced neither artists nor athletes performing, in our respective minor keys, the impossible: Darius by playing Division I basketball in a body that should have peaked in a church league, and me by loving him with a precision that masqueraded as professionalism.

He played. I watched. He risked. I repaired. That was the structure. It held for years. It holds in some ruined form to this day, with me in the vinyl chair and him in the hospital bed, except now the repairs are beyond me, and the only watching left is the kind I do in the notebook—the slow, retrospective watching that is not observation but autopsy.

## III.

November 22, 2010. An early-season conference game against Hofstra. I remember the date because I had written it on a roll of half-inch athletic tape and stuck it to the wall of the training room as a reminder to order new ultrasound gel, and after what happened, the date affixed itself to my memory the way a burr affixes itself to a sock: not because you chose it, but because you brushed against it at the wrong angle and now it will not let go.

The game was meaningless. Clement State was 3-2. Hofstra was 2-3. The gymnasium was perhaps a third full, if you counted the parents and the band. Darius was averaging eleven points and four assists, which was good for a walk-on who'd earned a scholarship after his redshirt year but not good enough to attract any attention beyond the local beat writer, a man named Fenstermacher who covered Clement State athletics with the weary dedication of someone assigned to document a glacier.

Seven minutes into the second half, Darius drove the lane from the left wing. He was in his usual mode—controlled, mechanical, expending the minimum energy to execute the maximum result, because his body could not afford waste the way a more gifted body could. The Hofstra center, a six-ten kid from Long Island whose name I have discarded, stepped into the lane to draw the charge. Darius's left foot came down on the center's shoe. I saw the ankle invert—I *saw it*—the lateral ligaments stretching past their elastic limit, the talus shifting in the mortise joint, and I was already rising from the bench, already reaching for the splint bag, already running the protocol in my head (RICE, X-ray, six to eight weeks), and then—

And then the ankle was fine.

Not *better*. Not *recovered.* Fine in the way that it had never happened. Darius was past the center, laying the ball off the glass, landing on two feet with the kind of soft, balanced follow-through I had spent years teaching him. The center was still standing where he had been, but now he was a half-step to the left, and the angle was different, and the foot had not come down on the shoe, and the sequence that I had watched frame by frame with the trained eye of someone who has seen three hundred ankle injuries was simply not in the world anymore.

I sat back down. My hands were shaking. I looked at the assistant trainer, DeAnna, who was sitting next to me. She was filling out a hydration log. She had not moved. She had not seen anything, because there had been, as far as the world was concerned, nothing to see.

But I had started to rise. My body had responded to the injury before my conscious mind had time to override it, and now I was sitting with the residue of an emergency in my nervous system and no emergency to attach it to. My pulse was elevated. My right hand was gripping the splint bag. I told myself I had imagined it—an occupational hallucination, the trainer's equivalent of a phantom vibration in your pocket.
