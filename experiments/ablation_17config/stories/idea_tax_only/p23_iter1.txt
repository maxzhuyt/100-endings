# The California Grand Opera Rush of 1849: A Corrective History

## Being a Revised Account of the Berlioni Expedition, the Founding of the Mesa Lyrica Settlement, and the Cultural Consequences Thereof

### By Dr. Ellsworth Trant, Kellogg Chair of Acoustic Geography, University of the Pacific (Revised Third Edition, 1987)

---

It is no longer necessary, one hopes, to rehearse the basic facts of the California Grand Opera Rush for an educated readership, and yet the persistence of certain populist mythologies — chief among them the notion that the westward migration of 1849 was motivated primarily by the discovery of gold at Sutter's Mill¹ — obliges the responsible historian to state, once more, what the documentary record has long made plain. The great migration was, in its overwhelming majority, an acoustic migration. The Department of the Interior's own survey data, compiled under the auspices of the Lyricism Enabling Act of 1848, confirm that between February of that year and the close of 1850, an estimated forty-seven thousand Americans traveled west of the Mississippi for the explicit purpose of staking territorial claims on sites of superior natural resonance: box canyons whose sandstone walls returned a human voice at intervals of 1.4 to 2.1 seconds, cliff formations whose geometry permitted sightlines for audiences of three hundred or more without architectural intervention, and, perhaps most coveted of all, the dry riverbeds of the interior Southwest, whose polished stone floors were found to conduct vibration with a fidelity that one surveyor's report described, in language that has since become canonical, as "the acoustic equivalent of standing inside a Stradivarius."²

The causes of the Rush have been thoroughly treated elsewhere and need not detain us.³ It is sufficient to note that by the winter of 1848, the eastern seaboard's opera infrastructure had reached a state of crisis that most cultural economists now regard as structurally inevitable. The construction boom of the 1830s had saturated every major city with competing houses — New York alone supported fourteen permanent companies by 1845 — while a series of catastrophic fires, the chronic instability of private patronage, and what the *Philadelphia Musical Gazette* termed "a surplus of ambition unmatched by any corresponding surplus of talent" had created conditions in which an entire generation of performers, impresarios, set designers, librettists, and their associated dependents found themselves economically displaced.⁴ When the first surveyor reports from the western territories began circulating in the autumn of 1848 — describing landscapes of almost unimaginable acoustic potential, free for the claiming, requiring no construction, no municipal permits, no appeasement of subscription boards — the migration became, in retrospect, less a matter of collective madness than of collective economic rationality.

The routes west were well established by the time of the events this account concerns. The principal overland trails had been mapped, if imperfectly, by advance parties from the Metropolitan Choral Expeditionary Company (founded 1847, dissolved 1851, following the Massacre at Timpanogos⁵), and a rudimentary system of territorial governance had begun to emerge along the major corridors. Stagecoach lines serviced several of the more accessible claim sites. A number of frontier towns — some mere encampments of canvas and ambition — had organized themselves around the provisioning of westbound companies, offering repair services for damaged instruments, emergency costume alterations, and, in at least one documented case, an on-site dramaturg.⁶

It is also necessary to acknowledge, as any responsible account must, that the westward expansion of operatic settlement was attended by the full measure of violence, displacement, and territorial conflict that characterized American expansion generally. The border skirmishes between rival companies are well documented; the appropriation of indigenous acoustic territories is a matter of extensive and damning record; and the so-called Turnout Wars of 1849, in which competing ballet militias fought pitched engagements along the Arkansas River over adjacent canyon claims, produced casualty figures that the U.S. Army's own reports describe as "unlikely to be exaggerated, despite their apparent implausibility."⁷ The grand cultural ambitions of the Rush were inseparable from its brutalities. This account does not seek to romanticize either.

What this account *does* seek is to correct the historical record on a narrower question — the question of the Berlioni Expedition of 1849–51, its catastrophic westward trajectory, and its unintended founding of what is now recognized as the most significant permanent operatic settlement west of the hundredth meridian. The standard accounts⁸ have treated the Berlioni Expedition as a curiosity — an eccentric footnote to the larger Rush, interesting primarily for its failures. This is, as the present author has argued at length in prior publications,⁹ a misreading so thoroughgoing as to constitute a kind of scholarly malpractice. The Berlioni Expedition was not a footnote. It was, in its accidental and catastrophic way, the event that transformed the Grand Opera Rush from a speculative land grab into a civilization.

But we are getting ahead of ourselves. We must begin, as the evidence demands, with Echo Mesa.

## I. Echo Mesa

The legend of Echo Mesa predates the Rush itself by at least a decade and possibly much longer, depending on which sources one is willing to credit. The earliest written reference appears in the 1836 journal of Corporal Amos Leach, a topographical surveyor attached to a U.S. Army mapping expedition in the Territory of New Mexico, who recorded the following entry on September 9 of that year:

> *Passed today through a canyon of unusual character. The walls rose some two hundred feet on either side, red sandstone, curved inward at the top like the walls of a great hall. A man in the party, Pvt. Durrance, began singing — some hymn or another, I do not know it — and the sound that returned to us was not the sound he had made. It was better. It was the sound he had meant to make. I cannot explain this more precisely. Durrance is not a good singer. For approximately nine seconds he was.*

Leach's account circulated in manuscript form for several years before being published in a small St. Louis periodical in 1841, where it was read by, among others, the Italian-born vocal instructor Giacomo Ferri, who included a discussion of its acoustic implications in his *Treatise on Natural Resonance and Its Applications to the Trained Voice* (1843).¹⁰ Ferri calculated, based on Leach's description of the canyon's dimensions and curvature, that the formation — if it existed as described — would produce a natural reverb with a decay time and harmonic enrichment profile "superior to any constructed performance space in the known world, including the Teatro di San Carlo, and I do not make this comparison lightly."

The problem, as Ferri himself acknowledged, was that Leach's journal provided no reliable coordinates. The canyon was encountered during a period of navigational confusion — the expedition had been lost for three days — and Leach's positional estimates were, by his own admission, "approximate at best." Subsequent expeditions sent specifically to locate the formation produced contradictory results. A survey party in 1844 reported finding a canyon matching Leach's physical description but noted that its acoustic properties were "ordinary." A second expedition in 1846 found no canyon at all but reported that their Comanche guides spoke of a formation called, in translation, "the place where the rock listens," which the guides refused to approach.¹¹ A daguerreotype purporting to show the mesa above the canyon — a flat-topped sandstone formation of distinctive reddish hue — surfaced in Santa Fe in 1847 and was reproduced in several Eastern publications. It has since been identified, almost certainly, as a manipulated image of Acoma Pueblo.¹²

By the time of the Rush, Echo Mesa had achieved a status in the acoustic prospecting community that was somewhere between geological hypothesis and holy grail. Most serious surveyors regarded it as probable that *some* formation of unusual acoustic properties existed in the general region described by Leach; whether this formation matched the increasingly extravagant descriptions that circulated in prospectors' camps was another matter. Echo Mesa had, as legends do, accumulated properties that no single physical site could plausibly possess. By 1848 it was said to amplify a whisper to the volume of a shout, to add harmonic overtones to any voice that entered its confines, to correct pitch, to extend breath, and — in one account that even the most credulous commentators have dismissed — to spontaneously generate orchestral accompaniment from the movement of wind through its stone apertures.¹³

The sober position, then and now, was that Echo Mesa was a real place that could not possibly be the place it had become.

Augusto "The Throat" Berlioni was not a man who trafficked in sober positions.

## II. Berlioni

Augusto Berlioni was born in Naples in 1811, the third son of a cobbler whose other two sons became, respectively, a cobbler and a fisherman who drowned.¹⁴ The family was not musical. Berlioni's own account of his artistic awakening — offered in several versions across decades of correspondence and public statements, no two of which agree on any material particular — centered on a childhood visit to the Teatro di San Carlo, where he claimed to have seen a performance of Rossini's *Mosè in Egitto* at the age of seven. "The Red Sea parted on that stage," Berlioni wrote in 1854, "and something parted in me that never closed again."¹⁵ Whether the visit actually occurred is unverifiable; what is certain is that by nineteen Berlioni had talked, bullied, or charmed his way into the Conservatorio di San Pietro a Majella, where his instructors found his voice to be, in the words of his principal teacher, "an instrument of enormous power deployed with the subtlety of a man beating a carpet."¹⁶

The Naples years produced a baritone of genuine if unrefined ability. Berlioni's range was remarkable: a low G that reviewers compared to "the sound of a cathedral settling into its foundations,"¹⁷ and a top F that he reached with visible physical effort but, by most accounts, reached. His deficiency was not in the voice itself but in his relationship to it. He sang, always, as though the room were larger than the room was. In intimate venues his performances were overwhelming, and not as a compliment. The Neapolitan critic Marcello Sala, reviewing Berlioni's 1836 *Don Giovanni* at the Teatro Nuovo, composed what would become the most frequently cited assessment of the man's instrument: "Signor Berlioni possesses a voice of architectural ambition that exceeds its acoustic foundations. He sings as though the walls should be further away. One leaves the theater feeling that the fault lies with the building."¹⁸
