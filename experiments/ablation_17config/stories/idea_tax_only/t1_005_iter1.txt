# BugOut Solutions

## I.

The fluorescent tube above the breakroom doorway has been flickering since March. Not the whole tube — just the left end, where the cathode is dying, throwing a little seizure of blue-white light every nine seconds or so. I've counted. There's not much I haven't counted.

Monday mornings at BugOut Solutions begin with the parking lot. I can see the whole thing from my desk — fourteen striped spaces, though the farthest three are technically shared with the podiatry office next door, a fact Phil has been quietly disputing for six years. The gravel section near the dumpster is ours. The asphalt is joint-use. Phil had a laminated map made. It's in the second drawer of the filing cabinet behind me, under a stack of time-off request forms no one has touched since we went digital, which we did the year I started, which was the year Phil started, which was eleven years ago last October.

Phil Krennic's sedan — a 2017 Hyundai Sonata, pewter, with a dent in the passenger door he told his wife was from a shopping cart but was actually from the night he sat in the Arby's parking lot and opened his door into a bollard while crying about his daughter — pulled into the lot at 7:47. It is always 7:47. On mornings after he's slept badly, he sits in the car for up to four minutes, gripping the steering wheel at ten and two, and whispers something to himself. This morning it was: *You're the guy. You're the guy they need.* He said it twice. Then he checked his teeth in the rearview, found nothing wrong, adjusted his BugOut polo where it pulled across the belly, and walked in.

"Morning, Darlene!"

"Morning, Phil."

"How's the queen of the operation? How's the backbone of this whole—"

"I'm fine, Phil. Thank you."

He set a coffee on my desk. I hadn't asked for it. He does this on Mondays when his weekend has gone a certain way — when the house has been too quiet, or when he's driven past the Wendy's on Calloway where his daughter used to work before she moved to Portland and stopped returning calls. The coffee was from the gas station on Decatur. Cream, two sugars. He's never asked how I take my coffee. I take it black. I drank it anyway.

"BugOut Solutions, how can I help you?"

The caller wanted to know if we serviced the east side of the county. We do. I told her about our residential packages, gave her the Tuesday callback number, and watched through the front window as Gary Meacham's truck swung into the lot — a red F-150, mud on the running boards, the CD case on his passenger seat still holding a Garth Brooks album his son had given him the Christmas before the accident, though Gary hasn't played it in two years because the disc is scratched in a way that makes it skip at the exact part of "The Dance" that was played at the service. Gary parked. Sat for eleven minutes. Stared at the sun visor where the photo is clipped. Then he came in, nodded at me, poured breakroom coffee into the mug that says WORLD'S OKAYEST EXTERMINATOR — a gift from the rest of the team two birthdays ago — and went to his desk in the back.

By 8:15, they were all in.

Gary Meacham, senior field tech, fifty-three, who smells like Lava soap and cedar chips and whose left knee predicts rain with an accuracy the National Weather Service should envy.

Sandra Ojeda, sales, twenty-nine, whose purse — currently stashed in the supply closet on the third shelf behind the toner cartridges — contains, among other things, a lipstick in a shade called Brick Dust, a Narcotics Anonymous newcomer chip she received the day before Thanksgiving, and a photograph of her mother standing in front of a house in Guanajuato that no longer exists.

Kevin Park, accounts and billing, forty-one, who eats the same lunch every day (turkey on wheat, yellow mustard, a single clementine) and who has been writing a novel on his phone during bathroom breaks for three years. The novel is about a man who works in a small office. He doesn't see the humor. It's actually quite beautiful in places.

Tamara Wells, scheduling coordinator, thirty-six, who hums without knowing she's humming — always the same song, a hymn her grandmother taught her — and whose hands shake slightly before ten a.m. because she has stopped taking her Lexapro without telling her doctor, because the prescription costs what it costs, and Tamara's definition of "fine" has expanded to include a great deal of trembling.

Marcus Reed, field tech, twenty-five, the newest hire, who still tucks his polo into his khakis with the earnest precision of someone who hasn't learned that nobody here cares, and who last night dreamed he showed up to work naked and no one noticed because no one ever notices Marcus, which is a dream about something other than nudity.

And Jolene Avery, assistant manager, forty-eight, who Phil calls his "right hand" and who has been quietly doing sixty percent of Phil's job for four years while Phil does the remaining forty with the decibel level of a man doing a hundred. Jolene keeps a cardigan on the back of her chair for when the AC leaks cold. She arrives at 7:30, before Phil, and leaves at 5:45, after him. She knows the names of every client's pet. She is, in the structural sense, the person holding BugOut Solutions together with two hands and a cardigan, and she is invisible in the way that competence is always invisible inside institutions that reward enthusiasm.

Phil stood in the breakroom doorway at 8:20, coffee raised.

"Monday, Monday! Who's ready to make some bugs regret their life choices?"

No one answered. Everyone smiled. The fluorescent tube above him flickered at its nine-second interval, and for a moment, his face was lit like a strobe photograph — all teeth and sweat and need.

The phone rang.

"BugOut Solutions, how can I help you?"

Termite inspection, residential, the Greenbriar subdivision. I pulled up the scheduling software. Behind me, through the breakroom doorway, I could hear Phil explaining his new idea for a client appreciation initiative. Tamara hummed. The microwave beeped — someone had reheated tilapia, and in thirty seconds the smell would colonize the entire western half of the office, and Gary would open the window by the copy machine even though it hasn't latched properly since July, and the afternoon would carry the faint overlay of fish and parking-lot heat.

I booked the appointment. I hung up. I replaced the handset in the cradle, which has a hairline crack I cover with a strip of Scotch tape every few weeks when the old strip yellows.

This is the desk. This is the view. This is what I see.

All of it.

## II.

The email arrived at 10:23 on a Wednesday.

I know this because I know everything that happens on the other side of Phil's glass wall, even when I shouldn't — even when his screen faces away from me and his voice drops to the mumble he uses for things he doesn't want overheard. The email was from Cynthia Rourke in corporate's regional oversight division, a woman Phil has spoken to exactly four times and who makes him sweat through his polo each time. The subject line was: **URGENT — Data Irregularity — BugOut Solutions Acct #4471.**

Phil read it once. He read it again. His blood pressure, which was 148 over 92 at his last physical — the one Dr. Nassif told him to follow up on but Phil filed under "deal with later," a drawer that is functionally infinite — rose by what I can only describe as a visible amount. The sweat stain on his back, which normally reaches mid-scapula by noon, arrived early and ambitious, darkening the fabric between his shoulder blades like a Rorschach test he was failing.

Client data. Route schedules, service histories, chemical application logs, the proprietary treatment protocols Phil himself had developed for the palmetto bug problem in the Terrace Heights accounts. Leaking. Systematically. To PestPro Alliance, the competitor three towns over whose owner, Dale Muncy, Phil had once called "a tick on the hindquarters of this industry" at a Chamber of Commerce mixer.

He stood up. He paced. Three lengths of the office, then four, then he stopped at the window and pressed his forehead against the glass the way he does when he's trying to look contemplative but is actually trying not to vomit. From my desk, I could see the fog his breath made on the pane.

Then he did something he has never done.

He closed the blinds.

In eleven years — through quarterly reviews, budget calls, two sexual harassment trainings, and the afternoon Marcus accidentally discharged a canister of residual pyrethroid in the hallway and everyone evacuated except Phil, who just opened his door wider to "let it breathe" — Phil Krennic has never closed his office blinds. The blinds are, to Phil, a symbol. His door, too. "Open-door policy," he says at every staff meeting, pointing at the literal door. "That's not a metaphor, people. Well, it is a metaphor. But it's also a door. And it's open."

The blinds went down with a clatter. The office, which had been steady in its midmorning rhythm of keystrokes and phone murmurs and Tamara's humming, went quiet in the way a room goes quiet when an animal senses a shift in barometric pressure.

Kevin looked up from his desk. Sandra's fingers paused over her keyboard. Jolene, who had been reviewing the week's service tickets at the workstation nearest Phil's office, turned her head half an inch — just enough to register the anomaly, not enough to be caught registering it.

I answered the phone.

"BugOut Solutions, how can I help you?"

A woman on Sycamore Lane had carpenter ants in her mailbox post. I took her information. I scheduled a Tuesday assessment. I replaced the handset.

Phil was in his office for forty-seven minutes. When he came out, the blinds stayed down. His face was the color of rare steak. His eyes had the glazed, determined shine of a man who has just made a decision that will cost him everything but feels, in this moment, like bravery.

He walked to my desk. He leaned on the counter the way he does when he wants to seem casual — forearms flat, one ankle crossed behind the other — and said:

"Darlene, I'm going to handle something in-house. Just want you to know."

"Okay, Phil."

"It's a sensitive matter. I don't want to worry the team."

"Okay."

"Corporate flagged a thing. A data thing. I'm going to look into it personally. Quietly."

"All right."

"I know these people, Darlene. I *hired* these people."

He looked at the breakroom, where the staff photo from last year's holiday party was thumbtacked to the corkboard — all seven of them in Santa hats Phil had distributed, everyone smiling the way people smile when they know the photo is almost over. Phil had positioned himself in the center of the group with his arms around Gary and Jolene, a posture of ownership he believed was a posture of love. In the photo, Gary's smile doesn't reach his eyes — it hasn't in two years — and Jolene is already half-turning toward the door, and Sandra is gripping the hem of her Santa hat the way she grips things when she wants a drink, which she will not have, which she has not had in four hundred and eleven days, but the wanting is a physical thing that lives in her hands.

Phil studied the photo.

"I know who they are," he said. "I'll figure this out."

He did not call Cynthia Rourke back. He did not email corporate security. He did not consult Jolene, who would have told him, gently and precisely, the four correct steps to take. Instead, he walked into the breakroom, stood in front of the seating chart he'd made for the monthly team lunches — a laminated grid with names in dry-erase marker, because Phil believes in the power of shared meals and assigned conversations — and began rearranging it, swapping names, staring at the grid with the intensity of a man decoding an ancient text.

From my desk, I watched him erase Marcus's name from between Tamara and Kevin and write it next to his own. He underlined it. Then he erased the underline. Then he drew it again.

The parking lot was bright and still. A cloud shaped like nothing crossed the sun and was gone. In his truck, still parked in the third space from the left, Gary's Garth Brooks CD sat in its cracked case and held its silence.

I understood, the way you understand weather — not with surprise but with the dull, encompassing knowledge of something arriving precisely on time — that Phil was going to break everything he had built, and that he would do it with both hands, and that his hands would be shaking, and that he would believe, up to the very last moment, that he was holding it together.

The phone rang.

"BugOut Solutions, how can I help you?"

## III.

He ordered nine pizzas for seven people.

This was the first sign. Phil's generosity has always been calibrated to excess — he brings donuts in dozens when a half-dozen would do, orders enough paper plates for a revival meeting — but nine pizzas for seven people represented something beyond his usual arithmetic of love. Nine pizzas was a man trying to build a fortress out of mozzarella. Nine pizzas was a man who believed, with the fervor of the recently terrified, that if he could just get everyone into the same room with enough food, the room itself would yield a confession.

He also made a playlist. I know this because I could hear it from my desk when he tested the Bluetooth speaker at 11:15 — a sequence of songs so aggressively innocuous they formed a kind of auditory wallpaper paste: "Walking on Sunshine," "Don't Stop Believin'," "Three Little Birds." He had printed a trivia sheet. Twenty questions. I could read it from my chair when he set it on the breakroom table: *What year was BugOut Solutions founded? What is our company mascot's name? (Answer: Buzz.) Who holds the record for most client callbacks in a single quarter?* The answer to the last one was Jolene, and the answer had been Jolene for three consecutive years, but the trivia sheet listed it as a question, which meant Phil wanted to say her name aloud in a context of celebration, which was the closest Phil could get to saying *thank you* without the machinery of sincerity overwhelming his capacity for speech.

They gathered at noon. From my desk, through the breakroom doorway, I could see the entire room: the folding table pushed against the wall, the stacked pizza boxes forming a cardboard skyline, the paper plates Phil had fanned out in an arrangement he clearly thought was festive. He had tucked a napkin into his collar. No one else had. He didn't seem to notice.

"All right, BugOut family! Grab a slice, grab a seat, and let's have some *fun*."

Tamara sat nearest the door, as she always does, because Tamara likes to know where the exit is in every room — a preference she traces to a night in her early twenties she does not discuss, and which I will not discuss either, except to say that the body remembers what the mind agrees to forget, and Tamara's body remembers the doorway of a room she left running. She took a slice of cheese pizza. Her hands shook. She put them in her lap.

Gary took pepperoni and sat heavily, his bad knee extended under the table like a log. Sandra sipped a Diet Coke and studied the trivia sheet with the expression of a student who hasn't done the reading. Kevin ate his turkey-on-wheat from home, the pizza untouched in front of him, loyal to routine in the way that only the deeply anxious are. Jolene took a slice she wouldn't finish and sat at the far end, already reviewing something on her phone — service tickets, probably, or the supply order she'd been meaning to submit, or the text from her sister about Thanksgiving that she'd been avoiding because Jolene's family is the kind of family that uses holidays as a delivery mechanism for grievances.

Marcus tucked his napkin into his collar, mirroring Phil, and when he realized no one else had, he quietly removed it and placed it on his knee.
