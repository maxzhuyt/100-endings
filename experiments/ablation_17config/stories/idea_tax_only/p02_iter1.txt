# The Mountain Does Not Care

## I.

Bryce Kirchner pressed "Confirm Booking" at 2:47 a.m. Pacific time, sitting on the floor of a studio apartment in Mar Vista that he rented for $3,200 a month and could no longer afford. The apartment had been leased during what he still referred to, on-air, as the "acceleration phase"—the eleven-week window in late 2021 when his podcast, *Unregulated with Bryce*, had briefly cracked the top fifty on Spotify's investing charts and a company called NovaMesh had paid him $40,000 a month to discuss the revolutionary potential of blockchain-based dental insurance. NovaMesh had since been indicted. Bryce had not been indicted, technically, though the phrase "unindicted co-narrator" had appeared in a Vice article that his mother had texted him without comment.

The retreat was called "Last Light Lodge." It was not a lodge. The website made this clear in a way that Bryce interpreted as branding rather than warning: *No structures. No electricity. No cell service. No other guests. You and the land, for seven days, learning what you're made of.* The cost was $4,500, which Bryce put on a credit card that had been declined twice at a Chevron that same week. The card went through. He chose to interpret this as the universe granting permission.

His plan was content. Pure, undeniable, algorithmic content. He would record a solo survival arc—"Seven Days Unregulated: Off the Grid, Off the Chain"—and release it as a limited series that would remind his remaining 1,200 Patreon subscribers (down from 19,000) that Bryce Kirchner was not a man who promoted fraudulent dental blockchain. Bryce Kirchner was a man who went into the Alaskan wilderness alone and came back harder. The narrative would be redemptive. The B-roll would be cinematic. He had purchased a waterproof microphone and a solar-powered battery pack. He had not purchased appropriate footwear.

He did not think about the last time he'd been in the same room with four specific people. He had trained himself, over twelve years, not to think about it. The training was imperfect. Some nights—cold nights, nights when the radiator in the Mar Vista apartment ticked in a rhythm that resembled, if you were not careful, the specific cadence of something he would not name—the training failed entirely, and he would lie on his back and stare at the ceiling and feel the old heat on the left side of his face, which was, his therapist had once told him, a somatic memory, which meant his body remembered what his mind had been wise enough to refuse.

He closed the laptop. He did not sleep. In the morning he began packing with the frantic, gleaming energy of a man who has located, at last, the exit.

Tessa Nakamura-Walsh received the targeted ad while lying in a bathtub filled with high-pH alkaline water that she had infused with crushed rose quartz and a proprietary blend of adaptogenic mushrooms. The water was the color of dishwater. She was not relaxed. She had not been relaxed since March, when the FDA had sent a letter—not a warning, not a citation, but a *letter*, which her attorney described as "the federal government clearing its throat before it begins to shout"—regarding certain claims made on the packaging of her flagship product, AquaVera Glacial Essence, a $38 bottle of water that Tessa's company described as "raw, unfiltered, and alive with the mineral intelligence of the earth."

The FDA's position was that water did not possess intelligence. Tessa's position was that the FDA had been captured by the municipal water lobby. Neither position was entirely wrong, but only one of them had subpoena power.

The ad—"Last Light Lodge: Seven Days of Radical Solitude in Untouched Alaska"—appeared between an Instagram story about cortisol and a sponsored post for a magnesium spray. Tessa clicked it with her wet thumb. She read the description three times. *No other guests.* The phrase sang to her. She had spent the last eleven months surrounded by attorneys, brand managers, crisis consultants, and a rotating cast of former followers who had taken to posting videos of themselves pouring AquaVera into toilets with the hashtag #RawSewage. What she needed was water that had never been touched by litigation. Glacial runoff. Snowmelt. The last honest water on earth.

She booked. She did not scroll down to the section of the website that explained, in small type, that the retreat accommodated groups of up to six. She would not have cared. The number six was, in her cosmology, a healing number—the number of water's molecular resonance, the number of points on a snowflake, the number of people who had been at that dinner in 2011 minus the one who had done the thing she did not talk about anymore, which left five, which was not six, which was why the memory was dissonant and needed to be released through breathwork and glacial immersion.

She drained the bathtub. The rose quartz collected in the drain like small, pink teeth.

Gerald Katz, J.D.—formerly Gerald Katz, J.D., LL.M. (Admiralty), partner (nonequity, then equity, then non- again, then severed) at Morley, Pratt & Levinson—found the listing on a legal forum. Not a travel forum. Not an adventure forum. A legal forum, specifically a subforum for disbarred attorneys that Gerald visited nightly the way a man with a phantom limb visits the place where his hand used to be.

Someone with the username DeadwoodEsq had posted: *Anyone else just need to go somewhere there are literally no laws? Like physically no jurisdiction? I found this place in Alaska.* And then a link.

Gerald clicked. He read the description with the close, annotative attention that had once made him the most feared admiralty litigator on the Eastern Seaboard—a title that sounded impressive until you learned how small the Eastern Seaboard admiralty bar actually was. The words *remote*, *unincorporated*, *outside municipal boundaries* lit up in his mind like case citations. He had spent twenty-three years in maritime law, which was—and Gerald would explain this to anyone who made the mistake of asking—not the same as regular law. Maritime law existed in the spaces between jurisdictions, in the gray water where federal and state and international authority blurred and overlapped and, sometimes, disappeared entirely. Gerald had lived in that blur. He had thrived in it. He had also, unfortunately, committed several acts within it that the Alaska Bar Association, the Oregon Bar Association, and ultimately the American Bar Association had determined constituted malpractice, fraud, and "a fundamental misunderstanding of the fiduciary relationship so profound as to approach the philosophical."

He had been disbarred in four states. He had not been disbarred in Alaska because he had never been barred there to begin with, a fact he clung to with the intensity of a man gripping a piece of wreckage in open water.

What Gerald wanted—what he had been unable to articulate to his court-appointed therapist, his ex-wife, or the disciplinary board—was a *reset*. Not a legal reset; he understood, intellectually, that disbarment was permanent. But a *jurisdictional* reset. A place so far from any court, any bar association, any precedent, that the very concepts of guilt and professional misconduct would lose their meaning. He believed, in the way that only a man trained in the law can believe something legally incoherent, that Alaska would restore him. That the wilderness operated under its own admiralty code. That if he went far enough from civilization, he would find himself back in the gray water where he had once been not guilty but something better: *pre-prosecuted.*

He booked. He packed a single suitcase containing five days of thermal underlayers, a waterproof copy of *Bowditch's American Practical Navigator*, and a laminated card listing the elements of adverse possession in all fifty states.

He did not think about the restraining order he had filed in November 2011. He did not think about why he had filed it, or against whom, or whether it had been, as someone had screamed at him through a closed door, "the most cowardly thing a person could do with a law degree." He thought, instead, about jurisdiction. He thought about the clean, blank space beyond the reach of courts.

The suitcase closed with a click that sounded, to Gerald, like an acquittal.

Margaux Olejnik had not tasted anything in seven weeks. Not "not tasted anything interesting." Not "experienced a diminished palate." Had not tasted. Had placed a 2016 Domaine de la Romanée-Conti on her tongue—a wine she had once described, in print, in *Wine Spectator*, as "the liquid memory of a Burgundian hillside at the moment the monks began to sing"—and experienced nothing. Absence. The gustatory equivalent of a dial tone.

She had tasted her way through three decades. She had been the youngest woman to pass the Master Sommelier examination on the first attempt. She had earned, and then lost, a Michelin star—not as a chef but as the sommelier whose pairings were so precise, so architecturally essential to the dining experience, that the *Guide* had cited her by name as the reason for the star, and then, when she left the restaurant in a dispute over whether to carry natural wines, had removed the star in the same sentence. She had been the palate. She had been the tongue.

And then, seven weeks ago, the tongue went dark. Her doctor called it ageusia and said it sometimes followed viral infections. Margaux had not had a viral infection. Her therapist suggested it was psychosomatic, a manifestation of grief or stress. Margaux had fired the therapist. Her own theory, which she had not shared with anyone, was that her palate had not been killed by a virus or by stress but by something older—a memory that had been living in the back of her mouth for twelve years, tainting everything, and that had finally, patiently, swallowed all remaining flavor.

She had read, in a forum for sommeliers with sensory disorders, that extreme cold could shock the olfactory and gustatory systems back to function. Something about the nerve pathways. Something about the cold stripping away accumulated sensory noise and leaving the receptors raw, newborn, ready to receive. The science was dubious. But Margaux was a sommelier, and sommeliers had always lived in the disputed territory between science and faith, between chemistry and the unquantifiable thing that happened when a great wine met a ready tongue.

She found the retreat through a travel site. She booked it in the same tone of voice with which she had once ordered a case of 1961 Pétrus for a private client—calm, certain, and leveraged beyond all rational limit.

She packed no wine. For the first time in thirty years, she traveled with no bottles, no tasting notes, no stemware. She packed thermal clothing and a single notebook with blank pages. If the cold worked—if the Alaskan air scoured her palate clean—she would need something to write on when the flavors came back. If it did not work, the blank pages would be appropriate.

On the flight from JFK to Anchorage, she sat in seat 14C and drank the complimentary ginger ale. She tasted nothing. Beside her, a man ate a bag of pretzels with the mechanical contentment of a person for whom taste was an afterthought rather than an identity, and Margaux envied him so deeply that she had to close her eyes, and behind her eyes, uninvited, was a kitchen in Connecticut, and a smell—not a taste but a smell, because smells, it turned out, survived when tastes did not—of peanut oil at 375 degrees, and she opened her eyes immediately and watched the wing of the plane cut through clouds that looked, in the late-afternoon light, like nothing. They looked like nothing at all.

The man who booked the fifth reservation did so from a public library in Bend, Oregon, using a guest computer and a prepaid Visa card purchased at a Fred Meyer. He entered his name as Keith. The booking form required a last name. He typed "Now." The system accepted it. Keith Now. The system did not care.

He had been Keith for four years. Before that he had been other things—other names, other lives, other configurations of a self that had not, in the end, been load-bearing. He did not think of the time before Keith as a narrative with a beginning and an end. He thought of it as a house he had lived in that had turned out to be built on something unsound, and rather than repair the foundation, he had walked out the front door and kept walking until the house was behind him and then below him and then, eventually, not even visible on the horizon line of his memory. This was not entirely true. On certain nights the house reassembled itself in his mind, and he was inside it again, and there was smoke, and there were voices, and there was the specific sound of a man he had once loved like a brother saying something that could not be unsaid while a deep fryer made a noise that deep fryers should not make, and then Keith would get up from wherever he was sleeping—a van, a shelter, a rented room, the back seat of his car outside Bend—and walk until the house collapsed again.

He chose the retreat because it was far. That was the entire calculus. He had been moving north and west for years, as though fleeing along a magnetic line, and Alaska was where the line ended, or continued into ocean, which was the same thing. The website promised solitude. Keith was not seeking solitude; Keith *was* solitude, in the way that a person becomes the condition they cannot escape. But the pictures showed mountains, and Keith had learned, in his four years of being Keith, that mountains did not ask questions, did not remember, did not require him to have been anyone before.

He printed the confirmation. He walked to his car. The car was a 2004 Subaru Outback with 247,000 miles and a rear window that did not close completely, which meant the back seat smelled permanently of pine and rain and the cold ceramic scent of Oregon in November. In the back seat was a sleeping bag, a camp stove, a cast-iron skillet, and a James Baldwin paperback with a broken spine. On the front seat was a photograph, facedown, that Keith had not looked at in three years but had not thrown away, which meant it was still exerting gravity, which meant the house was still there, somewhere below the horizon, waiting.

He started the engine. He drove north.

Five reservations. Five credit cards (one functional, two distressed, one prepaid, one under a brand name that the FBI was monitoring for unrelated reasons). Five people who had once, for a single weekend in November 2011, been in the same house in Westport, Connecticut, with a twenty-pound turkey, a thirty-six-quart deep fryer, a bottle of 2005 Château Margaux intended as the peace offering that would repair a fracture that had been widening since graduation—and who had left that house separately, in ambulances and rental cars and, in one case, a police cruiser, and had not spoken since.

Five flights to Anchorage, on five different days, on three different airlines. Five taxi rides to the floatplane dock on Lake Hood. One floatplane. One pilot—a sixty-three-year-old man named Harlan Voss who had been flying charters in Alaska for thirty-one years and whose left anterior descending artery had been narrowing, invisibly, for the last eight, a process so gradual and so patient that it constituted, in its own way, a kind of long-form planning.

One mountain. One lake. One bear, though they did not know about the bear yet.

Five people, each convinced they were going alone, each correct about the solitude they carried and wrong about every other thing.

## II.

Harlan Voss died the way most people die, which is to say without anyone's permission and at a time that was convenient for no one. The de Havilland Beaver had been in the air for forty-seven minutes. The five passengers were seated in a configuration that resembled, had anyone been in a position to observe it from above, the precise geometry of five people who did not want to be within six feet of one another but were confined to a fuselage designed to accommodate exactly this number of bodies.
