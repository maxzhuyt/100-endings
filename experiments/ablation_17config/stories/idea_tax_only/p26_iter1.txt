# The Wallpaper, the Pool, the War

---

## I.

The wallpaper at Murray's had a flock pattern, burgundy on burgundy, the kind that looks expensive until you touch it and realize it's just glue and shredded fabric pretending. There was a stain near the fire exit—champagne, or something worse—shaped like a hand reaching for something it would never get. I used to stare at that stain during the slow numbers when the men at the good tables were watching the stage and I could let my face rest.

No. That's not right. The flock wallpaper was at Ward's flat, the one in Wimpole Mews. Murray's had something else—a kind of damask, dark blue, or maybe that was the lighting. You could never tell what colour anything really was in that place. Everything looked like it was happening underwater.

It doesn't matter. The wallpaper doesn't matter.

What matters is that I am sitting in this room—this clean, featureless, perfectly adequate room with its view of a car park in Berkshire and its curtains the colour of weak tea—and I have been asked to remember, or I have decided to remember, or I simply cannot stop remembering, which is a different thing entirely, and less dignified than either.

The recorder is running. Or it isn't. There may be someone sitting across from me in the visitor's chair—a young woman with a notebook, or a young man with a camera that fits in his palm like a cigarette case, everything is so small now—or there may be no one. The medication makes the edges soft. I am told this is a comfort. I am told many things about comfort by people who arrange my pills in a plastic tray with the days of the week printed on it, as though time were a pillbox you could snap shut.

Christine.

I have to say her name now. I've been circling it the way you circle a glass of something when you know that once you drink it, the evening changes. Christine Keeler. Two words I have said ten thousand times—to journalists, to lawyers, to dinner companions who leaned in because they thought they were about to hear something salacious, and almost always were, because I have never been above giving people what they lean in for. It is, in its way, the only profession I ever mastered.

But I want to say her name differently now. I want to say it the way you handle a photograph that has been thumbed at the corners and left too long near a window, the image beginning to go pale at the edges while the centre holds.

This is a story about a girl who walked into rooms full of powerful men and believed she was the most electric thing in them.

She wasn't wrong. That was the whole bloody problem.

I can hear the trolley in the corridor. Four o'clock. They'll bring the tea in those ceramic cups with the rubberised bases so nothing slides, and a biscuit I won't eat, and someone will ask me if I'm comfortable, and I will say yes, because I am seventy-three years old and I learned before I was seventeen that the answer to most questions is the one that makes the questioner go away.

Christine learned that too. She just kept forgetting it at all the wrong moments.

Outside, the light is doing what English light does in November—apologising for itself. Coming in thin and grey through the window, lying across the linoleum like something that has been ironed flat. In 1961 the light was different. I know that's nostalgia or cataracts talking, but I swear it was different. The light in Soho fell in slants through doorways, and at Cliveden it came up off the water like something you could hold in your hand und burn yourself on.

I'm getting ahead. I always get ahead. That's the problem with a story you've lived inside for fifty-six years—you know where all the rooms are, so you keep walking into the wrong one first and having to back out and say, no, not yet, we haven't earned that room yet.

Let me start with Murray's. Let me start with the economy.

---

## II.

Murray's Cabaret Club, 16-18 Beak Street, Soho. Percy Murray ran it. The entrance was down a set of stairs, the way all the interesting places in London required you to descend, as if pleasure were something geological, layered under the ordinary pavement life of Regent Street shoppers and bus conductors who never once looked at the door and thought: *down there, right now, a girl from Birmingham is standing in feathers and no top and smiling at an arms dealer like he's said something clever, which he hasn't, but the champagne helps.*

I was that girl. Not always the arms dealer—sometimes it was a property man from Surrey, sometimes a backbencher doing something his constituency would not have voted for, sometimes a face from the Krays' circle who tipped heavenly and never touched you unless you touched first, which I respected, because manners are manners regardless of what else a person does for a living.

The pay was reasonable. The tips were better. The geography was everything.

Murray's had perhaps thirty tables, but only six that mattered—the ones nearest the stage, angled so the men sitting there could see the girls and the girls could see the men and everyone could pretend this mutual visibility was accidental, a happy arrangement of furniture. Christine called those tables "the auction block," which was the kind of thing she said that made you inhale sharply and then laugh, because she said it smiling, as if it were the name of a cocktail.

The rules were unwritten, which meant of course that everyone knew them.

You could accept a drink. You could sit at a table between numbers if Percy didn't catch you. You could give a telephone number, receive a telephone number, arrange to be somewhere after hours. You could not—and this was absolute, because Percy, for all his liberality, understood that a reputation for outright solicitation would bring the Vice Squad through his door like water through a drain—you could *not* negotiate terms on the premises. Whatever happened, happened elsewhere, and whatever else it was, it was your own affair, and your own affair was your own business, and if your business was sometimes transactional, well. The fifties were over. London was waking up. We were all conducting commerce of one kind or another.

I was good at it. I don't say that to boast. I say it the way a carpenter might say she was good with a lathe—it was a skill, I had it, it kept me fed and clothed and out of Birmingham, which was the holy trinity of my ambitions at seventeen.

Christine was different.

She'd arrived at Murray's shortly before me—a few months, maybe less. She'd come from a modelling job that wasn't, through a man who introduced her to another man, through the usual series of doors that opened for a certain kind of girl in a certain kind of London and led somewhere that was not quite what the doors had promised but was at least warm and had drinks. She was from Wraysbury, which is nowhere in particular, and before that her life had contained things she told me about only once, late at night, in pieces, and which I will not repeat here because they are hers and she is dead and the dead should be allowed to keep what the living took from them.

What I will say is this: she was beautiful in a way that was not negotiable. I mean that literally. Most beauty, in rooms like Murray's, was a proposition—*I am this, you may have access to this, here are the terms.* Christine's beauty did not propose. It *stated.* She walked as if the floor owed her something. She held a cigarette like it was a sceptre. She had dark hair that she wore like a decision she'd made that morning and might change by evening, and a laugh that started low and went nowhere quiet.

Men looked at her and something happened to their posture.

I watched this. I watched it the way you watch a card player who is very, very good and doesn't know yet that the house always wins, not because the house is smarter but because the house built the table.

There was one evening—this would have been late 1961, October perhaps, because I remember wanting a coat and not having one that was good enough for the street I was standing on—when a man at one of the good tables sent over a bottle of Veuve Clicquot. Not a glass. A bottle. With a card. The card said something—I don't remember what, something with his name on it, something that communicated that he was a person of resources who was accustomed to having his generosity received with appropriate enthusiasm.

Christine took the bottle. She didn't read the card. She poured herself a glass—into one of the house tumblers, not a flute, because the flutes were for the men's tables—and she drank it as if it were owed to her. Not defiantly, not provocatively, not performing anything at all. She simply drank it the way a person drinks water: because she was thirsty and it was there.

The man at the table smiled. His friends smiled. Something about it delighted them. I understood the delight and I understood its danger at the same time, but I could not have explained either, not then.

Now I can. What delighted them was that she was performing entitlement in a room where she had none. Her confidence was the product they were consuming. They could watch her behave as if she belonged in their world, and the pleasure was double: the thrill of her beauty *and* the knowledge that the belonging was a trick of light, a girl in borrowed glamour, something they could enjoy precisely because it would never threaten the actual order of anything.

She finished the glass. She set it down. She went back to the stage.

I thought: *I am watching a girl who is going to get everything she wants and none of it will be hers.*

But I didn't say that. I was seventeen. What I said was, "You could have at least read the card—he might have been good for dinner."

And she laughed. That laugh.

Stephen Ward was there that night, or a night close enough that memory has folded them together. He was often there—not as a customer, exactly, but as a *presence*, the way a host is present at a party that is not technically his. Percy tolerated him because Ward brought people in, interesting people—peers, diplomats, artists, the kind of men whose names on a membership ledger lent a sheen that no amount of burgundy flock wallpaper could achieve.

Ward was an osteopath. This is the first thing anyone tells you about him, and it is the most misleading, because it sounds medical and therefore respectable, and Stephen Ward was many things but respectable was not the right word—*credentialed*, maybe, or *plausible.* He had a practice in Devonshire Street. His patient list read like Burke's Peerage annotated by MI5. He fixed the backs of powerful men and in return those powerful men invited him to their houses both because he was genuinely charming—he was; I must be fair; he was one of the most charming men I have ever been in a room with—and because he served a function that their class required but could not name publicly: he was the man who knew girls.

Not a pimp. I want to be precise about this because precision is the only thing I can still offer that the newspapers never could. Ward did not take money for introductions. He did not negotiate terms. What he did was subtler and, I think, worse in its way, because at least a pimp is honest about the exchange. Ward *collected* people. Young women, beautiful women, women from backgrounds that made them available—he collected us the way another man might collect first editions, and he lent us out with the same careful generosity, and he was always so kind about it, so solicitous, so apparently *invested in our well-being*, that it was years before I understood that generosity in the absence of equality is just control wearing a nicer coat.

He bought Christine drinks. He bought me drinks. He invited us to his flat, where there were always interesting people and good wine and a sense of being *near* something important, something that hummed. He sketched Christine once—he was a very good artist; it's the thing about him that I cannot dismiss, because talent complicates every moral judgement, which is why we prefer our villains without it.

And then, one evening, leaning against the bar at Murray's, his collar slightly open, his face arranged in that expression of casual delight that was his most dangerous tool, he said:

"Darlings, I've got the cottage again this weekend. Cliveden. Bring your swimming things. There'll be people you should meet."

He said it as if it were an afterthought. As if the most consequential weekend of our lives were a footnote he was adding to a letter already written.

The most generous man I ever met who never paid for anything with his own money.

I said yes immediately. Christine said yes immediately. We were showgirls from nowhere, offered a weekend at one of the grandest estates in England.

Reader—or camera—or empty room—would you have said no?

## III.

Cliveden. The word alone is a kind of architecture.

You came up the drive and the house appeared above you like a sentence you weren't qualified to parse—all that Portland stone, all those windows, the terrace running the length of the south front like a viewing platform from which the ruling class could observe the Thames and, by extension, everything the Thames implied: commerce, empire, the movement of goods and bodies that paid for the stone you were looking at.

The Astors owned it. Lord Astor—Bill, as Ward called him, because Ward called everyone by their first name, which was both his charm and his trespass. Bill Astor let Ward use Spring Cottage, a small house on the grounds, in exchange for osteopathic treatment and the kind of social lubrication that Ward provided effortlessly: a steady supply of amusing, attractive, available people to populate the edges of Astor's weekends. We were the supply.

It was a Saturday in July 1961. The heat was absurd—un-English, shameless heat that made the grounds smell of grass and river water and something sweeter underneath, maybe the roses, maybe the money. Ward drove us down in his car. Christine sat in the front, her feet on the dashboard, her hair doing something that the wind should have ruined but didn't. I sat in the back, watching the suburbs give way to green, feeling the particular electricity of being taken somewhere I hadn't earned.

Spring Cottage was small, charming, deliberately modest in the way that only very rich estates can afford to make their outbuildings—*we have so much grandeur that we can afford this little pocket of simplicity.* Ward installed us there with towels and gin and a proprietary ease that I noticed and Christine did not, or noticed and didn't care about, which was often the same thing with her.

"The pool," he said, "is up past the parterre. You'll see it. I think Bill has people this weekend—government people, a few diplomats. Be delightful."

*Be delightful.* It was an instruction so casual it sounded like a compliment.

The pool at Cliveden sat in the grounds below the main house, visible from the terrace but separated by enough lawn and topiary that the people swimming could feel private while the people watching could feel superior. It had that quality that all the physical spaces in this story share: the appearance of openness concealing an absolute structure of access and permission. The pool was Lord Astor's. The water in it was Lord Astor's. The sunlight falling on it was, in some real sense, Lord Astor's, because he owned the grounds that framed it and the walls that sheltered it from wind.

We were in the pool when they came down from the house.

Now. The swimsuit. I have been asked about this so many times that the asking has replaced the memory, which is what happens when a moment becomes public property—everyone else's version crowds out the original until you are not remembering the event but remembering the last time you described it.

Here is what I will say: Christine was in the water. She may have been wearing a swimsuit borrowed from one of the staff. She may not have been wearing anything at all. I have given both versions. Both are true in the sense that matters, which is that Christine's body—in or out of a swimsuit—was the hinge on which the evening turned, and the country would later turn, and the difference between the clothed version and the naked version is the difference between a story about a girl at a party and a story about a *scandal*, and it was never Christine's body that determined which story got told. It was who was watching.
