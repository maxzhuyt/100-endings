# Los Leones del Bloque

## I.

The doors opened by themselves. That was the first thing. They sighed apart at the pressure of approach, and the air that came through carried nothing—not cold, not warm, not anything that could be called a smell. His daughter's hand was on the back of the wheelchair, and she was talking, and Ramón could hear every word she said clearly, the way you hear rain on a window you cannot open.

"—and the dining room is right through there, see, Papi? Big windows. And they said there's a courtyard in the back with benches, so when it's nice out—"

The carpet was the color of nothing. Not gray, not beige—the color in between those colors, the color that had been chosen because it could not offend. His shoes, when they touched it, made no sound. The rubber wheels of the chair made no sound. They moved down the hallway the way objects move underwater, and the hallway was lit from panels in the ceiling that produced a light without direction, without source, the kind of light that cast no shadow.

A woman behind a desk smiled and said something and his daughter said something back and there was a clipboard and a pen. The pen had a chain. He sat in the chair and watched his daughter's hand move across forms. She had his handwriting, the same leftward slant, though she didn't know this because she had never seen enough of his writing to notice.

The room was on the second floor. The elevator was silent. The hallway on the second floor was identical to the hallway on the first floor, which meant there was no hallway at all, only a continuation of the same surface, the same panels, the same air. Room 214. The door was open. The bed was narrow and adjustable, with a metal rail that folded down, and beside it a nightstand with a drawer and a lamp. The window faced east, or he thought east—no, it faced the parking lot, and beyond the parking lot a row of trees with white blossoms that he almost recognized as something but that did not smell like anything because the window did not open.

"Try the bed, Papi."

He sat. The mattress took him without resistance. There was no pushback, no structure beneath, just a slow sinking into foam that held the shape of whoever had been there before. Somewhere in the wall, or below the floor, or in the ceiling—impossible to tell—a machine hummed at a frequency just below hearing. Not a compressor, not a fan. Something that kept the temperature at the temperature it was, which was the temperature of no temperature.

His daughter was hanging a photograph on the wall. She had brought a small hammer and a nail, and the sound of the hammer was the only true sound he had heard since the doors, and it was over in two strikes. The photograph was of the grandchildren at the beach. He looked at it. They were laughing. The glass over the photograph reflected the overhead light, cutting a white bar across their faces.

"I'll come Sunday," she said. She kissed his forehead. Her perfume touched him and was gone. "You'll like it here, Papi. They have everything."

He did not unpack. The suitcase sat at the foot of the bed. On the nightstand, his daughter had placed a cup of water in a plastic cup. He listened for the building—for a television through drywall, for pipes, for footsteps, for someone calling someone's name down a corridor, for anything that would tell him a body was near, was living, was making noise because living was a noisy thing. The hum continued, constant, having no origin. Outside, the ornamental trees stood in a line. Their blossoms were white. They would not fruit, because they were ornamental, because they had been planted to be looked at, which meant they were not really trees but the idea of trees, the way this room was not a room but the idea of one, the way the air here was not air but its absence, pulled clean of everything that might tell you where you were or who had breathed it before you.

There was a word Ramón wanted. It sat in the back of his mind like a key in a pocket you can feel through the fabric but cannot reach. A word for what this was. Not *cárcel*. Not *hospital*. Somewhere the— no. Something about the— no. He closed his eyes, and even the dark behind his eyelids felt regulated, climate-controlled, the dark of a room where nothing had ever burned or been cooked or come in through the window on wind.

## II.

On the third morning, in the common room, a woman with a name tag that said GLORIA set a plastic cup of juice on the table in front of him. The cup touched the laminate surface and made a sound—a specific, small, hard *clack*—and the room fell away.

It fell away the way a curtain falls, all at once and completely, and he was outside, and it was July, and the heat sat on 178th Street like a hand pressing down on a drum skin, and under the streetlight on the north side of the block, between Audubon and St. Nicholas, the folding table was set up right where it always was, legs sunk a quarter inch into the softened asphalt, and there were bones on the table.

Not bones. Fichas. The double-nines, the good set, not the cheap ones from the dollar store but the heavy ones Papo had lifted from his cousin's social club in the Bronx and never given back. They were ivory-colored with brass spinners and they hit the table with a sound you could feel in your wrist. Manny was shuffling—two-handed, theatrical, sweeping the tiles face-down across the plywood surface in wide circles like a man conducting an orchestra that only played percussion. He shuffled for longer than was necessary, because Manny did everything for longer than was necessary, because the performance was the point.

"Shuffle or deal, coño, we're not at the Copacabana."

That was Papo. Papo was sitting with his chair tilted back on two legs, one foot braced on the table edge, a bottle of Presidente held against his chest where the condensation had made a dark circle on his Dominicans Don't Play t-shirt. His voice carried the way certain men's voices carry—it expanded to fill whatever space it entered, and the space tonight was the block itself, the whole block, the sound bouncing off the brick face of 618 and the fire escapes and the metal security gates of the bodega, which Chuíto had pulled halfway down because it was ten o'clock but he was still inside counting the register.

"Tu madre," Manny said, not looking up, still shuffling, and the shuffling sound was like water over river stones, like nothing else, like itself.

Julián sat in the third chair with his legs stretched out into the street, ankles crossed, watching the game he had not yet joined with the expression of a man who had been watching games his whole life and knew what every face meant before the first tile was played. He was the quietest of them. He had a beard then—this was the summer of the beard, before his wife made him shave it—and he stroked it when he was thinking, which was almost always.

Ramón was in the fourth chair, and in front of him was the notebook. Composition book, black and white marbled cover, the kind the kids used in school. He kept the scores. He kept them in columns, the dates at the top, and he kept not just who won but the margin of victory, and when the arguing started—it always started—he opened the book and read back the numbers and the numbers settled it. Every game. Every debt. Every boast that later needed proving or retracting. Manny once said Ramón's notebook was the Bible of the block, and Ramón had said no, the Bible had errors, and Papo had laughed so hard he spit beer on the table and they had to wipe down the tiles with Chuíto's dish towel.

The fifth chair was for Chuíto, and it was empty because Chuíto was still in the bodega, but his beer was on the table, open, warming, and every few minutes he called something through the half-closed gate—commentary, insult, a number he wanted Ramón to check—and his voice came through the metal slats like a voice through a confessional.

From the third floor of 618, through the window that never closed because the frame was warped, came the smell of Doña Carmen's habichuelas, thick with sofrito and the particular sweetness of the gandules she got from her sister's cousin in Villa Mella. The smell was part of the night. It belonged to the night the way the streetlight belonged to it and the heat and the sound of someone's radio playing Fernandito Villalona three buildings down, faint enough to be a memory even as it was happening.

"Juega," Manny said, and they drew.

Ramón drew. He looked at his tiles and arranged them, which was his way—arrangement, sequence, the pleasure of order—and across the table Papo was already groaning, which meant either his hand was terrible or he wanted them to think it was terrible, and in either case the groaning was sincere because Papo never faked anything, he simply felt everything at a volume that other people reserved for emergencies.

There was a moment, between the draw and the first play, when the five of them were quiet at the same time, which was rare, and in that moment you could hear the whole block—every window, every radio, every child still out on the sidewalk, every car turning onto Audubon with its bass vibrating the manhole covers—and the moment held, and then Manny cracked the first tile down onto the table like a man pronouncing sentence, and the game began.

Julián's wife called from the window. "Julián. Julián, ven acá." There was something in her voice—not anger, something older than anger, the particular frequency of a woman calling a man who would not come, who never came when she called, who would come when the game was done and not before. He raised one hand without turning. He did not go. His wife's voice hung in the air for a moment, then the window above went dark.

Papo was drinking his second Presidente, or his fourth, and his hands had the loose, exaggerated accuracy of a man who'd had four, and his chair was still tilted back, and someone—Manny—said "You're going to break your neck," and Papo said "I've been dying my whole life, brother, get in line," and everyone laughed because this was funny, because Papo's relationship with his own destruction had always been funny, because the alternative was something else, and the chair came down on all four legs with a sound like a gunshot and the game went on.

The dime landed. The double-five, Ramón's. He reached for it and the table was laminate and the tile was a plastic cup and the hand that held it shook and there was no streetlight and no radio and no smell of habichuelas and the common room was the common room and the humming was the humming and a woman named GLORIA was asking if he wanted more juice before he'd finished the first.

## III.

On Tuesdays and Thursdays, at two o'clock, the residents were gathered in the common room for Activity Hour, which this week was bingo. The card was placed in front of him. Numbers in a grid. The dauber beside it, a fat-barreled marker like a cigar someone had dipped in ink. Around the room, other people held their daubers already, gripped and ready, and the caller—a young man in a polo shirt with the facility's logo on the breast—stood at the front of the room with a wire cage full of balls and a microphone he did not need because the room was small but used because the microphone was part of the system and the system existed to be followed.

"B-seven."

Ramón heard the sound. The sound arrived whole—*bee-seven*—but the processing of it, the routing of sound through the corridor of comprehension to the room of meaning to the hallway of action, took time, and the time it took was longer than the time the caller gave, so by the time his eyes had found the B column and scanned for seven, the voice had already said the next number, and now there were two numbers in the hallway and neither had reached the room and a third was coming and the architecture of his attention could not hold them.

He looked at the card. The numbers sat in their grid. He knew what they were—he had always been good with numbers, the notebook, the columns, every margin of victory cataloged and retrievable—and he knew that B-7 was there, the second row, and his hand moved the dauber toward it and then stopped because the voice had said something else and the something else might have been a number he also had, and how do you mark the one you heard when the one you're hearing is already replacing it?

A staff member appeared beside him. Young, maybe Dominican, maybe not—her voice had no accent he could place, and she spoke to him in English with the measured sweetness of someone trained to speak to him this way. "I got you, Mr. Torres." She leaned over and dabbed B-7 with the dauber. Her hand was quick and the ink mark was clean and she smiled at him as if this were a thing they were doing together, a team effort, a collaboration.

He wanted to tell her something. Not about the bingo. About— there was a night when Manny— no, not that. Something about numbers. That he had been the one who kept the numbers. That there was a book, a composition book, and in that book every game for eleven years was recorded in his hand, in columns so straight you'd think he'd used a ruler, and that the numbers meant more than scores, they were a chronology, they were proof that five men had sat at a table on a particular night and played and argued and lived, and that the book was somewhere—in a box, in his daughter's closet, he thought, or was it in the—

"N-thirty-one."

He opened his mouth. What he wanted to say was: *I kept the count, I was the one who kept the count, every game for eleven years.* What arrived in the space between his brain and his tongue was: "Yo tenía el...the...el libro de los..." He saw the word *scores* form and dissolve. What came through was a sound that began as *números* and ended as something that was not a word in either language. He stopped. He looked at the dauber. He looked at the staff member. She smiled. She nodded as if he had said something she understood. She patted his hand twice, and her hand was warm and small and the kindness in it was real, and the fact that the kindness was real was worse than if it had not been, because the kindness assumed a man who needed his hand patted, and the man inside him who did not need his hand patted had no way to reach her, no way across the broken bridge, no apparatus to deliver himself into the room.

Across the common area, a television was tuned to a channel with bright colors and low voices. The caller called another number. The woman beside him daubed another square. He set the marker down. He folded his hands in his lap. The afternoon stretched in every direction, featureless, and in his chest the count went on—the games, the margins, seven-to-three, nine-to-two, the night Papo ran the table and they called him el presidente and he climbed onto the chair and gave a victory speech that lasted four minutes and referenced both Duarte and Héctor Lavoe—and none of it could get out. None of it could get out. The numbers in the grid stared up at him, meaning nothing, and behind his folded hands the numbers that meant everything turned in their column, uncalled, unmarked, his.
