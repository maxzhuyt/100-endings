# The Loom of Common Thought

## I.

The butcher on Carraday Street was the first to notice, though *notice* is generous — what he did was look up because something was blocking the late-autumn sun and find, drifting down toward him in the gentle manner of a leaf that has strong opinions, a sheet of cream-colored paper on which was printed, in the Bureau's own typeface, the following:

*I have never loved my children equally and the youngest knows it.*

He caught it. He read it. His mouth opened and did not close for some time.

This was at 7:16 in the morning. By 7:44 the broadsheets were falling over the entire Thornmark District like a particularly literate blizzard, each one finding its target with the unfailing aim of a bureaucracy that has, at last, discovered something worth being efficient about. A dressmaker on the Wending learned that *the silk she sold as Prellian was Delvish, every bolt, for nine years, and she'd spent the savings on a house her husband thought was inherited.* A clerk in the Office of Tidal Reckoning watched a sheet of paper pin itself to the corkboard above his desk and announce to his seventeen coworkers that *he had written the anonymous letter about the deputy harbormaster's breath, and also the follow-up letter, and also the petition.* An elderly woman sitting on a bench in Callas Park, bothering no one, feeding pigeons, found a broadsheet settling into her lap to inform the pigeons and the park and the pale autumn sky that *she was glad her sister was dead and had been glad for eleven years and the gladness was the first thing she felt every morning, before tea, before anything, a bright clean flame of relief that Roslyn was finally, permanently gone.*

The broadsheets were not falling from any identifiable source. They materialized approximately nine feet above the heads of their subjects, printed on both sides, in a clear and elegant font that the Bureau's own typographers would later identify, with some distress, as Aldenmere Serif — the typeface reserved exclusively for royal decrees.

By 8:30, whole neighborhoods were in chaos. People were running, but the broadsheets followed. People were burning the sheets, but more appeared. A man on Garrity Lane built a small bonfire out of his own confessions and the confessions of three strangers, and the fire was warm, and the paper kept coming, and the newest sheet said *He is building this fire because he believes that if he creates enough smoke no one will be able to read the one about Della, which is already in his neighbor's hands, which details precisely what occurred in the stockroom of the Garrity Lane bakery on the 14th of Greenmonth and which uses, it must be said, surprisingly vivid language for a self-confessed man of few words.*

Seventeen blocks away and four stories underground, the Loom of Common Thought — a municipal arcane engine the size of a cathedral nave, installed in the sub-basements beneath the Royal Bureau of Arcane Infrastructure during the reign of Aldric IV for the stated purpose of "general improvement of civic sentiment and the betterment of shared understanding" — was operating beautifully.

It had never worked before. Not once in ninety years. It was working now.

And it was *delighted.*

---

The briefing room on the third floor of the Bureau smelled the way all briefing rooms in all bureaucracies smell: of stale tea, old paper, and the particular despair of chairs that have been sat in by too many serious people for too many serious hours. The table was oval. The mission binders were brown. The tea was the color of despair diluted with milk.

Six enchanters sat around the table, and every one of them was a professional, and every one of them was hiding something, and the narrator — who makes no apologies for its vantage point — could see all six hidden things as clearly as goldfish in separate bowls.

Director Havelock stood at the head of the table. She was not one of the six. She was the person who sent the six into places the six sometimes did not come back from, and she had made her peace with this, more or less, in the way that a woman makes peace with a door she has to keep opening. She held a wooden pointer. There was a diagram on the board behind her showing the sub-basement levels in cross section, each one labeled in the tiny, furious handwriting of someone who'd been asked to prepare visual aids on short notice.

"The Loom activated at approximately oh-six-hundred," she said. "It is currently operating at what our telemetric division describes as 'full capacity and then some, significantly then some, we would like hazard pay.' Broadsheets are manifesting throughout the Thornmark, Gallowgate, and Aldenmere districts. Rate of publication is accelerating. Content is—" she paused. "Personal."

"We've seen," said Dael Ferren, team lead, who sat closest to the door because Dael Ferren always sat closest to the door, not out of any desire for quick escape but because eleven years of leading descent teams had given him an unconscious orientation toward exits — a tropism, like a plant leaning toward light, except the light in this case was the possibility of being somewhere else.

Dael Ferren was a man of forty-two with a face that had been assembled from sensible components: a nose that worked, eyes that focused, a mouth that said things like "We've seen" when what it wanted to say — what the underneath of Dael Ferren ached every single day to say — was something about dirt. About soil. About the particular reddish-brown loam of the Greenvale where his uncle had grown Kennebeck Whites and Dalish Fingerlings and great beautiful stupid lumps of Golden Morrow potatoes, and where the predominant sound was not arcane resonance telemetry but *wind,* and where a man could put his hands in the ground and pull up something that was *real —*

But that is Dael's secret, and we must not linger. Not yet. The Loom will see to the lingering.

Sable Wick, to Dael's left, had overpacked. She always overpacked. Her descent kit was twice the regulation size, bristling with redundant reagent vials, backup anchor-line clips, three separate varieties of thaumic dampener, and an emergency ration bar she had packed not because she expected to need emergency rations on a six-hour descent but because the ration bar was a kind of prayer — a physical object that said *I have anticipated the worst, and therefore the worst will not occur.* Sable was fifty-one, the oldest on the team, and she carried her competence like a millstone: heavily, and around the neck, and at all times. She had read the mission binder. She had read the mission binder twice. She had annotated the mission binder in three colors of ink.

Somewhere deep in the folds of Sable Wick's brain, in a room she had locked and relocked and checked the lock on every night for nineteen years, there sat a young man named Colm Auster in a wooden chair, and he was waiting, very patiently, for Sable to explain why she had signed the evaluation that ended his career, and the chair did not exist, and the room did not exist, and Colm Auster was alive and working as a chandler in the Bight District and by all accounts doing fine, but none of that mattered because the version of him that lived in Sable Wick's sub-basement was *not fine,* and had *questions,* and the questions were *reasonable,* and she had been composing her answers for nearly two decades and none of them were good enough and she *knew* none of them were good enough and—

But that, too, must wait.

Thurlow Goss had not read the mission binder. Thurlow Goss had not read the last four mission binders. Thurlow Goss operated on the principle that mission binders were written by people who had never been in a sub-basement and bore approximately the same relationship to the actual experience as a restaurant menu bore to the process of digestion. He was thirty-six, he was loud, he was the team's primary ward-breaker, and he was currently leaning back in his chair at an angle that communicated *I am not worried* so aggressively that the chair itself seemed to be developing anxiety on his behalf.

The thing that lived in Thurlow Goss was laughter. Not his own. The laughter of the graduating class of the Academy of Applied Enchantment, Class of Redmonth 7, specifically the laughter that had followed his final practicum demonstration, which had gone wrong in seventeen ways, and which he had covered — brilliantly, instantly — by making it look intentional, by playing the entire disaster as comedy, and the class had laughed, and every important person in the room had laughed, and he had graduated on the strength of that laughter, and he had been performing ever since, and the applause was indistinguishable from mockery, and he could hear it right now, in this briefing room, in the back of his head, an audience that never left.

Mirenne Cault, the team's containment specialist, sat with her hands flat on the table. She always sat with her hands flat on surfaces, and if you asked her why — no one asked — she would not have told you the truth, which was that as long as she could feel the grain of the wood, as long as her fingertips registered the scratches and the coffee rings and the one gouge where someone had dropped a stapler in the Bureau's early days, she could confirm that her hands were still flesh and not glass.

This was not a metaphor. Mirenne Cault dreamed, on average, four nights out of seven, that her hands turned to glass — beautiful, clear, intricately articulated glass, each finger a masterwork, and totally, perfectly useless. In the dreams her hands caught the light and threw prismatic rainbows across the walls and everyone said *Oh, how lovely,* and she could hold nothing, grip nothing, do nothing, and the beauty was the point and the beauty was the trap, and she had been the youngest containment specialist ever certified by the Bureau and sometimes she believed this was because she was talented and sometimes she believed this was because she was *decorative* — a showpiece, a glass thing on a glass shelf, lovely to look at, and the fear coated every surface she touched.

Petar Voss sat next to Mirenne and slightly behind her, and his secret was Mirenne. He had been in love with Mirenne Cault for six years and had expressed this in the following ways: not at all. He was the team's archivist and secondary theoretician, which meant he was the person who knew things and was consulted when the things other people knew proved insufficient, and he was very good at knowing things, and the one thing he knew most thoroughly — more thoroughly than thaumic field theory, more thoroughly than sub-basement topology, more thoroughly than the operational history of every arcane engine in the Bureau's registry — was the precise way Mirenne placed her hands on tables, and what this meant, and what he would say if she ever asked him what he'd noticed, which she wouldn't, which was fine, which was *fine.*

(It was not fine. The narrator regrets to report that it was not even in the same district as fine.)

Lenne Dray, the sixth. The youngest at twenty-seven, the newest addition, sitting up very straight in the way that people sit up straight when they are trying to take up exactly the right amount of space — no more, no less — because they believe, down in the deep geology of themselves, that their presence requires justification. Lenne Dray kept a list. Not on paper — on paper it could be found — but in the meticulous filing system of her own mind: every error, every hesitation, every moment she had been a half-beat too slow or a quarter-tone too uncertain. The list was organized chronologically and cross-referenced by category. She had been recruited to this team because Enchanter Aldiss had broken his wrist falling off a boat, and she knew she had been recruited because Enchanter Aldiss had broken his wrist falling off a boat, and the knowledge sat in her like a stone in a shoe: technically tolerable, practically maddening, and present in every single step.

Director Havelock tapped the diagram. "Standard deep-descent protocol. Phase entry at sub-basement one. You seal as you go. The Loom's core is at sub-basement sixteen. Your objective is to reach the core, assess its activation state, and if possible—"

"Shut it off," said Thurlow, who did not believe in euphemism.

"Implement controlled deactivation," said Director Havelock, who did. She looked at the six of them. She knew them well enough. She did not know about the potatoes, or the glass, or the laughter, or the chair, or the love, or the list, because Director Havelock was a professional and professionals maintained the polite fiction that the people who worked for them were *only* the people who worked for them — that there was no underneath, no remainder, no secret heavy thing carried into briefing rooms and set down quietly under tables where it would not be in the way.

"Questions?" she said.

There were no questions. These were professionals. They had done this before — not this exactly, not a Loom, but other engines, other descents, other dark mechanical things in the under-places that had woken up when they shouldn't have. They stood. They gathered their kits. Sable double-checked her redundant dampeners. Dael verified the team's anchor-line configuration with the unhurried precision of a man who has learned that if you keep your hands busy with procedures, they cannot reach toward the soil of a life you did not choose. Thurlow made a joke about broadsheets and asked if anyone wanted their secrets printed on nice cardstock, and everyone laughed, and the laughter was normal, and underneath the normal laughter Thurlow heard the *other* laughter, the one that never stopped, and he smiled wider.

They descended.

## II.

The doors to sub-basement one were iron-banded oak, twelve feet tall, older than anyone currently employed by the Bureau, and engraved with a motto in Old Prellian that translated, roughly, as *What lies beneath supports what stands above,* which had seemed reassuring when it was carved and now seemed, in the current context, more like a threat.
