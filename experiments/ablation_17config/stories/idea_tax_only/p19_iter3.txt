# The New System

## I.

The Swindon Regional Claims Processing Centre occupied the second through fifth floors of a building that had been, at various points in its forty-year existence, a tax office, a benefits administration hub, and briefly, during a restructuring in 2007, nothing at all. The building was clad in panels of a colour that had once been cream and was now the colour of cream left out too long. It sat on a road between a Tesco Metro and a veterinary surgery, and if you stood on the pavement opposite and looked up at the fourth floor, you would see fluorescent light behind grey venetian blinds and, occasionally, the silhouette of someone standing at the window, though never for long, because there was nothing to see from the window and there were claims to process.

The fourth floor handled home and contents. There were fourteen desks arranged in two rows of six and one row of two, the last row situated near the kitchen in an arrangement that had never been formally explained and that everyone understood to be a demotion. The carpet was blue-grey. The ceiling tiles were off-white, except for one above Sandra Fisk's desk that had a brownish watermark shaped, depending on the angle and the observer, like either Australia or a liver. The printers—there were two, a colour LaserJet that jammed and a monochrome workhorse that did not—were stationed beside the fire exit. The fire exit had not been tested in nine months. This was noted in the building's compliance file. No action had been taken.

Karen Plowes sat at desk four and was thinking about the Baxter claim. The Baxter claim involved a conservatory, a subsidence report, and an assessed value that Mr. Baxter disputed with the tenacity of a man who had nothing else to dispute. Karen had written to Mr. Baxter three times. Mr. Baxter had written back four times, because he had sent one letter before receiving any of hers, a pre-emptive strike that Karen considered, in her private and never-expressed estimation, to be quintessentially Baxter. She was wondering whether her performance review would mention the Baxter claim. She hoped it would not. She also hoped it would, because she had spent forty-one hours on it across three months and wanted someone, anyone, to acknowledge that forty-one hours had passed underneath her hands.

Dave Willetts was at desk nine, the one nearest the thermostat. Dave was cold. Dave was always cold, or always claimed to be cold, and the difference between these two states had ceased to matter to anyone on the fourth floor approximately three years ago. He had placed a handwritten sign below the thermostat reading: **PLEASE DO NOT ADJUST — DW**. Someone—Dave suspected Nigel, though he could not prove it and had not tried—had added, in different handwriting, a second line: **OR WHAT.** Dave had not removed the addendum. He nursed it. It gave him something to stare at during the long minutes between claims, a small injustice he could return to the way one returns to a loose tooth.

Nigel Greer, desk eleven, microwaved fish on Fridays. This was understood on the fourth floor as an act of aggression, though Nigel experienced it as an act of principle. He had been microwaving fish on Fridays since 2016, when a wellness email from HR had suggested employees consider "heart-healthy lunch alternatives," and Nigel had taken this suggestion seriously while simultaneously intuiting that his seriousness would irritate everyone around him, and the irritation had become, over the years, indistinguishable from the principle. He was thinking, at this particular moment, not about fish but about the six-nations rugby and whether Wales had any realistic chance against Ireland, and he had concluded, accurately, that they did not.

Paul Akonjo was twenty-four and occupied desk fourteen—one of the two near the kitchen, the demotion row—and he was scrolling through his phone beneath his desk with the fluid, absent dexterity of a generation that had learned to operate touchscreens before learning to operate tin openers. Paul was looking at a job listing. He was always looking at a job listing. He had not applied to any of them. He told himself, on a rotating basis, that the salary wasn't right, that the commute was wrong, that he wanted to "give it another six months." He had been giving it another six months for two years. Beneath the job listing, and beneath Paul's awareness of the job listing, there sat a dull, load-bearing suspicion that he would not leave because leaving required the belief that somewhere else would be different, and Paul, at twenty-four, had already lost that belief in the quietly catastrophic way young people lose beliefs: not through dramatic disillusionment but through insufficient evidence that any alternative was real.

Sandra Fisk, desk seven, had three photographs on her desk. One showed a springer spaniel named Captain. One showed a cocker spaniel named Brough. The third showed both dogs together on a beach in Tenby, ears airborne, mouths wide, the sea behind them a wrinkled grey. Sandra had taken the photograph herself and considered it, without embarrassment, to be one of the best things she had ever produced. She was, at this moment, circulating a birthday card for Kevin in underwriting—Kevin worked on the third floor, but Sandra's card circulation respected no jurisdictional boundaries—and she was monitoring, with the ambient vigilance of a woman who had spent twenty-two years in open-plan offices, the biscuit situation in the kitchen. The biscuits were Digestives. Sandra had opinions about this. She had expressed them. She would express them again.

Gerald Fenn, desk six, was stirring his tea. He stirred it anticlockwise, eleven rotations, which was four more than required by any rational theory of heat distribution but which had become, over the course of a career that now spanned eighteen years at this location and four years at a previous one that had closed, a motion as fixed and involuntary as breathing. Gerald was fifty-three. He had been passed over for promotion in 2018, when the position of Senior Claims Coordinator had gone to Marcus Webb, and again in 2021, when the position of Deputy Floor Manager had gone to someone from outside who had lasted seven months. Gerald had not protested either decision. He had updated his file. He had attended the announcement meetings. He had shaken hands with the appropriate stiffness. His marriage of nineteen years to Christine was in the final stages of dissolution. The decree nisi had been filed. The house in Wroughton was being valued. Gerald thought about this with the same regularity and detachment with which he checked the weather forecast—it was a condition, it was occurring, it would presumably resolve into something, and in the meantime there were claims to process.

Margaret Liu, desk two, knew she had a piece of spinach in her teeth and was calculating whether she could remove it without being observed. Brian Hooper, desk ten, was composing a fantasy in which he told his wife's cousin Raymond exactly what he thought of Raymond's opinions about immigration, a fantasy he rehearsed twice weekly and would never perform. Celia Dawes, desk three, was on hold with her GP's surgery and had been on hold for eleven minutes and would be on hold for nine more. Janet Lamb, desk thirteen—the other demotion desk—was working steadily and thinking about nothing in particular, and the nothing in particular had a quality of practice to it, as if Janet had been thinking about nothing for so long that she had become proficient, the way one becomes proficient at any repeated task.

There were fourteen desks, fourteen employees, fourteen sets of hands processing home and contents claims for a region that stretched from Wiltshire to the edge of Gloucestershire. The department head, Mr. Lorrimer, had departed the previous Friday. His farewell had been brief. There had been cake—Colin the Caterpillar, chosen by Sandra, funded by a collection that had yielded, after Gerald counted it in the kitchen, £23.40. Mr. Lorrimer had been with the company for twelve years. His departure had been described, in the email from Regional, as a "transition." The email thanked Mr. Lorrimer for his "years of dedicated contribution" and noted that a replacement would be "appointed in due course." The phrase "in due course" was, if one examined it closely, which no one did, perfectly and hermetically empty. It promised nothing. It specified nothing. It gestured toward a future with the vague, frictionless confidence of a departures board in an airport where no planes are scheduled.

The fourth floor processed claims. The printers printed. Dave was cold. Gerald stirred his tea. Outside the window, it was Swindon, and it was November, and the light was the colour of a photocopy of a photocopy of daylight, and nothing was wrong.

Nothing was wrong.

The building stood. The fluorescents hummed at a frequency of approximately one hundred hertz, which was below the threshold of conscious perception but above the threshold of effect, so that everyone on the fourth floor lived and worked inside a sound they could not hear but that worked on them anyway, the way a current works on a stone—not quickly, not violently, but with the absolute patience of a process that has nowhere else to be.

## II.

The new department head arrived on a Monday.

She was introduced in the second-floor conference room at 9:15, which was fifteen minutes after the official start of the working day, a timing that required employees to settle at their desks, open their systems, and then immediately stand up again and walk downstairs, a small procedural absurdity that several people noted internally and no one mentioned aloud. The conference room had eighteen chairs and smelled faintly of marker pen. A jug of water and six glasses had been set on the table, a ratio that suggested either optimism about thirst or a miscounting of attendees.

"Good morning," she said. "I'm Helen Alcott. I'll be overseeing this floor going forward."

She was neither young nor old. Her age was precisely the age at which age ceases to be an attribute and becomes merely a fact—somewhere past the boundary of middle, before the border of senior, in the administrative hinterland where people are simply *present* and have been for some time. Her hair was a shade of brown that resisted further description. Her suit was grey in the manner of institutional grey—not the grey of storm clouds or steel or any grey that called attention to itself, but the grey of surfaces designed to show neither dirt nor personality. She wore a lanyard with an ID badge. The photograph on the badge was recent and accurate, which was unusual enough that Karen Plowes noticed it and spent several seconds trying to articulate, to herself, why this bothered her, before giving up.

"I've been reading through the departmental files," Mrs. Alcott said. "You have a strong foundation. Good throughput. Claims-per-quarter figures that are—" She glanced down at a piece of paper that may or may not have contained figures. "—within range."

Within range of what, she did not say.

"I want to reassure you," she continued, "that my focus in these first weeks will be on listening. On understanding the rhythms of this floor. On making sure that the structures you rely on are serving you well."

She smiled. The smile involved the correct muscles. It appeared at the appropriate moment and lasted the appropriate duration and was, by every measurable criterion, a smile.

"There are a few procedural updates I'd like to walk you through. Nothing dramatic. The new filing templates for subsidence claims have been uploaded to the shared drive—you'll find them in the S-drive under 'Forms Updated November.' Tea and coffee supplies will now be requisitioned through the online portal rather than petty cash, which I know is a small change, but it does help with traceability. And we're asking that the kitchen be returned to a clean state by end of each day—just a matter of shared respect for the space."

She paused.

"I should also mention that we'll be implementing the new system over the coming weeks. I'll speak with each of you individually during your quarterly reviews, which I've scheduled according to the existing rota. If your date has been moved, you'll have received an updated calendar invite."

She let this sentence land. It landed the way all procedural sentences land—silently, weightlessly, absorbed into the atmosphere of a room where people have been trained, by years of meetings exactly like this one, to let sentences pass through them without friction.

"Are there any questions?"

For eleven seconds, no one spoke.

The room held fourteen employees. Of these fourteen, nine were looking at Mrs. Alcott, three were looking at the table, one—Nigel—was looking at the jug of water, and one—Gerald—was looking at the space just to the left of Mrs. Alcott's head, where the wall met the whiteboard, a junction of surfaces that offered nothing of interest and yet held his attention with a grip he did not understand and did not interrogate.

Eleven seconds.

The narrator of an institutional report would not note this silence. The minutes of the meeting, compiled afterward by no one and filed nowhere, would record only that the floor was briefed and that no questions were raised. But the silence had a texture. It was not the silence of satisfaction, nor the silence of people who have not yet gathered their thoughts. It was the silence of a room in which every occupant has simultaneously arrived at the edge of a question and simultaneously decided not to step forward. The silence of fourteen people performing, in perfect uncoordinated unison, the act of not asking.

Paul opened his mouth. Paul closed his mouth. Paul looked at his hands.

"Lovely," said Mrs. Alcott. "I'll let you get back to it."

They filed out. The corridor smelled of carpet adhesive and recycled air. They climbed the stairs. They returned to their desks. They sat down. They opened their systems. They processed claims.

In the mind of Karen Plowes: *Bit corporate. Probably brought in for the restructure. Wouldn't be surprised if there are redundancies by March.*

In the mind of Dave Willetts: *Didn't mention the heating. Not once. Bad sign.*

In the mind of Sandra Fisk: *Seemed nice enough. Reminded me of that woman from the Avon region audit. Professional. A bit smooth.*

In the mind of Nigel Greer: *"The new system." What new system? Some kind of software, probably. They're always rolling out some new software.*

In the mind of Paul Akonjo: *Doesn't matter. I'll be gone by spring.*

In the mind of Gerald Fenn: *Nothing. Or rather: something too shapeless to be called a thought, too quiet to be called a feeling. A faint alteration in the quality of the air, like the barometric drop before weather that never arrives. Gerald did not think something is wrong because the thought, to complete itself, would have required a specificity he could not supply. Wrong how? Wrong where? There was a woman in a grey suit who had said reasonable things in a reasonable order, and the meeting had ended, and he was back at his desk, and his tea had gone cold. He made a new tea. He stirred it. Eleven rotations, anticlockwise. He opened the Feldman claim. He processed.*

The afternoon passed. Mrs. Alcott's office—formerly Mr. Lorrimer's office, the small glass-fronted room at the end of the floor with a door that neither fully closed nor fully opened—was occupied now. Through the glass, she could be seen sitting at the desk, her posture symmetrical, her attention directed at something on the screen. The overhead light in her office seemed, to Brian Hooper, to be a slightly different colour than the lights over the open-plan floor—colder, or perhaps merely newer—but he mentioned this to no one, because the observation was not the kind of observation one mentioned, and because, in the time it took him to notice it and decide not to mention it, the difference had become less certain, and by the end of the day he would not have been able to say with confidence that it was real.

Mrs. Alcott did not leave her office for the remainder of the afternoon. At 5:17, which was two minutes after the official end of the working day, the fourth floor was empty.

During her first week, Mrs. Alcott did four things. She changed the brand of hand soap in the bathroom from a pink gel to a clear gel that had no discernible scent. She replaced the wall-mounted first aid kit, whose contents had expired in 2022, with an identical kit whose contents would expire in 2026. She removed a potted fern from the windowsill near the printers. The fern had been dying for eighteen months and had been the subject of a half-hearted custodial rotation in which employees watered it on a schedule no one followed, so that the fern received either no water or, on the occasions when guilt aligned with proximity, too much. Its removal was noted by Janet Lamb and by no one else. The fourth thing Mrs. Alcott did was send each employee a calendar invitation for their quarterly review, slotted into the existing rota with one modification: the reviews, which under Mr. Lorrimer had lasted twenty minutes, were now blocked for forty-five.
