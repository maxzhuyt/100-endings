## I.

**[ARCHIVAL STAMP: CMA-PR/09-001. TIMECODE: 00:00:00:01. FILM STOCK: STANDARD FIELD ISSUE, VISIBLE GRAIN. IMAGE: DESATURATED. CAMERA: HANDHELD, UNSTABLE. AUDIO: AMBIENT HUM, WIND ACROSS A DAMAGED MICROPHONE.]**

The first frame is a thumb. Then a smear of grey sky as the lens finds its focus, pulling through mud-light until the image resolves into a trench wall, sandbags stacked with the carelessness of people who have stopped believing sandbags work.

A woman's voice. Flat. Not monotone — flat the way a lake is flat after a storm has exhausted it.

"This is Edda Moss. Field Medic, Third Recon, Ninth Combined. I am also the platoon documentarian, by default, because nobody else would carry the camera. For the record, I am not trained in documentary filmmaking. I did a four-hour certification course in a basement in Provisional Linz that smelled like gasoline and the instructor was, I think, a ghost. He flickered twice during the module on focus-pulling. Nobody addressed it."

The camera pans. Slowly, unevenly — the operator's arm is tired, or wounded, or both. The trench stretches in both directions, a carved seam in a colorless landscape.

"None of what you're about to see has been corrected. No image stabilization. No color grading. No editorial adjustments for coherence, chronological or otherwise. If the footage appears degraded, it's because it is. If certain subjects appear to change physical form between cuts, they have. That is not a continuity error. That is the war."

She clears her throat. The sound is close and wet against the microphone.

"I need to explain Flickering, and I'm going to do it once, because I don't have enough tape to explain it twice and I don't have enough patience to explain it three times.

"When a living soldier dies on the battlefield — killed in action, mortal wound, clinical death, flat brain, the whole catalog — their soul jumps. It leaves the dead body and enters a newly available one. Different body. Could be any age. Any sex. Any build. Sometimes the transfer happens mid-sentence. Sometimes it happens in sleep and you wake up four inches shorter with someone else's surgical scar. The available body comes from — actually, nobody's told us where they come from. Quartermaster says they're 'allocated.' I've put in three requests for clarification. I've received none.

"The military designation is Persistent Consciousness Transfer. Everyone calls it Flickering. It happens to roughly one in eleven soldiers in active combat zones. It does not happen to civilians, or to rear-echelon personnel, or to chaplains, which has created a theological crisis I'm not qualified to summarize.

"Flickering is not optional. Flickering is not pleasant. Flickering is not, despite what the recruitment posters say, a tactical advantage. It is a chronic logistical inconvenience that smells like ozone and makes my job as a medic roughly four hundred percent harder, because I cannot maintain a patient history for someone whose body resets every time a piece of shrapnel finds them interesting."

A pause. Wind. Someone coughing off-camera, deep and wet. Then:

"I have seven Flickers in my platoon. I am going to introduce them to you now. I am not going to describe what they look like, because it doesn't matter. By the time you've finished watching this, most of them will have died and come back in faces I haven't seen yet. So I'm going to describe them by what stays."

The camera turns. Not smoothly — it catches on the sandbag rim, judders, then settles on a stretch of trench where five figures sit or lean in various postures of exhausted inactivity. One cleans a rifle with the hem of her shirt. One sleeps sitting up, chin on chest. Three eat from foil ration packets with the joyless synchrony of people who have learned to chew fast.

"The one humming — that atonal thing that's not a melody, more like someone remembering a melody from an adjacent room — that's Corporal Voss. She's been every age between fifteen and seventy since I've known her, and the humming never changes key, because it doesn't have a key. It has a mood. The mood is 'wrong.'

"The one who eats with her ration packet held six inches from her face, like she's reading it, like the calorie information is going to reveal something personal — that's Deshane. She reads everything. Crate labels. Boot sizes. I once saw her read a bullet casing like it was a fortune cookie.

"Lask —" The camera finds a small, elderly woman sitting with her legs pulled up against her chest, wearing a helmet two sizes too large that keeps sliding over her eyes. She pushes it up. It slides. She pushes it up. It slides. "Private Lask is currently in a body that I estimate at seventy or seventy-five years old. Last week she was a boy of about twelve. She has not psychologically adjusted either time. She has a way of saying 'Copy that' where both words sound like questions."

The camera pans further, past two figures Edda identifies by gesture — Hollis, who holsters his sidearm on the wrong hip in every body, always, "like his soul is left-handed even when his body isn't"; and Maret, who swears in a language no one can identify, possibly one she invented, "which would make it the single most creative act in the history of this platoon."

Then the camera stops on a stocky man sitting on an ammunition crate, eating a ration bar with the slow, studied disinterest of someone who has decided that chewing is beneath him but nutritionally unavoidable.

"That's Roan."

A beat.

"I think."

Another beat.

"He was a woman twenty minutes ago. Five foot two, dark hair, half a tattoo on her left shoulder that I'd started to recognize. Took a round during the supply inspection. Not even the shelling — she was just standing near a wall and a ricochet argument settled itself through her liver. Flash. And now — that." The camera zooms slightly. The stocky man blinks, chews, scratches the back of his neck. His fingers pause at the nape, feeling hair that isn't his. He makes a face — not horror, something closer to the face you make when you put on a coat and find someone else's receipt in the pocket. "He's been court-martialed twice. Once for desertion — he walked out of a firebase, they said. He says he walked out of the wrong body and his legs made a suggestion he didn't override. Once for insubordination, which from what I can gather consisted of telling a colonel something true in a voice the colonel didn't enjoy."

The stocky man looks up. Directly into the camera. He doesn't smile. He doesn't scowl. His face simply *arrives* at a kind of acknowledgment — a look that says *yes, I know you're recording this, and no, I'm not going to perform anything for you.*

He holds up the ration bar. "Moss," he says. "This tastes like the idea of food that someone described to a committee."

"That's Roan," Edda says, with something in her voice that the degraded audio almost hides. Almost. It's the sound of something being confirmed. Finding a key in the right lock.

Cut.

**[ARCHIVAL STAMP: TIMESTAMP GAP — 00:00:07:14 TO 00:00:07:22 MISSING.]**

The footage resumes on a title card. White text, bureaucratic serif font, centered on a black field:

> **THIS DOCUMENTATION IS PROPERTY OF THE COMBINED MORTAL ARCHIVES**
> **PARALLEL RECOVERY DIVISION**
> **UNAUTHORIZED VIEWING CONSTITUTES A CATEGORY 3 EXISTENTIAL INFRACTION**
> **VIEWER ASSUMES ALL ONTOLOGICAL RISK**

It holds for six seconds. In the lower left corner, barely legible, a handwritten annotation in what appears to be Edda's print: *If anyone actually reads this, I'm sorry about the audio in Sector 12. The microphone got blood in it. His blood, technically. Also technically not his.*

Cut.

**[TIMECODE: 00:00:07:22. FOOTAGE RESUMES. TRENCH. EVENING OR OVERCAST — LIGHT INDISTINGUISHABLE.]**

The camera is propped on something — a crate, a knee — and Edda's hands are visible in the lower frame, rolling gauze. In the background, the platoon has reshuffled. Voss, now in a different body (taller, male, but that same tuneless hum threading through the ambient noise like a persistent insect), is restringing wire along the trench wall. Lask's enormous helmet has been replaced; she has a new body, younger, sturdier, but she sits with the same pulled-up knees and the same baffled expression.

"People ask me — when people ask me, which is never, because nobody cares — they ask how I keep track. Seven soldiers, seven souls, and the bodies are just — weather. How do you know who's who?"

She finishes rolling the gauze. Sets it down. Picks up another.

"I don't always. That's the answer nobody wants to hear. Sometimes I don't know. Sometimes I spend twenty minutes treating a stranger before something — a phrase, a pause, a way of breathing through pain — clicks, and I think, oh. *Oh, that's Hollis under there.* And sometimes I'm wrong. Sometimes I patch up someone I think is Maret and it turns out to be a non-Flicker from another unit who just happens to swear creatively.

"So it's not recognition. It's — inference. Pattern. Residue.

"I look for the things that survive the transfer.

"The body doesn't survive. The face doesn't survive. The voice — mostly doesn't. Sometimes you get a similar larynx by coincidence and it's — almost worse, actually, hearing the right voice come out of the wrong face. Uncanny. Like a house with the furniture rearranged but the same radio playing in a room you can't find.

"What survives: cadence. Vocabulary. The sequence of thoughts. The specific gravity of a pause — how long someone holds silence before they fill it, and what they fill it with.

"Roan fills it with the middle of a sentence. He always starts talking like you just missed the beginning. Like the beginning was obvious and he's impatient with your need to hear it.

"Voss fills it with that hum.

"Deshane fills it by reading the nearest available text. I've seen her come back from a death, gasp awake in a new body, and before she checks her hands or her face or whether she has all her limbs, she reads the label on the nearest supply crate. I asked her why. She said, 'Words don't change when I do. They hold still. I need one thing that holds still.'

"I understood that perfectly. I didn't tell her I understood it."

Edda's hands stop rolling. She's staring at something off-camera. Her voice drops, not to a whisper but to the register of someone who has stopped thinking about being recorded.

"I identify my patients by their souls. I just don't have a box on the intake form for that."

The camera holds on her hands. They resume rolling.

Cut.

**[TIMECODE: 00:00:09:44. FOOTAGE: HEAVILY DEGRADED. FIVE SECONDS OF VISUAL STATIC. AUDIO: A THIN WOMAN'S VOICE, MID-SENTENCE, SAYING "— honestly think the salt rations are a form of psychological warfare against our own —"]**

The voice stops.

A sharp crack. Not dramatic — small, flat, the sound of something mechanical and terrible that doesn't care about drama.

The static clears for two frames. A thin woman — dark-haired, angular, mid-thirties — is folding forward, her hands reaching for a place on her chest that has opened without her permission. She makes a sound that is not a scream. It is, precisely, a voiced exhale of surprise, an *oh* that barely qualifies as a word.

Flash.

Not cinematic. Not a wash of light. A *flash* — fast, ugly, overexposing three frames into burnt white, a camera shutter's involuntary flinch.

Then the trench. A stocky man — the same stocky man from earlier — gasping awake on the duckboards. He's wearing boots that don't fit. They're the thin woman's boots, two sizes too small, and his feet are already crammed into them because the body materialized where she fell, wearing whatever the system decided to approximate.

He looks down at the boots. He looks at his hands. He opens his mouth.

"— side, but I'm willing to entertain the possibility that the pepper packets are a genuine act of —"

He keeps talking. He finishes the sentence about salt rations, mid-thought, in a different body, in a voice an octave lower, wearing a dead woman's boots.

Then he looks up. Finds the camera. His face — his new face, his ninth or tenth or twentieth face — performs a complicated expression that lands somewhere between *well, that happened* and *please don't make me acknowledge that that happened.*

"Moss," he says. "Write down that I prefer this body's knees. The last ones were a disaster."

Cut.

## II.

**[ARCHIVAL STAMP: CMA-PR/09-042. TIMECODE: 00:03:41:09. LOCATION SUBTITLE: SUPPLY CORRIDOR 7-EAST, CONTESTED. FILM QUALITY: MODERATE DEGRADATION. CAMERA: SHOULDER-MOUNTED, HEAVY BOUNCE. AUDIO: GUNFIRE, NOT DISTANT.]**

The footage is a mess.

Not artfully chaotic — actually, structurally unwatchable. The camera is on Edda's left shoulder, clipped to her medic's harness, and it catches what her body catches: the slam of a knee against trench floor, the lurch of a sprint across a gap, a sky that appears in a tilted slash and vanishes. The audio is layered and unintelligible — gunfire, someone shouting coordinates, the particular resonant *whump* of a ghost-round detonating, which sounds exactly like a large book being closed inside your inner ear.

Supply Corridor 7-East was, Edda's later voiceover explains, not supposed to be contested. "We were picking up antibiotics and ammunition from a cache that our own cartographers said was two hundred meters inside the mortal zone. Our own cartographers were working from maps that the ghost census bureau had redrawn three times that week without notifying anyone, because interdepartmental communication between the living and the dead has, and I want to be precise here, never once functioned."

The platoon walked into the corridor expecting crates. They found a ghost surveyor team taking measurements.

"They weren't armed. They had *clipboards*. One of them was measuring the width of a doorframe with a translucent tape measure. It would have been surreal if surreal hadn't been our baseline for six weeks."

Then a mortal sniper team from an adjacent unit, not Edda's, not briefed on the platoon's position, opened fire on the ghost surveyors. The surveyors dissolved — they do this when startled, apparently, lose coherence and scatter like ink in water before reconstituting elsewhere — and the sniper fire punched through the space they'd occupied and into the corridor where Edda's platoon had just arrived.

Three members went down in eleven seconds.

The camera catches it in fragments. A body folding — Edda can't tell whose. Then another. Then a sound like tearing fabric, which is a Flicker dying close enough to the microphone for the transfer discharge to register as audio. A flash that washes the frame white.

Then she's running.

"Medic up! Medic — Moss, *Moss,* over here —"

"Where — who's —"

"I don't know, I don't know, just — *here* —"
