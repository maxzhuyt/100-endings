# The Château Frequency

## I.

The letter arrived by courier on a Tuesday, which should tell you everything you need to know about the kind of people who sent it. No one sends good news by courier on a Tuesday. Tuesdays are for dentist appointments, for discovering the milk has turned, for the quiet unraveling of minor plans. A proper summons—the kind that changes the shape of your week, your year, your understanding of your position in the cosmos—should have the decency to arrive on a Thursday, when you've already surrendered to the futility of the workweek and have the emotional bandwidth to process the fact that your government considers you an asset of national importance, or at least an asset of national availability, which in my experience amounts to the same thing.

The courier was twenty-three, visibly uncomfortable in a suit that had been tailored for someone with different shoulders, and carrying a titanium-reinforced attaché case handcuffed to his wrist. I was carrying a half-eaten croissant and a garment bag containing seventeen meters of duchess satin that the Hartwell-Nguyens had rejected for the third time because the ivory was "too warm," which is not a real complaint but is nevertheless a complaint I have trained myself to receive with the expression of someone who takes the warmth of ivory as seriously as one takes a diagnosis.

"Ms. Vossey?"

"That depends entirely on what's in the case."

He did not laugh. Government couriers never laugh. They are selected, I believe, for their ability to withstand pleasantry the way certain deep-sea organisms withstand pressure: not through resilience but through the absence of the internal structures that pressure would otherwise destroy.

He required two forms of identification, a biometric thumbprint on a device that looked like it had been designed by someone who found smartphones too whimsical, and my verbal confirmation that I was, indeed, Marguerite Elise Vossey, dual citizen, born Aix-en-Provence, relocated London, currently operating at the corner of professional competence and personal stasis from a Clerkenwell flat I could almost afford. He handed me a cream-colored envelope of the heavy cotton variety that implies either a wedding invitation or the end of the world, and in this case—in the grand tradition of my life—it was both and neither.

I will not reproduce the letter in full because (a) it was fourteen pages long, (b) it was classified at a level that would make the reproduction a federal offense in nine countries, three of which I hold genuine affection for, and (c) it was written in the kind of bureaucratic prose that makes your eyes slide off the sentences like rain off limestone, which is a simile I would not have reached for if the letter had not concerned limestone specifically, and also a château, and also, improbably, extraterrestrials.

The gist was this: I had been recalled.

Recalled. As if I were a defective appliance, which, depending on whom you ask and how recently you've asked, is not an entirely inaccurate characterization. I had been recalled to a place I had successfully not thought about for nearly fifteen years, or rather—and this is an important distinction—a place I had thought about with such meticulous regularity that the thoughts had worn grooves so smooth they registered as absence, the way a river eventually becomes indistinguishable from the valley it has carved. I had been recalled to the Château de Fontblanche, which is in Provence, which is in the south of France, which is in the past, which is somewhere I do not go.

The reason for the recall was, depending on how literally you read the fourteen pages, that an extraterrestrial delegation had established contact with Earth six months prior—yes, I was also surprised this had not made the papers, though in retrospect the papers that year were focused on a cabinet reshuffle and the relative moral character of a footballer's second wife, so perhaps the editorial calculus was sound—and these extraterrestrials had, after a period of preliminary cultural exchange, agreed to a formal negotiation regarding technology transfer, navigational alliances, and something the letter referred to, without evident irony, as "interspecies emotional accreditation." The negotiations would be held at Fontblanche. The extraterrestrials had chosen the site.

They had chosen it, the letter explained in the strangulated passive voice of a bureaucracy that has been told something it does not know how to metabolize, because the limestone walls of the château resonated at a frequency they recognized. A frequency they associated, in their own taxonomy of detectable phenomena, with "the sustained evidence of pair-bonding among carbon-based bipedal organisms." They wanted to negotiate inside that resonance. They would negotiate nowhere else.

And they wanted, specifically, the original resonance sources.

Which was a very technical way of saying: they wanted us.

I put the letter on my kitchen counter, next to the half-eaten croissant and a gas bill I'd been ignoring with a diligence that bordered on the spiritual. I looked at the cream-colored envelope. I looked at the garment bag. I thought about the Hartwell-Nguyens' ivory, which was too warm, and about the last time I had been warm in any sense that mattered, and about the fact that of the five hundred and twelve weddings I had planned in the last decade, I had attended perhaps six as a guest, zero as a participant, and all five hundred and twelve as a woman standing just outside the radius of the thing she was orchestrating, which is a position I have perfected to the point where I sometimes suspect it is all I am: the person who builds the frame but is not in the photograph.

I called my assistant. "Lena, I need you to handle the Hartwell-Nguyens this week."

"Are you ill?"

"I have been recalled to a crumbling château by NATO, or whatever NATO is when it involves extraterrestrials."

"Is that a joke?"

"Lena, everything I say is a joke. That's how you know it's serious."

## II.

The drive from Marseille Provence Airport to Fontblanche takes forty minutes if you're efficient and ninety if you're me, because I asked the government driver to stop twice: once for coffee, which I needed, and once because the road crested a particular hill from which you can see the Luberon arranged against the sky like a set of knuckles pressed to blue fabric, and I had forgotten that view the way you forget the face of a person you loved, which is to say I had not forgotten it at all but had reclassified it as something that happened to someone else.

The driver, whose name was either Jean-Marc or Jean-Luc and who had the professionally neutral expression of a man assigned to transport a civilian he'd been told was important for reasons above his clearance, waited without comment. I appreciated this. I have planned enough events to understand the value of a person who can stand in a charged silence without attempting to defuse it.

Fontblanche appeared the way it always had: all at once, around a bend in the plane-tree-lined drive, the limestone catching whatever light was available and holding it a half-second longer than physics seemed to require. I had forgotten that too—the way the stone glowed. Not reflected. *Glowed*. As if it had stored every afternoon it had ever witnessed and was releasing them at a rate of one per glance.

But the gardens had gone. Not vanished, exactly—they were still there in outline, in the arthritic geometry of boxwood hedges that had once been ruthlessly maintained and were now growing with the undisciplined enthusiasm of things that have outlived their gardener. The lavender was wild. The gravel paths had surrendered their borders to a creeping thyme that smelled, when the tires crushed it, exactly the way it had smelled in September of the year I do not talk about.

Thyme. Of all the treacherous plants.

The facade was cracked. Not ruinously—the bones were still good, as they say in real estate and in the assessment of women over forty, which is a comparison I make with the full bitterness it deserves—but the surface had lost its composure. A fissure ran from the second-floor window of the east wing to a point just above the main entrance, a fracture line that looked, from the right angle, like the route a tear takes down a face that is trying very hard to remain presentable.

I am aware that I am projecting onto a building. I am a professional; I project onto centerpieces, color palettes, and the emotional resonance of napkin folds. Projection is not my weakness. It is my medium.

The government's additions were less elegant. Satellite arrays on the south lawn. A prefab communications hut in the courtyard, humming with the particular frequency of machines that are working harder than they should. Cables running along the base of the south wall, anchored to the limestone with brackets that had been drilled in—*drilled*—and I touched one of the drill holes as I passed it, and the stone was warm, and the warmth went through my finger and into the place behind my ribs where I keep the things I do not mention, and I pulled my hand back and put it in my pocket and thought very clearly about the Hartwell-Nguyens' seating chart, which had a problem at table nine involving a stepmother and a restraining order, and this was a problem I could solve, unlike the other problem, which was that I was standing inside a building that apparently *sang with the memory of love* and my hand was shaking.

A military liaison met me in the entrance hall. She was short, brunette, efficient, and operating at a speed that suggested she had consumed a quantity of espresso incompatible with civilian life.

"Ms. Vossey. Captain Aurélie Delacroix. I'll be your primary point of contact. The delegation arrives in seventy-two hours. You'll be briefed on protocol, dietary requirements—theirs, not yours, though we do have a chef—security parameters, and communication norms. They don't shake hands. They don't make what we'd call eye contact, although they have structures that serve a parallel function. They—"

"Captain."

"Yes?"

"Which room am I in?"

She consulted a tablet. "Third floor. East wing. Room 307."

Of course. Of course it was 307. Because the universe is not random; it is a wedding planner with a cruel sense of symmetry, and 307 was the room I'd lived in for that semester, the room with the window that looked out over the garden and, beyond it, to the place where the land fell away toward the valley and the light did the thing at dusk that I once spent an entire evening trying to describe to a person who was sitting beside me on the sill of that very window, and who said, eventually, "You don't have to describe it, Marguerite, you can just watch it," and I said something cutting, because I always said something cutting, because *describing* was what I did instead of *watching*, the way *planning* was what I did instead of *living*, and this is a realization I arrived at approximately eleven years too late and have been narrating around ever since.

I climbed the stairs.

The stairwell had the same acoustic. Your footsteps went up and the echo came back down changed—softened, the edges taken off, as if the stone were being gentle with the sound in a way the sound had not requested. I had forgotten that. (I had not forgotten that.) My rolling suitcase made a racket on the limestone treads that the walls tried to gentle and could not, because you cannot gentle a wheeled suitcase; some things announce themselves regardless.

Room 307 was smaller than I remembered, or I was larger, or both. The ceiling beams were the same dark oak. The floor was the same hexagonal terra-cotta tiles, each one slightly different from the next, the way faces in a family are slightly different: derivative and distinct. The bed was new. The view was not.

I stood at the window and did not look at the garden and did not look at the valley and did not look at the exact point on the sill where a person might sit with her legs drawn up and her chin on her knee and say the kind of thing that ruins you for windows and gardens and valleys for the next fifteen years of your otherwise functional life.

I unpacked my suitcase instead. I am very good at unpacking suitcases. I can establish order in a hotel room the way some people can establish order in a sentence: swiftly, with purpose, and with the understanding that the order is itself the message. Toiletries on the left side of the bathroom shelf. Garments in the wardrobe in the sequence they will be worn. Shoes beneath, paired and aligned. I put everything in its place, and then I put myself in the chair by the desk, and I opened my laptop, and I answered eleven emails about weddings that would happen regardless of whether extraterrestrials found humanity emotionally credible, and this was comforting, because weddings do not require emotional credibility. They require a timeline, a vendor list, and the willingness to believe that the right shade of ivory exists.

But before I started the emails, I put my hand flat on the wall beside the desk.

The stone was warm.

I told myself it was the sun. The east wing catches the afternoon light. That is a fact. That is a structural fact about a building. That is not—it is absolutely not—evidence that limestone holds anything other than calcium carbonate and the fossilized remains of organisms that died a hundred million years ago, which, if you think about it, is its own kind of love story: all those small, soft creatures, compressed by time and pressure into something that endures. But I did not think about it. I opened the laptop. I answered the emails. I did not think about it at all.

## III.

Solen arrived first, which was correct, because Solen always arrived first. It was—had always been—her one form of aggression: to be so punctual that everyone else's arrival felt like a minor delinquency.

She stepped out of a black government sedan at 8:47 a.m. on Wednesday, thirteen minutes before the scheduled time, wearing the kind of structured linen blazer that says *I live in Copenhagen now and have solved the problem of looking casually authoritative in natural fibers*. Her hair was shorter. Her posture was not. She had always held herself like a person who'd been told as a child that the world was watching, and she had never stopped believing it, even in rooms where no one was.

"Marguerite." She kissed both my cheeks, left then right, Copenhagen layered over a Parisian instinct she had never quite shed. "You look exactly the same."

This was a lie, and we both knew it, and we both let it stand, because Solen and I had always communicated through a carefully maintained garden of polite fictions, and the garden was still there, boxwood-neat, not a hedge out of place. She looked, for the record, ten years older in the way that certain Nordic-adopted French women look ten years older: more defined, not diminished, as if time were an editor who had removed the extraneous and left the argument clear.

"You look like someone who owns a linen blazer without irony," I said, and she almost smiled, which from Solen was a standing ovation.

The government handlers deposited her bags in 304. We did not discuss the room assignments. We discussed the weather, the chef (she had dietary restrictions now, which she delivered with the solemnity of a person announcing a religious conversion), and the geopolitical implications of first contact, in that order, because Solen had always organized conversations by ascending significance, saving the important thing for last, as if building a case.

"Have they told you why us?" she asked, standing in the entrance hall with her arms crossed, studying the cabling along the south wall with the expression of a systems analyst who has been shown someone else's wiring.

"Something about limestone and love," I said. "I paraphrase."

"You always do."

This was not a compliment.

Hugo was broader than I remembered. Not fat—he would never be fat; he had the metabolism of a hummingbird and the caloric intake of a sixteenth-century monk—but *spread*, as if whatever he'd been doing in the intervening years had required him to take up more space.
