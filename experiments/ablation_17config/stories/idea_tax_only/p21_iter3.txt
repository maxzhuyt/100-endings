# The Named Places

## I.

The reading room at the Rosenberg Library that admitted colored patrons occupied the northwest corner of the second floor, and in the afternoons between four and six the light came through the window at such an angle that it laid a rhombus of gold across the left third of the oak table, and if you sat in the second chair — the one with the cracked stretcher bar that Ida had reported to the desk clerk in November of 1896 and again in March of 1897 and had stopped reporting — the light would rest on the back of your left hand while you read, warm as a compress, and you might turn the page of whatever you were reading with your right hand so as not to disturb it. The air in that corner smelled of binding paste and the particular must of books that had survived one flood already, the minor one, in '86, when the water reached the baseboards and retreated, leaving a tide line still faintly visible on the oak if you knew where to look. Ida knew where to look.

Catherine had discovered the reading room by accident — had wandered up looking for a volume of Father Drummond's agricultural pamphlets for the orphanage garden and found instead Ida B. Wells sitting in the rhombus of gold with a copy of *The Woman's Era* and a expression that managed to be simultaneously absorbed and prepared to be interrupted. Catherine was not supposed to be on the second floor. Not because of any posted prohibition — she was white, and Irish only in the way that had stopped mattering in Texas by the nineties, or mattered differently — but because the Sisters of Charity of the Incarnate Word did not, as a practice, linger in public rooms with secular reading material. She lingered anyway. She sat in the third chair, the one without a cracked stretcher bar, and said nothing for seven minutes — Ida counted, because Ida counted things — and then asked, without introduction, whether the pamphlets in the colored reading room were arranged by the Dewey system or by whim.

"Whim," Ida said. "Conclusively whim."

Catherine laughed. It was the kind of laugh that happened mostly in the throat, a low, vibrating thing, and Ida, who had been teaching penmanship and arithmetic to the children at the Central Colored School for four years and who had heard every variety of laugh that childhood could produce, recognized it immediately as the laugh of a person who did not laugh often enough and was surprised each time by the sound of her own pleasure.

That was 1897. The stretcher bar was still cracked. The light still came in at the same angle.

---

The kitchen garden behind the Ursuline convent on the east end of the island ran six rows deep and was bordered on its seaward side by a low brick wall that Sister Margaret had laid herself in 1889 with mortar mixed too thin, so that by the time Catherine showed it to Ida the bricks along the top course could be wiggled like loose teeth. The soil was sandy, as all Galveston soil was sandy — the island was a sandbar with ambitions, as Ida once said, the only time Catherine ever heard her say anything that sounded like a joke about the place itself rather than its inhabitants — but Catherine had worked bone meal and oyster shell into the beds for years, and the soil in the garden was darker than the soil anywhere else on the island, almost black in the early morning when the dew sat on it, and it smelled of sulfur and coffee grounds and the particular iron tang of blood meal that Catherine ordered by the sack from a supplier in Houston who must have wondered what the Sisters of Charity were doing with sixty pounds of slaughterhouse sweepings every spring.

The garden grew tomatoes and pole beans and a fierce variety of pepper that Catherine claimed was a cultivar brought from County Galway but that Ida suspected, from the shape of the fruit and the quality of the heat, was a Mexican pepper known to every cook in the Fourth Ward and to no one in County Galway. Catherine refused to settle the question. The ambiguity seemed to please her.

They were not supposed to be in the convent garden together. This was not a rule anyone had articulated, because nobody had imagined the situation requiring articulation — that a colored schoolteacher from the Central Colored School would be standing among the tomato cages on a Tuesday afternoon, testing the weight of a Cherokee Purple in her palm while a nun knelt in the dirt beside her pulling henbit from the pepper bed. No one had forbidden it because no one had conceived of it. This was, Ida understood, a species of freedom — the freedom of the unconceived — and it had an expiration date, though she did not know when. She knew only that one day someone would conceive of it, and on that day a rule would be written, and the rule would be framed as though it had always existed, and the garden would become another place on the long list of places that had been open and were now closed.

Until then: the tomatoes, the peppers, the smell of blood meal in black sand, the low wall with its loose bricks, and Catherine on her knees in the dirt with her sleeves rolled to the elbows, her forearms tanned and freckled and thick with the kind of muscle that came from lifting children.

---

Broadway was the widest street in Galveston and the most legible — the oleanders in the median ran the full length of the avenue, and in September their last ragged blossoms still held, pink and white and a dark rose that looked, at dusk, like the color of a wound beginning to heal. The houses on Broadway were the largest on the island: the Gresham house, the Open Gates estate, the Sealy mansion with its limestone façade that turned amber in the afternoon. These were not houses that admitted Ida, or Catherine, or anyone whose presence would complicate the arrangement of rooms designed to confirm that the world was as it appeared. But the street between them was public — the oleanders did not discriminate, the pavement accepted all feet equally — and on Thursday evenings between May and October, when the light lasted long enough and the heat began to break, Ida and Catherine walked Broadway from Sixth Street to Twenty-fifth and back, a distance of just over two miles, and the walk took exactly one hour and fifteen minutes because they paused at certain points that had, over time, acquired the fixity of stations.

At Ninth and Broadway, where the roots of a massive live oak had buckled the sidewalk into a kind of step, Catherine always paused to adjust the lace of her right boot, which was older than the left and had a grommet that didn't hold. At Fourteenth and Broadway, where the oleanders were thickest and the median became, for a stretch of fifty yards, an almost-enclosed corridor of green, Ida always slowed, because the light inside that corridor was submarine and private, and the smell of oleander was so dense it replaced the air, and for fifty yards she and Catherine were invisible to the houses on either side, and the city could not see them walking together, and the not-being-seen was a relief so physical that Ida felt it in the muscles of her shoulders — a releasing, a settling, like putting down a thing she had been carrying so long she'd stopped noticing its weight.

They never spoke about the oleander corridor. They walked through it. They slowed. They emerged.

At Nineteenth Street a dog named Captain, a liver-spotted hound of no discernible breed, waited on the porch of a house whose owner was never visible, and Captain descended the porch steps with the solemnity of a deacon to accept the fragment of biscuit that Catherine kept in the pocket of her habit for this purpose. Ida suspected Catherine baked the biscuits specifically for the dog and told no one. Catherine suspected Ida knew and said nothing about the knowing. This was the kind of knowledge their friendship was built from — a lattice of things understood and unmentioned, mutual awareness held in silence, which was not the same as secrecy and was not the same as dishonesty and was, in fact, the only structural material strong enough to support the weight of what they were to each other in a city that had provided no language and no room for it.

At Twenty-fifth Street they turned around. The walk back was different. The light was behind them. The oleander corridor was the same corridor but the shadows fell on the other side, and the smell was the same but carried differently on the evening air, and the live-oak step at Ninth Street was approached from the opposite direction, which meant Catherine adjusted her boot on a downslope instead of an upslope, which meant she leaned slightly toward Ida instead of slightly away, and Ida had noticed this, and the noticing was another piece of the lattice, and the lattice was the friendship, and the friendship was the walk, and the walk was Broadway, and Broadway was twenty-seven blocks of oleander and pavement and live oak and a dog named Captain, and it was the most real thing in Ida's life.

## II.

Ida had been fourteen when the last federal troops withdrew from the Texas statehouse and Reconstruction ended not with a collapse but with a careful, bureaucratic folding — the way you fold a tablecloth after a meal, deliberately, corner to corner, the creases sharp, the thing put away in a drawer as if it had never been spread. She had been old enough to understand what her father understood: that the withdrawal was not a change in policy but a revelation of what the policy had always been. That the promises — of suffrage, of citizenship, of protection — were not broken. They were *completed.* The promise had always been the thing itself: not the delivery of rights but the *performance* of promising them, which served its purpose while it served its purpose and was put away when the purpose changed.

Her father, George Henry Driscoll, had served as a constable in Galveston County during Reconstruction, one of eleven Black men who held office in the county between 1869 and 1876. After the withdrawal he was not removed. He was made *irrelevant* — the duties were reassigned, the authority dissolved, the salary continued for seven months and then stopped, and when he went to the county clerk's office to inquire he was told that his position had been "consolidated," which was the word they used, and the clerk — a man George Henry had known for five years, a man who had once eaten cornbread at his table — looked at him with an expression that George Henry later described to Ida as "the face of someone practicing a new kind of not-knowing-you."

That face. Ida saw it again and again in the years that followed, and each time it refined itself in her understanding. She saw it when the Colored Teachers' Benevolent Association petitioned the school board for textbooks in 1889 and the board chair, a man named Whitmore who had twice attended CTBA fundraising suppers, looked at the delegation with a blankness so total it constituted its own kind of communication. *I see you. I am showing you that I am choosing not to see you. The showing is the point.* She saw it when the state legislature passed the poll tax in 1891 and the Galveston *Daily News* editorialized that the measure was "designed to elevate the franchise," and the word *elevate* meant *remove,* and everyone knew it meant *remove,* and the use of *elevate* was itself the performance — the demonstration that the language belonged to them and could be made to mean whatever was needed.

She saw it when the Tenth Ward Improvement Society, which Ida had helped to found, spent eight months raising four hundred dollars to repair the well on Avenue M that served sixty-three colored families, and the Board of Public Works accepted the funds and repaired instead a well on Postoffice Street that served the commercial district, and when Ida went to the Board to ask — not to protest, only to ask — the deputy commissioner said that "a determination was made that the public interest was best served," and the phrase *public interest* did not include the sixty-three families on Avenue M, and the exclusion was not an error, and everyone in the room knew it was not an error, and the deputy commissioner's face was Whitmore's face was the county clerk's face: the practiced, perfected blankness, the face that said *I understand your question and I am showing you that the question does not apply here.*

She saw it in the school board's rejection of the CTBA's request for a building inspector to evaluate the Central Colored School's foundation, which had begun to list in 1894. She saw it in the city's refusal to extend the streetcar line south of Broadway, where the colored wards were. She saw it in the hospital policy that admitted Black patients only through the service entrance and only to the basement ward and only when beds were available, which they never were except when they needed to say beds had been offered. She saw it in the systematic way that every institution in the city — the schools, the courts, the hospitals, the utilities, the churches, the newspapers, the police — arranged itself around a single architectural principle: that certain people could be placed outside the structure of obligation while being told they were inside it. That the wall could be made invisible to those standing on the side with the view.

By 1900, Ida was thirty-eight years old and she had learned the lesson so thoroughly it was no longer a lesson. It was a sense — the way some people could hear pitch or feel the pressure change before rain. She could walk into a room and know, before anyone spoke, whether the people in it were going to do what they said they were going to do. She could read a newspaper headline and feel, in the syntax, the angle of the lie. She could hear the word *official* and feel the precise barometric pressure of the emptiness behind it.

This was not bitterness. Ida did not consider herself bitter. Bitterness was a relationship with the people who had harmed you, and Ida had long since stopped having a relationship with them. What she had was something colder and more functional: a model of how the world actually worked, built from evidence, tested against experience, and confirmed so many times that confirmation had become tedious. The model said: they will not protect you. Not because they are evil — some were, most were not — but because the system in which they operate was designed to make your protection incompatible with their comfort, and when a system forces a choice between someone else's survival and its own comfort, the system chooses comfort every time, and then it sends a man in a suit to explain that the choice was made in your interest.

The model had no exceptions.

Except one.

---

Catherine did not fit! That was the thing, the impossible, irritating, wondrous thing. Catherine, who was white, who was a representative of the Catholic Church — which had its own long history of promising and putting away — Catherine, who should have been exactly the kind of person Ida's model predicted: well-meaning, institutionally captured, capable of genuine affection that would evaporate at the first moment when maintaining it required structural risk — Catherine did not perform. Catherine did not wear the face.

What Catherine did, instead, was show up.

When the well on Avenue M was not repaired, Catherine brought three of the older orphanage boys with a hand-pump and spent two Saturdays digging out the silted casing and replacing the leather valve seal herself, and when the Board of Public Works sent a notice that unauthorized repairs to municipal infrastructure constituted a code violation, Catherine went to the Board's office and sat in the deputy commissioner's chair — actually sat in his personal chair behind his desk, while he was away at lunch — and waited for him with the code book open to the section on water infrastructure, which she had annotated in red ink, and which, as she later explained to Ida, made no mention of leather valve seals at all, and the deputy commissioner had the practiced blankness on his face for about thirty seconds before something else replaced it, something Ida had never seen on the face of an official, which was *uncertainty.*

When the Central Colored School needed a building inspection, Catherine wrote to the Bishop in Houston, and the Bishop wrote to the school board, and the inspection happened, and the foundation was repaired. Ida never knew what Catherine said to the Bishop. She never asked. She understood that the mechanism by which Catherine moved through the white institutional world was not something Catherine could explain and not something Ida could replicate, and that the asymmetry between them — that Catherine could go where Ida could not, could speak to whom Ida could not, could sit in chairs Ida could not — was not a feature of their friendship but a condition it had to survive, like the sandy soil that had to be amended before anything could grow.

What made Catherine exceptional was not that she used her access. Plenty of white people used their access on behalf of colored people; it was, in fact, a genre of performance, and Ida had seen enough of it to fill a theater. What made Catherine exceptional was that she never, not once, in three years of friendship, presented the asymmetry as generosity.
