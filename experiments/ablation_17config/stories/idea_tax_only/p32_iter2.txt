# The Lodge

## I.

The bus climbed and the sophomore named Devon Watts threw up into a plastic bag for the third time since the valley floor and nobody sitting near him moved to help because they had already used up their help at the last rest stop and the rest stop before that, and besides, the bag was holding. Two rows back, Jeanine Calder pressed her right knee against the cold wall of the bus and counted the seconds until the ibuprofen kicked in. It would not kick in. The knee was not inflamed; it was structurally compromised — a partial tear of the lateral collateral ligament sustained three weeks ago during practice, unreported, because Jeanine Calder had waited four years to make the traveling roster and she would fence on the leg or she would not fence at all, and both of those options ended in the same place, which was the end of fencing. She had told no one. She had told God, briefly, in the shower, but God had not responded and the knee had not improved, and so the negotiation was over.

Across the aisle, Marcus Delane had his headphones on and his eyes closed and was not listening to music. The headphones were a barricade. He was thinking about the fact that his mother had not said good luck when she dropped him at the school parking lot at five a.m. She had said *don't get hurt*, which meant the same thing as *I don't believe you can win*, which meant the same thing as everything she had ever said to him about fencing, which was a sport she regarded as an affectation for a boy from their neighborhood, a costume he was putting on that would never fit.

In the front row, Coach Elaine Mori gripped her clipboard and stared at the bracket sheet she had printed at the hotel business center and already annotated with her cramped, furious handwriting. The annotations were lies of optimism. She had drawn arrows suggesting paths through the elimination rounds that depended on upsets she did not believe in. She believed in preparation, in footwork drills repeated until the gymnasium floor bore scuff marks like a language only she could read, and she believed in the absolute irrelevance of preparation when the resource gap was the size of the one between Ridgemont High School and St. Elias Preparatory Academy. St. Elias had a salle. Ridgemont had half a gymnasium shared with the junior varsity basketball team and a drama class that kept leaving set pieces on the run. St. Elias had a private coach for each weapon. Ridgemont had Elaine Mori, who coached all three, and Gary Luntz, the assistant, who was currently asleep against the window four rows back with a flask of Jim Beam tucked into the interior pocket of his parka like a secret organ. Gary was not an alcoholic. Gary was a man who had been asked to volunteer-coach a fencing team for a $400 seasonal stipend and had made a series of reasonable accommodations with the emotional demands of that arrangement.

The bus downshifted on a switchback and Devon Watts threw up again and this time the bag did not hold.

Outside the windows, the sky was a color that did not belong to any sky anyone on the bus had seen before. It was green-gray, underslung, compressed — the light that comes before certain kinds of weather that people in the valley do not have vocabulary for because they have never lived at altitude. Anna Chu, a freshman making her first trip, looked at it and thought *weird*. Tomás Garza, a junior who had grown up in the Sangre de Cristos before his family moved to Ridgemont's suburban lowlands, looked at it and thought *wrong*, but the thought was quiet and unfinished and was buried almost immediately by the thought that followed it, which was that he had forgotten to pack his body cord and would have to borrow one, which meant asking, which meant admitting. Marco Reyes saw the sky and said, to no one, "That looks like shit," and nobody answered because Marco Reyes said everything to no one. Devon Watts did not see the sky because he was on his hands and knees in the aisle. Coach Mori did not see it because she was staring at a bracket. Gary Luntz did not see it because he was asleep. The driver, a contract hire named Phil who had driven this mountain road for eleven years, saw it and felt something tighten in his lower back that was not a muscle but was instead the place where the body stores the knowledge that the conscious mind refuses to process. He said nothing. He was not paid to interpret weather. He was paid to deliver the bus.

Eleven consecutive losses to St. Elias Preparatory Academy. This was the number and everyone on the bus knew it the way you know the altitude of the place where you live — not as a fact you recite but as a pressure you have internalized so completely it has become the weight of the air you breathe. Eleven losses. Not close losses. The first three had been close, years ago, under a different Ridgemont coach. The rest were slaughters. Four years prior, a St. Elias épéeist had run the score to 15–2 in an individual bout and continued to attack after the fifteenth touch, landing two more hits while the referee shouted halt. The referee intervened. The stands — such as they were, at that particular regional — had laughed. Not all of them. Enough of them. The Ridgemont fencer who had been hit was now in college and no longer fenced and had not told anyone why she stopped, but the reason was a 5-2 composite of physics and humiliation: two unnecessary punctures through a lamé jacket, delivered by a seventeen-year-old who wanted to make a point about the ontological distance between their programs. That seventeen-year-old had graduated. It did not matter. St. Elias produced them serially, like a factory for beautiful indifference.

The bus rounded the final switchback and the lodge appeared. It was larger than any of them had expected — a sprawling timber-and-stone structure from the 1930s, three stories, with a steep-pitched roof designed to shed snow and a wraparound porch that gave it the look of a grande dame who had been left too long in the cold. The parking area was gravel, partially cleared, and across it, already occupying the prime position nearest the entrance, was the St. Elias bus. It was a touring coach, not a school bus — chartered from a company whose name was printed on the side in silver letters. On the flank: a decal for a sporting goods company, a decal for a financial services firm, a decal for an orthopedic clinic. The bus said, in its lettering and its cleanliness and its orientation on the gravel, *We arrived first because we always arrive first because arriving first is what arrival means.*

Devon Watts looked up from the aisle and saw it through the window and lay back down.

Jeanine Calder saw it and her knee pulsed as though the ligament had its own opinion.

Tomás Garza saw it and said a word under his breath in Spanish that his grandmother would have slapped him for.

Coach Mori saw it and put the bracket sheet in her bag with the care of someone filing a document they know to be fiction.

And then the bus pulled to a stop and the doors opened and the cold came in — not the cold of the valley, not the manageable theatrical cold of their November practices in the unheated half-gymnasium, but the cold of seven thousand feet in late January, a cold that entered the lungs like a physical object and announced that the body was not welcome here, that the body was a warm thing in a place that did not want warm things.

They filed off the bus. Thirty of them, if you counted Gary Luntz, which most of them did not. They wore parkas over tracksuits over thermals. They carried rolling bags of fencing gear that clattered over the gravel like the luggage of a traveling circus. They were Ridgemont. They were here because they had qualified, technically, through a regional tournament that St. Elias had not attended because St. Elias had qualified through a different, more prestigious regional that Ridgemont had not been invited to. They were here because the bracket required a certain number of teams and Ridgemont was, by elimination, one of them. They knew this. Every one of them knew this in a different way and in the same way, the way that thirty separate instruments can play the same note, each with its own timbre and each equally defeated by the pitch.

Vera Solokov was the last one off the bus.

She was seventeen. She was Ridgemont's first sabreist — their best fencer, by a margin that was visible to anyone who watched a single bout, a margin that was also the loneliest distance in the world because being the best on a team that always lost was like being the tallest building in a city that was sinking. She was five-foot-seven with dark hair cut short and a stillness to her face that people mistook for calm. It was not calm. It was the surface of a lake under which very large things moved in very organized patterns.

She came down the bus steps with her gear bag over one shoulder and she looked across the gravel lot at the lodge porch and she saw him.

Nico Kress was leaning against the porch railing. He wore a St. Elias warm-up jacket — navy blue, the school crest embroidered over the heart — and he was not wearing a hat or gloves despite the cold, because Nico Kress did not perform discomfort. He was tall. He was exactly the kind of tall that meant he had reach advantage on every strip he stepped onto and he knew it and he used it and he had never once apologized for having been born with arms like that. He was talking to another St. Elias fencer but he was not looking at them. He was looking at the Ridgemont bus. He was looking at the last person stepping off it.

The narrator enters Vera Solokov's mind and what the narrator finds there is not hatred. Hatred is simple. Hatred has a direction and a logic and when it is exhausted it lies down. What the narrator finds in Vera Solokov is a thing with teeth and a pulse and a history — a thing that tightened when Nico Kress's gaze found her across forty yards of gravel, a thing that did not tighten the way a muscle tightens in defense but the way a string tightens when it is tuned, the way a thing tightens when it is being made ready to produce a sound. She did not look away from him. She did not wave. She hefted her bag higher on her shoulder and walked toward the lodge, and the gravel crunched under her shoes, and the green-gray sky pressed down on the mountain, and Nico Kress watched her cross the lot with the careless posture of someone who has never once doubted the outcome of anything, and the thing inside Vera with teeth and a pulse and a history opened one eye and began, very quietly, to breathe.

## II.

The great hall of the lodge had been built eighty years ago for the purpose of impressing rich people with the idea that the wilderness could be tamed by exposed timber and large fireplaces, and it performed this function admirably under normal circumstances. Under the current circumstances — thirty-six conductive piste strips laid end to end over the hardwood floor, electronic scoring boxes cabled to a generator, a card table serving as the bout committee station, and ninety-odd teenagers in fencing whites milling between strips like an underfed army preparing for a war none of them had started — the great hall looked like what it was: a room being asked to be something it was never designed to become.

The tournament began at nine a.m. Seven schools. Three weapons. Individual and team events compressed into a two-day schedule that the bout committee had printed on paper and tacked to a corkboard near the coffee station, where it was studied with the intensity of scripture by coaches and ignored entirely by the fencers, who got their information by watching the strips.

Ridgemont lost the first bout. Épée, individual. Marcus Delane drew a fencer from Kittredge Academy and lasted three minutes. The score was 15–6 and the six touches were gifts — moments when the Kittredge fencer dropped his guard to adjust his body cord and Marcus lunged into the openings with the desperate accuracy of someone who understood that charity was the only economy in which he was solvent. He walked off the strip and sat down and put his mask on the floor and did not look at Coach Mori, who was already looking at the next bout sheet.

Ridgemont lost the second bout. Foil, individual. Anna Chu, the freshman, fenced with a mechanical correctness that was beautiful in the practice salle and useless against an opponent from Hargrove School who had no technique at all but who attacked with a continuous, sloppy aggression that the referee rewarded with right-of-way calls because the referee was human and humans interpret aggression as intention. The score was 15–9. Anna Chu walked off the strip and sat down next to Marcus Delane and they did not speak to each other because there was nothing to say that the score had not already said.

Ridgemont lost the third bout. Sabre, individual. Jeanine Calder, fencing on the knee she had not reported, won the first four touches on pure technique — her parry-ripostes were clean, her footwork was a lie told so convincingly that the leg almost believed it — and then, on the fifth touch, she pushed off her back leg on a fleche and the knee said no. Not loudly. The knee did not buckle or pop or do anything dramatic enough to elicit a medical halt. The knee simply subtracted forty percent of her explosiveness between one touch and the next, and the girl from St. Elias she was fencing felt the change the way a predator feels a limp and scored eleven consecutive touches. Fifteen–four. Jeanine walked off the strip without limping because she was Jeanine Calder and she would not — she would *not* — but in the space behind her sternum where she kept the things she would not let her face or her legs betray, something was folding in on itself with the slow, terrible patience of a building being demolished floor by floor.

And then the team sabre event began, and Vera Solokov stepped onto the strip.

Her opponent was a St. Elias fencer named Claire Bassett, whom Vera had lost to twice before — once at regionals last year, once at a summer invitational. Claire Bassett was good. Claire Bassett was the kind of good that came from twelve years of private coaching and a competitive travel schedule that had taken her to Paris and Budapest and a training camp outside Milan where the Italian national team's sabre coach had told her, personally, that her blade work was "interesting," which Claire Bassett had posted on her Instagram with three sword emojis and a hashtag that Vera had stared at for four minutes in the bathroom of her mother's apartment before putting the phone down. Vera did not have an Instagram account. Vera had a sabre and a bus pass and a fury so well organized that it resembled discipline.

The bout began. Vera came to the en garde line and saluted with a formality that was, in its precision, an act of aggression — a way of saying *I see all of you and I am not impressed by any of it.* Claire Bassett returned the salute with the easy, abbreviated motion of someone who had been taught that salutes were formalities and formalities were things you did quickly so you could get to the part that mattered.

The referee called *fence* and Vera attacked.

Not with her body first. With her blade. A preparation — a beat against Claire's blade in quarte, hard enough that the vibration traveled up Claire's arm and into her shoulder, a beat that said *this strip is mine and your weapon is here only because I permit it.* Then the advance, the arm extending, the cut to the flank that Claire parried but did not riposte in time because the beat had stolen a quarter-second of her reaction and a quarter-second at this level was everything. Touch. One–zero. Vera returned to the en garde line without celebrating, without pumping her fist, without doing any of the performative displays that fencers used to claim space and establish psychological dominance. She simply returned to the line. The absence of celebration was its own performance. It said: *I expected this.*

Two–zero. Three–zero. Claire Bassett called a halt to adjust her mask and used the pause to breathe in a way that she hoped looked routine and that Vera's eyes, watching from behind her own mask, catalogued as panic. Four–zero. The St. Elias bench was quiet. Coach Mori, for the first time all day, was leaning forward.

And then the lights went out.

Not the way lights go out in a storm — not the flicker, the brown-out, the momentary gap that allows the brain to catch up and file the experience under *temporary.* This was absolute.
