# FLUSH: A Killing on the Party Bus

---

## I.

There are fourteen of us, and we stand in a row.

We face east because the contracting company that delivered us — Hal's Honey Huts, Modesto, California, since 1994 — parked the flatbed facing west, and the forklifts unloaded us in the order we sat, which means we face the direction nobody intended us to face, which means we face the sunrise. I have watched three of them now from this position: Friday, Saturday, Sunday. Each time the light finds the same sequence. First Unit 1, whom I have never spoken to and whose door has not opened correctly since a man kicked it shut with such force during a Garth Brooks festival in Bakersfield that the hinge pin bent eleven degrees off true. Then Unit 2. Then Unit 3. On down the line, the light moving across our faces like a finger across piano keys, until it reaches me.

I am Unit 6. I am called Blue Royale.

This is not a name I was given. It is a name I chose, silently, during my fourth deployment — an outdoor wedding in Lodi where the mother of the bride entered me and wept for nine minutes and then said, to no one, *You are not what I pictured for her,* and I understood she did not mean me. But I felt, in that moment, the weight of being the room in which a woman revised her understanding of her daughter's life, and I thought: I should have a name equal to the gravity of what I hold. So: Blue Royale. Blue for the obvious reason. Royale because I am not ironic. I am earnest in my belief that what happens inside me matters.

The others do not name themselves. Unit 7 has a busted latch that allows its door to swing freely in any wind above four miles per hour, producing a rhythmic clapping sound that I initially found maddening and now find companionable, the way a monk might come to love the bell that wakes him. Unit 12 was tipped onto its side at 2:17 PM on Thursday by four young women in matching swimsuits who screamed and then photographed the wreckage and then walked away, and Unit 12 has not been righted. It lies on its side in the flattened grass like a casualty, its chemical reservoir pooled against its eastern wall, and I confess I do not look in its direction. There is an etiquette to disaster among us, which is to say: we do not acknowledge it. We stand. We receive. We do not discuss what we have received.

The SunScorch Music Festival occupies four hundred acres of what was, until Wednesday, a cattle pasture belonging to the Erikkson family, who leased it for a sum I do not know but which was large enough to persuade them to remove seven hundred linear feet of barbed-wire fencing and allow one hundred and forty thousand strangers to urinate on their land across three days. The grounds are arranged in concentric rings of decreasing intimacy: the main stage at the center, a chrome-and-LED cathedral visible from the interstate; then the secondary stages, named after brands of tequila; then the beer tents, the food trucks, the merchandise pavilions with their racks of shirts no one will wear twice; then the VIP hill, which is not meaningfully elevated but is fenced and therefore sacred; and finally, at the outermost ring, us. The toilets. The perimeter.

Between the nearest beer tent and our row runs a corridor of grass that was green on Wednesday and is now the color of a bruise. Forty thousand feet have beaten it into a path. It is perhaps eighty yards long. People walk it reluctantly, in groups or alone, with the purposeful hunch of pilgrims approaching a shrine they do not respect but cannot avoid. They arrive needing us. They leave pretending they do not know us.

I have held accountants and addicts. I have held a woman who sang the entirety of "Ave Maria" while emptying her bladder, hitting every note. I have held a teenage boy who entered with two friends on a dare and stayed, after they left, for forty-five minutes, reading something on his phone that made him so still I thought he had died. He had not died. He had found something on that phone that required stillness to survive, and I gave him the only thing I have ever been able to give anyone: a locked door and enough silence to feel what needed feeling.

This is my function. Not the other thing. The other thing is mechanical, chemical, a matter of volumes and reagents. My function — my *true* function — is to be the room where the performance stops. Every person who enters me has been, for hours, a version of themselves designed for other people's eyes. They have been loud or beautiful or competent or funny. They have been the self that gets them through the crowd. And then they lock my door, and that self falls away like a coat, and what is left is a body. Just a body. Needing. Processing. Mortal.

I hold that. I hold the mortal part.

The morning light has reached me now. It turns my walls the color of a vein. Inside, I am dark and blue and clean — the chemical reservoir freshly serviced at 5:00 AM by a woman named Dolores who opens each of us with a key she wears on a lanyard decorated with cartoon tacos, and who once, while servicing Unit 9, said aloud: *Lord, what people do.* She was not wrong. But she was not entirely right. What people do is not the remarkable thing. What people *feel,* while they are doing what they do, in the privacy of a locked blue room that they believe has no memory and no opinion—

That is the remarkable thing.

That is what I keep.

## II.

They arrived on a bus. Not a school bus and not a charter bus but something in between — a decommissioned airport shuttle painted black with the words **PARTY WAGON** stenciled on the side in a font that wanted to be menacing but achieved only a kind of tipsy enthusiasm, the W already peeling. It disgorged them at 1:43 PM on Friday: twelve men in matching custom tank tops the color of kindergarten safety vests, each printed with:

**DKΩ REUNION TOUR — STILL SENDING IT**

They were already sunburned. I do not know how. They had been on a bus. But their shoulders and the bridges of their noses were already the color of medium-rare flank steak, as though their skin had been preparing for exposure all week, preheating itself, so eager to participate in what was coming that it had begun to cook from the inside.

They established what one of them called "base camp" and another called "the command center" and a third called "right here, bro, right here" approximately ninety yards from the main stage, in a patch of grass they claimed with a blue tarp, a cooler the size of a child's coffin, and a Bluetooth speaker already playing something with an accusatory bass line. Their average age was, by my estimate, thirty-one. Their average volume was that of a man who has recently been told something he does not want to believe and has chosen to refute it by being louder than the fact.

I have observed fraternities before. There is a grammar to them — a syntax of belonging that is performed through repetition, ritual, and the relentless management of physical proximity. They stand close. They grip each other's shoulders. They repeat each other's names as though confirming, at twenty-second intervals, that everyone is still present, still accounted for, still inside the circle: *Garrett, Garrett, dude, Garrett. Tanner. Tanner, bro. Kyle, Kyle, KYLE.* The names are incantations. The names are a roll call against disappearance.

Over the next four hours, eight of the twelve used the porta-potty row. Three of them entered me.

---

The first was the one they called Garrett.

He locked my door with the deliberateness of a man entering a confessional. He did not use me — not in the mechanical sense. He stood. He placed both hands flat against my walls, one on each side, as though bracing himself, or as though trying to feel my pulse, and he whispered a name.

*Marin.*

He said it once. He said it with the careful articulation of a man who has been told not to say it — by therapists, by friends, by some agreement he signed or should have signed — and who has been saving it for a room with no witnesses. He said it the way you say a word in a language you're losing, testing whether your mouth still remembers the shape.

Then he left. He unlocked my door and walked out and within seven seconds I heard him shout, "GARRETT'S BACK, BABY, WHO NEEDS A BEER," and the circle closed around him again, and the name *Marin* stayed in me like a handprint on glass.

---

The second was the one they called Tanner.

Tanner was large. Tanner filled me. His shoulders nearly touched my walls simultaneously, which is not a thing I say to diminish him — it is a thing I say because when a man that large cries, the physics of it are different. The sound has nowhere to go. It stays close. It reverberates.

He cried for eleven seconds.

I counted. I count because counting is the only form of attention I can offer. I cannot touch. I cannot ask. I can only measure the duration of what I am given, and by measuring it, I can at least confirm: *this happened. This was real. Someone felt this, and it lasted this long, and I was here for it.*

Eleven seconds. Not a sob. Not a breakdown. A leak. A controlled, pressurized release — like a valve turned open and then shut — and in those eleven seconds his face, which I could see only as a shape in my dim interior, disassembled into something so private that I felt I should not have seen it, and then reassembled into the face he wore for his brothers, and he stood, and he flushed — though he had produced nothing to flush, the gesture was habitual, an alibi — and he left.

On the way out he said, to someone waiting: "Dude, do NOT go in there. I destroyed that thing."

Laughter. A shove. The circle.

---

The third was the one they called Marcus.

Marcus entered, locked the door, and removed his phone from the waterproof pouch around his neck. He activated the front-facing camera. For the next two minutes and ten seconds, he photographed his own face. Not selfies — not the performative, angled, chin-down photographs that people take to transmit to others. These were clinical. Close. He was examining something. He turned his head left, then right. He pressed a finger to his jaw. He pulled down the skin beneath his left eye. He held the phone six inches from his face and stared at the image with the detached concentration of a man looking at a mole his doctor hasn't seen yet.

He took four photographs. He studied each. He deleted each. Then he put his phone away and closed his eyes and stood in my darkness for forty-five seconds without moving.

I do not know what he was looking for. I know he did not find it. I know that the face he examined was the face of a man who had begun to suspect that time was doing something to him that his brothers, in the forgiving light of reunion, might not yet see — but that the camera, which forgives nothing, had already documented.

He left. He yelled something about tequila.
