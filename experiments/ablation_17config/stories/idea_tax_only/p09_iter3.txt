# The Mouth of Something Unspeakable

---

## I.

---

**THE TOMBSTONE EPITAPH**
*Tombstone, Arizona Territory*
*Wednesday, March 8, 1893*
*Page 2, Column 3*

**SINGULAR DESTRUCTION OF STOCK IN THE SAN PEDRO VALLEY**

The ranchers of the middle San Pedro, being men not generally disposed to fancy, have these past weeks reported to our offices a phenomenon that, while it may yet find explanation in the predatory habits of the mountain lion or some kindred agent, deserves public airing, if for no other reason than the number and sober character of the witnesses.

Mr. Elias Drennan, whose 640-acre homestead patent lies some nine miles east of Fairbank, reports the loss of fourteen head of cattle since the second week of February. The carcasses, discovered in groups of two and three across the bajada below the Dragoon foothills, exhibited, by Mr. Drennan's account, a uniformity of condition that marks them as unusual. Each animal was found wholly drained of blood, with no pooling visible upon the ground beneath or about the remains. The incisions — for incisions they appeared to be — followed a pattern Mr. Drennan describes as "geometrical," being composed of parallel lines and perfect arcs such as no claw or tooth in the experience of this territory's stockmen has been known to produce.

Mr. Drennan's account is corroborated in its essentials by Mr. F.W. Holladay, of the Tres Alamos grant, who has lost nine head under identical circumstances, and by Mr. Serrano, the Mexican vaquero employed at the Babocomari ranch headquarters, who discovered the most recent carcasses and who, we are informed, will no longer ride night patrol in the eastern pastures.

We advance no theory. We note only that the losses above enumerated occurred within a band of territory stretching some fifteen miles along the western base of the Dragoon Mountains, and that no carcass has been found above the 4,200-foot contour line, a fact to which we shall return if future developments warrant.

We invite the attention of the Territorial Livestock Board.

---

**THE TOMBSTONE EPITAPH**
*Tombstone, Arizona Territory*
*Saturday, April 1, 1893*
*Page 1, Column 4*

**THE SAN PEDRO VALLEY MYSTERY — CONTINUED AND DEEPENED**

We return to a subject upon which we had hoped the Territorial Livestock Board, or some officer of the county, might have by now offered comment. No such comment having arrived, it falls to this paper to extend its record.

Since our report of March 8 the losses in the San Pedro drainage have not ceased but multiplied. We have received testimony, sworn in several cases before Justice of the Peace W.R. Orrick, from no fewer than eleven ranchers and stock-tenders describing the destruction of upward of sixty head of cattle and horses between Fairbank and the southern end of the Sulphur Springs Valley. In every case the conditions are as previously described: complete exsanguination, no blood upon the ground, and the characteristic parallel and curvilinear wounds, which Mr. Holladay, in his second deposition, calls "as regular as if drawn with a compass and straight-edge."

To these facts we add the following:

First — That Mr. Thomas Bidwell, foreman of the Cochise Consolidated Mining Company's surface operations at the Middle Pass diggings, reports that several of his men have refused to work the night shift, citing sounds from the Dragoon canyons that Bidwell himself, who is not a fanciful man, describes as "like nothing produced by a beast I know of." He likens it to the resonance of a large bell struck underground.

Second — That Dr. Esteban Carranza of Contention City has examined three of the more recently discovered carcasses and states, in a letter to this editor, that the wounds were produced not by cutting but by "a process more consistent with absorption," the tissue at their margins being not severed but *dissolved*, as though by a chemical action of great subtlety and force.

Third — That the 4,200-foot contour line to which we previously referred no longer obtains. Carcasses have now been found at elevations exceeding 5,000 feet, on the western slopes of the Dragoons themselves.

We ask again: Where is the Territorial Livestock Board? Where is the sheriff? Where is any voice of authority competent to name what is occurring in this valley? The citizens of Cochise County deserve more than silence from their institutions. They deserve at minimum an investigation, and at maximum the truth.

**THE TOMBSTONE EPITAPH**
*Tombstone, Arizona Territory*
*Thursday, April 20, 1893*
*Page 1, Columns 1–2*

**TO THE CITIZENS OF COCHISE COUNTY — A MATTER BEYOND CATTLE**

It is with considerable reluctance that the editor of a paper devoted to the factual and the verifiable sets down the account that follows. We do so not because we have abandoned our commitment to reason but because reason itself demands that evidence be reported, howsoever it may discomfit.

On the night of April 14, a party of five men — Mr. Drennan, Mr. Holladay, Mr. Bidwell, and two miners in Mr. Bidwell's employ — rode into the Dragoon foothills with the intention of discovering the agent responsible for the destruction of stock heretofore reported in these columns. They were armed. They carried lanterns. They proceeded up Middle Pass canyon to a point approximately one mile above the Cochise Consolidated diggings.

It is here that the account of Mr. Holladay — the only member of the party willing to commit his observations to paper — diverges from anything we are able to reconcile with the natural order.

Mr. Holladay reports that the party, upon entering a box canyon below the ridge line, discovered the ground to be "warm, as though a fire burned beneath it." The rock faces, he states, were "sweating," being covered in a moisture he describes as viscous and possessing a "sweetish, mineral smell, like blood and like limestone." The horses would not advance. One of the miners' horses threw its rider and fled back down the canyon.

The sound previously described by Mr. Bidwell — the subterranean bell — was audible and, by Mr. Holladay's testimony, "was not beneath us any longer but around us, as though the canyon walls were producing it."

Mr. Holladay states that the party then observed, in the beam of Mr. Bidwell's lantern, what he will say only was "a shape, or a part of a shape" visible upon the canyon wall — not carved or painted upon the rock but *continuous with it*, as though the rock itself had organized into a form. He describes it as "a pattern that looked like it was breathing" and states that Mr. Drennan discharged his rifle into the wall, whereupon the rock "bled, or something in it bled."

The party retreated. Mr. Drennan has since refused to speak of the matter. The two miners in Mr. Bidwell's employ departed the county the following morning. Mr. Bidwell himself came to our offices on April 16 and sat for twenty minutes without speaking before leaving again.

We publish this account knowing it will be dismissed in certain quarters. We publish it because dismissal is not refutation. Something is occurring in the Dragoon Mountains. It has killed cattle. It has driven men from their employment and their composure. It is, by every evidence we can gather, *growing.*

We ask — we demand — that the authorities of this territory attend to it.

— John Philip Clum, Editor

&nbsp;

The issue of Saturday, May 6, 1893, contained no follow-up. Nor did the issue of May 13, nor May 20, nor any issue thereafter. The *Tombstone Epitaph* continued publication through the spring and summer of that year. It reported on mining claim disputes, a fire at the Russ House hotel, the result of a baseball match between Tombstone and Bisbee. In August it printed a lovely account of the monsoon rains.

The cattle deaths in the San Pedro Valley do not appear again.

The account of Holladay's party does not appear again.

The word *Dragoon*, in any context other than routine mining notices, does not appear again.

There was no retraction. There was no correction. There was no explanation. The paper did not say it had been mistaken. It did not say the mystery had been solved. It said nothing. It moved on. It entered, with the rest of the county and the territory and the century, into a silence so complete and so orderly that it could only have been built.

---

## II.

---

The road from Tucson is two hours of nothing and then the light changes. It changes because the Dragoons appear — not gradually, not the way ranges usually disclose themselves across a basin, but all at once, like a wall that was always there and has simply decided to become visible. Alma Jessup has driven this highway before, years ago, on a collecting trip that took her through the Chiricahuas, but she does not remember the Dragoons looking like this. They are darker than they should be. The limestone faces catch the morning sun and refuse it.

She pulls off at the Dragoon exit and follows the county road south. The air through the cracked window tastes of copper and caliche. Tailings piles rise in the middle distance, grey-white, like the mountains' dandruff. She checks the letter on the passenger seat — not Calloway's letters, those are in the trunk in their archival box, but the recent one, the one that brought her here. Typed on Cochise Consolidated Mining Company letterhead, signed by a Mr. Arthur Peck, Operations Supervisor, Middle Pass Diggings.

*Dr. Jessup,* it reads, though she is not a doctor and has corrected this in her reply, *we have exposed some remains and would value your assessment. The men will not work around them. I am told you are the person to contact for this kind of thing.*

This kind of thing. She does not know what kind of thing Mr. Peck means. She suspects a mammoth. The Dragoons are the right geology for Pleistocene megafauna — limestone karst, solution cavities, the kind of alkaline matrix that preserves bone beautifully. A mammoth would be good. A mammoth in situ, fully articulated, partially calcite-encased: that could be a paper. That could be the paper that moves her from the periphery to the center.

She passes through the town without stopping. It is barely a town — a post office, a bar, a shuttered assay office with a historical plaque she cannot read from the road. The mining company gate is three miles farther. A man in a hardhat waves her through without checking her name.

Peck meets her at the shaft house. He is fifty, sunburnt, with the compressed manner of a man who manages other men and does not enjoy it. He gives her a hardhat. He gives her a lamp. He does not shake her hand.

"How deep?" she asks.

"Two hundred feet. Give or take."

"And the collapse — when?"

"Eleven days ago. Routine blast opened a lateral chamber nobody knew was there. The men saw what was inside and walked out. I've had a crew of four willing to go back down. The rest won't."

"What did they see?"

Peck looks at her the way men in the field always look at her — calculating the distance between what she is and what they expected.

"They say bones," he says. "I say I don't know what they are. That's why you're here."

The cage drops. The shaft is wet, warmer than it should be at this depth. Alma counts the timbers as they descend — old Douglas fir, squared and fitted, the standard timbering of a hard-rock mine. At one hundred and fifty feet she notices that the grain of the wood has begun to curve. Not rot, not sag, not the bowing of insufficient support. The fibers of the timber are bending. They are bending inward, toward the center of the mountain, as though the wood had been alive when it was set and had continued, in its dead way, to grow toward something it wanted.

"That normal?" she says to Peck, touching a timber.

"That's new since the collapse," he says, and says nothing else.

The lateral chamber is behind a plywood barricade someone has nailed across the drift. Peck pulls the plywood aside. The air behind it is different — warmer, wetter, carrying a smell Alma associates with the insides of living things. Not decay. Not rot. Something metabolic. Something present-tense.

She steps through.

Her lamp finds the remains.

They are enormous. She has worked with sauropod material, with the great femurs and vertebrae of Jurassic giants, and this is larger. But it is not fossilized. The surface of the material — she will not yet call it bone — has the luminosity of living calcium, the wet mineral sheen of nacre or fresh antler. It is white in a way that makes the lamplight seem dirty. And it is wrong. Not wrong in the way of the unfamiliar — she has seen enough new species to know that strangeness recedes with study — but wrong in the way of the *impossible.* The structures she is looking at follow geometric principles. Right angles. Parallel curves. Surfaces that meet at mathematically precise intersections. Bone does not do this. Bone is a record of force and growth; it carries the irregularity of life. What she is looking at carries the regularity of *design.*

She sets down her pack. She unpacks her calipers, her measuring tape, her notebook. She takes measurements. She is careful. She records the apparent composition (calcium-ite?ite? calcium phosphate? — no, the crystalline structure is wrong for hydroxyapatite; it refar closer to calcite, but calcite is not a biological mineral, not at this scale, not organized like this). She records the geometry. She sketches the cross-section where the blast exposed an interior surface.

The interior surface has chambers.

She records this. She notes the size and regularity of the chambers. She thinks: pneumatic bone. She thinks: birds, theropods, the great hollow bones of flying things. She thinks: this is a find. This is *the* find.

She does not think: the chambers are connected by passages.

She does not think: the passages have the branching pattern of a circulatory system, not a skeletal one.

She does not think about the timbers bending toward this place, or the warmth of the air, or the wet metabolic smell, or the fact that the calcite surface, where her bare hand rests against it to steady herself while she sketches, is precisely body temperature.

She records fourteen measurements. Every one of them is exact. Every one of them is a detail of something she has no frame to see.

## III.

---

The room is above the company store. Someone has made it up for her — a cot, a desk, a washbasin, a window facing the Dragoons. The window faces the Dragoons. She did not choose the room. The room was ready. The window was already facing that direction.

She unpacks the archival box. She has been carrying it since Colorado — since the last of Calloway's letters arrived, three months after the county clerk recorded his death as a riding accident, two lines in a ledger, in handwriting that matched no other entry on the page.

Henry Calloway. She had known him only through correspondence, but she had known him well in the way that correspondence allows — which is to say, she had known his mind, his syntax, the way he organized an observation before committing it to paper. He wrote the way good scientists write: with precision that admitted pleasure, with a care for language that was itself a form of care for the world language described. He had read her monograph on Cretaceous marine fauna of the Western Interior Seaway — a paper no one else had read, published in a journal no one subscribed to — and had written to her with three questions so specific and so intelligent that she had cried, alone in her rented room in Golden, not from sadness but from the overwhelming relief of being *seen* by a mind that could see.
