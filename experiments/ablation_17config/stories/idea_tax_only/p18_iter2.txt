# The Child Without a Shadow

## I.

It was not discovered at my birth, which would have made a neater story and a simpler filing. It was discovered on a Tuesday in what I believe was autumn, though the light that day behaved like summer — that flat, unforgiving light that presses everything into its outline and demands an accounting of edges. My aunt Petra had set me on the kitchen floor in a square of sun that fell through the window above the sink. I was old enough to sit upright, young enough that sitting upright was still considered an achievement worth an audience. Petra stood at the counter slicing something. My aunt Elena was somewhere behind me, in the hallway perhaps, carrying laundry or not carrying laundry — she has since surrendered the memory of that morning, so I can only reconstruct her from the angles that remained.

The light held me in its square. I sat in it, and the floor around me was bright, and the floor beneath me was bright, and there was no part of the floor near my body that was dark.

Petra looked down. I imagine she was checking on me in the peripheral, mechanical way people check on children — not searching for anything, just confirming the persistence of a small life. But something about the evenness of the light around me made her look again. She set down the knife. She came around the counter and stood over me, and her shadow fell across the tiles in a long, faithful column, and mine did not fall at all.

She called for Elena. Elena came. They stood on either side of me looking down at the place where a shadow should have collected against my legs, my folded hands, the curve of my back, and found only floor. Elena knelt and moved her hand behind me slowly, as if she might find the shadow hiding, pressed flat against the baseboard or pooled behind the table leg. She did not find it. Petra picked me up and carried me to the window and held me in the direct light, turning me as you might turn a glass to look for a crack, and the light passed through my edges and struck the wall behind me with nothing subtracted.

They looked at each other. I do not know what that look contained. I was too young to read faces, and by the time I was old enough to ask, the document had already arrived and the question had been absorbed into a larger, more procedural silence.

I did not have a shadow. I want to be precise about what this means, because precision is the only freedom available to me, and I have practiced it the way other children practice instruments. It does not mean I was transparent. It does not mean light passed through me as through a window. I was opaque, solid, present — I cast a reflection, I blocked a doorway, I could be photographed and the photograph showed an ordinary child with dark hair and a serious mouth. But light, when it reached me, did not behave as it behaves when it reaches other solid things. It was not stopped. It was not consumed. It arrived at my surfaces and — I lack a better word for this — *continued.* As if I were a suggestion the light had considered and declined to argue with. As if my body were a draft of a body, convincing in all respects except the one that mattered to the Bureau of Unfinished Souls.

The Bureau's position, which I can recite because it has been read aloud to me in seven different hearing rooms across what I estimate to be four years, is that a shadowless person is an incomplete person — a soul whose issuance was interrupted, a life begun but not ratified, a being that exists in the administrative gap between intention and execution. Such a person is not, in the Bureau's terminology, *missing* a shadow. Such a person was never *issued* one. The distinction matters because a missing shadow implies a complete soul from which something has been taken, which would make the case a matter for a different department — Recovery of Lost Attributes, I think, or perhaps Restoration Services, though I have never seen either office and am not certain they exist in any form beyond their listing in the Bureau's directory, which is not a reliable indicator of existence. An *unissued* shadow, however, implies an unfinished soul, and an unfinished soul is, by codified precedent, the rightful custodial property of the Bureau of Unfinished Souls, pending review, evaluation, and final determination of status.

The claim was filed before my aunts thought to contest it. I say "before" but I should say "outside the sequence of" — the Bureau does not operate inside the same temporal current as the people who interact with it, and I have come to believe that a more accurate description is that the claim was filed *always,* that it existed as a standing condition of the Bureau's own architecture, and that my birth merely gave it a name to attach itself to. The document arrived eleven days after Petra noticed the light passing through me. It was on municipal letterhead. It was hand-delivered by a woman in a gray coat who did not wait for a signature. It informed my aunts that a preliminary claim of custodial interest had been registered regarding a minor shadowless person residing at their address, and that a hearing had been scheduled to assess the Bureau's jurisdictional standing, and that failure to appear would be interpreted as noncontesting, and that the hearing had already been rescheduled once due to an internal procedural adjustment, and that the new date was printed on an enclosed supplementary form, and that the enclosed supplementary form was available by request from the Bureau's Filing Annex during operating hours, and that operating hours were subject to revision without notice.

Elena opened the letter. Petra did not. This is the first thing I need you to understand about them, and I will spend the rest of my life trying to explain what it means.

## II.

Petra did not open the letter, because Petra understood something about the letter that Elena did not, though neither of them could have articulated what that understanding was at the time, and I am not certain I can articulate it now, though I have had longer to study it than either of them.

What Petra understood was this: the letter was not an invitation. It was not even, properly, a threat. It was a *door.* The moment you opened it, you were inside the Bureau's house, standing in the Bureau's hallway, breathing the Bureau's air, and every step you took was a step deeper in. Petra understood this in her spine, in the base of her skull, in whatever part of the human body recognizes a trap by the quality of the silence around it. She did not think this thought in words. She thought it in the language of her hands, which picked me up off the floor and held me against her chest, and her feet, which carried me to the hallway closet and then away from the hallway closet to the car and then in the car to Cousin Marco's house forty minutes east, and then away from Cousin Marco's house because Marco had a municipal job and could not be trusted, though he was kind, and then to a motel, and then to another motel, and then in circles on a highway in the dark while I slept against her collarbone and she drove with one hand and held me with the other and the headlights of other cars washed over us and made no shadow on the seat.

It would be easy to say Petra acted out of panic. People who heard about our case later often said this. The meticulous ones — the lawyers, the advocates, the people who came after — all said that Petra's initial reaction was understandable but counterproductive, that by fleeing she had forfeited the opportunity for an early-stage procedural challenge, that if she had simply attended the preliminary hearing with the right documentation, the Bureau's claim might have been dismissed on jurisdictional grounds before it metastasized into the thing it became.

I do not think they were right. I think Petra was the only person who ever responded to the Bureau correctly, and the proof of this is that her response was the only one the Bureau could not metabolize. Everything else — every filing, every appeal, every hearing attended and form submitted and clause contested — the Bureau ate and converted into more of itself. Petra's refusal to engage was the one thing that produced no procedural offspring. It was sterile. It generated nothing. And the Bureau, which feeds on generation, which exists only insofar as it produces the next form, the next hearing, the next sub-clause requiring the next sub-hearing — the Bureau had no mechanism for processing a woman who was simply not there.

But Petra could not be simply not there forever, because Elena was there, inside the letter, studying it.

Elena opened the letter and read it three times. The first time, I think, she read it the way anyone reads a threatening document — scanning for the worst news, the deadline, the consequence. The second time, she read it slowly, and I imagine her lips moved, because Elena's lips always moved when she was trying to hold language still long enough to see its seams. The third time, she read it with a pencil. She underlined. She circled. She drew a small star next to the paragraph about jurisdictional standing and a question mark next to the paragraph about the supplementary form, and she wrote in the margin, in her small square handwriting, *"which annex?"*

She stayed up that night at the kitchen table with the letter and a legal pad and three books she had driven to the library to borrow before it closed — one on family law, one on municipal guardianship procedures, and one on a topic I did not understand at the time and still do not fully understand, which seemed to concern the history of a government agency that may or may not have been a precursor to the Bureau, or may have been the Bureau under a different name, or may have been a department the Bureau had consumed and was still in the process of digesting. Elena read. She took notes. She wrote a draft of a letter in response to the letter, and then she crossed out the draft and wrote another draft, and then she crossed out the second draft and went back to the books, and at some point she fell asleep at the table with her face on the legal pad, and when she lifted her head in the morning, the ink of her notes had transferred to her cheek in reverse, and she went to the bathroom mirror and stood there trying to read them.

I know this because Elena told me later. Not in a moment of confidence — Elena did not have moments of confidence the way other people did; she had moments of *precision,* where she would suddenly need to establish the exact sequence of events as if failure to do so constituted a filing error in the record of her own life. She told me about the night with the legal pad the way she told me about everything: as evidence. And I held it, as I hold everything, because holding is what I do.

Elena composed the first formal response to the Bureau the following afternoon. It requested clarification of three procedural points, cited two sections of the guardianship code she believed were applicable, and asked that the hearing be stayed pending review of the Bureau's jurisdictional authority. She filed it by certified mail. She received a response nine days later acknowledging receipt and informing her that her request for a stay had been forwarded to the appropriate sub-office, and that the hearing date remained unchanged, and that a supplementary form regarding the stay request was enclosed, and that the supplementary form required notarization by a Bureau-recognized notary, and that a list of Bureau-recognized notaries was available by request from the Filing Annex.

She requested the list. The list arrived. It contained eleven names, six of which corresponded to addresses that did not exist, three of which corresponded to offices that were permanently closed, and two of which corresponded to the same person, a man named Almeida, who operated out of a room above a bakery in a town ninety minutes south and kept hours that changed according to a schedule he would not share. Elena found him. Elena got the form notarized. Elena filed the notarized form. Elena received a response. The response generated three new requirements.

She attacked each one.

I describe this not to suggest that Elena's work was futile — though it was — but to show you the shape of her love. Elena did not know how to hold a child against her chest and drive in circles in the dark. She did not know how to refuse a letter. She knew how to *read* a letter, and reading, for Elena, was not a passive act but a form of combat — slow, meticulous, conducted at the level of the sentence and the clause, where she believed all power ultimately resided. She was not wrong about this. Power does reside in the clause. What she did not yet understand — what it would take her years and the loss of several irreplaceable things to understand — was that the Bureau's clauses were not written to be mastered. They were written to be *studied.* There is a difference. A clause written to be mastered yields, eventually, to mastery. A clause written to be studied yields only to more study, which yields only to more clauses, which is not yielding at all but the opposite: a multiplication of surface that mimics the sensation of progress while the distance to the center remains precisely the same.

But I could not tell her this. I could not tell her because I was very young and did not yet have the words, and later, when I had the words, I could not tell her because by then she had invested so much of herself in the study that to tell her it was futile would have been to tell her that the particular shape her love had taken was the wrong shape, and I could not do this, not because it was untrue but because she would not have survived it, and I needed her to survive. I needed both of them. This was my understanding even then, even before I could have called it an understanding: I needed the one who ran and the one who read, and I needed them to keep doing what they did, and I needed never to tell either of them what I already knew, which was that neither method would work and that the only thing sustaining either of them was the belief that it might.

I stood in the doorway between the kitchen and the hallway and watched them. Petra was on her phone in the hallway, speaking to someone about a place we could go next. Elena was at the table, annotating. The light from the kitchen window fell across the floor and touched my feet and passed through me and made no shadow.

They were performing the same act. I could see this then and I can see it now and the seeing has never brought me any closer to being able to help.

## III.

The Bureau of Unfinished Souls is housed in a building I have entered many times and cannot describe consistently, because it is not a consistent building. I will tell you what it was the first time, which is the visit I remember most completely, though completeness may be the wrong word for a memory of a place that does not cohere.

From the outside: a municipal office. Single story. Beige stucco with a slightly darker beige trim that someone had chosen, at some point, as if the distinction between the two beiges mattered, and perhaps it did, inside the Bureau's aesthetic code, in the same way that the distinction between an *unissued* shadow and a *missing* shadow matters — invisibly, procedurally, totally. There was a parking lot with painted lines that did not quite accommodate standard-sized cars, so every vehicle was slightly crooked, slightly encroaching. A flagpole with no flag. A front door with a pull handle and a small sign that said BUREAU OF UNFINISHED SOULS — PUBLIC ENTRANCE and beneath it, in smaller letters, BY APPOINTMENT, and beneath that, in smaller letters still, something I could not read because the font was too small and Petra was carrying me and moving quickly and Elena was already ahead of us, holding the door.

Inside, the building was larger than its outside. I do not mean this metaphorically. I mean that the distance from the front door to the first interior wall was greater than the distance from the front door to the back of the building as seen from the parking lot. Not dramatically greater — perhaps twelve or fifteen additional feet — but enough that if you had been measuring, if you had carried a tape measure the way Elena would later carry a tape measure to her third visit, you would have found a discrepancy you could not resolve. Elena, when she discovered this, wrote it down in her notebook and tried to file a formal observation with the front desk clerk, who accepted the paper and placed it in a tray and did not look at it.

The corridors were lit by fluorescent tubes, some of which buzzed and some of which were silent and some of which appeared to be off and yet the hallway beneath them was illuminated anyway. I noticed this because I notice light — I have always noticed light; it is the medium of my condition, the substance that reveals what I lack. In the Bureau, light behaved the way I imagined it behaved everywhere, for everyone, before I learned that my experience of it was unusual: it arrived, and it did not make distinctions. It fell on the floor and the floor was evenly bright and no one cast a shadow, not me, not Petra, not Elena, not the clerks. I felt, for the first and only time in that building, a sensation I can only describe as architectural solidarity. The Bureau had been built for people like me.
