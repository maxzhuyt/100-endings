# *Oblique Section, Unadjusted: East Fork, Paria Tributary, 38°12′N, Elev. Undetermined*

---

## I.

The canyon is older than the idea of age.

Its lowest stratum — Vishnu schist, black and biotite-veined, metamorphosed under pressures that would liquefy the concept of pressure — was laid down as seafloor sediment one billion seven hundred million years before the survey parties entered it. Above the schist: the Great Unconformity, that vertiginous absence where a billion years of record simply vanished, eroded or never deposited, so that the Tapeats sandstone resting atop the schist is separated from it by a temporal gulf wider than the entire Phanerozoic. Above the Tapeats: Bright Angel shale, green-gray, deposited in quiet water. Above that: Muav limestone, the calcium carbonate remains of creatures that lived in a shallow Cambrian sea. Then the Devonian — Temple Butte formation, a thin irregular wedge. Then the Mississippian — Redwall limestone, three hundred feet of it, stained red by iron oxide leaching down from the formations above, its true color a blue-gray that the canyon reveals only in freshly fractured faces. Then the Supai Group: mudstones and sandstones, Pennsylvanian to Permian, laid down in tidal flats and river deltas when this place was a coastal plain at the edge of Pangaea. Then the Hermit shale, deep red, crumbling. Then the Coconino sandstone, cream-white, cross-bedded at the steep angles that mean wind — this was a dune sea, a Sahara, and fossilized in its beds are the trackways of reptiles that walked across it two hundred and seventy million years before anything called a mammal existed. Then the Toroweap formation. Then, crowning the rim like a capstone on a tomb, the Kaibab limestone, Permian, rich with the frozen outlines of brachiopods and crinoid stems, the topmost page of a book whose spine reaches down two vertical miles and whose first chapter predates multicellular life.

All of this was already ancient when the Rocky Mountains rose. All of this was already ancient when the river — whichever ancestor of the current river — began the cut that opened the canyon like the slow parting of flesh along a surgical incision, exposing to the dry air strata that had not seen light since the epochs in which they were deposited. The river did this work over six million years, which is to say, over a period of time so vast that six million years of human-scale events placed end to end — every war, every birth, every letter written or abandoned — would not produce a single visible scratch on the lowest wall.

The canyon's silence is not the silence of emptiness. It is the silence of saturation. The rock holds everything it has ever been told and is not interested in being asked.

Into this silence, in the late summer of 1871, two parties of men advanced from opposite ends.

From the south and west, following the old Ute trace along a dry tributary that did not yet appear on any published map, came the column attached to Lieutenant George M. Wheeler's United States Geographical Surveys West of the One Hundredth Meridian — a military enterprise, and marked as one: twelve soldiers of the escort detachment, six civilian teamsters, four packers, three scientists in sweat-darkened wool, and a line of eighteen mules carrying theodolites, chronometers, sextants, a Gambey prismatic compass, photographic plates in lightproof cases, mess supplies for six weeks, and a folded American flag that no one had been ordered to carry but that someone had packed anyway, cinched beneath a crate of lithographic crayons. The column moved in formation even where formation served no purpose, because formations were what the army knew.

From the north and east, following a route plotted by compass and approximated from secondhand Paiute descriptions, came a smaller party attached to Dr. Ferdinand V. Hayden's United States Geological Survey of the Territories — a civilian enterprise, and marked as one: no escort, no formation, no flag, but more specimen boxes than any sensible expedition would carry into terrain this broken. Seven men, nine mules, a spring wagon they'd already abandoned thirty miles back when the track dissolved into scree. They moved in the loose, self-organizing scatter of men who answered to expertise rather than rank. Someone was always lagging behind to strike at a promising outcrop with a hammer. Someone was always ahead, sketching.

Neither party knew the other was in the canyon.

The canyon knew. The canyon, in the way of very old things, did not distinguish between them.

## II.

The theodolite was a Troughton & Simms, ten-inch, with a silvered horizontal circle divided to twenty minutes of arc and readable by vernier to ten seconds. Levi Coe steadied its legs into the sandy soil of the benchmarked station and leveled the plate using the two spirit bubbles, adjusting the leveling screws with the tips of his fingers, not his palms — his palms were sweating in the ninety-seven-degree heat and the brass was too hot to grip — until both bubbles floated dead center, which he confirmed by rotating the alidade one hundred and eighty degrees to check for index error. There was a trace of error. There was almost always a trace of error. He corrected it, halved the residual, and accepted the imperfection with the disciplined calm of a man who found the pursuit of a perfect zero more sustaining than prayer.

"On station," he called.

From eighty yards down the talus slope, knee-deep in brittlebush, Corporal Dunlap raised the stadia rod — a Philadelphia rod, fourteen feet, graduated in hundredths of a foot, its red-and-white banding visible even against the violent geometry of the canyon wall behind him. Dunlap held the rod plumb by watching the bull's-eye level strapped to its base. He held it very still. He was good at holding things still. Before the survey he had been a farrier at Fort Yuma and before that he had been at Petersburg, where holding still had been its own kind of discipline, though of a different order.

Coe sighted through the telescope. The crosshairs settled on the rod. He read the stadia intercept: upper hair at 7.34, lower hair at 5.89. Difference: 1.45. Multiplied by the stadia constant of 100: distance to rod, 145 feet. He called this out. Behind him, kneeling on a flat ledge of Muav limestone that served as desk and table and, when the shadows aligned, a narrow shelf of shade, the recorder entered the number in the field book.

The recorder was Arthur Keyes. He wrote in a tight, leftward-leaning hand, each numeral discrete and unjoined, because joined numerals bred ambiguity and ambiguity in a survey book was a species of corruption. He noted the distance, the horizontal angle (212° 04′ 30″), the vertical angle (−6° 12′ 20″), the station number, the date, the time to the minute. He noted that the backsight was to Station 14, a chiseled cross on a Redwall limestone promontory 1,340 feet to the northeast. He noted a slight atmospheric shimmer at low elevation that might introduce refraction error into the vertical readings after noon. He underlined the word "shimmer" and then, dissatisfied with the imprecision, crossed it out and wrote "inferior mirage effect, est. displacement 4–8″ arc at ground level," which was worse, because now it was a guess dressed as a measurement, and he crossed that out too and simply wrote "correction pending" and moved on. The pencil did not pause. The pencil, in Keyes's hand, moved with the fluency of a bodily function. He had been recording since Albany, since the first days in James Hall's basement laboratory, where Hall had made them log every measurement in duplicate and triple-check every entry and had told them — standing over their desks, immense, bearded, tyrannical, right — that a man who could not be faithful to his field book could not be faithful to the rock.

The pencil noted the next angle. The pencil noted the rod reading. The pencil did not know that the date it had recorded — August 17 — was also the date, nine years earlier, of the engagement at Gainesville, which Keyes had survived and which, if he allowed himself the briefest downward glance into the well that opened behind every idle moment, he could still hear: the sound of a Minié ball entering the thigh of the man beside him, which was not a crack and not a thud but a strange wet snap, like a green branch breaking underwater. The pencil did not know this. The pencil wrote 214° 08′ 10″. The pencil moved on.

Coe called another angle. Keyes recorded it. The work continued.

Six miles northeast and four hundred feet higher in elevation, separated from the Wheeler party by a labyrinth of side canyons that the topographic sheet would eventually render as a tangle of contour lines so dense they merged into a bruise of brown ink, a man named Philo Brewer sat on a sandstone ledge and drew.

He drew with lithographic crayon on smooth-pressed paper clipped to a pine board. He was rendering the north-facing wall of the main canyon in cross-section: a stratigraphic column, vertically exaggerated by a factor of two, showing each formation in its correct thickness and contact relationship. He had been drawing for two hours. The column had reached the Redwall limestone, and he was recording its internal structure — the thin bedding planes, the chert nodules, the dissolution cavities plugged with red silt — with a precision that required him to look up at the actual wall every ten or fifteen seconds and then look down again, up and down, up and down, his eyes making the commute between the vast and the miniature, the three-hundred-foot cliff face and the six-inch rectangle on his board.

In the margins of the stratigraphic column, in a hand smaller and more personal than his labeling script, Brewer wrote notes. *Redwall ls., Mississippian. True color blue-gray where freshly exposed; staining from Fe₂O₃ infiltrate gives characteristic red in weathered faces. Some crinoid columnals in lower third. Upper contact with Supai Grp. appears conformable here but see discordance at station B-7. Chert nodules — irregular, dark, siliceous — abundant in middle third. Formation thickness est. 325 ft. at this section.* He paused. He touched the crayon to his tongue, a habit that tasted of wax and carbon and that his wife had once told him would kill him, and he had replied that there were faster things in the West that might accomplish the job first, and she had not been amused, and now she was in Philadelphia and he was here and the memory of that conversation — the kitchen, the late light on the pine table, the specific quality of her silence after he had said the unfunny thing — arrived uninvited and lodged itself between the chert notation and the next observation like a foreign inclusion in a sedimentary bed.

He blinked. He looked up at the wall.

*Upper Redwall: fossil hash layer at approx. 290 ft. above base. Crinoid, brachiopod, bryozoan fragments. Disarticulated. Current transport likely.*

He drew the fossil hash layer. His hand was steady.

Three yards to Brewer's left, sitting with his back against the same sandstone ledge, Marcus Vell was splitting cobbles. He had a geological hammer — a Estwing, the new steel-forged kind with the weight balanced toward the head — and a cold chisel, and he was working his way through a pile of hand specimens collected that morning from the talus below the Supai Group. Each cobble he split along its bedding plane, examined the fresh face with a ten-power Coddington loupe, and classified: lithic sandstone, medium-grained, subangular to subrounded, poor sorting, ferruginous cement, occasional mica flakes — continental, probably fluvial. He set the classified halves in a row on a canvas cloth weighted at the corners with stones, each specimen beside a numbered paper tag, and moved to the next.

The hammer struck. The cobble parted. The fresh face exhaled a faint mineral breath — the smell of newly broken stone, which is the smell of surfaces that have not been exposed to air since the Pennsylvanian — and Vell brought the loupe to his eye.
