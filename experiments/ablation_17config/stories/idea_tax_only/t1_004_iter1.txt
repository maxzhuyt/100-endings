# Harrow Flats

---

## I.

The drought that year was the kind that made you think God had simply wandered off. Not in anger — that would have been flattering — but in the manner of a man who puts down a book on a porch rail, walks inside for a glass of water, and never comes back. The sky over Harrow Flats went white in early June and stayed white through July like a bandage nobody had thought to change. The creek that fed the town's three working wells dropped to a trickle, then to a dark stain on the rocks, then to a memory the rocks themselves seemed embarrassed to hold. Hector Pell's cattle stood in the near pasture with their heads low and their ribs showing through like the slats of old furniture, and the Widow Marsh — who was neither a widow in the traditional sense nor technically a Marsh, though she'd been both in previous arrangements — stopped watering her garden on the fourth of July and let the tomatoes go to leather on the vine. It was the kind of heat that makes people mean, then makes them too tired to be mean, which in Harrow Flats amounted to something like peace.

There were, at that time, eleven people living in Harrow Flats and six who were not. This is how the arithmetic has always worked, and it is not especially complicated, though newcomers — on the rare occasions when there have been newcomers, which is to say almost never, which is to say twice in a hundred and forty years, and both times by accident — tend to make it more complicated than it needs to be.

There are seventeen souls. There have always been seventeen souls. When one of them dies, that soul — and let us not get into what is meant by *soul,* because the town tried that once, in 1877, convening a three-day philosophical tribunal that ended with Ephraim Ward punching the then-minister in the sternum and nobody speaking to anyone for a season — when one of them dies, the soul goes into a kind of holding. A waiting. The townsfolk do not have a satisfying word for it. Some have used *sleep,* though the Not-Yet-Reborn have, in their previous lives, universally reported that it is nothing like sleep. "Purgatory" has been floated. "The lobby" has been floated, more than once, by more than one soul, which suggests either a genuine shared experience or the kind of convergent metaphor that accumulates when a small group of people has been telling stories to itself since before the Civil War. In any case: the soul waits, and when the next baby is born within the town limits of Harrow Flats — which are technically unincorporated but spiritually absolute, marked by six cairns of stacked red stone that no one can remember building and no one has ever tried to move — the waiting soul enters the child, and the child grows up, and the soul accumulates.

*Accumulates* is the town's word. Not *remembers.* Memory, in Harrow Flats, is a more textured problem than it is elsewhere. The memories come back piecemeal, typically beginning around age six or seven, starting with emotions untethered from context — a sudden fury at a woman you've never met, a grief that pools in your chest on autumn afternoons for no reason your current life can supply — and sharpening, year by year, into something that resembles but never quite achieves full biographical recall. By twenty, most residents can name their previous selves. By thirty, they can narrate the broad arcs. The details stay mercifully soft. Mercifully, because Harrow Flats has had its share of violence, and a man who remembered clearly every blow he'd ever received across four lifetimes would be unfit for polite society, or indeed for any society, which in Harrow Flats are the same thing.

The schoolteacher is Della Bray. She has been the schoolteacher three times. Not because she has a vocation for it — her second self wanted to be a surveyor, and her first self appears to have wanted mostly to be left alone — but because no one else will do it, and the seven children of Harrow Flats require, by a town ordinance that Della herself wrote in 1903 and has been unable to repeal since, a minimum of four hours' instruction per day. She teaches from the same primer she selected in her first tenure, not out of nostalgia but because the town's single bookshelf has not been refreshed since 1919, when a crate of donated volumes arrived by freight wagon, was opened with great ceremony, and was found to contain fourteen copies of a Methodist hymnal and one water-damaged treatise on phrenology. Della teaches reading, arithmetic, Texas geography, and — because she cannot help herself, because the accumulated weight of three pedagogical lifetimes has made her into a kind of cultural calcification — manners. The children are not receptive. They have never been receptive. Several of them have been her students before, in different bodies, and they recall with fragmentary but sufficient clarity that she is bluffable on long division.

The sheriff is Tom Alden, who is on his second life and still relatively fresh, which in Harrow Flats means he has an energy about him that the older souls find either charming or exhausting. He is thirty-eight. His previous self — a woman named Clara Flood, rancher, occasional drunk, dead of pneumonia in 1932 — was arrested in 1921 by the then-sheriff, who was a man named George Prine, who is *also* currently alive but is presently nine years old and in Della Bray's classroom learning cursive for the fourth time with the sullen resignation of a man who already knows his penmanship will never improve, regardless of the hand he's given. Tom Alden arrested — or rather, Clara Flood was arrested by George Prine for — but this is the sort of sentence that collapses under the weight of its own pronouns, and the town has long since stopped trying to construct grammatically coherent accounts of cross-life interactions. The point is: Tom Alden knows what it is to be on both sides of a jail cell, and this has made him either a very fair sheriff or a very indifferent one, and no one, including Tom, can tell the difference.

There is no mystery to any of this. Mystery requires the possibility of revelation, and there is nothing in Harrow Flats left to reveal. The seventeen souls know who they are, and who they were, and — with the grim actuarial certainty of people who have watched the same population recycle for over a century — roughly who they will be. When Hector Pell dies (he is sixty-four, he has a cough he will not see a doctor about, the nearest doctor is forty miles away in a town called Redemption that is named with the kind of optimism Harrow Flats has never been guilty of), his soul will go into the lobby, and the next child born will carry him, and the child will grow up and begin to remember Hector's cattle and Hector's cough and Hector's particular way of swearing at the sky when it refuses to rain, and eventually the child will stop being the child and will start being Hector-plus-the-child, which is an identity that has no clean name and no clean feeling, and that is life in Harrow Flats, and it is not a good life, particularly, but it is *life,* and the distinction between those two things has sustained the town the way a thin creek sustains thin cattle — not well, not joyfully, but continuously, and in the end, continuity is the only argument Harrow Flats has ever been able to make on its own behalf.

This is not a story about the drought, though the drought will matter. This is not a story about the seventeen souls, though they are the only cast available. This is a story about a woman named Patience Greer and about what happened the summer the wagon came, and about the meeting, the old meeting, the same damn meeting they have held every Tuesday since 1859, and about what was decided there, and about what was not decided, and about the difference between those two things, which in Harrow Flats is the difference between everything.

But before we come to any of that, you will need to understand the ledger. And before we come to the ledger, you will need to understand the Tuesdays. And before we come to the Tuesdays — though we are coming to them now, and quickly, because this narrator has told this story in other forms and in other seasons and is not inclined to delay the unpleasant parts — you will need to understand that every soul in Harrow Flats is tired, and that tired people do not make good decisions, and that the same bad decisions, made by the same tired people, across the same flat and punishing decades, are not a cycle. They are a groove. A groove worn so deep that it becomes, after a while, indistinguishable from a grave.

## II.

Tuesday meetings were held in the building that served, depending on the hour and the need, as the town's saloon, its house of worship, its courtroom, and its general venue for the kinds of collective misery that require a roof. The building had been built in 1848 by a man named Josiah Cull — first life, first iteration, back when the souls were new to Harrow Flats and still believed the arrangement might be a temporary celestial error, the kind of thing that would clear up on its own like a rash — and it had been rebuilt twice, once after a fire and once after a structural argument about the load-bearing capacity of mesquite wood that the building itself settled by partially collapsing during a Sunday service. No one had died in the collapse, which was, by Harrow Flats' standards, an uneventful Sunday.

The room held, if you were generous with your definition of *held,* twenty people. Eleven seats were occupied by the living. The remaining six were represented by the ledger.

The ledger sat on a table at the front of the room, next to a water pitcher that had been empty since June, and it was the size and approximate weight of a family Bible, though it contained nothing holy and a great deal that was petty. It was bound in cowhide that had cracked along the spine and been re-bound with wire. Its pages were dense with handwriting in multiple hands across multiple decades, and it was, in the most literal and most suffocating sense, the government of Harrow Flats.

Here is how it worked, though *worked* is generous:

Every resident of Harrow Flats, upon reaching the age of majority — which the town had set at fourteen, because by fourteen the accumulated memories were generally clear enough to hold a person accountable for their previous selves' promises — was required to sit with the ledger and record three things. First: what they remembered of their previous lives' outstanding obligations. Debts owed. Debts held. Promises made. Grievances unresolved. Second: any standing votes or positions on town governance they wished to *carry forward* — which is to say, any opinion they had held so stubbornly across enough lifetimes that it had become, by a kind of democratic calcification, permanent. Third — and this was the part that people dreaded, the part that turned fourteen-year-olds pale and quiet, the part that no one discussed outside the ledger room but everyone discussed constantly — third: an accounting of what they had done wrong.

Not sins, exactly. The town had given up on the vocabulary of sin around the time the preacher and the saloon keeper turned out to be running on the same soul, which was in 1887, and which had occasioned a crisis of metaphysics so severe that the town had simply declined to resolve it, shelving the question of spiritual authority the way one shelves a jar of preserves that might have gone off: not opened, not discarded, just pushed to the back where it couldn't be smelled. Not sins. *Items.* The ledger called them *items.* Everyone knew they meant debts of the interpersonal kind — the betrayals, the cruelties, the cowardices, the hundred small refusals of decency that accumulate between people who cannot leave each other and cannot escape each other and cannot even, in the final accounting, outlast each other.

The ledger was, in short, the memory that individual memory was too soft to enforce. It was the court of appeals for a town that had no appeals process. It was the place where Harrow Flats kept its receipts.

Tom Alden, sheriff, second life, called the meeting to order at a quarter past six on a Tuesday evening in late July, and he did it with the enthusiasm of a man pulling a tick from his own neck.

"Meeting's open," he said. He did not bang a gavel. There was no gavel. There had been a gavel once — Josiah Cull had carved one from pecan wood in 1862 — but it had been thrown at someone during a meeting in 1909 and had exited through a window that was not, at the time, open, and no one had replaced it, and the window had been boarded over, and the board was still there, and so was the stain on it, which was not from the window.

"First item," Tom said. "Water rationing."

"Again?" said Hector Pell, from the second row of chairs, which were not arranged in rows so much as in the pattern of people who had entered a room and sat as far as possible from the people they were currently not speaking to. Several of the eleven were within arm's reach of each other. Several were not. The geometry of the seating was, to an experienced reader — and Harrow Flats had no other kind — a map of the town's current alliances, grudges, fatigues, and fragile truces.

"The wells are low," Tom said.

"The wells are always low."

"The wells are *lower.*"

"I put forward," said Della Bray, from the corner she had claimed in three consecutive lives and would not surrender, "that we revisit the 1912 allocation formula, which I wrote, and which has been ignored for —"

"The 1912 formula gives the schoolhouse a double share," said Amos Cull, who was a large man with large hands and the expression of someone who had been personally offended by the concept of time. He was the blacksmith. He had been a blacksmith twice and a farmer once and all three iterations had shared a quality of blunt, unsubtle resentment toward anything that smelled of preferential treatment for public institutions, which in Harrow Flats meant the schoolhouse and the graveyard, and Amos regarded both with equal suspicion. "It's always given the schoolhouse a double share. I voted against it in 1912. I voted against it in '38. I am voting against it now."

"You were a different person in 1912," said Patience Greer, very quietly, from the middle of the room, and the air in the room changed the way air changes when someone strikes a match near something that might be flammable.

A silence opened. It was not a surprised silence. It was the silence of people who know exactly what has been said and are taking a moment to calculate its cost.

"The formula," Tom Alden said, after a beat that lasted slightly too long, "is on the agenda. We'll come to it."

"We'll come to it," Della agreed.

"We'll come to it," said the Widow Marsh, who was eighty-one and on her fifth life and who had the serene, dangerous patience of a woman who had outlasted everyone in the room at least once and knew she would do it again.

They came to it. They also came to the question of Hector Pell's fence line, which had been in dispute since 1856, when the property it bounded had belonged to a man named Samuel Wick, who was currently a twelve-year-old girl named Flora sitting in the back of the room and scowling with a fury that was clearly not twelve years old. Flora had, the previous week, submitted a formal objection to Hector's fence via the ledger — a written statement, in handwriting that wobbled between a child's careful print and an adult's impatient cursive, asserting that the fence encroached on land that had been deeded to Samuel Wick in 1851 and that no subsequent incarnation had formally ceded.

"I am not," said Hector Pell, rubbing his face with both hands, "having a property argument with a child."

"You're not having it with a child," Tom said wearily. "You're having it with Samuel Wick-as-was, speaking through Flora, per the — per the custom."

"Hector moved it four feet north," Flora said, in a voice that was high and clear and absolutely seething. "He moved it four feet north in 1911 while Samuel was in the lobby waiting to be reborn and couldn't object, and nobody stopped him because Martha Pell was pregnant and everybody wanted to stay on Martha's good side in case the next baby turned out to be someone who owed them a favor."
