# Mother Silence and the Charter of Paradox Flats

---

## I.

You want to know about Mother Silence.

Everybody wants to know about Mother Silence. Sit down. No — closer. The fire doesn't bite and neither do I, not anymore, not with these teeth. Closer. You'll want to hear this the way it ought to be heard, which is with the smoke in your eyes and the chill at your back and somebody's voice doing what a voice is supposed to do, which is carry what the body can't.

I'm going to tell it the way it was told to me, which is the way it was told to the woman who told it to me, which is already three tellings from the truth and probably four from what actually happened, if what actually happened is even a thing you can get to anymore, like a road that's been paved over so many times you forget which layer is the original dirt. But that's all right. That's how a story earns its keep. A story that only gets told once isn't a story. It's a secret. And secrets die with the people stupid enough to keep them.

This would have been — oh, let me think. After the war but before the wire. After the Freedmen's Bureau folded up its tent but before the barbed wire cut the open range into a butcher's diagram. The 1870s, probably. Probably. My grandmother said '74 and my grandmother's cousin said '78 and a man in Cimarron once told me it never happened at all, which is how I knew for certain that it did, because the only stories people deny are the ones with teeth.

Paradox Flats. You won't find it on a survey map. The Atchison, Topeka and Santa Fe people had their own maps, their own names for things, and Paradox Flats was one of theirs — a company town in the southern territory, down where the land goes white and flat as a plate and the sky comes down so low you could knock your hat on it. A town with a charter. We'll get to the charter.

Now, before I go any further, I want to say one thing plainly, and then I won't say it again:

I don't know how it ended.

I know how the story ends, because the story has an ending — every story does, shaped like a nail, hammered down — and I'll give it to you because you came here for it. But whether the ending is true, whether the ending is what happened or what somebody decided *should* have happened, whether the charter was really — 

Well.

There are people who say the charter still exists. That it was never destroyed. That it sits in an iron box in a company archive in Topeka, Kansas, and that ever so often a lawyer's clerk will open the box and read it and close the box and lock it and walk away without saying a word, because the document is still, technically, in force. Because nobody with the authority to nullify it ever did.

There are people who say Paradox Flats is still there. That you can drive past it on the state highway, if you know the mile marker, and see the company buildings still standing, sun-bleached, the church with its clock still set to company time, and no one inside any of them, no one for years, but the doors unlocked, as if the whole town just stepped out for a moment and is expected back.

I don't know. I wasn't there. My grandmother was. My grandmother was eight years old.

But you want to know about Mother Silence.

All right.

---

## II.

She came into Paradox Flats on a Tuesday, which was a delivery day, which meant the company wagon would have been parked outside the storehouse and the men would have been unloading salt pork and flour in sacks stamped with the company seal — a locomotive wheel with the letters ATF centered inside it, the same seal that was on the scrip they used for money and the badges the enforcers wore on their coats and the metal plate bolted to the church door that said, in letters you could read from the street: **COMMUNITY OF PARADOX FLATS — EST. BY CHARTER — ATCHISON TERRITORIAL FREIGHT & LABOR CO.**

She walked in from the west road, which was the only road, and she was tall — everybody agrees she was tall — and dark-skinned and wearing a man's duster over a dress that had been mended so many times the original fabric was more rumor than cloth. She carried nothing. No bag, no bedroll, no canteen. This was notable. People who walked into Paradox Flats carried things, because they were arriving, and people who walked out of Paradox Flats carried nothing, because the company kept what you couldn't pay off. But here was a woman arriving with nothing, as if she had come from a place where nothing was all there was, or as if she had already been relieved of everything the world could take and was traveling now on pure remainder.

She did not speak.

This was the first thing they noticed, or the second thing — the first being her color, because Paradox Flats was not a town that had many Black residents, or many Black visitors, or many visitors at all, and because the American West was not, despite what certain dime novels would have you believe, a place where a Black woman could walk into a white settlement and expect the furniture of other people's faces to stay level. The furniture moved. She could see it move. A man on the storehouse porch set down his sack of flour and straightened up in a way that was not about his back. A woman in the doorway of the dry-goods store put her hand on the frame and then moved it to her hip, where it sat like a question she hadn't decided whether to ask. Two boys stopped chasing a dog and stared, not with malice but with the particular blankness of children who are registering a new category. Even the dog stopped.

She walked. That was all. She walked down the center of the only street, which was not paved, which was a white alkaline dust that puffed up under her boots and settled on the hem of her dress like flour, and people made room for her — not the way you make room for a lady, with a tip of the hat, but the way you make room for something you haven't classified yet, the way you'd step aside for a horse that has no rider.

At the company office, she stopped.

The company office was a wood-frame building, nicer than the others, with a real glass window — the only real glass in town — and behind that window, on a high desk that angled toward the light, there was a ledger. An enormous ledger, large as a family Bible, bound in brown calfskin. The ledger was open. It was always open. Everyone knew what it was. It was the Debt Register of Paradox Flats, and in it was written every resident's name, and beside every name was a number, and the number was what you owed, and the number never went down. You could see it through the glass. If you were close enough, if the light was right, you could find your own name and read your own number and know, to the cent, the precise weight of your life.

Mother Silence stood in front of that window.

She looked at the ledger for a long time. Her face, in the glass, was reflected over the page — her dark skin superimposed on the columns of names and numbers, as if she were a ghost the ledger was trying and failing to contain. People watched her from the corners of their attention, the way you watch a storm from inside a window. The company clerk, a man named Fossett, looked up from his desk inside and saw her face floating above his careful figures and did something strange: he flinched. Not at her. At the *absence* of her. Because he knew every name in that book. He had written every name in that book. And this woman's name was not there.

She was not in the ledger.

In a town where your existence was your debt, she did not exist. She had no balance. She had no account. She owed nothing, was owed nothing, and the elaborate machinery of obligation that kept every soul in Paradox Flats exactly where they were had no tooth that could catch on her.

Fossett looked down at his desk. He adjusted his pen. He did not look up again until she was gone.

She continued down the street. She passed the church, and on the church wall there was a clock, and the clock did not tell God's time — it told company time, which started at five in the morning when the shift whistle blew and ended at eight at night when the gate whistle blew, and Sunday was not the Sabbath but Supply Day, when the storehouse opened and you could spend your scrip on the things you needed to survive another week of earning scrip. She passed the well, which had a padlock on it — water was metered, water was company property, two buckets per household per day, it said so in the charter. She passed the fenced lot where three dark mules stood in the heat, mules that belonged to the company and were leased to residents at a rate that guaranteed the residents would never finish paying.

She saw all of it. She touched nothing. She took nothing.

But by the time she reached the far end of town and turned off the road toward the cluster of shacks where the margins lived — the Chinese rail workers, the drifters, the damaged — every soul in Paradox Flats knew she was there. They could not have said what she wanted. They could not have said what she was. But they felt it — the way you feel a finger drawn across a dusty surface, not by the finger itself but by the clean line it leaves behind, the sudden visibility of the dust that was there all along.

---

## III.

This was — I'm jumping forward now, three or four days — this was in the shack where Mr. Liang lived, the one at the edge of town with the canvas roof and the packed-dirt floor and the smell of black powder that never quite left, because black powder never quite leaves anything it touches. Mr. Liang had a table. On the table was the charter. And sitting across from Mr. Liang, reading the charter by the light of a tallow candle, was the child. Peck. Twelve years old, maybe thirteen — nobody could agree and Peck wasn't telling. Small for any age. Fingers like a pianist, if a pianist's fingers were also stained with walnut ink from a lifetime of forgery. Peck was reading aloud. Mr. Liang was listening with the fixed attention of a man who understands English well enough to dig a tunnel through a mountain on verbal instruction but not well enough to parse the particular dialect of English that lawyers use when they want to steal from you politely.

Mother Silence sat in the corner. She did not move.

Collie Bray was there too, leaning against the door frame, his one remaining arm crossed over his chest, his pinned-up right sleeve tucked neat as a letter into his belt. Collie had a face like a shovel — flat, hard, made for hitting the ground — and a voice like gravel being poured slowly into a pail, and he was the one who'd gotten the charter out of Fossett's office, which was not hard because Collie swept Fossett's office every night and Fossett didn't lock things away from a one-armed Confederate sweeper any more than you'd lock things away from a broom.

"Read it again," Collie said. "The part about the labor."

Peck licked a finger and turned back a page. The charter was seven pages, handwritten on legal stock, the ink so dark and the hand so steady it looked engraved. Across the top of the first page, in copperplate:

**CHARTER OF INCORPORATION AND MUTUAL COVENANT**
*For the Township of Paradox Flats, Arizona Territory*
*Established Under the Authority of the Atchison Territorial Freight & Labor Company*

"Article Four," Peck said. Peck's voice was thin and clear and carried no particular childhood in it. "Section Two. 'Each resident of the Township, in consideration of the protections, provisions, and community benefits extended by the Company, shall commit to a term of voluntary mutual labor obligation, the duration and nature of which shall be determined in accordance with the resident's assessed capacity and the community's collective need, as evaluated quarterly by the Company's appointed assessor.'"

"Read it again slower," Collie said.

"'Voluntary mutual labor obligation,'" Peck repeated. "'Duration and nature determined by the Company's appointed assessor.'"

"There's your teeth," Collie said. "Voluntary. But the company decides what you volunteer for and for how long."

"Keep going," Mr. Liang said, quietly.

"Article Four, Section Five." Peck's finger moved down the page. "'Any resident who departs the Township boundaries prior to the full satisfaction of their assessed labor obligation and accrued debts of community provision shall thereby forfeit —'" Peck paused and looked up, and in the candlelight, the child's face had the flat, strange expression of someone who has just found a spider in their shoe. "'— shall thereby forfeit all unpaid wages, all claims to property real or personal within the Township, all tools and equipment furnished by the Company, and all accumulated credits of community benefit. Furthermore, the departing resident acknowledges and accepts that such departure constitutes a voluntary termination of the Company's obligation to provide protection, shelter, medical provision, and access to the Township water supply, and that the Company bears no liability for any consequence arising from said departure, including but not limited to exposure, destitution, starvation, or death.'"

Silence. Not Mother Silence — the other kind. The kind that fills a room when something has been said that can't be unsaid.

"Read it one more time," Collie said.

"I don't need to," Peck said. "It says if you leave, you leave with nothing. No money. No clothes if the clothes are company clothes, which they all are. No water, because the well is inside the boundary. No food, because the storehouse is inside the boundary. You walk out of this town, you walk into the desert in your skin and whatever God gave you under it, and the company says in writing that if you die out there, it's your own voluntary choice." Peck put the charter down. "It's a bill of sale. They just used nicer words."

Mr. Liang said nothing for a moment. Then he said: "My contract. The rail contract. 1867. Same words. 'Voluntary mutual obligation.' I sign in San Francisco. I don't read well. The translator — " He stopped. He picked up a brass casing from the table and turned it in his fingers. "The translator worked for the company. I didn't know that then."

"Nobody signs this thing knowing what it says," Collie said. "Hell, I signed it. I came in with no arm and no name and they said, 'Sign here, we'll give you a roof,' and I signed and that was that. You don't read the teeth when you're starving. You just chew."

Peck said, very quietly: "I didn't sign mine."

The room went still again.

"Fossett signed it for me," Peck said. "He signed my name on the resident roll and he put my mark next to it — my mark, not a real one, he had me forge a mark when I was nine, told me it was a game, told me I was clever, and then he put the mark next to the name and that was my signature on the charter. I was nine." The child's voice had not changed pitch or volume. "He told me that's what the mark was for. He told me everyone did it. I found out later that some of the others — the ones who couldn't read, the ones who couldn't write — he had me forge *their* marks too. On their charter signatures. They never even held the pen."

Mother Silence, in the corner, had not moved. But something in the room had shifted, the way air shifts when a door opens in another room, and all four of them felt it.
