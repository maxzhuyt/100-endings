# The Contessa's Salon

## I.

The marmalade was Seville, which meant someone had planned ahead, which meant someone had imagined this weekend as the kind of weekend where the marmalade mattered. I mention this because it is easier to describe the marmalade than to describe the empty chair.

Saturday morning. The Château d'Esmé, eleven kilometers above the village of Lurs-en-Vercors by a road that had, as of approximately three o'clock that morning, ceased to exist beneath two meters of wet alpine snow. I descended the staircase at half past eight—later than I prefer, earlier than I could justify, dressed in a severity of black cashmere that communicated, I hoped, that I was a woman who had slept—and found the long table set for nine.

We had been ten.

The linen was extraordinary. I record this without apology. The linen was ivory Belgian damask with a hand-rolled hem, the sort of cloth that has opinions about how you treat it, and it had been ironed with the kind of religious precision that suggested Mme. Vautrin's staff understood that among this particular gathering, a wrinkled tablecloth would be discussed more savagely than a murder. There were nine settings. Nine crystal glasses catching the blue-white snow-light that poured through the east windows with the indifference of a thing that does not know it is beautiful. Nine small pots of coffee. Nine linen napkins folded into bishops' mitres, which was either a joke or a warning.

I counted twice. One does.

"The snow," said Hugo von Halbach, from behind the barricade of his sister's shoulder, "is apparently quite serious." He said it the way one might announce a change in government—gravely, with an air of personal inconvenience—and reached for the butter.

"The road is completely impassable," confirmed his twin, Klara, buttering her own toast with the mirror-image hand. The von Halbachs did everything in stereo. It was, depending on one's tolerance for symmetry, either charming or an atrocity. "Gaspard says perhaps Tuesday. Perhaps Wednesday. He was wonderfully vague."

"Gaspard," I said, sitting, "is the groundskeeper or the goat?"

"Margaux." Klara smiled without showing her teeth, which was her version of a standing ovation. "How fortunate that you're trapped here with us. We'd have no one to be dreadful to otherwise."

"You'd manage."

I poured my coffee. It was dark and slightly bitter and exactly the temperature at which coffee becomes an argument for the continued existence of civilization, and I drank it while performing, with the full resources of my peripheral vision, an audit of the room.

Present: the von Halbachs, dressed in coordinated dove gray, splitting a single croissant with the solemnity of a eucharist. Mme. Vautrin, our hostess by proxy—the château belonged to a foundation she administered, or a foundation administered her, the arrangement was characteristically opaque—drinking something herbal and looking as though she had swallowed a fishbone sometime during the night and was too well-bred to mention it. Young Ludo Ferreira, twenty-six years old and aware of it, his beauty a kind of ambient weather system that disrupted the atmospheric pressure of any room he entered, eating nothing, staring at the snow with an expression that on a less handsome face would have been called fear but on his was merely *brooding*. And Desmond Kreye, literary critic, career executioner, a man whose semicolons had more victims than the Borgias, reading a small leather-bound volume and ignoring all of us with the practiced totality of someone who had elevated his own indifference to an art form.

Absent: Étienne Boillot.

No one said his name.

I want to be precise about this because precision is the only currency I have ever trusted, and because what happened over the next two days requires that you understand how a room full of extraordinarily articulate people can, by common and instantaneous consensus, choose not to articulate the one thing that matters. Étienne Boillot—novelist, provocateur, a man who wore a cravat in the twenty-first century and could not be argued out of it—was not at the table. His place had not been set. Not *removed*, you understand. Not cleared away as though he had once existed and had now departed. Simply never set. As though the table had always been for nine. As though the number ten had been a rumor.

"This marmalade," Hugo announced, "is a serious enterprise."

"Seville oranges," said Klara. "You can taste the suffering."

"One can always taste the suffering, darling. That's what makes French hospitality so nutritious."

I laughed—we all laughed, or produced the muscular arrangements that laughter requires—and the sound hung in the cold bright air of that dining room like something that had been built rather than felt. The château was stone and silence and the kind of cold that lives in the walls of very old buildings and comes out at night to touch your face. The east windows framed a landscape that had been, thirty-six hours earlier, a rather severe Alpine valley of dark pines and gray rock. It was now a blank. A white so total it seemed to vibrate. The snow had eaten the road, the tree line, the village below, the entire concept of *elsewhere*. We were inside a paperweight and someone had shaken it and now we must sit here, amid the swirling white, and eat our Seville marmalade and not say the name Étienne Boillot.

I did not say it either. I want that established. I was not brave.

Instead I thought about the previous evening—the opening dinner of Mme. Vautrin's Literary Retreat, an annual event I had attended three times and which I continued to attend for reasons I preferred to file under *professional obligation* rather than the more honest label, which was *hunger*—and specifically I thought about the final hour of that dinner, and more specifically I thought about the sound I had heard through the wall of my room at something past midnight.

My room shared a wall with Étienne's. The walls of the Château d'Esmé were thick—they had been built to withstand sieges, which made them well suited to literary weekends—but not so thick that they could fully absorb what I heard. It was not a scream. I want to be clear. I have described, in my published criticism, the way lesser writers reach for the scream when what they mean is something much worse, and I will not make the error myself. What I heard through the wall was laughter. Étienne's laughter—I knew it, that particular bronchitic wheeze he deployed when someone had surprised him into genuine pleasure, which was seldom, because he was a man for whom pleasure was largely theoretical—and then a silence so complete that it had texture, and then what I can only describe as nothing. Not silence, which is the absence of sound. *Nothing*, which is the absence of the *possibility* of sound. As though the room on the other side of the wall had simply ceased to contain a room's worth of air.

I pulled the duvet over my head. I am not proud of this.

At the dinner itself, which had been twelve hours before and which I was now reviewing in my mind with the compulsive, scene-by-scene attention I usually reserve for novels that have personally offended me, there had been ten of us at table, and the tenth—the one who had not been on the original guest list, the one Mme. Vautrin had introduced with the particular flutter of a hostess who knows she has produced a coup but is not entirely certain she has not produced a catastrophe—was a woman I did not recognize.

Tall. That was the first thing. Tall in a way that reorganized the geometry of the room around her, that made the rest of us into satellites. Dark hair worn in a way that suggested she had spent either four hours or four seconds arranging it. A dress that was technically a dress in the sense that it provided a notional boundary between her body and the open air, but which seemed to regard this function as beneath its dignity. There was a quantity of décolletage that I would describe as *editorial*—it made a *statement*, and the statement was that fabric was a compromise she was willing to tolerate but not to celebrate.

She had spent the final hour of the evening listening to Étienne.

I watched this from across the salon, from behind a glass of Château d'Yquem that I was not drinking so much as using as a lens, and what I observed was this: she listened as though Étienne Boillot—Étienne, who had published four novels of recursive autofiction that read as though Proust had been rewritten by a man who had once visited a feeling and decided not to stay, Étienne and his catastrophic cravat—was the most important person who had ever lived. She listened the way a portrait painter looks at a face. Not with admiration. With *appetite*. She turned toward him the full wattage of a concentration so total, so annihilating in its focus, that I could feel its heat from across the room, and Étienne—poor, mediocre, vain Étienne—*bloomed* under it. I watched his posture change. I watched him become, for the first and perhaps only time in his wretched literary life, the person he had always imagined himself to be in the author photograph.

He was not, let the record show, worth that kind of attention. He was barely worth a second printing.

I finished my coffee. Saturday morning. The snow. The marmalade. Nine place settings on Belgian damask.

"Has anyone," I said, with the careful lightness of a woman selecting which wire to cut, "seen Étienne this morning?"

The silence that followed was the most well-constructed silence I have ever experienced, and I have sat through Pinter.

"I believe," Mme. Vautrin said, after a pause so long it could have been subdivided into apartments and rented, "that M. Boillot may have found alternate arrangements." She delivered this with a smile of such determined hospitality that it could have been used to insulate a house. "More coffee, anyone?"

No one asked what *alternate arrangements* meant. No one asked how a man might reach alternate arrangements through two meters of snow on an impassable mountain road in the dark. No one asked because we were literary people, and literary people understand that certain sentences are not *meant* to be interrogated; they are meant to be *accepted*, the way one accepts a load-bearing wall—not because it is beautiful but because the ceiling depends on it.

I accepted. I took more coffee. I did not look at the chair where Étienne was not sitting. I absolutely did not look toward the far end of the table, where the woman—the tall, unhurried, impossible woman whose name I did not yet know—was not sitting either, because her place was also unset, and this meant either that she had departed or that she had never been a guest at all, and I was not yet prepared to consider what either of those possibilities required of me.

The snow continued. The marmalade was Seville. The château held us the way a cupped hand holds water: temporarily, with the full understanding that it will, eventually, open.

## II.

After breakfast, the remaining guests distributed themselves through the château like a sentence being diagrammed—each seeking the dependent clause that felt most grammatically distant from the others. The von Halbachs annexed the morning room. Desmond Kreye established himself in the library with the air of a man staking a territorial claim that he expected to defend with lethal force. Ludo drifted toward the conservatory, which was cold and glassed and filled with the kind of gray-green plants that look as though they have read too much Huysmans. Mme. Vautrin disappeared into the domestic quarters, presumably to negotiate with reality on behalf of us all.

I circulated. I have always circulated. It is, I think, the verb that best describes my passage through life generally: I move through rooms, I observe their contents, I render my verdicts, I move on. A reviewer in the most literal sense. I have been told by two former husbands and any number of former friends that this makes me "impossible to reach," a criticism I accept with the same equanimity I would accept being told that a mountain is "difficult to climb." The mountain is not attempting to be climbed. The mountain is busy being a mountain.

I began, that morning, with the von Halbachs.

Hugo and Klara von Halbach had built their joint career on a single, endlessly renewable resource: each other. Their memoirs—published simultaneously, always, by rival houses, a marketing arrangement that their agent described as "synergistic" and that I had described, in the *London Review of Books*, as "a Ponzi scheme of the self"—offered dueling accounts of an identical childhood that seemed to have taken place in two different centuries. In Hugo's version, their upbringing in a crumbling Bavarian *Schloss* was a gothic carnival of aristocratic dysfunction. In Klara's, it was merely sad. They had weaponized their disagreement so effectively that it was now impossible to determine whether they actually remembered different childhoods or had simply discovered that contradiction was more profitable than consensus.

They sat on opposing settees in the morning room, their shoulders at precisely the same angle, their faces performing precisely the same expression of studied unconcern, and they were not discussing Étienne Boillot.

"Hugo," I said, settling into a chair that smelled of lavender and the nineteenth century, "you look as though you've seen something distasteful."

"I've seen the weather forecast. Does that count?"

"Klara?"

"I've seen Hugo's face, which is functionally equivalent."

They volleyed like this—always like this, every exchange a rehearsal for the memoirs, every quip already earmarked for a chapter—and I catalogued them with the attention they deserved, which was considerable but finite. The von Halbachs were dangerous in the way that all twin acts are dangerous: their intimacy was a closed circuit that had no need of external *connection*, and this made them, I suspected, resistant to whatever had happened to Étienne. Whatever hunger The Contessa fed upon—and I was, even then, constructing my theory, my taxonomy, my *review* of the situation, because that is what I do when I am afraid, I write my way toward a thesis—the von Halbachs were unlikely to feel it. They already had an audience. They already had the thing every writer is supposed to want. They had each other.

I envied them so much in that moment that I had to leave the room.

Ludo Ferreira, by contrast, was a banquet of vulnerability. I found him in the conservatory, standing among the dying ferns with his hands in his pockets and his face turned toward the snow, and the sight of him annoyed me in the specific way that youth and beauty always annoy me, which is to say: completely.

He was twenty-six. His first collection, *Corpo Aberto*, had been published the previous spring by a Lisbon press that favored hand-sewn bindings and poets who looked as though they had been carved from Mediterranean driftwood. The poems were—I searched for the honest word, the fair word, and settled instead on the devastating one—*promising*. They had the raw, muscular energy of someone who has felt everything and organized nothing, someone who believes that intensity is the same as insight, which it is not, which it never has been, but which, at twenty-six, is close enough to sustain the illusion. His English was accented and slightly too formal, as though he had learned it from books he loved too much to learn from casually, and he spoke with the earnest, devastating directness of a person who has not yet learned that sincerity is the most expensive thing you can offer another human being.

"Lady Margaux," he said, turning from the window. He insisted on the honorific. I had asked him to stop. He had not stopped. "Do you think we should be concerned?"

"About the weather? Certainly. About the marmalade? Provisionally."

"About Étienne."

He said it plainly. Without art, without deflection, without the seven layers of protective irony that any sensible person wraps around a dangerous sentence before releasing it into the world. He simply said the name. I could have struck him.

"Étienne," I said, "is a grown man with a novelist's instinct for dramatic exits. I'm sure he's found a way to make his absence maximally inconvenient for everyone."

Ludo looked at me. There was something in his eyes that I did not want to see—not fear, not quite, but the *precursor* to fear, the moment before the eye blinks, when the body knows what the mind has not yet admitted—and I looked away, toward the ferns, which were easier.
