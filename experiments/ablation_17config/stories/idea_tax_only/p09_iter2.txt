# Love Is a Haunted House

## I.

The first thing you see is rain. Not dramatic rain, not cinematic rain that catches light and falls in slow motion across a protagonist's upturned face, but Portland rain — the misting, ambient, barely-committed-to kind that doesn't so much fall as simply *persist*. The camera shakes. This is handheld footage, and whoever is operating the camera is trying to hold an umbrella at the same time, doing justice to neither task. A bike lane. A row of houses painted colors that would be illegal in most other American cities. A woman walking a dog that outweighs her by a meaningful margin.

Then a sign: hand-painted, slightly crooked, bolted to a door that was once blue and is now the memory of blue. ROMANTIC CARTOGRAPHY SOCIETY, it says, in letters that can't quite agree on a font. Beneath that, smaller: *est. 2019*. And beneath that, smallest of all, in handwriting that belongs to someone who ran out of room: *by appointment & also not*.

"Right," the narrator says.

His voice arrives the way a certain kind of British voice always arrives in American documentary — with the settled, drawing-room confidence of someone who has been asked to explain the colonies. It's a good voice. It knows it's a good voice. There is a slight rolling quality to it, a perpetual readiness to be amused, and beneath the amusement, the faintest spackle of contempt — not for the subjects specifically, but for the business of having subjects at all.

"In 2019," the narrator says, "in a city already home to the world's smallest park, a vegan strip club, and a man who has been unicycling in a Darth Vader mask while playing flaming bagpipes for so long that no one looks up anymore — in *this* city, five people decided to do something absolutely no one asked for."

Cut to interior. The room behind the blue door is small, slope-ceilinged, and lit by a combination of overhead fluorescents and what appears to be a strand of Christmas lights that has been up long enough to constitute a load-bearing structure. Every flat surface is covered. Maps — hand-drawn, printed, photocopied, layered — bloom from the walls in overlapping profusion. A corkboard dominates one side of the room, dense with pushpins and red thread, and if you look at it too long it begins to resemble either a conspiracy theory or a cardiovascular system.

Five people sit in folding chairs arranged in a rough semicircle. They look exactly like five people in Portland who have founded something called the Romantic Cartography Society would look, and they know it, and this knowledge does not appear to concern them.

The narrator introduces them.

**Margot**, 41, who co-founded the group and whose resting expression suggests she is solving a math problem she will not share with you. **Dean**, 38, who co-founded the group with Margot approximately four months after they stopped dating each other, a chronological detail the narrator emphasizes with audible relish. **Yun-seo**, 29, the quietest, who sits slightly apart from the others and whose map work — the camera finds it on the wall — is beautiful enough to still the narrator for a beat. **Tomás**, 33, who is the one the camera likes; who is, in fact, the one everything likes — light settles on him differently, and he speaks in the way that makes editors lean forward in their chairs. **Lila**, 52, the oldest, former city planner, who joined the group after seeing a flyer at her laundromat and who describes herself, in her talking head, as "a woman who has made a series of choices."

They map locations across Portland where people have fallen in love.

That is the premise. The narrator lets it sit. You can hear the space he has left for the audience to produce the correct response, which is: *That's adorable. That's delusional. That's the kind of gentle, twee, only-in-Portland thing I can watch for twenty minutes while I eat dinner and feel briefly better about humanity without being asked to do anything about it.*

The process, as filmed: the group interviews strangers. They stand in coffee shops and parks and on MAX platforms with clipboards, asking people if they can point to the precise location where they fell in love. Not where they met. Not where they first kissed or first slept together or first decided to keep going. Where they *fell in love*. People are confused by the question. Many people say they don't remember. Some people give an answer and then immediately change it. A few people start crying, and the documentary's editors use these reaction shots the way documentary editors always do — held a beat too long, the ambient sound allowed to breathe, the subject's discomfort becoming a performance they did not consent to give.

The answers are plotted on maps. Margot has a system: she cross-references locations and flags any site named independently by three or more people. These sites are marked with red pins on the corkboard. There are, at the time of filming, forty-seven red pins.

"What *do* they do with these locations?" the narrator asks, in a tone that makes the question sound both reasonable and gently absurd, like asking a golden retriever about its tax strategy. "They visit them. That's it. They simply go, and they stand there, and they — well."

He pauses.

"They see what there is to see, I suppose."

---

The laundromat is on SE Division Street, in a strip mall that also contains a nail salon, a place that sells crystals and CBD and the overlap between the two, and a vacancy where a sandwich shop used to be. The laundromat is called Suds of Love, which is a name someone gave it in 2005, and the sign is faded to the point where it now reads *Su s o ove*, which, as the narrator observes, "somehow suits it better."

Inside: fluorescent lighting of the kind that makes everyone look like they are awaiting sentencing. Industrial linoleum, beige with flecks of darker beige, curling at the edges. Two rows of front-loading washers, a row of dryers along the back wall, four of which have hand-written OUT OF ORDER signs taped to them in the same handwriting, possibly dating to the same epoch. A folding table with a wobble. A vending machine for single-load detergent pods, the mechanical kind with the metal coil that always catches on the packaging.

Someone's towels are tumbling in a dryer — thick towels, the color of rust, revolving in their endless, lethargic rotation with the dryer's heat-thinned hum beneath them.

The group enters. The camera follows them, handheld, the frame jostling when the operator navigates the narrow aisle between machines. This is the laundromat that three separate couples — interviewed independently, months apart, by different members of the group — identified as the site where they fell in love. Three red pins on the corkboard, stacked so close they touch.

Margot leads. She walks past the washers, past the folding table, toward the back of the room where the out-of-order dryers stand in their row like decommissioned soldiers. The overhead light in this section flickers — not dramatically, not in the horror-movie way that signals *something is here*, but in the way fluorescent lights flicker in laundromats because the ballast is old and the landlord has determined that fixing it is not a thing they intend to do.

"So this is it," the narrator says. "The back of a laundromat. The group stands in the flickering light and—"

He stops.

The footage is the footage. It has been analyzed in the months since the series aired, slowed frame-by-frame, run through every filter available to people who believe and people who are desperate not to. It has not been explained. It has been debunked four times by four different experts who have each debunked it differently, which, as Margot later observes, is its own kind of evidence.

In the back of the laundromat, near the out-of-order dryers, two figures are dancing.

They are slow-dancing — the old kind, the close kind, the kind where one person's hand is on the other person's lower back and the other person's head is almost but not quite resting on the first person's shoulder. They are turning slowly. There is no music. The only sounds on the audio track are the dryer's hum, the rhythmic *shush-thump* of the tumbling towels, the flicker-buzz of the bad ballast, and from somewhere outside, barely, a car with a modified muffler passing on Division Street.

The figures are translucent.

Not transparent — not invisible with an outline, not vapor, not the visual language of any ghost anyone has seen in any movie. Translucent. The out-of-order signs on the dryers behind them are legible through their bodies, but dimly, the way text is legible through a fogged window. They have weight. They have specificity. One of them — the taller one — is wearing what appears to be a flannel shirt with the sleeves rolled to the elbows. The other has their hair pulled back. The hand on the lower back is a real hand, or was, and it rests there with the settled ease of a gesture performed ten thousand times.

They are not aware of the camera. They are not aware of the group. They are dancing with each other in the back of a Suds of Love on a Tuesday, and the light passes through them, and the towels keep tumbling.

The camera holds.

On the audio track, someone's breathing changes. It thickens, catches, becomes the breathless staccato of someone trying not to cry and failing. It is Yun-seo. She is standing closest to the figures, and her hands are at her sides, and she is weeping in the way that does not involve the face — her expression is almost blank, but her chest is heaving, and the tears are falling with the indifferent gravity of rain.

A voice from behind the camera — the camera operator, a woman named Dana who will become important later for reasons no one anticipates — says: "Should we — should we stop? Should we stop filming?"

No one answers.

The figures keep dancing. The taller one shifts their weight, and the shorter one follows, and their feet — if they have feet, if what they have can be called that — move across the linoleum without sound. The lean of the shorter one's head increases by a degree. It is the most intimate gesture the camera has ever recorded, and the camera has no idea what to do with it.

One of the washers, the one closest to the camera, enters its spin cycle. The whole machine lurches into vibration, and the sound fills the audio track — a mechanical, rising whine that sounds, if you are in a certain frame of mind, like the building is keening.

The narrator tries. You can hear him try. There is a breath — the specific breath of a man who is about to deploy a sentence he has already composed, something with a wry observation and a trailing qualifier, something designed to hold the thing at arm's length so the viewer can look at it without having to *be* in it.

The breath goes nowhere. The sentence does not arrive.

The figures dance.

The footage runs for another ninety seconds before someone — it is never clear who — says, quietly, "Okay." And the camera pulls back, and the group files out of the laundromat into the flat gray light of Division Street.

In the parking lot, Tomás lights a cigarette with hands that are shaking, and when Dean says, "I didn't know you smoked," Tomás says, "I don't," and this exchange makes the final cut because an editor recognized it as the only thing in twenty minutes of footage that sounded like a show.

## II.

"So," the narrator says.

There is a quality to his voice in the next segment that is different from the first — not shaken, exactly, but *rebooted*. He has gone away, it seems, and come back with his apparatus intact, and if the apparatus is now performing its composure rather than simply possessing it, the average viewer cannot tell and is not asked to.

"The Romantic Cartography Society was founded in the autumn of 2019 by Margot Okafor-Reeves and Dean Pruitt, approximately four months after the end of their romantic relationship — a timeline that, depending on your perspective, represents either remarkable emotional maturity or the most elaborate form of denial since the Flat Earth Society went to sea."

This is the domestication portion of the episode. The narrator provides the context that context provides: biographical detail, chronology, the kind of origin story that makes the improbable feel inevitable in retrospect. Margot, a data analyst, and Dean, a graphic designer, had dated for two years before reaching what Margot describes, in her talking-head interview, as "the end of the part where we could keep pretending we wanted the same things." ("She means I didn't want to get married," Dean says in his talking head, with the particular cheer of someone who has made peace with being the lesser party in a breakup. "And she means she was right that I should have.")

After the breakup, Margot did not want to stop spending time with Dean — a desire she articulates with the precision of someone who has been in therapy for exactly long enough to be annoying about it. Dean did not want to stop spending time with Margot — a desire he articulates by shrugging and saying, "She's my favorite person." They needed a project. Something structured. Something that could hold the shape of their intimacy without requiring it to be the kind of intimacy it had stopped being.

"It started as a joke," Margot says in her interview.

"It started as an art project," Dean says in his.

"It started," the narrator says, "as both — which is to say, as neither."

They began asking people. Where did you fall in love? Not *how*, not *why*, not *with whom*, but *where*? What was the specific geography of the moment the thing happened? And the answers came, and they were strange and particular — a bus stop on Hawthorne, a parking garage stairwell, the bulk grains aisle at WinCo — and when you put enough strange and particular answers on a map, the map began to look like something. Like the city had a second anatomy underneath its official one, a system of invisible organs that no one had charted because no one had thought to chart them.

They told friends. Friends told friends. Yun-seo arrived first, drawn by the maps. ("I saw one of Margot's overlays on Instagram," she says, "and I thought, someone is finally mapping the thing I've always felt was there." The narrator begins to rephrase this as "a kind of sentimental geocaching," and Yun-seo, without heat, without drama, says: "No, it isn't." The narrator pauses. Recalibrates. On the audio track, you can hear a very small exhalation, the minimal sound of a controlled surprise.) Tomás came next — brought by Dean, who knew him from a rock climbing gym and recognized in him the specific charisma that every group project needs to survive its own earnestness. Lila came last: she saw the flyer, she had recently retired from city planning, she was, as she says, "a woman looking for a reason to keep leaving the house."

The narrator tries again. "The group describes themselves as researchers, though of a sort no university would recognize. They are, in essence, romantics in the most clinical sense — cataloguers of an emotion they approach with the rigor of—"

"You keep trying to make us sound like we don't know how we sound," Tomás says.

It is the talking-head interview. Tomás is looking directly into the camera, which he has been told not to do and which he has continued to do with exactly the frequency required to seem guileless rather than defiant. His expression is not hostile. It is not even particularly intense. It is merely the look of a man who has noticed what is being done and has decided, calmly, not to be the thing it is being done to.

"We know how we sound," he says.
