# The Cleanest Man in Garland County

## I.

The caulk along the backsplash had separated from the tile by three-sixteenths of an inch. Not uniformly—it bowed outward in the center like a lip pulling back from teeth, worst at the seam where the countertop met the wall behind the faucet, which was the exact place you'd expect failure given that every time you ran the tap, micro-splash deposited minerals on the silicone surface and the wet-dry cycle worked the bond loose over six to fourteen months depending on the product. He'd used DAP Kwik Seal in this kitchen. The Kwik Seal was rated for twenty-year adhesion under ideal conditions, but the conditions were not ideal because the conditions were never ideal, because conditions were a name people gave to entropy when they didn't want to admit entropy was winning.

Mace Pruitt stood in his underwear at 5:47 a.m. and stared at the caulk.

Behind the separation, he could not see mold. But he could not confirm the absence of mold. The gap created a capillary channel approximately one and a half millimeters wide, which was sufficient for moisture intrusion, which given the ambient humidity of Garland County, Arkansas, in July—averaging 78 percent at dawn, he'd checked—meant the probability of *Aspergillus niger* colonization behind the bead was not zero. It was in fact the kind of probability that a responsible person would treat as certainty, because the difference between "probably not yet" and "definitely yes" was a delta of about three weeks, and the difference between treating it now and treating it later was the difference between a razor blade and a caulk gun versus tearing out the backsplash to the cement board.

He picked up his coffee. Truck stop coffee, a Styrofoam cup from the Valero on 7 South. He'd bought it nine minutes ago. It had been dispensed at approximately 175°F, which he knew not from a thermometer but from the delay the machine imposed between the grinding cycle and the pour—the Bunn AXIOM machines at Valero stations held water at 200°F and the eight-second transit through the mixing chamber and the thermal loss to the Styrofoam knocked it down to a range of 170 to 180, depending on cup volume and whether the lid was on during the pour. His lid had not been on during the pour. He'd watched the girl behind the counter fail to position it before dispensing, which meant an additional three to five degrees lost to surface evaporation. So call it 172 when it hit his hand. That was nine minutes ago. Styrofoam's insulative properties meant a cooling rate of roughly 1.5 degrees per minute in a 74°F room. His kitchen was 74°F because the window unit was dying and he'd measured the output air at twelve degrees below ambient, which brought a 97-degree exterior down to only 85 at the vent and mixed to about 74 at the counter where he was standing because the thermal gradient in a single-wide was a function of—

The coffee was at 158.5°F. Give or take.

It should not be in Styrofoam. Polystyrene leaching began above 160°F under prolonged exposure, and while nine minutes was not prolonged, Mace did not care for the taste that the cup gave the coffee, which was a taste like plastic remembering it had once been petroleum, which it had. He should pour it into the ceramic mug that was sitting in the drying rack. The ceramic mug had been washed eleven hours ago with Dawn Professional at a concentration of approximately one tablespoon per gallon at a water temperature of—he'd used the hot tap, which on his water heater was set to 120°F, which was below the 171°F required for chemical-free sanitization under FDA Food Code Section 4-501.110, but the Dawn's surfactant action at that concentration compensated for the thermal deficit such that the mug could be considered acceptably clean by the standard of a private residence, though it would fail in a commercial establishment, which his kitchen was not, although he sometimes thought of it as one, or rather could not stop thinking of every kitchen as one, which was the problem, or had been the problem, or was the ongoing and apparently permanent condition of having been a health inspector for the Arkansas Department of Health for nineteen years and then not being one anymore.

He did not pour the coffee into the ceramic mug.

He drank it from the Styrofoam, standing at the counter in his underwear, and while he drank it he catalogued the kitchen—not voluntarily and not involuntarily but in the way that breathing was not voluntary and not involuntary, in the way that the lungs simply did what lungs did, which was the same way his eyes did what they did, which was: score.

The refrigerator: the gasket on the upper-right corner of the door was compressed unevenly, creating a seal failure of approximately two millimeters along a four-inch stretch, which meant cold air was bleeding out at a rate sufficient to cause the compressor to cycle 15 to 20 percent more frequently than necessary, which raised the internal temperature by one to two degrees, which placed the interior at an estimated 39 to 40°F, which was within the acceptable range of 32 to 41°F but trending upward. The gasket needed replacing. He'd noted this eleven days ago and had not replaced it because a replacement gasket for a Frigidaire FFTR1821QS cost $42.67 on Amazon and he had $113 in his checking account and the electric bill was $89 and the lot rent was due in six days.

The floor: vinyl composite tile, twelve-by-twelve, installed by the trailer's previous owner with a grout line of approximately one-eighth inch. Grout was discolored in the high-traffic path between the counter and the hallway—not mold, just ground-in dirt, clay-colored, the iron-rich soil of the Ouachitas tracking in on boots and never quite yielding to a mop. He mopped every third day with a 1:32 bleach solution. The solution was correct. The mop head was seven months old, which was four months past the point where a cotton mop head's filament structure degraded enough to reduce absorption by 30 percent. He needed a new mop head. The mop head cost $6.49 at the Dollar General on Albert Pike. He would buy the mop head. He would not replace the gasket. These were the kinds of decisions that constituted a life.

In the bathroom mirror, which he arrived at after walking eleven steps down the hallway, stepping over the buckled seam where two sections of subfloor no longer met flush—a trip hazard he'd reported to the park management in writing, three times, with photographs, using the language of OSHA 1910.22(a)(1), which governed walking-working surfaces, though OSHA did not technically govern residential trailer parks and the park management had told him this, twice, the second time without patience—

In the mirror: his face. Fifty-three years old. Skin that had been sun-damaged in the specific pattern of a man who'd spent two decades walking from his state-issued vehicle to the back entrances of restaurants in central Arkansas, meaning the left side more than the right because you always walked with the building on your right so you could see the dumpster enclosure and the grease-trap access on approach. His left temple had three actinic keratoses that a dermatologist had told him to watch. He watched them every morning. They did not change. His teeth were acceptable. His gums were receding at a rate consistent with age and with the fact that he'd been brushing with a medium-bristle toothbrush for thirty years because no one had corrected him until a dental hygienist in 2019 told him soft-bristle was the standard and he'd said, "Ma'am, I've been using medium for thirty years," and she'd said, "That's why your gums are receding," and he'd switched to soft but the damage, as with most damage, had already been structural.

His eyes were brown. They looked, even to him, like they were performing an audit.

He put on pants. He put on a shirt—short-sleeve button-down, pockets on both breasts, the kind of shirt a health inspector wore so he could carry a thermometer in one pocket and a pen in the other. He'd been buying this same shirt from the same rack at Walmart for nineteen years and had continued buying it after they let him go because it was the shirt he owned and because the pockets were useful and because he did not want to think about the third reason, which was that the shirt made him feel like a man who was going somewhere to do something that mattered.

He carried the thermometer in the left pocket. A ThermoWorks Thermapen ONE, $105, which he'd bought in 2021 when he still had savings and which was the single most expensive object in the trailer that was not the truck or the trailer itself. He carried it every day. He had not been employed as a health inspector for fourteen months. He carried it every day.

Outside: the truck, a 2007 Chevy Silverado with 214,000 miles and a check-engine light that had been on since 2022 and which indicated, according to the OBD-II scanner he'd borrowed from a neighbor, a P0420 code—catalytic converter efficiency below threshold—which was an emissions issue, not a drivability issue, which meant he could ignore it, except he could not ignore it, because it was a code, it was a failure, it was a system performing outside its designed parameters, and every time the light came on when he started the engine he felt the small familiar nausea of knowing something was wrong and doing nothing about it.

He started the engine. The light came on. The nausea arrived. He backed out of the lot.

On Albert Pike Road, heading toward the 7 intersection, he passed the Waffle House at mile marker 3 and noted automatically that the dumpster lid was open—a violation of the Arkansas Rules Pertaining to Food Establishments, Section 8-304.11, which required refuse receptacles to be closed when not in active use, which they never were at 6:02 a.m. because the morning shift didn't take out trash until after the breakfast rush, which meant the lid had been open since the night shift's last bag at approximately 11:30 p.m., which meant seven hours of access for rodents, raccoons, and feral cats, the latter of which were an established population in this stretch of Albert Pike because the motel behind the Exxon fed them, which was its own violation of basic sanitation principle though not technically covered under the food code because the motel was not a food establishment although they did offer a "continental breakfast" that Mace had once inspected and found to include a waffle iron with burned batter dating back, by colorimetric assessment of the carbonization, at least two weeks.

He noted the open dumpster lid the way he noted everything: with total clarity, total precision, and absolutely no authority to do anything about it.

## II.

The call came from Dale, who ran a handyman service out of a converted gas station on East Grand and who gave Mace work when he had it, which was intermittently, which was the only frequency at which Mace received anything anymore.

"Kitchen cleanup," Dale said. "Not a remodel. A restaurant that's transitioning ownership. New people want a deep clean before they take possession. I told them I had a guy who was particular."

"What restaurant?"

"The Jade Pearl. Used to be Jade Pearl Chinese, on the strip by the lake. The one with the green awning."

Mace knew the Jade Pearl. He'd inspected it twice during his tenure, in 2016 and again in 2019. In 2016, it had scored a 92—three minor violations involving the temperature of a buffet steam table, the absence of a hair restraint on a prep cook, and a handwashing sink that was accessible but blocked by a stack of bus tubs. In 2019, it scored an 87—the same steam table issue, which was now a repeat violation, plus a mouse dropping finding behind the dry storage shelving unit, plus a damaged ceiling tile above the dish area that showed water intrusion, plus a chemical storage issue where the sanitizer was shelved above the food prep area. He'd given the owner, a man named Tran who spoke limited English and called Mace "sir" in a way that made Mace feel simultaneously respected and guilty, a corrective timeline of fourteen days on the critical violations.

He'd never gone back for the re-inspection. Two months later, he was terminated.

The termination was officially a reduction in force. The state had consolidated the northwest and central districts, eliminated four field inspector positions, and kept the inspectors with seniority, of which Mace had plenty, and the inspectors who had not filed complaints about their supervisors' scoring inconsistencies, of which Mace had filed seven. The complaints were documented. The retaliatory termination was not. He'd been told by a lawyer in Little Rock that he had a case, probably, if he wanted to spend two years and $15,000 he didn't have. He did not have it. He drove home and sat in the parking lot of his trailer and stared at the check-engine light until it became a symbol of everything and then he went inside and mopped the floor with a 1:32 bleach solution and that was the end of his career.

He drove to the Jade Pearl.

It was worse than he expected, which meant it was approximately as bad as he'd calculated on the drive over, since his expectations and his calculations had diverged years ago. He expected, always, that things would be manageable. He calculated, always, that they would not.

The green awning was down on one side, the mount bolt having pulled free from the fascia board, which suggested either wind damage or rot in the wood behind the stucco, which was itself cracked in a branching pattern consistent with settling—the foundation on these lakeside strip-mall units was notorious for movement because the soil was Ozark shale mixed with fill dirt from the lake construction in the 1950s and had never been properly compacted. The cracking was cosmetic. Probably. Unless it extended through the substrate to the framing, in which case it was structural, and the damage behind the interior walls would be—

He went inside.

The smell hit him first, which was correct. Smell was always first. The nose processed olfactory data faster than the eye processed visual data; this was evolutionary, a survival mechanism, because the things that could kill you—spoilage gases, combustion byproducts, predator musk—often announced themselves chemically before they announced themselves visually. The smell in the Jade Pearl was a composite: rancid cooking oil, dominant, the unmistakable sour-sweet signature of lipid oxidation in a fryer that had not been drained and cleaned at closure; mildew, secondary, the wet-carpet note of *Stachybotrys* or *Cladosporium* growing somewhere with persistent moisture; and underneath both of these, faint but legible to a trained nose, the ammonia-adjacent sharpness of rodent urine, which meant the mouse problem from 2019 had not been addressed and had almost certainly escalated from mice to rats, because mouse populations in commercial kitchens followed a predictable arc from colonization to carrying-capacity crisis to displacement by *Rattus norvegicus*, which outcompeted *Mus musculus* in food-rich environments within eighteen to twenty-four months.

He set his bucket down. He had been hired to clean. He was, for the moment, unable to stop inspecting.

The kitchen: twelve-by-twenty, stainless steel prep tables bolted to the wall on the north side, a six-burner range with a wok station on the south, the walk-in cooler in the northwest corner, the three-compartment sink along the east wall. Standard Chinese-restaurant layout for a unit this size. He'd seen a hundred of them. He'd scored a hundred of them.

Walk-in cooler: he opened the door. The compressor was running—someone had left the power on, or the new owners had turned it on in advance of the transition. He pulled the Thermapen from his pocket and inserted the probe into the ambient air near the middle shelf.

52.4°F.

He stared at the readout. The acceptable range for a walk-in cooler was 32 to 41°F. At 52.4, every item in this unit was in the temperature danger zone and had been since—well, it was impossible to say since when, which was the problem with temperature abuse: without continuous monitoring, you could not determine the duration of the excursion, and without the duration, you could not assess the degree of risk, which meant you had to assume the worst, which was always total loss.
