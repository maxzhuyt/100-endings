# Mat

## I.

The files weighed nothing. That was always the thing about them. You could hold a life in a manila folder and your wrist wouldn't know it.

She sat in the third chair from the end of the hallway bench, the one closest to the fire exit. The fluorescent tube above her had a faulty ballast — it buzzed at sixty hertz, a sound most people filtered out but that she tracked the way she tracked the bailiff's hands when he passed her for the second time, his right thumb hooked in his belt six inches from the radio, his left swinging free. He was not a threat. He was bored. She went back to the files.

Five rubber-banded stacks, each one thin enough to read in under four minutes. That thinness was its own kind of information. A thick file meant someone had been paying attention. Thick files had psych evaluations with complete sentences, school records with teacher comments, foster placement histories annotated by caseworkers who'd met the child more than once. These files were thin the way a meal is thin. Not because the material didn't exist but because no one had bothered to gather it.

She started with the top one.

*R. Bankston. DOB 04/11/2008. 16 years old.* Aggravated assault, reduced from aggravated battery, continued twice, pending. Group home placement since age eleven. Prior foster placements — three, all disrupted, all listing the same boilerplate language: *Youth displayed escalating behaviors inconsistent with placement capacity.* Which meant he'd gotten bigger and louder and someone got scared. Medical flag: fractured orbital bone, age twelve, origin listed as "undetermined." She read "undetermined" the way she read everything — as a word chosen to replace a word someone did not want to write.

The second file had a coffee ring on its cover, a dark brown circle soaked through the county seal. It had sat on someone's desk, under someone's mug, for long enough to stain. No one had been reading it.

*D. Quiros. DOB 08/29/2008. 16 years old.* Two counts of property destruction. One truancy complaint. Psychological evaluation, two paragraphs long, noting "oppositional tendencies" and "reluctance to engage with therapeutic modalities." The evaluation was nine months old and had not been followed up. She noted the evaluator's name. She'd seen it before. He did batch assessments for the county, fifteen-minute sessions, carbonless forms.

*T. Beale. DOB 01/03/2009. 15 years old.* Weapons charge — she slowed here, because weapons charges were weight-bearing in a file — and then she read the details. A folding pocketknife with a three-inch blade, found during a school search, reported by a teacher who also noted that the boy had been sleeping in class. The sleeping wasn't charged. The knife was. Prior placements: seven. She counted them twice. Seven placements in four years. Medical: asthma, moderate persistent, prescription lapsed.

*J. Okafor. DOB 12/14/2007. 16 years old.* Assault, simple. She read the narrative, what little there was. An altercation at a bus stop with an adult male, thirty-four years old, who had — according to the police summary — "initiated verbal contact" with the boy. The adult male was not charged. The boy was. His placement history was shorter: two foster homes, one group home, the condemned one. The physical description was sparse but included a note from his intake screening: *Youth presents as significantly below expected height and weight for age.*

The last file.

*M. Gaines. DOB 06/22/2008. 16 years old.* Two assaults. One resisting arrest. A prior adjudication at fourteen, six months in a juvenile residential facility, released to the group home eleven months ago. His file was the thinnest of the five, which should have been impossible given the density of his record, and she understood what that meant too. Someone had pulled pages. Someone had decided what his official history would contain. There was a single-page behavioral summary from the residential facility, handwritten, which was unusual. It said: *M. is not without capacity for self-regulation. He requires consistent, predictable structure and responds negatively to perceived injustice. He will test every boundary and respect the ones that hold.* It was the most human sentence she had read in any of the five files. It had been written by someone who had looked at the boy. She noted the handwriting — small, even, left-slanting. A left-handed person who had been taught to write with their right.

She closed the last file and reassembled the stack. Rubber band around the middle. She placed them on her lap and waited.

The hallway was government beige. The bench was bolted to the wall. The fire exit was fourteen feet to her left and opened onto a parking lot she had already walked when she arrived, checking sight lines out of a habit she could not shut off and had stopped trying to. She sat with her spine an inch from the chairback, not because she was anxious but because her body had been trained in a period of her life she did not discuss — trained to remain ready, to keep her hips under her shoulders, to never let a flat surface hold her weight when her own structure could hold it instead. She sat like a person who had been hit from behind and had organized her entire spatial life around the impossibility of it happening again.

The courtroom door opened. The bailiff — right thumb still on the belt, left hand now carrying a paper cup of water — gestured her in.

Judge Amara Pine's chambers were smaller than the courtroom suggested. A desk, two chairs for counsel, a window that looked onto a ventilation shaft. The judge was already seated, reading something on her monitor, and did not look up immediately. Assistant District Attorney Morales was in the left chair. The chair on the right was empty. No defense counsel. No caseworker. No one from the Department of Children and Family Services, which was either an oversight or an admission.

"Ms. Jefferies," the judge said, still reading her screen. "Sit down."

She sat. She placed the files on her lap. She waited.

"You've been GAL-certified for three years," the judge said. It was not a question.

"Yes."

"And you're currently carrying how many active cases?"

"Eleven."

"You're about to carry sixteen." The judge turned from the screen and looked at her for the first time. Amara Pine was fifty-eight years old, dark-skinned, short-haired, and had the particular exhaustion of a person who processed human damage on a calendar schedule. Her eyes moved across Nyla Jefferies the way Nyla's eyes had moved across the files — assessing, cataloguing, deciding what mattered. "You've read them."

"Yes."

"Then you know the Glenwood group home was condemned Thursday. Structural failure during a code inspection — the county had been flagged twice and didn't act. Twenty-three residents were emergency-placed over the weekend. Eighteen have been absorbed into existing licensed capacity. These five cannot be placed."

Morales shifted in his chair. He was holding a pen he had not used.

"There is no bed space," the judge continued. "There is no foster home in this county or the three adjoining counties that will take a teenage boy with a pending juvenile matter, let alone five of them. I have called. My clerk has called. DCFS has made — I am told — exhaustive efforts. The alternative is emergency detention at the county juvenile facility, which is currently at one hundred twelve percent capacity and is itself under federal monitoring for conditions of confinement." She paused. "I am not sending these boys to that facility."

Nyla said nothing. She knew what was coming the way she knew, in the old days, when a combination was about to land — not from the fist itself but from the weight shift that preceded it.

"You are licensed for temporary emergency foster placement. You have completed the training. You have the physical space — your home study lists three bedrooms and a finished basement."

"Two of the bedrooms are occupied," Nyla said. "I use one. The other is an office."

"Then you have one bedroom, a finished basement, and whatever you can arrange. This is an emergency placement, Ms. Jefferies. The standard is not comfort. The standard is not-detention."

The judge opened a folder on her desk and began signing. Five signature pages. Five temporary custody orders. Each one transferred legal responsibility for a human being from the State of Illinois to Nyla Marie Jefferies, DOB 03/15/1989, thirty-five years old, unmarried, no dependents, employed part-time as a court-appointed guardian ad litem and part-time as a something she had described on her application as "independent fitness consulting," which was accurate in the way that the files were accurate — technically, structurally, and emptied of everything that mattered.

"Ms. Jefferies."

"Yes."

"I need you to sign these."

She uncapped the pen from her jacket pocket — her own pen, always, because she did not use pens that had been handled by unknown persons, a habit she ascribed to hygiene when people asked and to nothing when they didn't — and signed five times. The same flat, efficient signature she put on court filings and grocery receipts and the lease renewal she'd completed that morning. A signature that gave nothing away. A small *N*, a smaller *J*, and a line.

"The boys have pending matters before this court and others," the judge said. "We'll schedule a consolidated status hearing in two weeks. I want a report on placement stability, any incidents, and a preliminary plan for structure. Not a treatment plan. A *structure* plan. Something that looks like daily life."

Nyla collected her copies. She stood. She placed the copies inside the file stack, reapplied the rubber band, and held the whole assembly at her side, arm straight, fingers wrapped around the bottom edge so the files wouldn't shift. She walked to the door.

"Ms. Jefferies."

She stopped. She did not turn fully. She angled her body forty-five degrees, which gave her sightline to both the judge and the exit. An old habit. A permanent one.

"No one else raised their hand," the judge said. The sentence was not apology. It was not gratitude. It was the barest possible form of honesty available in a room with a court reporter. It acknowledged, without stating, that what was being done to Nyla Jefferies was unreasonable, and that the only alternative was something being done to five boys that was worse.

Nyla took the files and left.

In the hallway, the fluorescent tube was still buzzing. The bailiff was gone. The bench was empty. She walked to the fire exit, pushed the bar, stepped into the parking lot's mid-February cold, and stood beside her car for eleven seconds before opening the door. She was not composing herself. She was not overwhelmed. She was running the logistics the way she ran everything — sequentially, without emotion, the way you plan a training camp: how many beds, how many meals, how many square feet, how many ways out of every room.

The files sat on the passenger seat. Five thin folders. Five names she had already memorized because she memorized everything she read, a skill she had never been tested for and never discussed. Bankston. Quiros. Beale. Okafor. Gaines.

She started the car.

## II.

The duplex had been a rental property owned by a church elder who had died owing back taxes, and the church had repurposed the upper unit as overflow storage and the lower unit as — the pastor's phrase — "a ministry of presence," which meant it was available when no one else needed it. Nyla had secured it with a phone call and a lie, telling the church administrator she needed a temporary meeting space for a court-supervised youth program. Which was close enough to the truth that the paperwork wouldn't collapse under scrutiny, and far enough from the truth that she didn't have to explain why she needed five beds in a building that had none.

She'd spent the previous night buying cots from a surplus store on West Eighty-Seventh and assembling them in the lower unit's two bedrooms and front room. Three in the larger bedroom, two in the front room. She'd considered the geometry of it — who would sleep near whom, who would be closest to the door — and then stopped, because she was making assignments for boys she hadn't met and the files were not the boys.

The lower unit had a kitchen with a gas stove that worked and a refrigerator that hummed at a frequency she didn't like. A bathroom with a shower. A front room. Two bedrooms. A back door that opened onto a concrete slab and then an alley. She'd checked the back door's lock, found it insufficient, and installed a deadbolt herself at six in the morning, drilling into the frame with a cordless she kept in the trunk of her car because she kept tools in her car the way other people kept snacks. The windows were secure. The radiators worked. The place smelled like Pine-Sol and old upholstery and something sweet beneath both that was maybe incense or maybe mold.

She had stocked the refrigerator. Rice, eggs, bread, peanut butter, ground turkey, canned beans, onions, hot sauce, orange juice, milk. Foods that were cheap, calorie-dense, and would not offend anyone's palate badly enough to become a point of conflict. She'd been this person before — not a mother, not a caretaker, but a logistician of survival. She had fed herself this way for years while training, measuring food not as pleasure but as fuel. She could feed five more.

The first boy arrived at 1:17 p.m.

The caseworker was a woman Nyla had seen in court twice before, Michelle something, mid-forties, overworked in a way that showed in her cuticles — bitten, ragged, the hands of someone who processed anxiety through her fingers. She came through the front door first, scanning the room, and Nyla watched her eyes land on the cots and register the arrangement without comment. Then the boy.

Terrence Beale. Fifteen years old. The weapons charge — the pocketknife. He came through the door behind the caseworker and stopped just inside the threshold, not because he was studying the room but because he had *learned* not to move into unfamiliar spaces until the geometry resolved. Nyla recognized the pause. It was the same pause she made in every new room she entered, the momentary full-body scan that looked, to someone untrained, like hesitation but was actually the opposite: it was *readiness* calculating the terms of its engagement.

He was thin. Not emaciated, but undermuscled in a way that spoke of inconsistent meals and cortisol doing its slow catabolic work. His shoulders were narrow but his frame was long — he would be tall if someone fed him consistently for a year, which no one had. He stood with his weight on his left foot and his right foot angled slightly outward, a stance that allowed quick lateral movement. He didn't know he was doing it. His body knew.

"This is the placement," Michelle said, and the sentence was directed at neither of them and both of them.

"Terrence," Nyla said.

He looked at her. His eyes went to her hands first — she noted that — then to her shoulders, then to her face. He was reading her the way she read rooms and she felt the strange double-exposure of recognizing her own habit in a fifteen-year-old boy whose file said *weapons charge* and whose body said *I was not safe and I made a decision.*

"The bedroom on the right has two cots. Front room has two. You get first choice."

He chose the front room, the cot nearest the back door. She noted this without comment.

Michelle left a plastic bag of his belongings — one change of clothes, a toothbrush still in its packaging, a phone with a cracked screen and no charging cable — and handed Nyla a manila envelope of forms and disappeared into her car with the air of a person fleeing the scene of an accident she'd been required to witness.

Terrence sat on his cot. He did not unpack the plastic bag. He put his hands on his knees and looked at the wall and breathed, and Nyla went into the kitchen and started rice because the sound of a pot filling with water was a sound that existed in every kind of home and she needed this space to begin sounding like one.
