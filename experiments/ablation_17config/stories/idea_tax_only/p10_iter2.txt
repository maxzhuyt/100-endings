# Eaten Alive

## I.

I should tell you about the thing my body does.

Not yet the parking garage. Not yet the shape. First the context, because context is what I'm good at — I'm a grief counselor, or I was, and grief counselors understand that nothing means anything without the frame around it. You don't walk into a session and say *tell me about your dead husband*. You say *tell me about the last ordinary Tuesday*. You build the frame. You earn the awful thing.

So: the frame.

My name is Marcus Harlow. I'm forty-one. I practiced out of a rented office on Poplar Avenue in Memphis for eleven years, second floor of a converted dental building, which meant the waiting room always smelled faintly of clove and nitrous oxide, which honestly was not the worst ambient mood for a grief counselor's practice. I had a degree from Vanderbilt, a LCSW certification, a modest reputation for being good with the newly bereaved — meaning the ones still in that white-hot first phase where the loss is so total it becomes almost physical, a phantom limb the size of a person. I could sit with that. I was good at sitting with that.

I was also divorced. Caitlin left in 2019, which she'd earned, and the thing she said during the mediation — without cruelty, in the measured tone of someone who'd rehearsed — was that living with me was like living with a very warm hologram. "You're emotionally absent," she said, "but somehow always *there*." She meant it as a paradox. She meant that I was the most attentive man she'd ever met and that nothing about my attention ever actually *landed*. That I listened with the profound, professional focus of a man who had learned listening the way you learn a second language — fluently, even idiomatically, but always with some faint accent that marked it as acquired rather than native.

She wasn't wrong. I've thought about this a lot. But what Caitlin didn't know — what I didn't tell her, in eleven years of marriage, because there was no version of telling her that I could rehearse to completion without the sentence collapsing under its own weight — was that the emotional absence she diagnosed was not absence at all. It was management. It was a man running a very complicated switchboard twenty-four hours a day, routing signals, suppressing noise, trying to keep the whole infrastructure from lighting up at the wrong moment.

Because of the condition.

Okay. Here it is. The thing my body does.

When I'm afraid — not startled, not mildly anxious, but genuinely, cortically *afraid*, the kind of fear that floods your bloodstream and tells your hindbrain you are about to die — my body responds with sexual arousal. Full. Immediate. Catastrophic. The clinical term is *excitation transfer*, though my second therapist preferred *autonomic cross-wiring*, and my first therapist preferred to pretend I hadn't said it. The DSM doesn't have a clean category for it. My endocrinologist described it once as "a misfiring of the sympathetic nervous system in which the fight-or-flight cascade erroneously activates the arousal pathway," and then he paused, and I could see him composing his face into the expression of a man who was not fascinated, and he said, "It's more common than people think." It is not more common than people think. He was being kind.

The greatest hits. You want the greatest hits. Everyone wants the greatest hits, the same way everyone slows down for car accidents, and I have learned that telling these upfront, fast, with clinical detachment and the controlled self-deprecation of a late-night talk-show guest promoting a memoir, is the only way to own them before they own me:

The fender bender. 2011, intersection of Union and McLean, a Buick ran a red and clipped my driver's side quarter panel at maybe fifteen miles an hour. No injuries. Airbags didn't even deploy. But the impact — that half-second of *oh God this is it* before the crunch resolved into something minor — was enough. I had to sit in the car for twenty minutes after the other driver came to exchange insurance information. He knocked on my window. I pretended to be on the phone. He could see that I was not on the phone.

The turbulence. 2015, Southwest flight from Memphis to Denver, a pocket of clear-air turbulence that dropped the plane what felt like a hundred feet in two seconds. The woman next to me grabbed my arm. The oxygen masks did not deploy but people screamed as though they had. My body responded with a thoroughness that bordered on editorial. I spent the remaining ninety minutes of the flight with a copy of *SkyMall* magazine on my lap, pricing a solar-powered garden gnome with an intensity that concerned the flight attendant.

The coffee mug. 2017. A client — a man named Gerald, fifty-eight, who had lost his wife to pancreatic cancer and was six months into what I'd been tracking as a transition from complicated grief into intermittent explosive expression — picked up his coffee mug and threw it at the wall behind my head. It shattered. A piece nicked my ear. And Gerald's rage — which was real, which was earned, which was the sound of a man who had been told to *process* his loss one too many times — turned to something else when he saw what was happening to me behind my desk. I don't know what he thought. I don't know if he thought I was mocking him. He never came back. I referred him to a colleague and spent the rest of the afternoon in my office with the door locked, sitting on the floor, pressing my thumbs into my eye sockets.

That one still visits me. Three in the morning, lying in bed, turning it over. Gerald's face. The confusion, then the disgust, then something worse than disgust — *comprehension*. As though he'd suddenly understood something about me that explained everything, and the thing he'd understood was wrong, and I could never correct it because the correction would require telling the truth, and the truth was worse.

You see the architecture of the problem.

My body insists on reaching toward the things that terrify me. Not metaphorically. Literally, visibly, with the subtlety of a billboard. Every alarm my nervous system sounds gets translated, through whatever scrambled wiring runs between my adrenal glands and my groin, into a signal that looks — to any outside observer, to any *inside* observer, to my own appalled consciousness — like desire. Want. *Reaching toward.*

And here's the thing I figured out, the thing that structured my entire adult life: grief was safe.

Sadness didn't trigger it. Being in a room with someone weeping for their dead child, their dead mother, their dead marriage — it registered as sorrow, and sorrow apparently ran on different wiring. I could sit with grief. I could sit with despair. I could hold space, as we say in the profession, for the most annihilating losses a human being can sustain, and my body would behave. It would be appropriate. It would be *still*.

So I built my life there. In the one room where the switchboard was quiet. I became very, very good at grief because grief was the one country where I could pass for normal.

Caitlin was wrong about the absence. What she read as emotional distance was emotional *triage*. I had organized my entire existence around sadness because sadness was the only emotion that didn't betray me, and I'd gotten so good at living inside it — other people's sadness, always other people's — that I forgot how to live anywhere else. You can't be emotionally present when you've built your whole self around avoidance. You can't land when your entire purpose is not to launch.

Eleven years of marriage. One good year, one okay year, nine years of a warm hologram.

So.

The parking garage.

It was a Wednesday in October. I'd stayed late at the office, not because I had clients but because I'd been rearranging my resource shelf, which is what I do when I'm avoiding going home to an apartment that still smells, fourteen months after the divorce, like Caitlin's hand lotion. The parking garage on Madison was three-quarters empty. That particular quality of fluorescent light that makes concrete look like bone. My footsteps echoing.

I felt it before I saw anything. A shift in the air pressure, or what my body interpreted as a shift in the air pressure, though I have since considered the possibility that what I actually felt was something much older than pressure — something my species has been feeling in dark places for two hundred thousand years and has never successfully named. The hair on my arms stood up. Not the metaphor. The actual follicular event.

Then I saw her.

I say *her*. The shape was at the far end of the garage, maybe fifty yards, leaning against a support column. Leaning is the wrong word. Occupying the column's space in a way that suggested the column had been built around her. The proportions were wrong. Not dramatically, not movie-monster wrong — subtly wrong, the way a sentence is wrong when one word has been replaced with its near-synonym and you can feel the error but can't locate it. Too tall. Shoulders a half-inch too wide for the hips, or hips a half-inch too narrow for the shoulders. The head tilted at an angle that a human neck would allow but a human brain would not choose. And the eyes — I couldn't see the eyes from that distance, I want to be clear about that, the rational part of me insists on being clear — but I *felt* them, the way you feel the edge of a building when you're standing on a rooftop and you haven't looked down but your body knows.

And my body did what my body does.

Immediately. Comprehensively. With the enthusiastic, full-orchestra commitment of a physiological system that has never once read the room.

I stood in a parking garage in Memphis, Tennessee, fifty yards from something that was not human, something that made every cell in my body scream *run*, and I got the most definitive erection of my adult life.

The shape moved. Not toward me — it shifted against the column, a recalibration, and I understood with perfect, crystalline terror that it was *watching* my response. That it could see. That at fifty yards, in fluorescent light, it could read my body the way I read a client's grief: fluently, precisely, with professional interest.

Then the lights in the garage went out — some timer, some automatic shutoff — and when they buzzed back on three seconds later, the column was empty.

I drove home at nineteen miles an hour. I held the steering wheel at ten and two. I took a shower so hot it left a rash on my chest. I did not sleep.

And the thing I couldn't stop thinking — the thought that sat in my skull for the next three weeks like a stone in a shoe — was not *what was that* or *am I in danger* or even *why did my body do that*, because I knew why, I always knew why, the wiring is the wiring.

The thing I couldn't stop thinking was: it looked *surprised*.

## II.

Three weeks. Twenty-one days of rational daylight in which the parking garage receded into the category of things that probably didn't happen, or happened differently than I remembered, or happened exactly as I remembered but admitted a non-supernatural explanation that I simply hadn't identified yet.

I went to work. I sat with clients. Denise Waverly, whose husband had died on a riding mower, told me she'd started sleeping in his shirts. I held space for that. Kevin Park, twenty-three, whose sister had been killed by a drunk driver, sat in my office and didn't speak for forty-five minutes and I let the silence hold because silence, in grief work, is not absence — it's the room where the unsayable lives. And my body behaved. My body was exemplary. I was in the country of sadness and the wiring worked fine, and I thought: maybe it was nothing. Maybe the parking garage was a woman with unusual proportions and a dramatic sense of personal space and I hallucinated the rest. Maybe I've been alone so long that my nervous system is inventing threats to justify its own baroque malfunctions. Maybe I should call my endocrinologist.

Then she knocked on my door.

A Thursday. 8:47 PM. I was eating pad thai from the place on Cooper that Caitlin and I used to order from, because punishment is a dish best served in a repurposed takeout container with a memory attached to it. The knock was normal. Three knocks, the standard social-contract knock, the knock of a UPS driver or a neighbor with a noise complaint.

I opened the door.

She was beautiful.

I want to be precise about this because precision is all I have and even precision is going to fail me in about thirty seconds. She was beautiful the way a deep lake is beautiful, the way a cliff face is beautiful — in a way that contained, without contradiction, the possibility of your death. Her face was symmetrical. Not approximately symmetrical, the way human faces are, where the left eye sits a millimeter higher and the jaw shifts slightly at the hinge. *Symmetrical*. The kind of symmetry that, once you notice it, makes every human face you've ever seen look like a rough draft. Her mouth was slightly too wide. Not in a way you'd notice in a photograph, but in person, standing in a doorway, it created the impression that her smile used more of her face than a smile should need. Her eyes were dark, and the darkness had *depth* — not brown-dark, not black-dark, but dark the way a well is dark, the way the ocean is dark below the photic zone, dark in a way that suggested *continuation*. That if you looked long enough you'd find something at the bottom and it would not be a color.

She was wearing a green jacket and jeans and her hair was pulled back and she looked like a woman who lived in an apartment complex in midtown Memphis, and she looked like nothing that had ever lived in any place built by human hands.

"Hi," she said. "I just moved into 4C. I'm Sable."

Her voice was warm. Her voice was the kind of warm that makes you think of specific things — a hand on the back of your neck, a drink you didn't know you needed, the first five minutes of a hot bath. Her voice made me think the world was a fundamentally safe place, and the part of my brain that was still online, the part that had not yet been annexed by the catastrophe taking place below my waist, understood that this was *exactly what the voice was designed to do*.

"Marcus," I said. My voice cracked on the second syllable. I was forty-one years old.

"I locked myself out," she said. "Which is — I mean, I've been in the building for six hours and I've already locked myself out, so that tells you everything about me as a person." She laughed. The laugh was good. The laugh was *too* good — it landed exactly where a laugh should land, hit exactly the right note of self-deprecation, lasted exactly the right duration. It was a laugh that had been *practiced until it was indistinguishable from spontaneous*, and the only reason I could tell was that I am a man who has spent his entire professional life detecting performed emotion, and even then I was only about sixty percent sure.

"Do you want me to call the building manager?" I said. I was holding the pad thai container in front of my body. I was holding it at approximately the height you would hold a pad thai container if you were a man desperate to obstruct a specific sight line. She glanced down. Not at the pad thai. Past the pad thai. Just for a moment, a flicker of the eyes so brief it barely qualified as movement.

She looked back at my face. And her expression — this is the part I've replayed a thousand times, the part I will never satisfactorily decode — her expression *shifted*. Not to hunger, though hunger was there, a substratum, a geological layer beneath whatever was happening on the surface. The shift was smaller and stranger. Her eyebrows moved a quarter-inch. Her too-wide mouth parted slightly. The symmetry of her face, for one half-second, broke — the left side reacting before the right, the way a real face reacts, the way a face reacts when it's *genuinely surprised*.

She had expected me to be afraid. She could read it on me, the terror, the full-body klaxon of a nervous system that knew *exactly* what was standing in my doorway. She had expected the fear.

She had not expected *this*.

"The building manager," she repeated, and there was a beat — a tiny, arrhythmic pause that I recognized because I hear pauses for a living, I read the silences between sentences the way a cardiologist reads the spaces between heartbeats — and in that pause she was *recalculating*. "That would be great," she said. "If it's not too much trouble."
