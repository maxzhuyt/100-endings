# The Shape of His Goodbye

## 1.

The thing about keeping score is that nobody watches the scorekeeper.

Eleven years I've been sitting behind the console at Sawmill Lanes on Tuesday nights, keeping book for the Gravel & Sons Heating team because somebody has to and because I have reading glasses and a long memory and no particular interest in rolling a fourteen-pound ball at wooden pins when I could be sitting down with a Diet Vernors and a pencil. I still keep paper alongside the electronic. Habit. The machine counts; I verify. You'd be surprised how often the machine is wrong. Or maybe you wouldn't. Machines don't watch. They calculate.

I am telling you this because I want you to understand what it means when I say the numbers stopped making sense on April 14th.

Gary Petrovic has bowled on this team since 2016. He is a brake technician at Meineke. He has a mustache that his wife, Dawn, has been asking him to shave since their wedding. He carries a 161 average, which I can tell you because I carry it for him in my head the way other women carry their children's birthdays or the price of gas. One-sixty-one. That's Gary. That's what Gary *is*, numerically, on a Tuesday night: a man who will finish somewhere between 148 and 174, who will leave the 10-pin standing twice per game because he refuses to adjust his entry angle, who will say "there it is" after every strike whether the strike was lucky or skillful.

On April 14th, Gary threw seven consecutive strikes across games two and three. That alone would have been a career night. But it wasn't the streak that made me put down my Vernors. It was the *sameness*. I have watched thousands of bowling balls leave thousands of hands under these fluorescent lights, and what I can tell you is that no human release is perfectly identical. There is always variation — the wrist cocks a quarter-degree differently, or the thumb clears the hole a fraction early, or the plant foot slides an inch long. It's the reason a 161 bowler averages 161 instead of 300. The body is not a machine.

Seven times, Gary's hand opened at the same point. Seven times, the ball entered the pocket at what I estimated — and later confirmed with the slow-motion replay Linda's nephew pulled off the alley's security feed — was 5.8 degrees. His shoulders, his hips, the moment of release, the follow-through: spliced footage. Identical. Like someone had copied the first frame seven times and pressed play.

"There it is," Gary said. Seven times.

I marked it. I said nothing.

Linda Huang has been on the team for four years. Podiatrist. Good arm, inconsistent footwork, career average 147 on a generous night. During her backswing in game two, frame six, something happened that I have replayed in my memory perhaps two hundred times since and still cannot describe with confidence. The ball was at the top of her arc, her body committed to a release path that I could see — because I have been watching release paths for eleven years — would send it wide left, probably into the gutter. And then Linda *adjusted*. Not in the way a skilled bowler adjusts, which is to say with a compensating wrist movement born of muscle memory. She adjusted the way a hummingbird adjusts: mid-flight, outside of time, as though the air around her had thickened enough to give her a second chance at the physics. The ball came down her corrected line and struck the headpin flush. She didn't blink. She didn't seem to know she'd done anything unusual.

I marked it. I said nothing.

Terrence Mills joined us in January. He is younger than the rest of us by a comfortable margin — thirty-one to Gary's fifty-four, Linda's forty-eight, my own sixty-two — and built like a man who once played football and never fully accepted that he'd stopped. His bowling is all power, no touch, and his average reflects this: 138, with wild variance, capable of a 180 game followed by a 109. He treats the pins like they have personally offended him.

In game three, frame four, Terrence released a ball with a sound I had never heard before. Every bowler in the surrounding four lanes looked up. The ball did not roll so much as detonate through the pin deck. All ten pins went down instantly, several of them airborne, and the 7-pin — which is a regulation pin, three pounds six ounces of maple coated in plastic — cracked. Not chipped. *Cracked*. A jagged fault line from crown to base, visible when the pin-setter cycled it back and Terrence walked over and picked it up and held it and looked at it and then looked at me.

I held his eyes. I wrote down the strike. I said nothing.

And then there was Marcus.

Marcus Hall is thirty-seven. He teaches kindergarten at Wickliffe Elementary. He is the gentlest person I have ever known at length, which is a distinction I make carefully because I have known many people who perform gentleness for a season and then set it down; Marcus has carried it for the four years I've known him with a steadiness that suggests it isn't a performance but a structural fact. He averages 155. He bowls with a secondhand ball he bought at a garage sale. He always asks how my knees are doing before I ask, and he listens to the answer.

In game three, frame eight, Marcus was staring at a 7-10 split.

You need to understand what a 7-10 split means. In bowling, it is the catastrophe. The 7-pin stands alone on the far left; the 10-pin stands alone on the far right. They are separated by roughly thirty-six inches of empty deck. To convert a 7-10 split, a bowler must hit one pin at such a precise angle that the pin ricochets off the side wall and crosses the entire deck to knock down the other. Professional bowlers convert this spare less than one percent of the time. In eleven years of Tuesday-night league, I have never seen it done.

Marcus stood at the line. He was very still. He looked at the pins with an expression I've turned over in my mind many times since — not concentration, not focus, but something closer to *resignation*. The way you look at a math problem whose answer you already know but are required by the test format to show your work. He threw. The ball clipped the 7-pin's right edge at what must have been a mathematically exact angle because the pin spun into the wall, rebounded, skated across the pin deck like it was on a rail, and caught the 10-pin dead center.

He didn't celebrate. He didn't react. He sat back down and rubbed the heel of his throwing hand and said, "Lucky."

I looked at my scorebook. I looked at the numbers I had written over the course of three games. I looked at Gary, who was staring at his own hand with faint confusion. I looked at Linda, who was sipping her beer with her eyes half-closed like she was listening to music no one else could hear. I looked at Terrence, who was flexing his fingers open and closed, open and closed. I looked at Marcus, who was looking at the floor.

I said nothing. I closed the book. I drove everyone home.

I dropped off Gary first, then Linda, then Terrence. Marcus was last because Marcus is always last, because Marcus lives farthest east and because he uses the drive time to grade worksheets with a penlight in the passenger seat. That night he didn't grade anything. He sat with his hands on his knees and watched the road.

I almost spoke. I had the sentence ready — *something happened tonight* — solid and blunt and shaped like a thing that could be set on the dashboard between us. But I looked at his face in the light from the passing streetlamps and I saw that he already knew. He already knew something had happened. He already knew I'd seen it. And he was waiting to find out if I was going to say it aloud or let it live in the numbers where it was safe.

I let it live.

I pulled into his apartment complex and he gathered his bag and his ball and said, "Thanks, Deb. Same time next week."

"Same time next week."

He got out. He walked to his door. I sat in the parking lot with the engine running for forty-five seconds longer than was normal. Then I drove home, brushed my teeth, and sat on the edge of my bed with my scorebook open on my lap and added the columns again. They still didn't make sense.

They still don't.

## 2.

Three Tuesdays later, Linda's ball started curving *before she released it.*

I mean that literally. In frame two of game one, I watched — and I was the only one watching from the angle that showed it — the ball begin its hook while it was still in Linda's hand, as though the lane's friction pattern had reached backward through time and started pulling. Linda seemed unsurprised. She adjusted her wrist to match a curve she shouldn't have been able to feel yet, and the result was the most elegant strike I've seen thrown in that building.

Gary, meanwhile, had stopped saying "there it is." He didn't need to. Every ball went exactly where he said it would, and he'd begun calling his shots aloud before his approach — "Brooklyn pocket, half-inch right of center" — not out of arrogance but with the dazed, clinical wonder of a man describing a hallucination that keeps turning out to be real. His three-game series was 279, 268, 289. His career average was 161.

And Terrence. During practice frames, Terrence had rolled a ball so hard that the impact sound drew the night manager out from behind the shoe counter with his mouth open. When the pins reset, two of them wobbled. Something in the mechanism ground and stuck. The night manager looked at Terrence. Terrence looked at the night manager. "Bad pin-setter," Terrence said. The night manager did not look convinced.

After games, we lingered in the parking lot. This was not unusual. We had always lingered — it is one of the things I've liked about this team, the twelve or fifteen minutes of standing around in the dark between the last frame and the drive home, talking about nothing, the kind of aimless post-social warmth that I understand is unusual for adults and that I have never taken for granted. But this night the lingering had a different quality. A voltage. Everyone was leaning against their cars and looking at their hands or their shoes or the sky and nobody was leaving and nobody was speaking and I understood, because I have been watching groups of people negotiate unspoken knowledge for decades, that we were standing on the lip of something and waiting for someone to tip us over.

It was Terrence who went first, because it is always Terrence who goes first.

"Okay," he said. He was leaning against his F-150 with his arms crossed. "So is anyone else—" He stopped. He uncrossed his arms. He held up his right hand and squeezed, and the quarter he was holding between his thumb and forefinger bent in half with a sound like a staple going through cardboard. He held up the bent quarter. "Because *that's* been happening."

The silence lasted three seconds. Then Gary laughed — a short, nervous, relieved sound — and said, "Show of hands. Who's got weird shit going on."

Nobody raised a hand. Then everybody raised a hand.

What followed was fifteen minutes that I've thought about more than any fifteen minutes of my life, and I've had a life with some notable fifteen-minute stretches — the call from the oncologist about the lump in my left breast that turned out to be benign, the moment my daughter's first breath caught and held. This was different. This was five people standing in a bowling-alley parking lot under a buzzing sodium lamp trying to explain the impossible to each other using the only vocabulary they had, which was the vocabulary of rec-league bowlers and brake technicians and podiatrists and kindergarten teachers and sixty-two-year-old women who keep score.

Gary went second. He cupped his hands in front of him and moved them slowly apart, and as he did he described — tracing invisible lines in the air like a man mapping a subway system only he could see — the trajectory of his last twelve shots, the entry angle, the deflection pattern, the pin carry, all of it. "It's like I can see the math," he said. "Not numbers. Not like a calculator. It's *spatial*. I look at the lane and I see every possible line the ball could take, and one of them is brighter than the others, and all I have to do is throw it down the bright one." He paused. "I got a C-minus in high school geometry."

Linda was sitting on the hood of her Civic. She pulled her knees up and hugged them and said, "Everything slows down." She said it simply, without drama, the way you'd describe a change in the weather. "Not all the time. But when I'm in my approach, when the ball is moving — everything else just... decelerates. I can see the oil pattern on the lane. I can feel the ball's rotation in my hand before I let go. Last week I adjusted my release in the middle of my backswing, and I know that doesn't make sense, that's not how time works, except it is. For me. Now."

Terrence held up the bent quarter again. Then he gripped the bumper of his truck with one hand and lifted the rear end six inches off the ground. The suspension groaned. He set it back down carefully and said, "Carol thinks I have a glandular thing."

And then everyone looked at Marcus.

Marcus was standing slightly apart from the group — not dramatically, not enough that anyone else would have clocked it as intentional, but I did, because I always know where Marcus is in a room or a parking lot or a world. He had his hands in the pockets of his windbreaker. The sodium light gave his face an amber cast that made him look older.

"Marcus," Gary said. "Come on, man. What's yours?"

Marcus took his hands out of his pockets. He looked at each of us in turn — Gary, Linda, Terrence, me — and I had the unsettling sensation that he wasn't just looking at us but *reading* us, the way I read a scoresheet, except his scoresheet included every possible future frame.

"I just..." he started. He stopped. He looked at the asphalt. "I know what's going to happen next."

Nobody spoke.

"Not everything," he said quietly. "Not — I can't tell you the lottery numbers. It's people. It's..." He pressed his fingers against his temples. "If I look at someone, I can see the probabilities. What they'll say, what they'll do, how they'll react. It's not like a vision. It's like — you know when you've read a book so many times you know the next sentence before you turn the page? It's like that, except the book is everyone around me, all the time, and I never asked to read it."

Terrence said, "Dude, that's *awesome*."

Marcus looked at Terrence. His expression was perfectly neutral. "You're going to say 'we should enter the National Amateur Circuit.'"

Terrence's mouth opened. Closed. Opened again. "No I wasn't."

"You were going to wait another four seconds and then say 'we should enter the National Amateur Circuit' and then follow it with 'I'm serious' when people laughed."

A beat.

"We should enter the National Amateur Circuit," Terrence said, in the defeated voice of a man whose free will has just been gently mugged. "I'm serious."

Everyone laughed. Linda doubled over on the hood of her Civic. Gary braced himself against his car door. Terrence, despite himself, grinned. The laughter was loud and helpless and cracked open with relief, the sound of five people who had been carrying a secret for three weeks and could finally set it down in the open air.

I was leaning against the hood of my Caravan with my arms folded. I laughed too. I am not immune to the comic dimensions of five middle-aged-to-youngish rec-league bowlers from suburban Columbus standing in a parking lot discovering they have superpowers. It is objectively absurd. The sodium light buzzed. Somewhere across the lot, a car alarm went off and stopped. The Sawmill Lanes sign flickered its perpetual, half-dead flicker.

But I was watching Marcus.

Everyone else was laughing. Marcus was smiling — or rather, his mouth was arranged in the shape of a smile, the way a good teacher's mouth is always arranged in the shape of the appropriate response. His eyes were not participating. His eyes were doing the thing I would come to recognize over the next several months, which was *running the numbers* — calculating, predicting, watching the probability trees branch and branch and branch.
