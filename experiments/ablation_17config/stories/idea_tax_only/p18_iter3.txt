# The Meridian Commission

## I.

You will want to know who is telling you this, and why, and whether any of it can be trusted. I will answer the second question last.

My name is Phineas Grout. From May of 1871 through the autumn of 1873, I served as chain-man to the United States Meridian Commission, Western Division, under the direction of the Department of the Interior. If you do not know what a chain-man does, I will tell you, because it is germane. A chain-man carries. He carries the Gunter's chain — sixty-six feet of tempered steel links, precisely calibrated, heavier than you would suppose. He carries the ranging rods. He carries the station stakes, the tacks, the plumb bobs, the extra thermometers the astronomer insists upon for refraction corrections. He carries what he is told to carry to where he is told to carry it, and then he holds whatever he has carried very still while other men, higher men, read angles and record numbers. He is the most observed member of any survey crew — every instrument, at some point in the day, is pointed at him or at what he holds — and yet he is the least *seen*. You look through the chain-man the way you look through a window. He is the medium, not the subject.

I am telling you this because I was the medium for eleven years, and in that time I developed the habit of watching without the expectation of being watched in return. It is a useful habit. I commend it to you.

In September of 1883, a Congressional mandate arrived at the Department of the Interior directing that the six surviving members of the original Meridian Commission reassemble and complete the Bitterroot baseline survey that had been — the language was careful, then as now — *suspended* in the field season of 1873. Funds were appropriated. Letters were dispatched. The six of us were called back to the work we had left in the mountains a decade prior, to the line we had drawn partway across the divide before we stopped drawing it.

I am writing this record because someone will need it. I do not say this for dramatic effect. I say it the way I would note a bench-mark elevation: as a fact of position, established for the use of those who come after. You are the one who comes after. You are the eventual investigator, whether you know it yet or not, and this document is your triangulation point. Everything I set down here I set down for your use.

Whether you should trust it is a question I will leave, with some care, to you.

## II.

Before I tell you what happened when we reassembled, you will need to know what we had become. Ten years is a long survey line. It deforms the ground it crosses.

There were seven of us originally. Now there were six, because Harlan Coles was dead. Coles was the lead triangulator, the man who chose the stations and read the angles that stitched the landscape into mathematics. I will come to Coles. Not yet. He is the gap in this record, the point I will establish by approaching from every other direction, the way you fix an inaccessible summit by triangulating from the stations around it. For now, set him aside. He is dead, and the manner of his death is the reason you are reading this.

The survivors, then. I will render them as I knew them — which is to say, at a distance, holding a rod, watching.

**Aldous Bickle, Astronomer.** In 1873, Bickle could fix latitude to within a tenth of an arc-second by timing star transits with a pocket chronometer while the rest of us held lanterns and swatted insects. By 1883 he was teaching celestial navigation at the Thatcher Maritime Institute in Bridgeport, Connecticut, an academy whose accreditation had been revoked three years prior for reasons the school's remaining brochure described as "administrative restructuring." The boys who sat in Bickle's classroom would never go to sea. Most of them had been sent to Thatcher by families who could not afford better and did not know worse. Bickle taught them to find Polaris, to read a sextant, to calculate hour angles — all of it precise, rigorous, and perfectly useless, because the boys would go on to clerk in shops and shoe factories along the Connecticut coast, and the stars would remain where Bickle had placed them, fixed and irrelevant. He drank. This was widely known. What was less widely known was what he drank *from*: a small leather-cased flask he had carried in the field in '73, originally intended for mercury — the liquid metal we used to create an artificial horizon for the astronomical observations. The mercury was long gone. The flask still smelled of it, faintly, a sweet metallic breath that would eventually have killed him if the whiskey did not finish first. I mention the flask because Bickle was a man who could not stop putting his mouth to the instrument of his own precision. You may draw from this what you will.

**Josiah Kern, Topographer.** Kern was the one who read the living earth. Where Bickle watched the sky and Coles measured angles, Kern interpreted the shape of things — ridges, drainages, the fall line of a slope, the way a valley telegraphed its geology through the texture of its vegetation. In the decade since, Kern had gone to the railroads. He surveyed rights-of-way for the Northern Pacific and grew, if not rich, then comfortable in a way that insulated him from the kind of ruin the rest of us courted. Kern's house in Helena had a porch, a wife, and a barometer mounted in the hallway that he checked every morning with the same focus he once applied to reading contour. He had become cautious. This was the word his wife used in her letter accepting the Commission's travel arrangements — "Josiah has become a cautious man, and I trust you will understand if he requires certain assurances" — and it was the right word, because caution in Kern expressed itself spatially: he sat with his back to walls, he chose the uphill side of any path, and he never, in the field or out of it, allowed another man to stand above him on a slope. This had not been true before 1873. You will understand when I describe the ridge.

**Everett Hull, Computer.** I use the word in its older sense. Hull was a human calculator, a man whose purpose on the Commission was to receive the raw observations — angles, distances, azimuths, star times — and reduce them to coordinates. He converted the world into numbers and the numbers into position. He was, in 1873, the fastest and most reliable computer the Department of the Interior had ever employed, capable of closing a triangulation network in his head while the rest of us were still unpacking the logarithm tables. By 1883, Hull had not worked for the Department in seven years. He had been released — retired, the paperwork said, though he was barely forty — after it was discovered that he had spent the better part of three years recomputing the observations from the 1873 survey. Not the extant observations. Not the measurements we had actually completed before the work stopped. Hull had been computing *the survey that did not exist*: running the reductions for stations we never occupied, closing triangles across baselines we never measured, extending the network over a Bitterroot divide he had not visited since the day we left it. He had filled eleven ledgers with these calculations. The numbers were, by every standard of geodetic practice, flawless. They simply did not correspond to anything that had happened in the physical world. When I visited him in his sister's house in Baltimore before we departed, he showed me the ledgers with a calm that I found more troubling than any agitation. "The line is there, Grout," he said, touching the page. "The line was always there. We simply stopped measuring it." He looked at me with perfect lucidity. "But it did not stop."

**Morris Crayle, Naturalist-Recorder.** Crayle had been the Commission's double function: he recorded the official logbook of observations and field notes, and he collected botanical and geological specimens along the survey route. He was the one who wrote things down. In this he was my closest counterpart — I carried the instruments, he carried the record — though his status was considerably above mine, because a man's words are considered more important than a man's labor, a hierarchy I have had a lifetime to contemplate. After 1873, Crayle disappeared into the Bureau of Indian Affairs. He took a position at the Flathead reservation agency in western Montana, not far from where we had worked, and for ten years he recorded distributions: not of plant species across elevation gradients, but of annuity goods across tribal rolls. Flour, blankets, seed, tobacco — counted, apportioned, entered into ledgers that were sent to Washington and read by no one Crayle ever met. When the Commission's letter reached him, he rode to the Missoula post office and sent a single-line reply: "I will come. I have kept my pencils sharp." I cannot tell you whether this was meant as humor, as bitterness, or as a plain statement of professional readiness. With Crayle, all three were always simultaneously true. What I can tell you is that in ten years at Flathead, Crayle had not once, so far as I could determine, gone into the mountains. He had lived at the foot of the Bitterroot range — close enough to see the peaks from his office window — and he had not set foot above the valley floor. A naturalist who will not go into the field is making a particular kind of statement. Crayle had been making it, silently, to an audience of no one, for a decade.

**Phineas Grout, Chain-man.** I remained at the Department of the Interior. I filed other men's numbers. I maintained survey records, organized expedition manifests, calibrated instruments that returned from the field in other men's hands. I was, in the Department's official correspondence, listed as a clerk, Level 2, with responsibility for the Western Division survey archives. The 1873 Bitterroot survey occupied a single drawer in the archive room: three field books, a partial triangulation diagram, one set of astronomical observations, and a cover memorandum that described the survey's status as *suspended due to field conditions*. I had written that memorandum. No one had asked me to. No one had told me what to write. I wrote *suspended* because *suspended* suggested a future resumption, and because the alternative — *terminated*, *abandoned*, *rendered impossible by the death of the lead triangulator under circumstances that five surviving crew members cannot agree upon* — would have required specificity that no one at the Department wanted to see committed to paper. I maintained where the term *suspended* persisted, undisturbed, for ten years. It was a beautiful invisible fiction, and I tended it the way a groundskeeper tends a grave.

And Coles. Harlan Coles, lead triangulator. I have said I would come to him, and I have circled him now long enough that you can see the shape of the absence. Six portraits and a gap. I am not being coy. I am being precise. The manner in which I describe Coles — the *order* in which I describe him — is itself a piece of evidence, and I would rather you noticed my control of the sequence than that I pretended not to exercise it. A man who tells you he has no agenda is a man whose agenda requires your inattention.

Coles will emerge. He will emerge the way he did on the ridge — from multiple directions at once, none of them quite converging.

## III.

This is what I can tell you about the afternoon of August 19, 1873, on the Bitterroot ridge above the South Fork of Lolo Creek, at what would have been Station 14 in our triangulation network.

This is also what I cannot tell you.

The two quantities are not as different as you might expect.

Coles had gone up the ridge with Kern to reconnoiter the station site. This is agreed. Bickle was below, at Station 12, completing a round of angles. This is agreed. Hull was at the base camp with the computation ledgers. Crayle was between the camp and the ridge, collecting specimens along the creek — blue camas, I believe, though the field notebook is ambiguous on this point. I was approximately four hundred vertical feet below the proposed station site, at the far end of the chain line, holding a ranging rod plumb against a slope of loose talus. I could see, depending on when you are asking about, either everything below me or nothing above. The rod requires your attention. You hold it and you watch the plumb line and you do not look up.

These are the points of agreement. Now the accounts diverge.

Kern states — stated, in his initial deposition to the Department, and again in his letter responding to the reassembly mandate, and again on the first night of the second survey, sitting across a fire whose light made his face look hewn — that Coles was reckless. That Coles reached the proposed station site, found the rock outcrop unsuitable for a monument, and insisted on climbing farther along the ridge to find better ground. That Kern warned him the footing was poor. That Coles went anyway, because Coles always went anyway, and that the sound Kern heard was the sound of a man whose boot found the edge of what was not there. A fall. An accident born of the triangulator's constitutional inability to accept that a sightline might not exist where he wanted it to exist. In Kern's telling, the land killed Coles, and the land was blameless because Coles had been warned.

Bickle's account, delivered in the clipped cadence of a man accustomed to dictating star times, puts the emphasis elsewhere. Bickle was not on the ridge. He was below, at Station 12, but he had his theodolite trained on the ridge line because he was waiting for Coles and Kern to signal that Station 14 was established. Through the telescope — inverted, magnified, swimming in the refraction of an August afternoon — he saw two figures on the rock. He is clear about this. Two figures, close together, and then a period of what he calls "gestural agitation" — arms moving in a way that did not resemble the normal semaphore of survey signaling — and then one figure where there had been two.
