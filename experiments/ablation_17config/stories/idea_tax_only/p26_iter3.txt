# Evidence for the Wrongful Death Suit Against God

---

This is a voice memo. I want that stated at the top because I don't know what format this will be in by the time anyone finds it — if anyone finds it — and I want the record to reflect that I'm speaking this aloud, in real time, into a phone with eleven percent battery, in the back of a government-issued Chevy Suburban that smells like someone else's Subway sandwich, at 9:47 p.m. Eastern Standard Time on what may or may not be the last Thursday in the history of Thursdays.

My name is Glen Pasternack. I am — was — am the senior personal aide to Dalton Mirova, known publicly as Paladin, known to the United States Department of Defense as Asset One, known to me as the reason I have been on beta-blockers since I was thirty-one. I have held this position for twelve years. In that time I have managed his calendar, his public statements, his dietary restrictions, and the exposed wiring of his emotional life. I have a GS-14 pay grade and no dental.

The asteroid — I should say this clearly — the asteroid designated 2041 QM7 is approximately eighteen hours from impact. Projected strike zone is the mid-Atlantic, which sounds almost gentle until you learn that "mid-Atlantic impact" is a polite way of saying "the ocean turns into a wall and Europe and the Eastern Seaboard both stop existing, and then the dust blots out the sun, and then everything else stops existing too, more slowly and worse." This has been confirmed independently by NASA, ESA, the Chinese National Space Administration, and a guy at Caltech who, when the numbers came back, reportedly said "Oh" and then sat down on the floor of his lab and didn't get up for forty-five minutes. The point being: this is real.

Dalton can stop it. I want that on the record too. Dalton can fly at speeds that embarrass physics. Dalton can exert force sufficient to redirect a body of that mass if he intercepts it far enough out. Dalton is the only thing on this planet, or off it, that constitutes a viable response. NATO knows this. The Joint Chiefs know this. The Secretary-General gave a televised address four hours ago in which he said, with the grim composure of a man who has been told to project calm while his hands shake below the podium, that "our greatest protector is rising to meet this challenge as we speak."

Our greatest protector is not rising to meet this challenge.

Our greatest protector is currently sitting in a Courtyard by Marriott in Arlington, Virginia, rehearsing — and I need you to absorb this fully — *rehearsing an apology speech to his college roommate, Todd*, because Todd's wife told Dalton at a barbecue in 2038 that Todd had felt "a little abandoned" after Dalton moved to D.C., and Dalton has decided, tonight, eighteen hours before the possible end of all human life, that he cannot leave the planet's atmosphere without first getting this right, without first making sure the phrasing adequately conveys remorse without being "too heavy," because — and this is a direct quote from eleven minutes ago — "If I go up there and I don't come back, I need Todd to know I heard him. I can't die with Todd thinking I didn't hear him."

Todd, for context, could not pick Dalton out of a lineup. I have met Todd. Todd has season tickets to the Nationals and a Traeger grill he won't shut up about and the emotional interior of a golden retriever. Todd is fine. Todd has been fine his entire life. Todd does not lie awake at night wondering whether Dalton Mirova heard him. Todd said "a little abandoned" the way you say "a little chilly" when someone else controls the thermostat — it was an observation, not a wound. But Dalton received it as a wound. Dalton receives everything as a wound.

And now: the world.

I'm going to explain how we got here. I'm going to do it by telling you about Dalton's prom night, because it's the same story. It has always been the same story. If you're listening to this after the impact — if "after the impact" is a thing that exists, if there is anyone left with ears and a device that plays .m4a files — I need you to understand that humanity did not die because of a rock. Rocks are honest. Rocks have trajectories that can be calculated and they do not deviate from those trajectories because their mothers used a period instead of an exclamation point. Humanity, if it dies, will die because a man who could bench-press a tectonic plate never once in his life developed the ability to sit with the possibility that someone, somewhere, might be mildly disappointed in him.

This is my evidence. This is my testimony. If there's a court, if there's anything after this that resembles accountability— God, the universe, whoever signed off on giving *this particular man* the power to move mountains and the emotional stability of a sand castle at high tide — I want this on the record.

Consider it a wrongful death suit.

Against God.

---

Dalton Mirova was seventeen years old. I wasn't there — he was before my time — but I've seen the footage so many times that it's replaced whatever real memories I might've had of my own prom, which is fine, because I didn't go to mine, because I was the kind of person even then who stayed home doing something practical while everyone else did something human, but we'll get to that.

The footage is from three sources: the school's security cameras, a local news helicopter that arrived after, and the dashboard camera of a Berks County sheriff's deputy who was parked across the street eating a hoagie when the event occurred. I've watched all three angles. I've synced them. I could narrate this in my sleep and I have, in fact, narrated it in my sleep, according to two ex-girlfriends and one ex-boyfriend, all of whom left me for reasons that are becoming clearer to me as I sit in this car.

Here is what happened:

Dalton's powers had manifested about four months earlier. He was still new to them. He could fly, but not well — the early footage looks like someone threw a person and the person kept disagreeing with gravity without offering a coherent alternative. He was strong, but the strength was erratic, tied to his adrenaline, emotional in a way that the later military testing would confirm and the DoD would spend a fortune trying to train out of him, unsuccessfully, because you cannot train a river not to flow downhill.

There was a girl named Margaux Kimble. This is a real name — it's been in the public record since the Congressional hearings, so I'm not revealing anything — and I want to be precise about what Margaux Kimble did and did not do, because what she did and did not do is the entire point.

What Margaux Kimble did: She said to Dalton, in the hallway outside AP Chemistry, approximately nine days before prom, "It would be funny if you came." She said this. Multiple witnesses confirm it. She was talking to a small group, not to Dalton specifically, but Dalton was in the group, and she made eye contact with him briefly — briefly — during the word "funny."

What Margaux Kimble did not do: She did not ask Dalton to prom. She did not indicate romantic interest. She did not say "you" to Dalton specifically. She used a conditional. "It would be funny if you came." This is, grammatically and socially, one of the lowest-commitment utterances available in the English language. It is the verbal equivalent of a shrug.

Dalton went to prom.

He wore a suit his mother had helped him pick out — navy, slightly too big in the shoulders, which is heartbreaking in retrospect because his shoulders would later become the thing that stopped a cargo ship from capsizing in the Strait of Malacca, but at seventeen they were just shoulders that hadn't figured out what they were yet. He had a haircut. He had clearly, based on the security footage, applied cologne, because you can see the way he keeps unconsciously turning his own wrist toward his nose as he walks in, checking, rechecking. He walked into that gym the way anyone walks into a room where the person they want is going to be — he walked in like the room was a question and he was hoping to be the answer.

Margaux Kimble was already there. She was with friends. She was wearing a green dress, which I mention only because Dalton mentioned it in his Congressional testimony seven years later, unprompted, in the middle of a question about structural damage estimates, which tells you everything.

What happened next: Nothing. That's what happened. Margaux said hi. Margaux danced with other people, because she had come with other people, because she had not come with Dalton, because she had not asked Dalton, because "it would be funny if you came" is not an invitation. Margaux, at one point, looked at Dalton across the room, smiled in the benign, unfocused way you smile at someone you vaguely recognize, and turned back to her friends.

Dalton stood near the bleachers for approximately forty minutes.

I've watched those forty minutes more than anything else. The security camera in the northwest corner of the gym has the best angle, and if you pull the footage and watch his face — really watch it — you can see something happening that I don't have the vocabulary for, that I'm not sure anyone has the vocabulary for. He isn't angry. He isn't crying. He's *recalculating.* You can see it. Every few seconds his expression shifts, like he's running a new simulation: *Maybe she didn't see me. Maybe I should go over there. Maybe I should wait. Maybe that smile meant something. Maybe it didn't. Maybe I should leave. But if I leave, she'll think I don't care. But if I stay, she'll think I'm desperate. But—* Forty minutes of this. Forty minutes of a seventeen-year-old boy standing next to a folding table of grocery-store cookies, trying to solve an equation that has no solution, because the equation is *Does she like me?* and the answer is *She has not thought about it at all,* and that answer — the null answer, the absence of data — is the one thing his mind cannot metabolize.

What finally triggered the event — and "triggered" is the word used in the DoD file, sanitized, clinical, as if we're talking about a mechanical failure and not a child's heart doing the only thing it knew how to do — was this: Margaux Kimble's date, a boy named Kieran Somebody, put his hand on the small of her back as they walked toward the photo station. That's it. A hand. The small of a back. A universal and essentially meaningless gesture of escort.

Dalton's power discharge was radial. It began at his feet — you can see the gym floor buckle first, the hardwood splitting outward in concentric rings like a stone dropped in a pond — and expanded at a rate that the subsequent physics analysis would clock at roughly two hundred feet per second. The gym walls went first, then the school's east wing, then the street, then the row of shops across from the school — a dry cleaner, a Verizon store, a place that sold custom cakes for occasions exactly like prom. Then the next block. Then the next.

Three city blocks. That's the number. Three blocks of Dalton's hometown reduced to what the insurance industry would later classify, with its particular genius for understatement, as "total structural loss." Forty-seven buildings. Two hundred and twelve vehicles. One small public park with a gazebo where, according to a later lawsuit I helped settle, a couple had been married six hours earlier.

No one died.

I need to sit with this for a moment, because the assistant in me — the functionary, the manager, the man with the clipboard — wants to move past it with a note about miraculous outcomes and move on to the next data point. But the person in me, the version of me that is sitting in this car eighteen hours from possible extinction and has finally run out of professionalism — that person needs to say: nobody has ever died. In twenty-three years and dozens of incidents, across five continents and two oceans and one event that technically occurred in low Earth orbit, no bystander has ever been killed by Dalton's power discharges. Not one. People have been thrown. People have been buried. People have been hospitalized, terrified, displaced. But the casualties column reads zero, and it reads zero every single time, and this is either the most statistically improbable streak in the history of probability or — and this is the part that keeps me up at night, this is the part that makes me address this memo to God, specifically — the power *knows.* It knows where the people are. It goes around them. It flattens buildings and threads the needle around every human body inside, as if the power itself has a conscience that its owner lacks — no, not lacks. Dalton has a conscience. Dalton has too much conscience. That's the whole problem.

It's more like — the power has *priorities* that Dalton doesn't have. The power protects people. Dalton protects himself. And the self he's protecting isn't his body, which is essentially invulnerable, but something much softer and much worse: his idea of himself as someone who is loved.

Three blocks of rubble. Dust still rising. Car alarms singing a twelve-part dissonance.

Dalton, standing in the epicenter, suit torn, looking at the space where Margaux Kimble had been standing (she was fine — she'd been thrown clear, she landed on a pile of gym mats that had been in a storage closet that no longer existed, she had a sprained wrist and a fear of loud noises that would persist for years). And a first responder — a firefighter, the first on scene — approached him and said something. We don't have audio. We have the dashcam, which was across the street at a bad angle. But the firefighter testified later, under oath, about what Dalton said.

The firefighter said: "Son, are you hurt?"

And Dalton said: "Do you think she's mad at me?"

Not: *What happened.* Not: *Is everyone okay.* Not: *Oh God, oh God, what did I do.* Those came later — minutes later, hours later, in the long and genuinely agonized aftermath that I do not want to minimize because Dalton's guilt is real, his guilt is always real, his guilt is the most authentic thing about him. But *first*. Before the guilt, before the horror, before anything else could reach the surface of his consciousness and gasp for air — the question. The only question. The question that has never once, in twenty-three years, not been his first question:

*Am I still loved?*

Forty-eight hours ago. The Pentagon. Conference room E-2B-7, which is not its official designation but I don't remember the official designation because my brain has replaced it with the memory of what happened in that room and the designation doesn't matter and nothing matters and I have — okay. Focus.

The briefing was classified COSMIC TOP SECRET, which is a real classification level and which, under the circumstances, was also an unintentional piece of poetry that no one in the room was in a condition to appreciate. Fourteen people. Secretary of Defense. Chairman of the Joint Chiefs. NATO Supreme Allied Commander via secure video. A woman from JPL whose name I didn't catch because she spoke very quietly and I was standing against the wall in my usual position, which is against the wall, which is where the assistant stands, which is where I have always stood.

Dalton was seated at the head of the table. They put him there. They always put him there, and he always does the thing where he says "Oh, I don't need to be at the head" and then sits at the head, and I can never tell if the false modesty is calculated or simply so deeply embedded in his operating system that it *is* his operating system — the constant performance of not wanting what he wants so that someone else will insist he have it.

The JPL woman put up the slides. The asteroid. The trajectory. The mass. The timeline.

I have been in the room for a lot of bad news. I was in the room when the Yellowstone caldera sensors went haywire (false alarm).
