### As Compiled by His Opponent

**Edited, Annotated, and Presented by Yasuda Katsuro**
*Eighth Dan, Tenshin Katori Shintō-ryū*

---

*Published posthumously by instruction of the compiler.*
*Meiwa Press, Tokyo.*

## COMPILER'S PREFACE

I will be dead before this reaches you. This is not sentiment. It is logistics. The cancer has eaten through the lower spine and now works upward with the diligence of a student who has finally found his calling. My doctors tell me weeks. My body tells me less.

I have arranged for this manuscript to reach the publisher three days after my death. This is cowardice, and I name it so. A braver man would have sat in the bookshops and watched people read it. A braver man would have published forty years ago. A braver man would have done a great many things that I have not done, and I suspect a braver man would not have needed to.

What you hold is a collection of thirty-seven letters written to me over four decades by the monk Hideo, formerly Murakami Hideo, formerly the man whom no living swordsman could defeat. They arrived at irregular intervals—sometimes three in a single month, sometimes silence for years so long I believed him dead. They were sent from a succession of mountain temples, never the same one twice in sequence, though he seemed to rotate among four or five. They were written on whatever paper was available to him: fine washi, newsprint, once the blank inner wrapper of a packet of barley tea. One was written on a plank of hinoki wood so thin I could read through it when I held it to the window.

I have never answered a single letter. He never provided a return address. This was, I believe, part of the terms.

I present them here in chronological order with my annotations, which I have tried to keep brief and which have in every case run longer than I intended. The reader will forgive an old man. When one has been silent for forty years, the valve, once opened, does not easily close.

A note on context, for those too young to remember or too foreign to care:

The Sixth All-Japan Classical Martial Arts Tournament of 1983 is remembered, when it is remembered at all, for its final match. I will not rehearse the details that have been rehearsed elsewhere. Murakami Hideo entered the tournament undefeated in formal competition across eleven years and thirty-one bouts. I entered as the fourth seed from Chiba Prefecture, a technician of moderate renown, expected to be eliminated in the quarterfinals. Through a combination of favorable draws, the injury of a superior opponent, and what I must honestly call three performances that exceeded anything I had produced before or have since, I reached the final. The hall was sold out. NHK carried it on a fifteen-minute delay.

Eight seconds into the match, Murakami Hideo lowered his bokken, bowed to me, bowed to the judges, and walked out of the hall.

He was never seen in competition again. Within a year, he had taken monastic vows. Within two, he had disappeared so thoroughly that even the martial arts press—which in those years would track a retired sensei to the ends of Hokkaido for a photograph and a quote—lost his trail entirely.

The speculation was extensive and, as speculation always is, both wildly creative and uniformly wrong. He had been diagnosed with a terminal illness. He had suffered a spiritual crisis. He had been bribed. He had thrown the match in protest of the tournament's judging criteria. He had experienced satori in the instant before the first exchange and realized that all combat was vanity.

I participated in none of this speculation. I accepted the championship with the grace expected of me and returned to Chiba with a trophy I have never displayed and a rank I have never felt I earned. I opened a dojo. I married. I taught. I grew old. I told no one about the letters.

Not because I was asked for silence. I was never asked.

Because I did not know what to say about them except to say what I could not bring myself to say, which is also what Hideo could not bring himself to say, which is the subject and substance of every letter in this collection, and which I will now, for the thirty-eight years I have had to prepare this sentence, fail to say again:

He saw something in my stance. He chose not to use it. He chose exile instead, and he spent forty years trying to tell me what it was, and he never did, and I have spent forty years trying to ask, and I never did, and now we are out of time.

This is enough context. The letters will do the rest. Or they will not. That, too, is part of the problem.

—Yasuda Katsuro
Chiba, October 2023

## LETTER ONE

*Received November 1983. Written on white washi, brushwork precise but hurried. No return address. Postmarked Nagano Prefecture.*

---

Yasuda,

You will be surprised to hear from me. No—you will be surprised that I am writing at all, to anyone, but you will not be surprised that if I were to write to anyone it would be to you. I suspect you understand this. I suspect you understand a great deal more than either of us would find comfortable.

I have taken the tonsure at a temple whose name I will not give. The abbot is a kind man who asks no questions, or rather who asks the same question every day—*Have you eaten?*—and accepts my answer regardless of its truth. I wake at four. I sweep. I chant sutras I do not believe in a language I am only beginning to study. The mountains here are severe. The cold is useful.

I want to tell you why I forfeited the match.

I have written the above sentence and now I find I must sit with it for some time.

I forfeited the match because—

I am not yet ready to write what follows. I set out today to write it and I find the brush will not complete the stroke. You will think this is metaphor. The brush literally stops. My hand, which has never in my life refused an instruction, refuses this one.

I will try again in a later letter. I wanted you to know that I will try.

There is a pine tree outside my window that the other monks call Old Monk because it is bent in the posture of prostration. I do not think it is prostrating. I think it is listening to the ground. I think it heard something once, long ago, and it has spent its whole life leaning closer and closer, trying to hear it again.

Do not try to find me. I say this not as a command but as a kindness. What I have to tell you is better written than spoken. Spoken, I would have to see your face, and I am not equal to your face.

Hideo

---

**COMPILER'S NOTE:** *I received this letter on a Tuesday. I know this because I was grading a student's examination forms when my wife brought the mail, and I only graded examinations on Tuesdays. I read it standing in the hallway. I read it again sitting on the engawa. I read it a third time in the dojo after everyone had gone home, kneeling in seiza on the polished floor with the letter in my lap, and I will confess that the third reading was not really reading; it was simply holding the paper and breathing.*

*I knew, of course. I want to state this clearly at the outset, though it is the thing I have never stated, clearly or otherwise, to anyone. Eight seconds is a short time in the abstract. In a match, it is a completed world. In those eight seconds I watched Hideo assume his opening stance, and I watched his eyes—which were always the most precise instruments in any room he entered—I watched his eyes move over me with the efficiency of a man reading a sentence he already knows by heart, and I watched something change in them. Not fear. Hideo never experienced anything so simple as fear. Something closer to grief.*

*And then he lowered his bokken. And then he bowed. And then he left.*

*I have replayed those eight seconds more than I have replayed any other eight seconds of my life, including the eight seconds during which my first child drew her first breath, and I say this with full awareness of what it reveals about my character. In those eight seconds, Hideo read me. He read me the way a calligrapher reads a brushstroke—not just the ink that is there but the pressure, the speed, the angle, the tremor, the history of every choice the hand made and every choice it refused. He read my stance and he found something in it.*

*Something he would not use.*

*I have spent forty years trying to identify it myself. I have stood before mirrors. I have reviewed the NHK footage—seven seconds of it, before the cameras cut away to the stunned judges. I have asked my most advanced students to attack me and watched their eyes for the moment of recognition that never comes, because they are not Hideo, and what Hideo saw is evidently not visible to lesser eyes.*

*I will say more on this later. For now: I received the letter. I did not reply. I could not reply, as no address was given. But I also would not have replied. I understood even then that his silence and my silence were load-bearing walls, and that if either of us removed one, the entire structure would come down on us both.*

## LETTER TWO

*Received February 1984. Written on thin brown paper, possibly repurposed packaging. Postmarked Niigata Prefecture.*

---

Yasuda,

Snow here for three weeks without pause. The abbot says this is normal. I do not believe him. I think the sky has made a decision and is seeing it through. I respect this.

I have been thinking about your kamae.

No. I must be more precise. I have been thinking about the problem of seeing something that the person you have seen it in does not know is visible. Is the seeing a kind of theft? I take something from you—knowledge about your own body—that you did not offer and cannot retrieve. You go on as before, but I carry a piece of you that you do not know you have given. In what sense are you still whole?

The monks here practice a form of seated meditation in which the goal, as I understand it, is to observe one's thoughts without attachment. To see them rise and pass away like weather. I have tried this. I find that one thought does not pass. It sits at the center of the others like a stone in a stream, and the stream bends around it, and after an hour of meditation I have simply a very detailed map of the shape of the stone.

I am trying to approach what I need to tell you from the correct direction. The difficulty is that all directions seem incorrect. From the north, it sounds like confession, and I am not confessing, because confession implies wrongdoing and I do not yet know whether what I did was wrong. From the south, it sounds like boasting—*I saw what no one else could see*—and that is obscene to me. From the east, it sounds like a warning, and a warning given forty years late is not a warning; it is cruelty. From the west, it sounds like an apology, and an apology for what? For not defeating you? For not allowing you to be defeated? For choosing, in the space of a breath, the one path that would leave both of us unharmed and in doing so harming us both beyond all repair?

The pine tree is now under so much snow it looks as if it is not merely listening to the ground but being pulled into it.

I will write again. I am aware that these letters accomplish nothing. I am aware that they are the opposite of accomplishment—that each one adds another layer of unfinished business to a matter already so encrusted with the unfinished that the original shape is lost. I write them anyway. A monk with no practice is merely an unemployed man living in the mountains.

Hideo

---

**COMPILER'S NOTE:** *The word he uses for my kamae—my combat stance, my guard, the fundamental posture from which all technique proceeds—is written with an unusual emphasis, the brush pressed harder on the first stroke, as though he began the word with the intention of driving it through the paper. I mention this because Hideo's brushwork, even in haste, was a secondary art form. His sensei, the late Murakami Teijiro, was a noted calligrapher, and Hideo absorbed this the way he absorbed everything: totally, as a landscape absorbs rain.*

*He says he is thinking about my kamae. What he does not say—what he will not say in this letter or in the thirty-five that follow—is what he saw in it. He circles it. He alludes to it. He constructs elaborate philosophical frameworks that lead, by impeccable logic, to the doorstep of the thing itself, and then he steps back and begins again from a different direction.*

*I have sometimes thought, in my less charitable moments, that he was toying with me. A cat with a half-dead mouse, batting it back and forth with each letter. But I do not truly believe this. I believe the thing he saw was so simple, so structural, so utterly beyond remedy, that to name it plainly would have been an act of destruction—of me, of my art, of the forty years of teaching I built on a stance that contained, at its very center, like a flaw in a sword blade visible only at the exact angle of killing light, a door through which death could walk anytime it wished.*

*He could not name it because to name it was to stab me with it.*

*And I could not ask because to ask was to hand him the blade.*

## LETTER FIVE

*Received March 1986. Written on pale blue stationery, possibly obtained from a visitor to the temple. Postmarked Yamagata Prefecture.*

---

*[Letters Three and Four, received June 1984 and January 1985 respectively, deal primarily with Hideo's monastic routine, his growing engagement with Zen practice, and a lengthy digression on the proper maintenance of wooden floors. They contain no direct reference to the tournament or to the matter at hand. I include them in the complete appendix but omit them here. The reader may accuse me of editorializing. The reader is correct. —Y.K.]*

---

Yasuda,

I have been reading Hagakure. I know this is unfashionable. The modern budoka treats Hagakure the way a modern doctor treats Galen—with a polite nod and an impolite distance. But I find in Tsunetomo something that resonates with my situation, or rather, I find that my situation resonates with something in Tsunetomo, and the distinction matters.

He writes: *The Way of the Samurai is found in death.* This is always quoted and always misunderstood. He does not mean that the samurai should seek death. He means that the samurai, having already accepted death, is freed from the fear of it, and therefore freed to act with total clarity. The question that interests me—the question that has kept me awake now for three years—is what happens when the acceptance of death produces not clarity but its opposite. What happens when the samurai sees clearly enough to see that clarity itself is the problem.

I will be specific. I will try to be specific.

When I assumed my stance against you in the Budokan—

The brush stops again. I have been staring at the above sentence for twenty minutes. The light is failing. I will continue.
