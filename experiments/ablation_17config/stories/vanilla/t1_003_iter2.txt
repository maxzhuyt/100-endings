## ACT ONE: "ACCUMULATION"

---

**OVER BLACK:**

The sound of breathing. Ragged. Young. Four distinct sets of lungs working too hard.

Then: digging. Metal scraping dirt. Frantic, arrhythmic.

A CHILD'S VOICE (later we'll know it as young Mara):

> **YOUNG MARA (V.O.)**
> Deeper. It has to be deeper.

Another voice, cracking with pubescent panic:

> **YOUNG THEO (V.O.)**
> That's deep enough. That's deep enough, just put it in—

> **YOUNG DEX (V.O.)**
> Shut up. Shut up shut up shut up—

A fourth voice. Eerily calm:

> **YOUNG JUNE (V.O.)**
> We never talk about this. After tonight, this didn't happen.

The sound of something heavy dropping into earth. Then shoveling—faster now, burying. A flashlight beam sweeps across the darkness and they all gasp.

A fifth voice. Adult. Male. Trembling with something worse than anger:

> **YOUNG GLENN (V.O.)**
> I see you.

**EXT. SUBURBAN HOUSE — PRESENT DAY — MORNING**

From outside, the house looks normal. Beige siding. Trimmed lawn. A welcome mat that says BLESS THIS MESS in cheerful script.

A MAIL CARRIER approaches. He tries to push mail through the slot, but it won't go. He pushes harder. Something behind the door shifts, and we hear a muffled avalanche—the dense, textile-and-paper sound of packed objects collapsing against each other.

He gives up. Leaves the mail balanced on the doorknob.

**TITLE CARD: MARA**

---

**INT. MARA'S HOUSE — CONTINUOUS**

MARA WELLINGTON, 32, stands in her kitchen—or rather, in the eighteen-inch-wide corridor that remains of her kitchen. She is neat. Almost aggressively neat. Hair in a precise bun, pressed blouse, minimal jewelry. Her person is immaculate. Her house is a topographic survey of twenty years of unprocessed terror.

The walls aren't visible. What exists instead are strata: newspapers at the bottom (yellowed, decade-old, organized by date but never discarded), then retail bags still containing their purchases (tags attached, items never worn or used), then moving boxes labeled with a system only Mara understands—colored dots, cryptic abbreviations, a taxonomy of need.

She moves through it all with the fluid grace of a spelunker navigating a familiar cave system. She doesn't bump anything. Doesn't dislodge anything. Her body has memorized every protrusion and bottleneck.

She reaches the front door. Sees the mail on the floor where it's slid under. Normal envelopes and one that's different—off-white, no return address, her name written in red marker with the blocky precision of someone who learned penmanship by tracing stencils.

She opens it. Reads.

Her face doesn't change expression. But her hand moves, slowly and unconsciously, to grip the nearest object—a stack of takeout menus rubber-banded together, kept for seven years—and she holds it against her chest the way a child holds a stuffed animal.

**INSERT — THE LETTER:**

> *Mara,*
>
> *I know what you buried at Camp Abenaki in the summer of 2004.*
>
> *But more importantly, I know what you've been burying ever since.*

**TITLE CARD: DEX**

**INT. UPSCALE DEPARTMENT STORE — DAY**

DEX OKAFOR-HAYES, 32, is handsome in the way that makes security guards relax. Open face. Easy smile. Dressed expensively but not ostentatiously. He looks like the kind of man who buys things. Who has every reason to be here.

He's browsing men's accessories. A SALES ASSOCIATE approaches.

> **SALES ASSOCIATE**
> Can I help you find something?

> **DEX**
> Just looking. My anniversary's next week. Fourteen years.

> **SALES ASSOCIATE**
> Congratulations! What's fourteen? Ivory?

> **DEX**
> *(charming smile)*
> I think they updated it. Fourteen is gold jewelry now.

> **SALES ASSOCIATE**
> Well, you're in the wrong department then. Let me show you—

She turns. Dex follows. The camera stays.

From the angle Dex just occupied, we can see: his left hand, during the brief moment the associate was looking at his face, has already deposited a $400 silk tie into the interior pocket of his jacket. The movement was so smooth, so structurally integrated into his gestures, that even watching it, you could convince yourself it didn't happen.

He follows the associate to jewelry. He tries on watches. He asks about return policies. He buys a $1,200 gold bracelet with his American Express card—legitimate purchase, receipt in hand.

At no point during this transaction does anyone notice that his right hand has palmed a pair of diamond studs from the adjacent display.

**EXT. DEPARTMENT STORE — MOMENTS LATER**

Dex exits. The detectors don't beep—the studs were too small for magnetic tags. He stands on the sidewalk and breathes. Not the elated breath of a victorious thief. The shuddering exhale of a man who's just completed a ritual he doesn't want and can't refuse.

He opens his car. The bracelet goes on the passenger seat—a genuine gift. The tie and studs go into the center console, which, when it opens, reveals a hollow packed tight with small stolen objects. Dozens of them. Maybe a hundred. Like a magpie's nest.

He starts the car. His phone is on the dash. He sees a notification—an email. He opens it.

Same letter. Same red handwriting, digitized. Same demands.

Dex stares at his phone for a long time. Then he looks at the center console. Then back at the phone.

> **DEX**
> *(quietly)*
> Fuck.

**TITLE CARD: JUNE**

**INT. RESTAURANT KITCHEN — NIGHT**

Steam. Shouting. The controlled chaos of a professional kitchen during peak service.

JUNE VERACRUZ, 32, works the line. She's a sous chef—fast hands, incredible knife work, the thousand-yard stare of someone who's been in culinary trenches for a decade. She's lean. Not healthy lean. Lean in the way that implies maintenance, effort, an ongoing negotiation with her own body.

She plates a dish. It's beautiful—architectural, precise, every component calibrated to the millimeter. She slides it to the pass.

> **HEAD CHEF (O.S.)**
> Service!

June wipes her station. Wipes it again. Checks the next ticket.

**LATER — INT. RESTAURANT KITCHEN — AFTER CLOSE**

The kitchen is empty. June stands in front of the walk-in refrigerator. The steel door reflects a distorted version of her face.

She opens it. Steps inside. Cold air billows.

She stands among the shelves of prep—labeled, dated, cling-wrapped. She picks up a container of chocolate ganache. Opens it. Gets a spoon.

She eats. Methodically. Not tasting. The first few spoonfuls are fast, then they slow; you can see her trying to stop, the spoon hovering. Then something in her expression hardens—a wall going up behind her eyes—and the pace increases. She finishes the ganache. Opens a container of crème anglaise. Finishes that.

**INT. RESTAURANT BATHROOM — MOMENTS LATER**

June stands at the sink, running water at full blast. She looks at herself in the mirror. Her eyes are red. Her hand is shaking.

She pulls out her phone. A text from someone named CASS: *Come home soon, love. I made that tea you like.*

June starts to type a response. Stops. Opens her email instead. Sees the letter.

She reads it leaning against the bathroom wall. Slides down to the tiles. Keeps reading.

When she finishes, she lets out a sound that's half-laugh, half-sob. Because of course. Of course this is happening.

**TITLE CARD: THEO**

**INT. THEO'S APARTMENT — NIGHT**

THEO PARK, 32, sits in a beautiful apartment that's almost empty. Not minimalist—depleted. The kind of space that once had nice things and now has their ghosts: slightly-less-faded rectangles on walls where art hung, furniture indentations in carpet where a couch used to be. He sits on a folding chair at a card table. This is his dining room, living room, and office.

Three monitors glow in front of him. One shows a poker client—he's in four simultaneous tournaments. One shows a sports betting platform with lines updating in real time. One shows his bank account.

The bank account shows $847.32.

The poker client shows he's up $12,000 across his tournaments.

We might think this is a recovery story. But then we see the sports betting platform: he's placed $11,600 in parlay bets across seventeen NBA games happening tonight. The implied probability of hitting the full parlay is approximately 0.003%.

Theo watches the monitors with the calm of a man sitting in a building he's set on fire. There's no anxiety. Anxiety would mean there was still hope.

His phone buzzes. Not an email—the physical letter, photographed and texted to him by his building's front desk (he doesn't go downstairs anymore; the building manager likes him; slides stuff under his door).

He reads it. His eyes, which have been dead for most of this scene, flicker. Something kindles. Not hope exactly. Interest. The way a gambler's pupils dilate when the stakes get genuinely dangerous.

> **THEO**
> *(to himself, crunching numbers in his head)*
> Camp Abenaki. Four-hour drive. Letter says Friday midnight, today's Wednesday. Probability this is law enforcement... low. Probability it's one of the other three... lower. Probability it's real...

He looks at his monitors. Then at his empty apartment. He does the math. Not the gambling math—the life math. Assets, liabilities, trajectory. Where he'll be in six months if nothing changes.

> **THEO (CONT'D)**
> *(standing)*
> Positive expected value.

He closes all three monitors. The screens go dark. The apartment goes dark. Theo leaves and, for the first time in what's clearly a long time, he goes downstairs.

**EXT. CAMP ABENAKI — FRONT GATE — FRIDAY — 11:45 PM**

The camp sits at the end of a dirt road in dense Vermont forest. It's been closed for eighteen years. The wooden sign—CAMP ABENAKI EST. 1971—hangs crooked from one chain. The other chain has rusted and broken. Beyond the gate: dark buildings, overgrown paths, the silver-black shimmer of a lake.

A car arrives. Headlights sweep the trees. Then another. Then a third.

The fourth person came by bus and walked two miles from the nearest highway stop, because of course he sold his car six months ago.

They converge at the gate. Four people who haven't been in the same physical space in twenty years. Four people who made a pact as twelve-year-olds to never speak again, and kept it.

MARA gets out first. She's wearing her precise bun and her pressed blouse and she's carrying a bag that's much too large for an overnight trip—because she couldn't decide what she'd need, so she brought options for every contingency.

DEX gets out second. He sees Mara and for a moment his face does something complicated—recognition, affection, guilt, longing—all of it cycling through in two seconds before his social armor reconstructs itself and he waves casually, as if they'd seen each other last week.

JUNE gets out third. She doesn't wave. She leans against her car and crosses her arms and stares at the camp sign and the expression on her face is one of deep, nauseated déjà vu.
