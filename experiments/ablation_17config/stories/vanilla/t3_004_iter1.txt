# The Commissar's Confession

---

## I. Procession

The wheel finds every stone in Paris.

I know this because I am counting them—the jolts, the intervals between jolts, the particular music of iron rims against cobblestone that changes pitch when the street narrows near the Rue Saint-Honoré, where the buildings lean together like conspirators and the echo sharpens into something almost vocal, almost a word I could parse if I were not also counting heads. Seven. I was told seven but I count them again because the mind requires occupation the way a wound requires pressure, and there are seven, and the tumbril finds another stone, and the woman third from the rear—Marguerite—has not looked at me yet.

She will not look at me.

This is either mercy or accusation and I have lost the faculty for distinguishing between them.

Citizen-Inspector Aubert. That is the name they will write in the register. Citizen-Inspector Jean-Luc Aubert, assigned by order of the Committee of Public Safety to oversee the conveyance and execution of seven enemies of the Republic, condemned by the Revolutionary Tribunal on charges of conspiracy, sedition, and correspondence with foreign powers, this fifth day of Messidor in the Year Two of the Republic One and Indivisible. The clerk's handwriting will be precise. The clerk's handwriting is always precise. I have noticed this about Greffier Danton—no relation, he insists, no relation, his face blanching each time someone speaks the name—that his letters maintain an almost defiant beauty, each one formed with the patience of a monk illuminating scripture, as though penmanship itself were a form of patriotism, as though the Republic might be sustained by the angle of a serif.

Seven.

She taught me that word means something. Not the number. Justice. The actual weight of it, the way it sits in the mouth like a stone you cannot swallow and cannot spit out. Marguerite Dessain, née Clouet, widow of the printer Dessain who published Condorcet until publishing Condorcet became the sort of enterprise that ended with your typeface melted into musket balls and your body in the Seine. She is forty-three. Her hands are ink-stained even now. I have wondered, in the nights when wondering was all I could do without alerting the man who sleeps in the next room—Garreau, my shadow, Robespierre's gift to me, a watcher assigned to watch the watcher—whether the ink has simply become part of her skin, whether it has migrated into the architecture of her, the way the Revolution has migrated into mine.

A stone. The wheel. I was counting.

The crowd along the Rue Saint-Honoré is thinner than it was in Nivôse. I have noted this in my reports—the official ones, the ones Garreau reads—as evidence of the public's satisfied confidence in revolutionary justice. The spectacle grows routine. The blade falls. The Republic endures. Citizens return to their bread and their denunciations. But Brisseau—who is dead now, three months dead, his body in the mass grave at Picpus, though I did not learn this until six weeks after the fact, six weeks during which I left messages at the agreed-upon locations and interpreted silence as caution rather than what silence actually meant—Brisseau would have read the thinning crowds differently. He would have said: *They are not satisfied. They are afraid. There is a difference, and the difference is the distance between a republic and a tyranny, and we are walking it, Jean-Luc, we are walking it one corpse at a time.*

Brisseau.

I will come back to Brisseau.

I cannot come back to Brisseau because Brisseau is at Picpus in a grave with ninety others and the soil there is clay, they tell me, red clay that holds the rain, and I cannot think about what the rain does to the clay and what the clay holds and I was counting stones.

The woman at the front of the tumbril is weeping. I do not know her name. The charge sheet says Anne-Marie Leclerc, seamstress, but this tells me nothing that matters and everything the Republic requires, and these two quantities have been diverging for some time now, like roads that share an origin but not a destination, and I have been walking both of them and my legs—

The metaphor collapses. They all collapse now. I had a facility for language once. Brisseau said so. He said: *You speak like a man who reads, which is dangerous in this city, so speak less and listen more, and when you write your reports for the Committee, write them in the language of the Committee—the flat, declarative, virtuous language that mistakes repetition for sincerity and volume for truth.* And I learned. God help me, I learned so well that the language colonized me, and there are nights now when I think in their cadences, when even my private thoughts arrive dressed in the Republic's vocabulary, and I cannot find the man underneath, the man who stood in Marguerite's kitchen in the winter of '92 and read the proofs of a pamphlet arguing that terror and virtue are not synonyms.

They are not synonyms.

But try telling that to the crowd, which has come alive now as we turn onto the Place de la Révolution and the scaffold rises into view with its peculiar geometry—the uprights, the crossbeam, the blade suspended at its apex like a period waiting to end a sentence—and some part of my mind, the part that has been trained by three years of double-entry bookkeeping in the ledger of deception, notes that the scaffold has been freshly painted. Red. The wood is painted red. I had not known this until now, or I had known it and not permitted myself to see it, because seeing is a choice and I have been very careful about what I choose to see, because each act of genuine seeing is a crack in the surface of the man the Committee believes me to be, and Garreau is always watching.

Garreau is two steps behind me. I feel him there the way you feel a draft from an unseen window—a coldness with direction, with intention. He was a butcher before the Revolution. He told me this with pride. The Committee found him useful for the same reason the charnel houses found him useful: he is unbothered by the mechanics of flesh. His hands are clean. He keeps them very clean, scrubbing them with lye each morning, and this fastidiousness strikes me as the most terrifying thing about him—not the capacity for violence but the hygiene that follows it, the scrubbing, the restoration of surfaces, as though the work of death were simply another mess to be managed.

Seven condemned.

Three still hidden.

This is the calculation. This is the mathematics that has replaced my heartbeat, that drums beneath every thought and every step: *seven condemned, three still hidden, and the distance between these numbers is the measure of what remains of my soul, if soul is even a word the Republic permits, which it does not, the Supreme Being having replaced all prior metaphysics as of the Festival of—*

Stop.

I must stop.

The tumbril has stopped. We have arrived.

## II. Recollection

How it began:

Not with ideology. I want to be clear about this, though clarity is a luxury I can no longer afford. It began with Brisseau leaning across a table in a café on the Île Saint-Louis in the autumn of 1791, his face sharp with the particular intelligence that would eventually kill him, and saying: *The Revolution is eating itself. You can see this. You are inside it and you can see this, which makes you either a coward or an asset, and I am gambling that you are the latter.*

I was twenty-six. I had joined the police under the old regime—the *ancien régime*, we are required to call it now, as though the word *ancien* could contain the entirety of what was lost and what deserved to be lost and what we did not know we were losing until it was gone. I had joined because my father had been a clerk in the Châtelet and his father before him and the narrow inheritance of administrative service was all I understood. The Revolution found me already in place, already inside the machinery, and the machinery simply changed its name and continued grinding, and I continued feeding it, and if there was a moment when I might have stepped away—

There was no moment. That is the truth. The machinery does not pause. The machinery has no seams, no joints where a man might insert his fingers and pry. You are either inside it or beneath it, and I was inside it, and Brisseau saw this and understood that a man inside the machinery might redirect it, might gum the gears with misinformation, might ensure that certain names never appeared on certain lists, might lose a file, misdate a warrant, misdirect a patrol—

*Might save lives*, he said, and his eyes were the eyes of a man who believed this was possible, and I wanted to share his belief more than I wanted anything I had ever wanted, more than bread, more than love, more than the particular silence that falls over Paris at dawn in October when the mist rises from the river and the city holds its breath and you can almost—almost—believe it will be gentle today.

Brisseau was my handler. This is the professional term. He handled me the way a potter handles clay—with pressure and patience and the certain knowledge that the thing being shaped has no say in its final form. He gave me the network, name by name, like pearls threaded onto a string: Dessain the printer. Marguerite, after Dessain was gone. Leclair the physician, who could forge the health certificates that allowed condemned men to be transferred to hospitals from which they could be spirited out of the city. Père Arnaud, who was not a priest—the Republic had eaten the priests—but who maintained the cadence and the contacts of one, moving through the Faubourg Saint-Antoine with the authority of a man who has heard confession and knows that every human being is guilty of something and redeemable from everything.

And others. I will not name the others because three of them are still alive and the calculation—

Seven condemned. Three still hidden. If I—

The crowd presses forward. They want to see. They always want to see. I have watched the faces of the crowd at forty-seven executions—I have counted, you see, I count everything now, counting is the mathematics of survival, the abacus I run between my fingers when the world becomes incomprehensible—and the faces are never what you expect. Not bloodlust. Not horror. Something between. A stillness. The face of a person watching something they know they should not watch but cannot stop watching because the watching has become part of the social contract, part of the price of citizenship, and to look away would be to invite the question *Why does Citizen So-and-so look away?* and the question would be noted and the notation would become a file and the file would become a warrant and the warrant—

The machinery. Always the machinery.

Marguerite Dessain is being helped from the tumbril. Helped. The word is grotesque. She is being removed from the cart that is carrying her to her death by the hands of a guard who is performing his function with the automated courtesy of a factotum opening a carriage door, and her hands are bound behind her back and her hair has been cut—they cut the hair, they always cut the hair, the blade requires a clean path—and the absence of her hair changes the geometry of her face so that she looks younger, or older, or simply like someone else, someone I have not sat across from in a kitchen that smelled of ink and bread and the particular warmth of a fire that has been tended by someone who understands that fire, like democracy, requires constant attention or it dies.

She looked at me.

She is looking at me now.

What is in her face. I will try to say what is in her face because someone should say it and there will be no one else, and the clerk will not write it, and history will not record it, and if I do not hold it in my mind it will be as though it never existed, and I refuse—I refuse this one thing, this single thing—

What is in her face is: *I know.*

Not accusation. Not forgiveness. Knowledge. She knows what I am. She has known, perhaps, longer than I have known myself. She knows I am the man who sat in her kitchen and argued about Rousseau while the ink dried on the proofs—it was always Rousseau, she said, the men always come back to Rousseau, to the general will, the social contract, as though a man who abandoned his children could be trusted to design a society for other people's children—and she knows I am the man who fed the names to the Committee, not her name, never her name, but the architecture of the network, the shapes and shadows of it, because that was the price of remaining inside the machinery, you had to give them something, you had to feed the beast enough to keep it from smelling the food you were hiding, and the something I gave them—

I gave them Leclair. I did not know I was giving them Leclair. I reported a pattern of transfers from the Conciergerie to the Hôtel-Dieu that I could not explain, and I reported it because Garreau had noted the same pattern and was composing his own report, and if my report did not arrive first then the gap between what I saw and what I claimed to see would become visible, and the visibility would become suspicion, and—

Leclair was arrested on the third of Ventôse. He was dead by the ninth.

What Marguerite's face contains is the knowledge that I am both things. The man in the kitchen and the man with the report. The infiltrator and the instrument. The hand that hid the names and the hand that signed the warrants. She knows this because she is intelligent in the way that Brisseau was intelligent—not with the intelligence of systems and strategies but with the intelligence of recognition, the ability to look at a human face and read the weather inside it.

And she does not look away.

## III. Arithmetic

Here is the calculation. Hold it in your mind as I hold it in mine, turning it like a stone, examining each facet for the crack that would let the light in:

There are seven condemned. Among them, Marguerite. Among them, Anne-Marie Leclerc, who I do not know and who is weeping. Among them, Citizen Moreau, whose pamphlets I never read but whose name appeared on a list I did not compile. Among them, four others whose faces I have studied and whose crimes I have reviewed and whose lives are threads I cannot trace to any fabric I recognize.

There are three still hidden. I will not name them, not even here, not even in the privacy of this narration that no one will ever hear because it is occurring inside my skull and my skull is a room with no doors, only windows, and the windows face inward. But they exist. One of them has papers prepared by Leclair before Leclair was taken—papers good enough to pass inspection at any checkpoint between here and the Swiss border. One of them is caring for Marguerite's daughter, who is nine, whose name is Céleste, whom I have met twice, whose fingers were stained with ink like her mother's, a hereditary marking, a family crest more honest than any the old aristocracy devised.

If I act now. If I create a disturbance. If I discover, suddenly and conveniently, a procedural error in the warrant—the date is wrong, the seal is irregular, the tribunal's notation does not match the registry—then Marguerite might be returned to the Conciergerie for review, and in the chaos of the return, in the paperwork and the disputes and the jurisdictional vanity of the clerks, there might be a window.

But the window would be my exposure. Garreau would see. Garreau, two steps behind me, with his clean hands and his butcher's patience—Garreau would see the performance in it, would smell the theatre the way a dog smells fear, which is to say chemically, beneath the level of thought, in the animal register where truth lives because truth is too stupid to disguise itself.
