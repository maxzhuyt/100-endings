# PROLOGUE: THE PROBLEM OF FOREVER

**[FOOTAGE: A modest two-story craftsman home in Bend, Oregon. October 2023. The house has a well-maintained garden. A RING doorbell camera captures a DoorDash driver leaving Thai food on the porch. From inside, muffled shouting.]**

**DR. AMELIA THORNTON (V.O.):**
On any given Tuesday evening, in any given American suburb, a married couple is arguing about dinner. This is not extraordinary. What is extraordinary is when that argument contains a specific, unimpeachable citation of a previous dinner disagreement from 1911.

**[CUT TO: Interior. A living room decorated in a style that can only be described as "177 years of accumulated taste, most of it conflicting." A Victorian writing desk sits beside a mid-century modern sofa. On the wall, what appears to be an original Monet hangs next to a framed poster from the 1977 Portland Rose Festival. MARGARET GREENBRIAR-CHEN, who appears to be a woman in her early thirties with auburn hair and an expression of composed, aristocratic exhaustion, sits in an armchair. JAMES CHEN, who appears to be a man of roughly thirty with East Asian features and the particular cheerful stubbornness of someone who has been hit by two trains and a municipal bus and found all three experiences merely inconvenient, sits across from her on the sofa.]**

**MARGARET:**
He ordered pad see ew. He *always* orders pad see ew. He has ordered pad see ew, or the local equivalent, in every city we have lived in since we first encountered Thai cuisine in—

**JAMES:**
Don't.

**MARGARET:**
—Bangkok, 1923, at a street stall on Charoen Krung Road, on a Wednesday, at approximately seven-fifteen in the evening. It was humid. You were wearing a linen suit you'd purchased in Singapore the previous March. You said, and I quote, "This is extraordinary. I could eat this forever." And you *have*, James. You have eaten it *forever*.

**JAMES:**
*(to camera)*
She does this.

**MARGARET:**
I do this because it *happened*. Everything I say happened, happened. That is my entire *situation*, James.

**JAMES:**
*(leaning forward, with the calm, roguish half-smile of a man who has learned that no argument can actually kill him, because nothing can)*
And my entire situation is that it doesn't matter. None of it matters. We'll still be here tomorrow. We'll be here in a thousand years. You can remember every grain of rice from 1923 and I *still* won't be sorry about the pad see ew.

**[Margaret stares at him for a long, terrible moment.]**

**MARGARET:**
Pestilent harridan.

**JAMES:**
Oh, *come on*—

**MARGARET:**
September fourteenth, 1897. My mother had been dead for six years. You said it about her *posthumously*.

**JAMES:**
I was *drunk*. It was—there was context—

**MARGARET:**
There is always context. I *have* all the context. I have the complete unabridged context of every terrible thing you've said since the Fillmore administration, and context does not help you as often as you think it does.

**[James looks at the camera. He has the expression of a man who has been having this exact conversation, with minor variations, since before the invention of the automobile.]**

**JAMES:**
I love my wife.

**MARGARET:**
That is not the issue and has never been the issue.

# EPISODE ONE: "A MOST PECULIAR COURTSHIP"
## Boston, 1847–1870

---

## Chapter 1: Before

**DR. THORNTON (V.O.):**
To understand the Greenbriar-Chen marriage, one must first understand the world that made it. In 1847, the city of Boston was a crucible of American reinvention—abolitionist, industrializing, and roughly two decades into what parahistorians now call the First Manifestation Period, when the phenomenon of puberty-onset extranormal abilities was still new enough to terrify and old enough to have developed its own tentative social architecture.

**[ARCHIVAL IMAGE: A daguerreotype of Boston Harbor, circa 1845.]**

**DR. THORNTON (V.O.):**
The language hadn't even settled. They were called "the Touched," "the Peculiars," "the Gifted," "the Marked," "the Afflicted"—the terminology depending largely on whether the speaker was a Unitarian minister or a Catholic one. The first confirmed manifestation in the historical record is generally dated to 1819, in a French village called Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, where a thirteen-year-old girl named Céleste Morel reportedly began to levitate during Sunday mass. By 1847, roughly one in every eight hundred humans manifested some extranormal ability at puberty. The abilities were random, unpredictable, and wildly unequal. One child might gain the ability to change the color of small objects. Another might become functionally indestructible.

The social consequences were, as you might imagine, chaotic.

**[CUT TO: DR. PATRICIA HUANG, Professor of Parahistory, MIT.]**

**DR. HUANG:**
The thing people forget is how *unevenly* it was understood in those early decades. There's no internet. There's no mass media in any modern sense. So you have a girl in Boston who manifests the ability to, say, perfectly recall every sensory experience she's ever had, and her family might know of one or two other Touched individuals in their social circle, but they have no framework for it. No medical framework, no legal framework. The Catholic Church issued its first encyclical on the Touched in 1834, and it was—well, it was not helpful. It essentially said, "We don't know if this is God or the Devil, so everyone please remain calm," which of course had the opposite effect.

**DR. THORNTON (V.O.):**
Margaret Abigail Greenbriar was born on March 7, 1830, in the Beacon Hill neighborhood of Boston, the second daughter of Reverend Elias Greenbriar, a Congregationalist minister of modest reputation, and his wife, Dorothy, née Hale, who by all surviving accounts was a formidable woman of strong opinions and stronger tea.

**[ARCHIVAL IMAGE: A hand-tinted daguerreotype of the Greenbriar family, circa 1843. Reverend Greenbriar is thin-faced and solemn. Dorothy Greenbriar is not smiling, but projects an aura suggesting she could smile if she wanted to and has chosen not to as a matter of policy. Two girls stand before them. The elder, Catherine, looks directly at the camera. The younger, Margaret, age approximately thirteen, has a slightly unfocused expression that later biographers have interpreted as the early manifestation of her ability.]**

**DR. THORNTON (V.O.):**
Margaret's manifestation occurred in the autumn of 1843, when she was thirteen. The ability consolidated over several months, as was typical. By early 1844, she could recall with perfect fidelity every sensory experience she had ever had—every conversation, every page of every book, every meal, every sound, every smell. She later described the experience to the journalist Ida Tarbell in a 1904 interview that remains the most detailed first-person account we have of her early life.

**[ON SCREEN: Excerpt from "The Woman Who Cannot Forget," by Ida Tarbell, *McClure's Magazine*, March 1904. An actress reads the passage in voice-over.]**

**MARGARET (V.O., 1904 INTERVIEW):**
*"It was as though a great fog had lifted from all the rooms of my memory at once, and I found that nothing had ever been discarded—every moment I had ever lived was still there, perfectly preserved, like specimens in glass jars, and I could take any one of them down and examine it as though it were happening again. The relief of it was extraordinary. I had always felt that I was losing things—that life was a river carrying everything away. And then suddenly it wasn't. Nothing was lost. Nothing would ever be lost again.*

*I did not yet understand that this was not entirely a gift."*

---

**DR. THORNTON (V.O.):**
James Wen-Long Chen was born on June 12, 1828, in Boston's small but growing Chinese community, the son of Chen Wei-Ming, a merchant who had emigrated from Guangzhou in 1820, and his wife, Sarah Chen, née Alcott—no relation to the Concord Alcotts, a point James would spend the next two centuries wearily clarifying.

**[CUT TO: DR. MICHAEL CAREY, Professor of Asian-American Studies, UCLA.]**

**DR. CAREY:**
The Chen family is fascinating because they represent this very early wave of Chinese presence in New England that predates the Gold Rush migration by decades. Wei-Ming Chen was a tea merchant, reasonably successful, and he married a white American woman, which was—it happened, in that era, more than people think, but it was not *easy*. James grew up biracial in antebellum Boston, which meant he occupied a very specific and very uncomfortable social position. Too Chinese for Beacon Hill, too American for the Chinese trading networks, too prosperous to be ignored, too unusual to be accepted.

**DR. THORNTON (V.O.):**
James's manifestation occurred in 1841, when he was thirteen. It announced itself in the most dramatic fashion possible: he fell from the roof of his father's warehouse, a distance of approximately thirty-five feet, onto cobblestones, and stood up without injury.

**[ON SCREEN: A modern animated reconstruction of the fall, based on contemporary accounts.]**

**DR. THORNTON (V.O.):**
Over the following months, the full scope of his ability became apparent. James Chen could not be killed. He could not, in fact, be meaningfully injured. Blades did not cut him. Fire did not burn him. He did not bruise, did not bleed, did not break. His body simply... refused to be damaged.

It's important to distinguish this from the more common "enhanced durability" manifestations documented in the period. James did not merely heal quickly. He was not merely resistant to harm. He was, as far as any test then or since has been able to determine, *perfectly and completely invulnerable to all physical harm*. This includes aging. His cells do not degrade. He has remained, biologically, approximately thirty years old for the past 183 years.

He has described the experience of his ability as follows:

**[CUT TO: James, in the 2023 Bend, Oregon living room.]**

**JAMES:**
It's not that I don't feel things. I feel the *impact*. When that bus hit me in 1987—

**MARGARET:**
*(from off-camera)* 
Portland. November 3rd. Southeast Belmont Street. The number 15 bus. You were jaywalking. You were jaywalking because you were looking at a woman in a red coat.

**JAMES:**
I was *not* looking at—this is exactly what I—

*(He takes a breath. Returns his attention to the camera.)*

I feel the impact. I feel pressure. I feel temperature. If you put my hand in a fire, I'd feel warmth, and then more warmth, and then a kind of... firm warmth. But there's a ceiling. The sensation just plateaus. Nothing crosses the line into actual pain, and nothing leaves a mark. I haven't had a bruise since 1841.

**DR. THORNTON:**
*(off-camera)*
Do you miss it? Pain?

**JAMES:**
*(A long pause. He looks toward the room where Margaret is.)*

I miss *stakes*. When nothing can hurt you, it's... it changes your relationship with consequence. Everything physical becomes theoretical. The only things that can actually reach me are—

*(He stops.)*

Well. Emotional things. Words. Memories.

*(Another pause.)*

Which is, of course, exactly what my wife specializes in. So that's... that's the whole marriage, really, in a nutshell. She can't forget anything, and I can't die. It's like God designed us as a matched set of punishments for each other, except we also happen to be in love, which makes it worse.

## Chapter 2: The Courtship

**DR. THORNTON (V.O.):**
James Chen and Margaret Greenbriar met on October 14, 1847, at a lecture on "The Moral Condition of the Peculiar Individual" held at the Boston Athenæum. Margaret was seventeen. James was nineteen. The lecture was, by all accounts, terrible.

**[CUT TO: Margaret, Bend, Oregon, 2023.]**

**MARGARET:**
The speaker was a Reverend Dr. Horace Whitfield, and he was arguing—poorly—that the Touched were evidence of divine providence, that each gift was calibrated by God to the moral character of its recipient. Which was, even by the standards of 1847, obvious nonsense. There was a woman in Lowell at the time whose only ability was that she could make milk curdle by looking at it. What theological conclusion are we meant to draw from *that*?

I was there because my father felt it was important for me to participate in what he called "the public conversation about your condition." James was there because his father had business at the Athenæum and James had nothing better to do. We were seated in the same row. I was trying to take notes—old habit, before my ability, and I hadn't quite accepted I didn't need to anymore—and my pencil rolled off my lap and he picked it up and handed it to me and said, "I don't think you're missing much."

*(She pauses. Her expression does something complicated—something that holds 176 years of subsequent experience but is, for a moment, reaching back through all of it to locate that specific instant.)*

He had a lovely smile. He still does, the bastard. It's the same smile. Exactly the same. I can compare, you understand. I can put them side by side in my memory, the smile from 1847 and the smile from this morning, and they are *identical down to the individual muscle movements of his face*, and that is either the most romantic thing in the world or the most horrifying, and I have never been able to decide which.

---

**DR. THORNTON (V.O.):**
The courtship of James Chen and Margaret Greenbriar was, even by the standards of the era, unusual. Both were Touched—which created an immediate bond in a society that still regarded extranormal abilities with deep suspicion. Both were intellectually acute, socially marginal in different ways—she by her strange "condition," he by his race—and both possessed a quality that every surviving account describes with some variation of the same word: *stubbornness*.
