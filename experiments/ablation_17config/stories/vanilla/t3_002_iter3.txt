# The Last Watch of the *Mara Celeste*

---

You should know that I am not a brave woman.

I want that said plainly, at the front of things, before anyone makes of this story something it isn't. I was the cook on the *Mara Celeste* for eleven years, and in eleven years I never once did anything worth remarking on. I made rice. I made fish stew that Wen said tasted like the ocean apologizing for itself. I kept the galley clean. I played cards badly. That is the whole of my courage, and I will not have anyone dress it up now simply because I happened to stay.

People stay for all kinds of reasons. Most of them stupid.

---

The letters started in March.

I know because the mail came through Bridgetown and Wen always had me sort it while she handled the harbormaster. There'd been the usual things—supply invoices, a postcard from her niece in Taipei, the marine weather almanac she subscribed to and read like scripture. Then, between the almanac and an advertisement for deck sealant, a envelope with the pale blue return address of the Shandong Maritime Credit Union.

I didn't open it. That wasn't my business. But I watched Wen open it at the chart table that evening while I brought her dinner, and I saw the way her hands went still. Not shaking. That would have been something I understood. Just still, the way water goes still before it decides what it wants to become.

She folded the letter into a square no bigger than a playing card. Put it in her breast pocket. Ate her dinner. Said the grouper was overdone, which it was.

That was the last time she complained about my cooking. I think she stopped tasting it after that. I think she stopped tasting most things.

---

You have to understand about the *Celeste* to understand any of the rest of it.

She was a forty-six-foot ketch, wooden-hulled, built in 1971 at a yard in Keelung that doesn't exist anymore. Wen's grandfather—his name was Wen Bohai, and she kept a photograph of him in the wheelhouse that was so sun-bleached you could barely make out his face, just the white suggestion of a grin beneath a cap—he'd commissioned her for the Taiwan Strait cargo trade. Copra and dried fish, mostly. Small freight that the big shipping lines couldn't be bothered with.

The *Celeste* had been through four typhoons, two engine rebuilds, a complete replanking of her port side, and one small fire in the head that Wen blamed on a deckhand named Óscar who was no longer with us by the time I came aboard and therefore could not defend himself. Her teak was dark as coffee. Her brass was green in the places Wen couldn't reach and blazing everywhere else. She leaked in eleven places that we knew of and probably others that kept their secrets.

She was not, by any rational measure, worth saving.

But I have spent enough of my life around boats to know that rational measures have nothing to do with it. There are people who own boats and people who belong to them. Wen belonged to the *Celeste* the way a rib belongs to a chest. You could remove it, certainly. You would simply unmake the thing it was protecting.

---

The second letter came in April. The third in May. By June they came in twos, then threes, and the return addresses multiplied—the credit union, then a collections agency in Hong Kong, then a law office in Hamilton, Bermuda, that I later learned had been retained to execute a maritime lien.

I learned what a maritime lien was in the way you learn most things at sea: too late to do anything about it.

Wen explained it to me once, flatly, while I was chopping scallions. "They've put a claim on her, Lihua. When we make port—any port with a cooperative admiralty court—they can seize her. Bermuda, Nassau, San Juan. They're all cooperative."

"What about—"

"Bridgetown too."

I swept the scallions into the pot. "What about not making port?"

She looked at me then. Really looked, which Wen did so rarely that when it happened you felt it in your sternum, like a door opening onto a cold night.

"That," she said, "is the relevant question."

---

We didn't discuss it again directly. That was how things were between us—between Wen and anyone, really, but especially between Wen and me. We had an understanding built on eleven years of adjacency. She captained. I cooked. We played cards at 0600 every morning, a game called *Big Two* that her grandfather had taught her and she had taught me and at which she won with such consistency that I suspected the knowledge had been transmitted incomplete.

We did not discuss feelings. We did not discuss the future. We existed in the daily ceremony of small tasks performed in the presence of another person, and if that sounds like something less than intimacy, then you have never been at sea, and I cannot explain it to you.

But I noticed things. I noticed that she stopped opening the letters and started putting them directly into the galley stove. I noticed that she began checking the *Celeste's* hull each evening with a flashlight, running her hand along the planking the way you'd check a child for fever. I noticed that she altered our course in late July—just a few degrees south, away from our usual circuit, away from any port where a man in a suit could step aboard with papers and take everything her grandfather had left her.

I noticed. I said nothing. I made dinner.

That was the shape of my cowardice, and I want you to see it clearly.

---

The typhoon.

They named it Banyan. I don't know why they name them, as though giving a thing a word could make it comprehensible. You cannot make a typhoon comprehensible. You can only survive one, or you can fail to, and in either case you will not have understood what happened. You will only have endured it or you will have stopped.

The marine weather service out of San Juan issued the advisory on the fourteenth of September. I know the date because I had written it at the top of the provisions log—*September 14, rice 20kg, dried fish 8kg, tea (oolong) 1 tin*—and I remember staring at my own handwriting while the radio crackled out coordinates and wind speeds that sounded like lies.

A category four system, east-northeast, tracking west. Forward speed twelve knots. Sustained winds 140 miles per hour. The projected path put it across our position in approximately thirty-one hours.

Wen listened to the advisory from the wheelhouse. I listened from the galley, which was six steps away. I could see the back of her neck, very straight, and her hands on the wheel, and the way the light from the instrument panel made her look like a painting of a saint in some church I'd never been to—illuminated from below, half in shadow, expression unknowable.

When the broadcast ended she reached over and turned off the radio. Not down. Off.

"Lihua," she said, without turning around.

"Captain."

"There's a shipping lane nineteen nautical miles north. If I put you on the emergency raft tomorrow morning, you could be picked up within hours. The EPIRB will—"

"No."

"I wasn't asking."

"Good, because I wasn't answering. I was refusing."

She turned around then. In the green glow of the instruments her face was very calm, the way a reef is calm—because it is made of stone and has already survived everything that matters.

"This is not bravery, what I'm doing," she said. "I want you to understand that. I am not going down with my ship. I am staying on my ship so that it remains *my ship*. Do you see the difference?"

"I see it."

"Then you see that there's no reason for you to be here."

I thought about this. I thought about the shipping lane and the life raft and the EPIRB broadcasting its distress into a sky full of people whose job it was to answer. I thought about Bermuda, and the men with their lien, and the way Wen touched the *Celeste's* timber. I thought about the scallions and the card games and the eleven years of mornings.

"The rice will be ready in twenty minutes," I said. "If you want it with egg, say so now, because I'm not reheating."

---

The first day was wind.

Not the typhoon yet. Just its breath—the long exhalation that precedes the scream. The swells built from four feet to eight to twelve in the space of an afternoon, and the *Celeste* rode them the way she'd always ridden everything: stubbornly, with complaint, her timber groaning like an old woman climbing stairs.

I secured the galley. Lashed the pots, stowed the knives, tied down everything that could become a projectile. You learn this when you cook on boats—that every object in a kitchen is, under the right conditions, a weapon. A can of coconut milk in a beam sea can fracture a skull. A cast-iron pan in a knockdown will go through a bulkhead. You learn to think of your kitchen as an armory that you are perpetually disarming.

Wen stayed at the wheel. She'd been at the wheel for nine hours when I brought her tea at sunset—oolong, strong, no sugar, the way she'd taken it every day for eleven years. She took the cup without looking at me. Her eyes were on the horizon, which had begun to take on the color of a bruise, that terrible yellow-purple that means the atmosphere is holding something it cannot hold much longer.

"Play you for the last of the condensed milk," she said.

"Now?"

"When else?"

So we played *Big Two* on the chart table while the barometer dropped like a stone in water. She won in four hands. She always won in four hands. I'd stopped believing it was luck around year three, but I never caught her cheating, and in eleven years of looking that's as close to innocence as I'm willing to grant anyone.

The tea went cold. It sat on the chart table between us, and neither of us drank it, and neither of us moved it, and after a while it became less a cup of tea and more a small monument to the fact that ordinary things were still possible.

---

The second day was water.

Not the clean water of waves but the insidious water, the water that finds the cracks. The *Celeste* leaked in eleven places, and by midmorning of the second day she had discovered a twelfth, a thirteenth, a fourteenth. The bilge pumps ran until they screamed, and then they coughed, and then one of them stopped.

I went below with buckets. Wen came down from the wheelhouse and we bailed together in the dark because the generator had failed and the emergency lights lasted forty minutes and then we were working by the feel of water against our shins: ankle-deep, then calf-deep, then knee-deep before we gained back an inch.

She didn't speak. I didn't speak. There was nothing to say that the water wasn't already saying. We bailed with a rhythm that was almost musical, almost peaceful—the slosh of the bucket, the heave, the pour, the slosh again—and in the dark I could hear her breathing and she could hear mine and that was enough. That was the whole conversation.

When the second pump failed she stopped bailing. Stood very still in the water, which was at mid-thigh now and blood-warm. I heard her hand touch the hull—I couldn't see it but I heard the sound of her palm on the wood, that flat intimate sound.

"All right, old girl," she said. Not to me. "All right."

We went back topside. The wind had found its scream.

---

This is the part that people will want me to describe, and I am telling you now that I cannot describe it, not because I don't remember but because the memory exists in a language that has no words, only textures. The texture of the rope she used to lash herself to the mast—three-strand nylon, half-inch, frayed at the bitter end. The texture of the rain, which was not rain but a horizontal wall of water that hit the skin like gravel. The texture of the wheel under her hands when I brought her tea that she couldn't drink because the wind would have torn the cup from her fingers.

I made that tea anyway. I boiled water on the gimballed stove that was the last thing still working in the galley, and I brewed it properly—three minutes, loose leaf, the way she liked—and I brought it to her in her cup, the tin one with the dent in the rim where she'd dropped it in Martinique two years ago. And she looked at the cup. And she looked at me.

And I held it while she drank.

The *Celeste* was listing fifteen degrees to port. The mainsail had shredded hours ago and the jib was a rag and the sea anchor was the only thing keeping us bow-on to the waves, which were black and tall as buildings and capped with foam that glowed white in the dark like the teeth of something.

Wen at the mast. Ropes across her chest. Her hair plastered flat. Her hands—and here is the thing I keep returning to, the detail that I hold like a stone in my pocket—her hands were not gripping. They were resting. On the wheel, on the wood, the way you'd rest your hands on the shoulders of someone you loved.

---

The third day.

Morning came not as light but as a lessening of dark. The sky went from black to grey, which meant only that we could now see what was killing us, which I do not recommend as an improvement.

The *Celeste* was low in the water. Her bow dipped into each wave and came up slower each time, the way a tired swimmer lifts their head—still trying, still trying, but with a resignation that tells you the next time or the time after will be the last.

At 0600 I laid out the cards on the chart table. The table was at an angle because the list had worsened to twenty degrees, and I had to wedge a coffee can under one leg to keep the cards from sliding. Water sloshed across the cabin sole. The barometer, which had been falling for two days, had settled at a number I didn't know barometers could reach.

Wen came below. She was soaked through and her lips were blue and she moved like something that had forgotten it was allowed to stop moving. She sat across from me. Picked up her cards. Sorted them.

"Your lead," she said.

I played a pair of fives. She played a pair of nines. I played a pair of queens. She played a pair of aces. The hull groaned—a sound like a cello string tightened past its limit, a sound that contained within it the specific knowledge of what comes after the note breaks.

"I have the condensed milk," she said. "From yesterday. If you want it for the rice."

"There's no rice. The stove's gone under."

"Ah." She played a straight. "Condensed milk on its own, then."

"That's disgusting, Captain."

"Many things are."

She won in four hands. I never did find out how.

---

The water came up through the cabin sole around noon. Not a leak anymore—a surrender. The planks breathed it in, and it pooled around our feet, and the *Celeste* settled into the sea the way an old dog settles onto a familiar floor: slowly, with great and practiced exhaustion.

Wen went back to the wheel. I followed. There was nowhere else to go, and there was nowhere else I wanted to be. This is the thing I need you to understand, the thing I cannot make anyone understand who wasn't there—I did not stay out of loyalty. I did not stay out of love, though that word is as close as any I have found and still not close enough. I stayed because in eleven years of mornings the woman at that wheel and the woman who brought her tea had become, through sheer repetition, a single fact. You cannot divide a fact. You can only state it.

We stood at the wheel together. The sea was all around us, not beneath us—around, like a room we were slowly descending into. The waves had lost their cruelty. The wind had lost its voice. The typhoon was moving on, losing interest the way all things lose interest, and what it left behind was a grey and heaving silence and a boat that was no longer a boat but a memory rehearsing its own disappearance.

"Lihua."

"Captain."

"The men in Bermuda. The repo men." She said this word—*repo*
