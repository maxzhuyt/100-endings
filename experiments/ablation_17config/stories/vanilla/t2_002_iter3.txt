# THE HOA BOARD WILL NOW COME TO ORDER

## A Novel

---

# PART ONE: THE RAMP

---

## Chapter 1
## NOTICE OF VIOLATION

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Meredith would later consider unnecessarily cruel. Not because Tuesday held any particular significance in her cosmology of grief, but because her husband had fallen on a Monday—had lain on the garage floor for forty-seven minutes while his left side negotiated its permanent and unconditional surrender to the blood clot colonizing his brain—and she had spent that entire Monday night in the neurological ICU at St. Francis learning a new vocabulary. *Hemorrhagic stroke. Left hemiplegia. Modified Rankin Scale.* She had been an actuary for thirty-one years. She liked new vocabularies. She wrote the terms in the small Moleskine notebook she kept in her purse, the same way she'd once written down *mortality table* and *loss ratio* and *stochastic modeling* as a twenty-two-year-old intern at Pacific Mutual, back when understanding a system's language felt like the first step toward mastering the system itself.

She had not yet learned that some systems do not reward mastery. That particular lesson would come later, delivered on HOA letterhead.

The envelope was waiting in the mailbox when the medical transport van brought Harold home on Tuesday afternoon. She tucked it under her arm while she supervised the two paramedics maneuvering Harold's wheelchair up the temporary plywood ramp that their son, Kevin, had built over the front steps at six o'clock that morning. Kevin was an environmental attorney who lived forty minutes away in Claremont and had arrived with a circular saw, a YouTube tutorial queued on his phone, and the particular tight-jawed expression he'd been wearing since the hospital, which Meredith recognized as her own face reflected in masculine miniature. The ramp was not pretty. It was plywood and two-by-fours and the angle was, she suspected, slightly steeper than ideal. But Harold's wheelchair rolled up it and through the front door and into the living room, where the hospital bed that Medicare was renting them for $247 a month had been installed in front of the bay window so Harold could watch the hummingbirds at the feeder he'd hung eleven years ago.

Harold did not watch the hummingbirds. Harold was engaged in the slow, exhausting work of relearning how to swallow his own saliva without aspiration. His right eye tracked her when she moved. His left eye did not track anything.

Meredith set the envelope on the kitchen counter and made Harold's lunch—pureed sweet potato thinned with chicken broth, per the speech therapist's instructions—and fed it to him in careful quarter-teaspoons while narrating the hummingbird activity outside the window as though she were providing color commentary for a sporting event Harold had not asked to watch. She wiped his chin. She administered his medications. She transferred him from the wheelchair to the bed with the pivot technique the occupational therapist had demonstrated, a movement that required her to bear his weight against her chest for a terrible, intimate moment, his head lolling against her shoulder, his right hand gripping her forearm with surprising strength while his left hand hung useless. He smelled like hospital. He had always smelled like sandalwood and the particular warm-copper scent of a man who spent weekends doing light carpentry in the garage. Now he smelled like hospital, and she added this to the list of losses she was maintaining in a separate section of the Moleskine, though she had not yet determined what actuarial table could adequately model the probability distribution of griefs.

She opened the letter at 3:47 PM.

It was from the Laurel Estates Homeowners Association, Planned Community, Inc., and it informed her that a "structure" had been "erected or installed" on the property at 414 Sycamore Court in violation of Section 7.04(b) of the CC&Rs (Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions), which prohibited exterior modifications without prior written approval from the Architectural Review Committee. The structure was described as "wooden ramp, front entrance, unpainted." She was directed to submit a formal modification request to the ARC within fourteen days or remove the structure. Failure to comply would result in daily fines of $50.00, assessed beginning on the fifteenth day and continuing until compliance was achieved, per Section 12.02(a)(iii).

The letter was signed by Douglas P. Whitmore, President, Laurel Estates HOA Board of Directors.

Below his printed name was his actual signature, which Meredith had seen many times on the annual assessment letters and the seasonal newsletters and the notices about coyote sightings and holiday decoration guidelines. It was a generous signature, confident and legible, the kind of signature produced by a man who had never experienced a moment of genuine uncertainty about whether he was doing the right thing.

Beneath the signature was a handwritten note in blue ballpoint:

*Meredith—hoping Harold is on the mend! Banana bread on your porch. —Doug*

She put the letter down. She picked it up. She read it again. She performed these actions three times, which was the minimum number required to confirm that the document said what it appeared to say, and then she folded it precisely along its original creases and returned it to the envelope and placed it in the Moleskine, in a new section, behind *Medical Vocabulary* and *Inventory of Losses* and ahead of several blank pages she had not yet assigned a purpose.

Then she went to the front porch and found the banana bread.

It was excellent banana bread. Doug Whitmore's banana bread was, by universal consensus, the best in Laurel Estates, possibly the best in the township. He used brown butter, and there was a rumor—never confirmed, because Doug deflected recipe inquiries with the folksy modesty of a man who understood that mystique was a form of power—that he added a tablespoon of miso paste. The loaf was wrapped in cellophane with a small card that said *From Doug's Kitchen* above a clipart illustration of a stand mixer.

Meredith stood on her porch holding the banana bread and looking at the plywood ramp and thinking about what it meant that a man could, in a single gesture, threaten her with financial penalties for keeping her disabled husband accessible to his own home and also bring her homemade baked goods, and experience no apparent friction between these two acts. She had spent her career modeling risk, pricing uncertainty, building mathematical frameworks to contain the chaos of human loss within predictable parameters. She understood expected values and confidence intervals and the long elegant tails of catastrophic probability curves.

She had never adequately modeled whatever Doug Whitmore was.

She ate a slice of the banana bread. She was sixty-seven years old, and her husband was dying by degrees in what had been their living room and was now a hospital suite, and she was not going to deny herself banana bread on principle. It was the best she'd ever tasted.

She saved the rest for Harold.

He couldn't eat it—too dense, aspiration risk—but she described it to him in detail while she fed him his pureed dinner, and his right eye tracked her face, and she chose to interpret this as amusement.

## Chapter 2
## THE ARC OF COMPLIANCE

She submitted the modification request on Wednesday. It was four pages long, single-spaced, because Meredith had spent three decades writing actuarial reports and did not know how to be brief when precision was available. She included Harold's medical records, the occupational therapist's recommendation for home accessibility modifications, three comparable ramp designs from the ADA residential guidelines, a materials list, a proposed paint color (Navajo White, to match the existing trim, per Section 7.02(a) of the CC&Rs), and a cover letter explaining, in language she considered restrained, that her husband of forty-one years had suffered a catastrophic stroke and could not enter or exit his own home without the ramp, and that she would be happy to bring the structure into full aesthetic compliance as soon as she was not spending every waking moment preventing him from choking to death.

The Architectural Review Committee met on the first Thursday of each month. She had missed the October meeting by three days. The next meeting was November 2nd.

She called Doug Whitmore.

He answered on the second ring, because Doug Whitmore always answered on the second ring. The first ring was for people who were desperate. The third ring was for people who didn't matter. The second ring communicated availability without eagerness—a calibration so precise that Meredith, who appreciated calibration, almost admired it.

"Meredith! How's our patient?"

She noted the *our*. She filed it.

"Doug, I've submitted the modification request for the wheelchair ramp. The next ARC meeting isn't until November. I'm asking if the committee can review it before then, given the medical circumstances."

"Oh, absolutely, I completely understand the urgency. Let me look into whether we can arrange a special session. You know I'd love to just wave a magic wand—" He laughed, the warm conspiratorial laugh of a man inviting you to join him in lamenting the constraints that bound his otherwise boundless generosity. "But we've got procedures, you know? If I start making exceptions, then everyone wants an exception, and pretty soon the CC&Rs are just suggestions and, well." Another laugh. "You know how it goes."

"The ramp is medically necessary, Doug."

"And the committee is going to take that very seriously, I'm sure of it. Very seriously. I'll push for an early review. How's the banana bread?"

"When would the fines begin?"

Brief silence. Not uncomfortable—Doug Whitmore did not do uncomfortable silences. An *organizing* silence, like a man straightening papers on a desk.

"Well, technically, per the bylaws, the fourteen-day clock started when the notice was issued. So that would be... October 17th. But listen, Meredith, let's not get ahead of ourselves with the fine talk. I'm sure we'll have this sorted well before then."

October 17th was fifteen days away. The ARC met on November 2nd, which was eighteen days away. This meant that even if the committee approved the ramp at the first available meeting, she would have accrued three days of fines—$150—in the gap between the deadline and the review. She performed this calculation instantly, the way she had once calculated loss reserves and premium deficiencies, and she understood that Doug had also performed this calculation, and that the architecture of the timeline was not accidental.

"Doug. It's a wheelchair ramp."

"And I hear you, Meredith. I really do. But you know, last year the Patersons put up a pergola without approval, and we had to fine them too, and if people hear that we let the process slide for one person—even for a really good reason, and this is a really good reason—then we've undermined the whole system. The CC&Rs protect everyone's property values. That's what people forget."

"Harold can't walk."

"I know. And my heart goes out to you both, it truly does. Tell you what—I'll bring over another loaf this weekend. I'm trying a new recipe with cardamom."

He inquired about Harold's prognosis with an attentiveness that could not be distinguished, by any available instrument, from genuine compassion. He recommended a physical therapist his mother had used. He said "You're in our prayers" with the effortless sincerity of a man to whom all statements of goodwill came effortlessly, because goodwill was the medium in which he swam, the water he breathed, the element so native to his self-conception that the possibility of contradiction between his words and his actions existed in a dimension he had never visited and did not believe in.

Meredith hung up. She looked at Harold, who was sleeping in the hospital bed with his mouth slightly open, his breathing producing a faint wet rattle that the pulmonologist said might or might not indicate developing pneumonia.

She opened the Moleskine to the section she had not yet assigned a purpose.

At the top of the first blank page, in her precise actuarial hand, she wrote:

*DOUG.*

Then, beneath it:

*What are the bylaws, exactly?*

## Chapter 3
## ASSEMBLING THE TABLE

The CC&Rs of Laurel Estates Homeowners Association were forty-seven pages long, adopted in 1987, and amended fourteen times. The bylaws were an additional thirty-one pages. Together they constituted what Meredith, after three nights of systematic reading at the kitchen table while Harold's oxygen concentrator hummed its metronomic accompaniment from the living room, recognized as a document of extraordinary and largely untested power.

She had spent her career reading dense regulatory filings—insurance codes, NAIC model laws, state compliance bulletins—and she understood that complexity was not the same as strength. Sometimes complexity was weakness. Sometimes a system's intricacy was precisely what made it vulnerable, because intricate systems had joints, and joints could be stressed.

The Laurel Estates bylaws had many joints.

She found the first one on page 14, Section 5.03(c): quorum requirements for the annual meeting. She found the second on page 19, Section 8.01(a): procedures for calling a special election. She found the third on page 23, Section 9.04(b)(ii): grounds for removal of an officer, which included—she read this three times to be certain—"misappropriation or misapplication of Association funds."

This third joint was interesting. But it required evidence she did not have.

What she had, after three nights of reading, was a structural map of an organization that had been governed for fifteen years by a man who relied on no one else reading the bylaws carefully, because careful reading was tedious and Doug was charming and the banana bread was excellent and most people had better things to do than parse subordinate clauses in a document governing fence heights and guest parking.

Meredith did not, at this moment, have better things to do. Her husband was dying. Her world had contracted to the dimensions of a hospital bed and a plywood ramp and a sixty-seven-year-old body that ached from performing patient transfers it had not been designed for. She could not fix Harold's brain. She could not reverse the clot or regrow the dead tissue or restore the man who had built birdhouses in the garage on Saturday mornings while listening to Thelonious Monk. She could not do any of this.

But she could read bylaws.

---

She called Janet Okafor-Reiss on a Thursday evening.

Janet had been the stenographer for the Riverside County Superior Court for twenty-two years before a incident that Janet referred to, when pressed, as "the Barstow situation" and the California Shorthand Reporters Board referred to, in their formal censure, as "material and systematic alteration of the trial record in *People v. Kendrick*, constituting a fundamental breach of professional ethics and public trust." The details were contested, or rather they were not contested—Janet had, in fact, inserted into the official transcript a passage from Audre Lorde's *The Uses of Anger* in place of the prosecutor's closing argument—but the context was complicated. The prosecutor had been Janet's ex-husband. The defendant had been a nineteen-year-old kid caught with an ounce of marijuana. The passage from Lorde was, Janet maintained, more legally relevant than anything her ex-husband had said, which was probably true but was not the point.

Janet had been stripped of her certification, fined, and narrowly avoided criminal contempt charges. She now worked as a freelance transcriptionist for a medical billing company and lived four houses down from Meredith on Sycamore Court, where she maintained a garden of native California plants that the HOA cited her for twice yearly (Section 7.08(a): "All front yard landscaping shall consist primarily of turf grass or approved ground cover") and which she refused, on ecological and aesthetic and deeply personal principle, to remove.

"Janet," Meredith said. "Can you still do shorthand?"

"I can do two hundred forty words a minute with ninety-eight percent accuracy, which is twelve words a minute faster than the woman they replaced me with, who also, for the record, doesn't know the difference between a semicolon and a hole in the ground. Why?"

"I need someone to take minutes at the annual HOA meeting."

"The HOA takes its own minutes. Linda Ferris does them."

"Linda Ferris takes minutes that say what Doug Whitmore wants them to say. I've compared the last three years of meeting minutes to the audio recordings posted on the HOA website. There are discrepancies."

Silence. Then: "
