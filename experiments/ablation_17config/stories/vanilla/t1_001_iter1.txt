**Edited and Annotated by Morimoto Kenji**

*Translated from the Japanese by Elaine Satō*

## Compiler's Preface

I am dying in the customary way—slowly, and with too much time to reconsider.

The physicians at Keio Hospital have given me language I do not need. I have spent sixty-one years reading the body's intentions, its declarations of commitment and its feints. I know what my body is saying. It is saying what bodies eventually say.

This is not self-pity. I mention it only because it explains why I am publishing these letters now, rather than honoring the silence that has been, for forty years, the governing condition of my life.

Hideo's letters began arriving in the autumn of 1984, seven months after the Twelfth All-Japan Invitational Tournament in Kyoto, where he forfeited our final match in the forty-third second of the second round. He had not spoken to me before that day. He has not spoken to me since. The letters were delivered by ordinary post, without return address, though the postmarks over the years have traced a slow pilgrimage across Tōhoku, Shikoku, and finally what I believe to be a small temple in Niigata Prefecture, where I am told he still lives, though "lives" may be too strong a word for what monks do.

I did not write back. I will explain why when I can, which may be never.

What I can say is this: I have read each letter so many times that the early ones have begun to lose their ink to the oil of my fingers, the paper thinning where I grip the margins. I mention this so the reader understands the condition of the originals and forgives the occasional lacunae in the transcription. What appears as [*illegible*] may be my doing. The letters came to me intact. It is the reading that has damaged them.

There are thirty-seven letters. I have included all of them, even the ones that say nearly nothing, because I have come to believe that the ones that say nearly nothing say the most. I have added annotations where I felt they were needed and in several places where I know they are not needed but could not stop myself. I ask the reader to understand that these annotations are not scholarly apparatus. They are the only replies I know how to make.

Hideo and I are the last two people alive who were on that platform in Kyoto. Everyone who watched is either dead or has forgotten. The match has become a story, and the story has become several different stories depending on who tells it, and all of them are wrong, including, I suspect, the one I am about to tell.

But I am old, and I am dying, and the letters are here, and the silence has become heavier than what it was designed to carry.

Morimoto Kenji
Tokyo, Japan
March 2024

## A Note on the Twelfth All-Japan Invitational

*For readers unfamiliar with the events of April 7, 1984:*

The Twelfth All-Japan Invitational was held at the Butokuden in Kyoto, the oldest martial arts training hall in Japan. The tournament followed the traditional elimination format for kendō, single-match rounds extending to a maximum of five minutes, with extensions at the judges' discretion. Forty-eight competitors entered. By the final match, only two remained: Hideo Tadashi, then thirty-one years old, holder of the Emperor's Cup for three consecutive years, widely regarded as the most technically gifted kendōka of the postwar period; and Morimoto Kenji, then twenty-nine, a relative unknown from Sendai who had advanced through the bracket with a style the *Asahi Shimbun* described as "inauspicious but effective."

Their final match lasted forty-three seconds. Surviving footage—grainy, shot from a poor angle—shows the two men assume their positions, bow, and begin. Hideo advances. Morimoto assumes a defensive posture. For approximately thirty seconds, neither man strikes. Then Hideo steps back, removes his *men* (helmet), places it on the floor, bows, and walks off the platform. He was never seen in competition again.

The footage does not explain anything. It has been analyzed by kendō scholars, biomechanics researchers, and at least one doctoral candidate in philosophy. The thirty seconds of stillness have been called "the most densely interpreted half-minute in martial arts history." But the camera was positioned behind Morimoto, so we see only his back—steady, apparently calm—and the face of Hideo beyond him, which the resolution does not permit us to read.

Morimoto accepted the trophy. He competed for three more years, winning two national titles, before retiring to teach at a small dōjō in Shibuya. He never spoke publicly about the final match. When asked, he offered variations of the same response: "Hideo made his decision. It is not my place to explain it."

He has, it seems, been waiting forty years to explain it. Or rather—as will become clear—waiting forty years to confess that he cannot.

—E.S.

## Letter One

*Postmarked Yamagata Prefecture, November 2, 1984*

---

Morimoto,

I am writing to you from a place I will not name, in circumstances I did not foresee. I have been here since June. The temple is small. The monks do not know who I was, or if they do, they have the grace to pretend otherwise, which in a monastery amounts to the same thing.

I will not ask how you are. I have forfeited the right to inquire after you, along with everything else I forfeited in April. But I find that I must write, in the same way that a man with a stone in his shoe must eventually stop walking, though stopping is its own kind of pain.

I want to tell you why I did what I did.

I have written that sentence and looked at it for a long time.

I want to tell you why I did what I did, but I find that wanting and doing are, as always, separated by exactly the distance that matters. Perhaps in my next letter. Perhaps the act of writing this one will make the next one possible, the way the first step up a mountain is said to contain all the others.

I do not expect you to reply. I am not sure I am writing to you. I may be writing to the idea of you, which is a different thing entirely—a silhouette made of what I remember and what I fear, and the place where those two overlap, which is most of it.

The temple has a garden. I rake the gravel in the mornings. The other monks do this as meditation. I do it because the lines in the gravel stay where you put them, which is more than can be said for most things.

Forgive me for writing. Forgive me for what I will say when I learn how to say it. Forgive me in advance, which is the only kind of forgiveness I have the courage to request.

Hideo

---

**Compiler's Note:** *I received this letter on November 5, 1984, at my apartment in Sendai. I recognized the handwriting immediately, though I had only seen it once—on a tournament registration form I happened to glimpse in the officials' tent before the quarterfinals. I do not know why I remembered it. I do not know why I remember anything I remember about that day, except that the mind preserves what it needs for whatever trial it senses approaching.*

*I read the letter four times. Then I put it in a drawer. Then I took it out of the drawer and read it again. Then I sat on my kitchen floor for what must have been a very long time, because when I stood up, the light had changed.*

*He says he wants to tell me why. I want to say: I know why. But I have never been certain enough to say it, and the space between almost certain and certain is exactly the width of a sword.*

## Letter Two

*Postmarked Yamagata Prefecture, February 14, 1985*

---

Morimoto,

It has been winter here for a long time. The other monks say this is normal. I think they mean the weather.

I have been thinking about the *Book of Five Rings*, which you will find predictable, since every man who retreats from the world eventually picks up Musashi, the way every man who cannot sleep eventually picks up a bottle. Musashi writes that the Way of the warrior is the simultaneous practice of pen and sword. I always understood this as metaphor. Now I rake gravel and write letters to a man who will not respond, and I think Musashi was being literal, and that the practice of the pen is precisely as dangerous as the practice of the sword, only slower.

I said in my last letter that I would explain. I will not explain today either. But I will come closer, the way a man circling a well comes closer to the water with every pass while never quite looking in.

Do you remember the thirty seconds? I know you do. I know that you, like me, have divided those thirty seconds into smaller and smaller units, the way a physicist divides the atom and finds, inside it, another universe. I have thirty seconds. Inside those thirty seconds I have found forty years. I suspect I will find more.

What I want to say is this: in those thirty seconds, I saw something. And what I saw changed the nature of every choice that was available to me, so that where there had been many choices, there was suddenly only one, and it was the one I made, and it was not a choice at all but a door closing behind me as I passed through it.

You will want to know what I saw. I am not ready to tell you. I may never be ready. But I want you to know that there was a seeing—that my forfeiture was not madness, or cowardice, or the mysterious spiritual crisis the newspapers preferred. It was a response to something real. It was, in its way, the most precise thing I have ever done.

The garden is covered in snow. There is nothing to rake. I find this a relief.

Hideo

---

**Compiler's Note:** *He says he saw something. I know what he saw. That is—I have always believed I know what he saw. I have spent forty years knowing it in the way one knows a thing one has never confirmed: with the body rather than the mind, the knowledge sitting in the gut like an undigested meal, nourishing nothing, refusing to leave.*

*I should explain. But I find I cannot explain in the same way he cannot explain, and for the same reason, which is that the explanation would require one of us to say the thing plainly, and to say it plainly would be to do the thing that the silence was constructed to prevent.*

*The reader will be frustrated. I am sorry. I am frustrated too. I have been frustrated for forty years.*

## Letter Five

*Postmarked Yamagata Prefecture, January 12, 1987*

---

Morimoto,

I have been reading the Hagakure again. Yamamoto Tsunetomo writes: *"The Way of the Warrior is found in death."* Every child in every dōjō learns this. Every child misunderstands it. I misunderstood it for thirty years. I believed it meant that the warrior must be willing to die. Now I believe it means something else.

The warrior must be willing to kill.

This seems obvious. It is not obvious. The willingness to die is easy—it is a romantic notion, a young man's notion, and it costs nothing until the moment of payment, which most young men assume will never come. The willingness to kill is the real test, because the cost is paid immediately and in a currency that cannot be earned back, and the man who pays it is not the same man afterward as before, and the difference is not something that can be explained to someone who has not experienced it, which is why I am explaining it so badly.

I held the Emperor's Cup for three years. I won forty-six consecutive matches. In not one of those matches was I required to confront the willingness I am describing, because —

I have crossed out the next sentence four times. I will try again.

Because in forty-six consecutive matches, I never saw what I saw in you.

There. That is closer. That is not close enough, but it is closer, and for today, closer will have to do.

The other monks have begun to notice that I spend my evenings writing. One of them asked me if I was composing a memoir. I said no. He asked if I was writing poetry. I said I was not sure. He said: "If you are not sure whether something is poetry, it is either very good poetry or a letter to someone you have wronged." I did not know monks could be perceptive about anything other than meditation. It appears I was wrong about that too.

Hideo

---

**Compiler's Note:** *Letter Five is the first time he comes close. "I never saw what I saw in you." I have read this sentence more times than any other sentence in the collection. It is an almost-door. He opens it a crack, and through the crack comes a thin light, and the light illuminates nothing because he shuts the door immediately and returns to Yamamoto Tsunetomo and the safety of philosophy.*

*I should note here what the reader may have begun to suspect: I know what he saw because it was my body he was reading. The flaw—I will call it a flaw, because I do not have a better word, and because the letters will eventually approach the word without using it—the flaw was mine. It lived in me. I carried it into every match for thirty years. If Hideo saw it, then he saw something that was true, and his response to that truth is the subject of these letters, and my response to his response is the subject of these annotations, and together they form a conversation in which each speaker says everything except the one thing that matters.*

*I will say more when I can. Which may be never. But I am learning, from Hideo's letters, that "when I can" is a promise with no expiration date, and that the act of deferral is itself a kind of statement, and that forty years of deferred statements can fill a book, though not the silence the book was meant to replace.*

## Letter Seven

*Postmarked Akita Prefecture, October 3, 1988*

---

Morimoto,

I have moved to a different temple. The abbot in Yamagata found my letters. Not the contents—he did not read them—but the act. He said that a monk who writes to the world has not yet left the world, and that I was welcome to stay but should consider whether the staying was honest. I could not answer him. So I left.

The new temple is smaller. The monks here do not speak at all. This suits me. I have discovered that silence between men who have chosen silence is a different substance from silence between men who have failed at speech. The first is a discipline. The second is a wound that has been dressed to look like a discipline, and I have spent four years applying the bandage.

I want to write today about the mechanics of what I saw. I want to be technical. I think if I am technical I can avoid the other thing—the thing that is not technical, the thing that lives underneath the technique like a body under ice.
