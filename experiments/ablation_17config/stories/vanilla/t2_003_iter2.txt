# THE ACCOUNT OF PERDITION GULCH

## Being a History of What Was Held and What Was Released

---

## PART ONE: THE ARCHITECTURE OF HOLDING

---

### NOW

The sickness came first to the boy.

This is known because the narrator knows it, though no living soul in Perdition Gulch marked the moment. The boy himself—Purl, ten years old by the best estimation of anyone who'd thought to estimate, feral-haired and barefoot even in November when the ground froze hard enough to ring like struck iron—did not announce it. He had never announced anything. He communicated in fragments: a tug on a sleeve, a finger pointed at a man's chest where the thought lived that the man thought was his alone, a slow and solemn nod or a violent shake of his narrow head. He did not trade in words because words were redundant. He heard what people meant before they downgraded it into language.

On the morning of October 3rd, 1887, Purl woke on the straw tick behind Crenshaw's surgery and heard nothing.

Not silence—he knew silence, had felt it in the minds of men asleep or recently dead, and it had a texture, like river stones under still water. This was not that. This was absence. A door that had always been open was now not merely closed but *gone*, the wall sealed smooth as if no door had ever hung there, and when he reached for the whisper-thoughts of Silas Crenshaw grinding coffee twelve feet away in the front room, his mind touched only the inside of his own skull.

He sat there for a long time. The straw tick crinkled beneath him with each breath.

We will return to the boy. We have all the time that the boy does not.

---

The town of Perdition Gulch occupied a position on the map of the Arizona Territory that most cartographers treated as a suggestion. It sat in a crease between two ridgelines of red rock, the kind of geological arrangement that looked, from sufficient elevation, like a wound that had not been sutured properly. The single road in was the single road out. Water came from a spring that had been reliable for six hundred years before the Apache knew to name it and that had begun, in the drought of 1884, to thin its output like a man conserving his last canteen.

Four hundred and twelve people lived there. This is an exact number. Eulalia Voss could have told you each of their names, the configuration of their faces, the dates they arrived, and what they wore on the day she first looked at them. She could have told you the text of every letter nailed to the post office wall and the pattern of grain in each board of the church floor and the precise number of needles on the ponderosa pine that stood alone in the yard of the assay office, which she had looked at once, on a Tuesday, four years ago, for roughly eleven seconds.

Eulalia could have told you anything she had ever seen. What she could not tell you—what none of them could tell you—was why they were what they were.

This is the first truth of the five who kept Perdition Gulch: they did not understand themselves.

They understood only the *keeping*.

---

### THEN

They found each other the way iron filings find a magnet—not by choice, but by the dumb pull of a force that none of them could name and all of them could feel.

Eulalia Voss arrived first, in 1862, when the town was twelve buildings and a territorial ambition. She was twenty-three then. She came from Pennsylvania, where she had been dismissed from a millinery position because her employer found it "unwholesome" that she could reproduce from memory the private correspondence she'd glimpsed on his desk. She had tried to explain that she couldn't *not* see it, that everything she'd ever witnessed was still present behind her eyes in perfect fidelity—the letters, yes, but also the roses on the wallpaper of her childhood bedroom and the arrangement of fish on a market cart in Philadelphia when she was nine and the face of her mother in the last hour of her mother's life, every capillary of blood visible in the whites of the dying woman's eyes—but explanation had not been what was wanted. What was wanted was that she be gone.

She came west because the West was large enough to be forgotten in, which was the great irony of Eulalia's life. She could forget nothing. She sought a land of forgetting and arrived in it carrying every instant she had ever lived, each one as sharp and present as the moment she'd first collected it.

She told no one what she could do. She kept ledgers. She wrote journals. She committed the town to pages not because she needed to—her memory was the more perfect record—but because the act of writing made her feel ordinary. A woman with a pen and a notebook was a woman with a hobby. A woman who could close her eyes and replay the entirety of a conversation held six years prior, word for word, gesture for gesture, including the smell of the room and the angle of light through the window—that woman was something else.

Silas Crenshaw came in 1868. He had been a field surgeon in the late war, though for which side depended on which year you asked about. He had discovered his ability the way many men discover unwelcome truths about themselves: in violence, and by accident. A Minié ball had entered his abdomen two inches left of the navel at Shiloh, and he had lain in a ditch for nineteen hours waiting to die, and when he did not die he assumed he had been lucky. The second wound, a bayonet through the thigh at Chickamauga, healed in four hours and left not even a scar. After that, he stopped assuming.

By the time he reached Perdition Gulch, he had catalogued his limits. He could not regrow what was taken—a finger once lost would stay lost. He could not survive what was instantly fatal; a bullet through the brain or the severing of his head would end him as permanently as it would anyone. But everything between a scratch and a mortal blow was temporary. Cuts sealed. Bones knit. Fevers broke in minutes. He carried in his body a physician's entire dream of what the human form could be, and it had made him, by the time he was forty, the most exhausted man in the territory.

Because he could not stop *not dying*, and the vigor of his own flesh was a mockery when set beside the patients he could not save with any skill or prayer.

He told Eulalia first. Not because he trusted her but because she looked at him one morning across the dry goods counter and said, "You were shot yesterday through the right hand, Mr. Crenshaw, and I see no wound today," and her voice carried no judgment and no wonder—only the flat, cataloguing tone of a woman who collected facts the way other women collected china.

"Yes," he said.

"I see," she said.

And there it was. The first joining.

---

### NOW

Nettie Harrow had not slept in thirty-one hours, and this would have been unremarkable—she rarely slept well—except that for every one of those thirty-one hours, she had been alone.

*Alone.* The word did not convey. Could not convey. Imagine you have lived your entire life hearing a second heartbeat alongside your own, a consciousness as intimate and constant as breath, so that you have never once in thirty-seven years experienced a thought that was not also *felt* by another, verified by another, held by another. Imagine that the boundary between you and your sister was not the boundary that other people assumed—two bodies, two skulls, two names—but was instead a permeable, shimmering thing, more like the surface of a pond than a wall, so that thoughts and sensations passed between you in both directions at all times, not as telepathy (that was the boy's affliction) but as a *shared being*, a single awareness housed in two sets of bones.

Now imagine silence.

Clara had not died. Clara was asleep in the next room, in the house they had shared since 1871. Nettie could hear her breathing through the pine-plank wall. But she could not *feel* her. The shared space—the space that had no name in any language Nettie knew—was empty. She reached for her sister's mind the way a woman reaches for a railing in the dark, and there was nothing there, and she stumbled, though she was sitting perfectly still in a ladder-back chair.

She pressed her palm flat against the wall. On the other side, Clara breathed on. *Clara alone in Clara.* Nettie alone in Nettie. Two women for the first time in their lives.

The sickness. Whatever it was. Wherever it had come from.

It was taking everything.

---

Silas noticed ten days after the boy did.

He was splitting wood behind the surgery—a task he performed with more force than necessary because the violence of it pleased him, the axe biting into pine, the clean fracture along the grain—when the blade slipped and opened a gash across his left palm from the base of his thumb to the heel of his hand. Deep. He could see the white gleam of tendon.

He set down the axe. Held his hand up. Watched.

And watched.

And the blood continued to come, flowing freely over his wrist and into his cuff, and the wound did not seal, and the flesh did not knit, and for the first time since 1862, Silas Crenshaw felt pain *persist*.

He wrapped his hand in his shirt and walked to Eulalia's house because Eulalia's house was where they had always gone when the world shifted beneath them. It was the fixed point. Eulalia, whose memory was the town's memory, who could tell you what any of them had been and done and said—Eulalia was the archive, and you went to an archive when you needed to understand what was happening by understanding what had happened before.

He found her at her desk. Her thirty-sixth journal was open before her, the ink still drying on a passage he would never read.

"Something's wrong," he said, and held up his wrapped hand.

She looked at the blood. She looked at him.

"Purl came to see me this morning," she said. "He knocked on my door, Silas. He *knocked*. In ten years that child has never knocked because he always knew whether I was receiving visitors before he reached my porch." She paused. "He stood on my porch and he knocked and when I opened the door he looked at me the way a dog looks at a locked gate."

"He can't hear."

"He can't hear."

Silas sat down. The chair creaked beneath him. His hand throbbed.

"And the twins?"

"I haven't been to see the twins."

"Eulalia."

She looked at him then, and there was something in her face that Silas had never seen in twenty years of knowing her. Eulalia's face was a composed instrument. She arranged it the way she arranged her journals—carefully, with purpose, each expression a decision. But now there was a fracture in the composure, a thing leaking through, and it took Silas a moment to name it.

*Relief.*

"What about you?" he asked.

"I can still remember," she said. "I remember everything. I always remember everything."

She picked up her pen. She wrote something he couldn't see. And she did not volunteer more, and he did not press, because among the rules of the five—unwritten, understood—was this: you did not insist on another's truth. You waited for it to be offered, and if it was never offered, you lived without it.

This had seemed like wisdom for twenty years. It was beginning to seem like something else.

---

### THEN

The massacre happened on the ninth of July, 1879, and the word *happened* is doing more work than it should.

Things that *happen* carry an implication of passivity. Weather happens. A massacre is *chosen*—by the killers to perform it, yes, but also, and here is where the architecture of guilt makes its true and terrible shape, by those who know it is coming and elect to stand aside.

The five knew it was coming.

Purl heard it first, in the usual way. He was sitting on the porch of the Harrow sisters' house—he spent most of his time near one or another of them, this feral, voiceless satellite orbiting the constellation of their strangeness—and he heard the thoughts of fourteen men riding in from the east. Fourteen minds full of kerosene and gunmetal. Fourteen sets of intentions so vivid and so red that the boy flinched as though struck and pressed his palms over his ears, though the sound was not in his ears.

He went to Nettie. Or to Clara. It made no difference—what one knew, the other knew, and what the twins knew was already being walked to Eulalia's door because the twins had seen the riders from the upstairs window, a plume of dust on the eastern road, too many horses for a social call, and Eulalia would remember having heard a conversation in the saloon three weeks earlier—she remembered all conversations, always—in which a man named Briggs told another man named Fontaine that the Jicarilla families camped in the draw south of town were "a problem that solves itself if you just let the right men know where to find it."

They gathered. The five of them. In Eulalia's kitchen, which was small and overwarm in July, the stove unlit but the walls holding the day's heat like fired clay. Purl sat on the floor. The twins occupied one chair, or rather Clara sat and Nettie stood behind her with her hands on Clara's shoulders, the two of them so still they might have been a single organism paused in thought. Silas leaned against the doorframe. Eulalia stood at the window, watching the dust plume grow.

"Fourteen men," Nettie said, or Clara said—it did not matter; the voice came from the shared place. "Armed. Riding for the draw."

"There are forty-three people in that camp," Eulalia said. She had walked past it six days earlier. She had seen every face. She would always see every face. "Nineteen of them are children."

"And if we stop it," Silas said.

The sentence did not need finishing. They had discussed this before—not this specific horror but the *category* of horror, the hypothetical they had all known was not hypothetical.
