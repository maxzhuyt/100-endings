# The Commissar's Confession

## I.

There is a stain on the east wall of the anteroom that resembles, if one tilts one's head slightly to the left and allows the afternoon light to do its work, the silhouette of a dog or possibly a goat, and I have studied this stain on eleven separate occasions while waiting for the clerk to finish sorting the day's denunciations into their three piles—priority, secondary, deferred—and each time the shape has shifted slightly, not because the stain has changed but because the light moves, of course the light moves, it is late Messidor and the sun is still high at five o'clock, and the denunciations today number forty-three, which is below the weekly average of sixty-one but above yesterday's count of thirty-nine, and the clerk's hands move with an efficiency that I have noted before, the left hand lifting the sheet, the eyes scanning—four seconds, five at most—and the right hand depositing, priority, secondary, deferred, priority, priority, secondary, and the scratch of his pen when a name requires annotation, and I am thinking about none of this. I am thinking about all of it. I am thinking that the ink on my own fingers has not been fully scrubbed from under the nail of the left index finger and that this is the third Tuesday I have failed to purchase soap from the commissary and that the word *commissary* shares a root with *commissar* and *commission* and *commit*, from the Latin *committere*, to bring together, to entrust, which is what Citizen Robespierre does when he entrusts the Committee's work to men such as myself, and the door opens.

Not the clerk's door. The inner door. The one behind the clerk.

The clerk's hands stop.

I am told to enter.

The room is smaller than its reputation. I have been inside it four times before—twice to deliver dossiers on suspects in the Hébert affair, once to receive instructions regarding a printer on the Rue Saint-Jacques, once for a matter I will not think about now—and each time the room has seemed to contract around the desk, the desk with its precise disorder, papers arranged in clusters that only he can navigate, and the pen moves, I hear the pen before I see him, the scratch-scratch-pause-scratch of his writing, and the light from the window catches the left lens of his spectacles and turns it into a small white sun, and I cannot see his eyes behind that sun, and I stand.

He does not look up.

The pen continues. The scratch is regular. I count: four strokes, pause, six strokes, pause, two strokes, pause. He is not drafting legislation; the rhythm is wrong. He is making a list.

The room smells of paper and of something acidic beneath the paper—vinegar, perhaps, or the remnant of a poultice, because it is known that his skin troubles him, that he applies compresses of orange water, that his jaw aches, though one does not speak of this, one does not speak of Citizen Robespierre's jaw, one speaks of the Republic's mandible, its teeth, its bite—

He looks up.

The spectacles settle. The white sun in the left lens resolves into a pale eye, then two pale eyes, then a face that I cannot hold in focus because something in me has already begun to move backward, away, the way an animal retreats from a sound it cannot place, and he says my name—my official name, the name on my warrants, the name that is not the name I was given but the name I took when the name I was given became dangerous, three years ago, when the moderate network provided me with papers and a function and a desk from which to do work that I will not think about now—

He says: You will oversee the executions tomorrow. Personally.

I say: Of course, Citizen.

He says: This is the list.

He slides it across the desk. The paper is smaller than I expect. It is not an official document. It is not printed. It is written in his hand, the same hand that was scratching when I entered, and the ink is still faintly wet on the last name, and the names—

The first name is Duval. Pierre Duval, age forty-one, former clerk of the Commune, whom I recruited in Ventôse of the previous year at a café on the Quai de la Mégisserie after determining through four separate conversations that his disillusionment was genuine and not performed, and whose wife has a mole above her left eyebrow and whose daughter is seven and does not know that her father passes information through a loose brick behind the Church of Saint-Merri—

The second name is Legendre. Not the deputy—a different Legendre, Anne-Marie Legendre, who operates a laundry on the Rue de la Huchette and who receives encoded messages in the pockets of garments left for washing, and who once, when I asked her if she was afraid, said *I was born afraid, citizen, this is nothing new*—

The third name is one I do not know. Beaulieu. I do not know this name. This name is not mine.

The fourth name is Groulx. Michel Groulx, a tanner—no, this is not right. The name is Groulx but the man is not a tanner, the man is a former seminary student who abandoned his orders in '91 and who serves as a courier between the network's eastern contacts and its center, and who walks with a limp from a fall he took as a child, his left leg—

The fifth name is one I do not know.

The sixth name is one I do not know.

The seventh name is Marest. The widow Marest. The printer's widow. Citizeness Marest, née Colombe, whose husband was killed at the Tuileries and who continued the printing operation alone, who taught me—

I fold the list.

I fold it once, and then I fold it again, and the paper is heavy stock, good paper, the kind that takes ink well, the kind that does not bleed, and I place it inside my coat, against the left breast, where it sits with the weight of a letter or a playing card or nothing at all, and I say—

I say: It will be done, Citizen.

And he says nothing. The pen has already resumed.

I leave the room. I pass the clerk, whose hands have resumed their sorting. I pass the stain on the east wall—goat, not dog, the light has moved—and I descend the stairs, and I am walking, and the list is against my chest, and I have folded lists before. For three years I have folded things and placed them inside my coat and carried them through streets in which any face might be the face that knows my other name, and the fold is the same fold, the gesture is the same gesture, my hand performed it without instruction, the way a hand turns a key or signs a warrant, but what is folded now is not what was folded then. What is folded now—

I am walking. I do not think about what is folded now.

The weather is fair.

## II.

The room is twelve paces by nine. I have measured it—not formally, not with instruments, but in the way one measures a space one inhabits alone, by walking its perimeter during the hours when sleep refuses to arrive. The window faces east onto the courtyard of a building whose function I have never determined. The candle is tallow, not beeswax, because beeswax is for citizens of higher revolutionary merit, and the flame lists to the left, meaning the window's seal is failing again, and the list is unfolded on the table before me.

Seven names.

I will work this now. I will work it properly, as the handler taught me: establish the nodes, establish the links, assess exposure, calculate downstream.

Seven names. Of these, four are mine—Duval, Legendre, Groulx, and the widow Marest. Three are not mine—Beaulieu, a name that belongs to no network I know; Fournier, possibly the Fournier who was denounced last month by his own section for hoarding, or possibly another Fournier, Paris is dense with Fourniers; and Clément, about whom I know nothing.

The four who are mine. They are connected to me by knowledge—they know me, or they know someone who knows me, or they know a dead drop that I service, or they know a phrase that I authored. The connections are:

Duval: recruited by me directly. Knows my face. Does not know my real name but knows the name I use in network operations, which is a third name, neither the name I was born with nor the name on my warrants. If Duval is interrogated—

But Duval is not being interrogated. Duval is being executed. There is no interrogation scheduled. The list is a list of executions, not a list of interrogations, which means the Committee has already determined guilt, or what it calls guilt, which is—

Stay with the arithmetic. If Duval is not interrogated, then Duval's knowledge dies with Duval. But if the execution is delayed, or if someone intervenes—

If I intervene. If I use my authority as the oversight inspector to alter the list. Remove a name. Claim an error. I have done this once before, in Germinal, for a man named Tissot who was placed on a list through a confusion of addresses, and the alteration went unquestioned because errors are frequent and the bureaucracy is vast and no one reviews the reviewed, but—

But that was one name, and the name was not connected to me, and the alteration was performed under the cover of genuine administrative correction. If I remove Duval's name, the correction must be justified. On what grounds? A claim of mistaken identity? Duval is Duval; there is no other Duval in the Conciergerie. A claim that Duval possesses intelligence valuable to the Committee? Possible. This is possible. I could argue—

But if I argue for Duval, I must explain why I know that Duval possesses valuable intelligence, and the only way I could know this is if I have interrogated Duval myself or if I possess prior knowledge of Duval's activities, and either explanation opens a line of inquiry that terminates at me.

All right. Not Duval. Legendre.

Legendre operates the laundry. If Legendre is removed from the list, Legendre returns to the Conciergerie's holding cells, where Legendre will be listed for a future batch. This gains days, perhaps a week. In a week, perhaps—perhaps what? Perhaps Robespierre falls. There are rumors. The Convention is restless. Tallien, Barras, Fouché—they are circling. But rumors have circled before, and Robespierre has not fallen, and a week is not—

And even if a week is enough, the act of removing Legendre's name draws attention to Legendre, and attention to Legendre draws attention to the laundry, and the laundry is still operational, and the three members of the network who are *not* on this list—Bercy, Morel, and the boy, the boy whose name I use only in my own mind, Jean-Claude, sixteen years old—the three who remain free use the laundry's dead drop. If the laundry is investigated because of my intervention for Legendre, the dead drop is discovered, and Bercy, Morel, and Jean-Claude are exposed.

Three lives for one.

Unless I can also relocate the dead drop. But relocating the dead drop requires contacting Bercy, and contacting Bercy requires the signal protocol, and the signal protocol requires forty-eight hours' notice, and I have—I have tonight. I have tonight and the hours before dawn.

Unless I contact Bercy tonight, which violates protocol, which means approaching Bercy without the signal, which means Bercy will not trust the approach, because the protocol exists precisely to prevent approaches that might be coerced, and for all Bercy knows, I have been arrested and turned and am approaching under duress—

Her hands. The widow's hands when they lifted the type from the case. The left hand faster than the right, selecting from the compartments with a precision that seemed less like memory than like gravity, the letters falling into her fingers as if the fingers were magnetized, and the ink—the ink was on her hands always, not just during typesetting but after, at the margins of her palms and in the creases of her knuckles, a permanent stain that she did not try to remove because, she said—

She said—

The arithmetic. Continue the arithmetic.

Groulx. If I remove Groulx's name. Groulx is the courier. Groulx's route connects the eastern contacts—two men in the Faubourg Saint-Antoine whose names I have never been told, a deliberate compartmentalization—to the network's center, which is the printing shop, which is the widow, which is—

Which is already on the list. If the widow is on the list, the printing shop is compromised. If the printing shop is compromised, the eastern contacts will receive no further communications regardless of whether Groulx survives to carry them. Saving Groulx saves a man, yes, a man with a limp and a seminary education and a way of quoting Augustine that makes Augustine sound like a sans-culotte, but saving Groulx does not save the network's function, because the function is already severed at the widow.

Unless I save the widow.

If I save the widow—

But this is the same calculation. The same branching tree. If I intervene for the widow, the intervention traces to me, because the widow is the one name on this list most directly connected to my operational role, and anyone investigating why an inspector sought to rescue a condemned printer would find the pamphlets, would find the press, would find the encoding technique that the widow developed—a system of deliberate misprints that conveyed information through their pattern, every third misprint marking a letter in a substitution cipher—and the cipher would be broken, and every pamphlet that passed through the network in the last eighteen months would become legible, and every name embedded in those pamphlets would become a name on a list, and the list would be longer than seven.

She said that ink does not wash off because it is not *on* the skin. It is *in* the skin. It enters through the pores. The lead in the type does the same. She said that a printer carries every word she has ever set in the tissue of her hands, and I asked if this frightened her, and—

Stop.

I refold the list.

The same fold. The same crease. I fold it along the lines that already exist, because the paper remembers where it has been folded before, and my hands remember, and I place it back inside my coat, against the left breast, and the calculation is complete, and the result is: nothing. The result is that every path from intervention to rescue passes through my exposure, and my exposure passes through the exposure of Bercy, Morel, and Jean-Claude, and the balance is four lives saved against three lives lost, except that the four lives saved are not actually saved, they are merely deferred, because a person removed from one list is placed on the next, and the next, and the machinery does not forget, and—

The result is nothing.

The candle has burned down by a third. The room is twelve paces by nine. The window faces east, and the dawn will come through that window, and when it comes I will dress and walk to the Conciergerie and read the names aloud and oversee the binding of the condemned and walk beside the tumbrel to the Place de la Révolution and watch the blade fall seven times, and I will do this because the arithmetic permits nothing else, and the arithmetic is correct, I have checked it, I have checked it forward and backward and sideways, and it is correct, and the ink—

The ink was in her hands. She said it does not wash off.

## III.

The printing shop on the Rue Saint-Jacques was three steps below the street, and the smell preceded the sight—a smell that was not one smell but several compressed into a single atmosphere: the metallic tang of the lead type in its cases, the vegetal thickness of the ink (linseed oil, lampblack, something resinous I never identified), the damp of the stone walls, and beneath all of this the faint sweetness of the paper itself, which she stored in wrapped bundles on wooden shelves and which absorbed the basement's moisture and gave it back as a scent like old bread.
