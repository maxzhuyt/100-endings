# The Last Watch of the Mara Celeste

I want to tell someone about Captain Wen. About the tea and the cards and the last three days. I am not sure telling is the right word. I am doing it anyway.

I was the cook. I had been the cook for eleven years. Before that I was a cook on the *Hua Lien* out of Kaohsiung and before that I was a cook in my mother's restaurant, which is to say I washed the woks and slept under the cutting table and learned the word for every part of a stove before I learned the word for "holiday." I say this not so you understand me. I say it so you understand that when I describe things I describe them the way I see them, which is from the galley, which is from below.

Captain Wen. I will start with the letters.

---

Seven months before the storm, the letters began arriving. I know this because I brought the mail aboard in Surabaya and I saw the envelopes. They were from a bank in Singapore. I do not remember which bank. The envelopes were white with a blue stripe along the edge, the blue of something trying to look trustworthy. The first few, Wen opened and read and put away in the chart table's second drawer, the one with the sticky runner. I know because I heard the drawer when I was bringing coffee up. The sticking sound, then the slam.

After two months the envelopes stopped going into the drawer. They stayed on the chart table. I saw them when I brought coffee or when I cleared plates Wen forgot she'd left there. They gathered at the table's edge the way silt gathers at a river bend. One had a coffee ring on it the exact diameter of Wen's mug, which was the tin mug with the dented rim she would not let me replace. Another had been used to mark a page in the tide tables. I glimpsed a word on one of them once. The word was "forfeiture." I did not know what it meant at the time. I understood its weight from its sound, which is the sound of a door shutting in a room you have already left.

After four months Wen stopped opening them at all. They accumulated under dividers and parallel rules and the corner of the barometer that sat on the chart table because its bulkhead mount had cracked. Unopened. I could see this because the flaps were still sealed, still showing that blue stripe. I did not mention them. It was not my place.

In Colombo I heard Wen on the satellite phone. I was not listening. The galley vent feeds into the same trunk as the bridge vent and I cannot close my ears. She said very little. The other voice did most of the talking. When she hung up she did not come down for dinner. I left a plate outside the bridge door. In the morning the plate was where I'd left it, and the rice had dried to a skin, and I took it back down and scraped it into the bin and said nothing.

Men from the insurance company were waiting in Bermuda. I know this because the first mate told the engineer and the engineer told me while I was cutting onions. He said they would take possession of the ship. He used that word, "possession," like it was a straightforward thing, like a ship was a thing that could be picked up and held in a hand.

Bermuda was three weeks away. Then the course changed and it was four. Then the storm came and Bermuda stopped mattering, or maybe it was the only thing that mattered. I am not sure. I am not sure about most of it.

---

The *Mara Celeste* was built in 1972 in a yard outside Keelung. Wen's grandfather commissioned her. I do not know the details of this because Wen did not give details about her family the way a person opens a drawer and shows you its contents. You got a handle here, a hinge there. Eleven years of galley proximity and I assembled perhaps a box.

The wheel was original. Teak. It had eight spokes and I once counted the grooves worn into the handles and there were none because the grooves had been polished out by hands. Two generations of the same hands, I suppose, because Wen's fingers were long in the same way her grandfather's must have been long—the wear pattern in the wood was smooth and deep in the places you'd expect from a long-fingered grip. I touched the wheel once, when I was cleaning the bridge, and the wood was warm the way skin is warm. I pulled my hand back. It felt like touching someone's shoulder when they didn't know you were there.

Wen told me one thing directly. It was late, we were in the Celebes Sea, and I had brought tea and she was looking at a chart but not seeing it. She said that when she was a girl her grandfather sawed a berth to fit her. He measured her with a piece of rope—shoulder to sole—and then he cut the boards to that length exactly. She said she slept in that berth whenever she was aboard, which was whenever she could. She said she had never slept as well since.

She said this and then she picked up her pencil and went back to the chart and I went back down to the galley. She did not mention it again. Neither did I. Some things are said once and then they exist in the air between two people like the smell of a dish you cooked last week—gone if you try to find it, present if you stop trying.

I think about that berth. I think about a man measuring a child with a piece of rope. The care of it. The precision. A space made for exactly one body. Not a bunk that anyone could sleep in but a place that said: you, this size, this shape, here.

The bank letters said: not yours. The berth said: only yours.

I do not know how a person reconciles those two things. I think perhaps you cannot and the failure to reconcile is what happened next.

---

The typhoon arrived on a Wednesday. I know it was a Wednesday because I had written "Wed" on the menu board in the galley, beside "congee, pickled radish, egg." I did not alter the menu board for the remaining three days, and when I last saw it the word "Wed" was still there, and the congee had long since gone cold in its pot, and the pickled radish was on the floor in brine and broken glass.

I will not tell you about the wind the way you expect. I did not stand on the deck and feel it. I was below in the galley and what I felt was the cans leaving the shelves. Not falling—leaving, as if they had decided to go. One moment a row of tinned tomatoes, the next moment the air full of rolling metal and the sound of dented aluminum on steel deck plate. The stove was gimbaled, designed to swing with the ship's motion. It swung past its stops. I heard the pin shear and the stove slammed to port and then to starboard and the stockpot I'd been heating went across the galley in a sheet. I caught the stove with my hip and held it. I do not know for how long. My hip was purple for two weeks, or what I think was two weeks.

I made tea at 0600. This is what I did every morning. I boiled water in the kettle that was clamped to the stove rail and I measured loose leaves into Wen's tin mug—the dented one—and I poured the water and let it steep four minutes. Wen drank it black and strong enough to make your eyes sting. I carried it up. The ladder was a negotiation. The ship rolled and I held the mug in one hand and the rail in the other and the tea slopped over the rim and burned my knuckles and I did not spill it.

Wen was at the wheel. She was wearing her gray jacket, the one with the fraying cuffs she would not let me mend. Her hands were on the spokes at ten and two. She was looking ahead at something I could not see, and I understood that what she was looking at was a wave or a series of waves or the absence of something she expected, and that her looking was a kind of reading I would never be able to do. She was reading the water the way I read a broth—by what moves beneath the surface.

I set the cup in the holder beside the wheel.

She did not look at it.

I went back down.

At 1400 I came up again because I had made soup, because that is what I do, I make things. The tea was still in the holder. It had gone the color of rust. Cold. The surface shivered each time the hull took a wave. A tiny sea inside a tin cup.

I took the cup and poured the tea over the side and rinsed it and brought fresh tea and put it in the same holder. Wen's hands had not moved from ten and two. I do not think she had moved at all in eight hours except to turn the wheel.

She said, "Thank you, cook."

She did not drink the fresh tea either.

I went back down.

---

By the second morning the *Mara Celeste* was taking water in the forward hold and the list was noticeable from the galley—my cutting board slid to port if I did not wedge it, and the water in the sink no longer drained evenly. The sound was different too. A ship in a storm makes a specific complaint, a vocabulary of creaks and groans I had spent eleven years learning, and now there were words in it I had not heard before. Lower sounds. Longer.

Wen gathered the crew on the bridge at 0700. I was not crew, or I was crew in the same way the stove was—functional, permanent, no rank. I stood in the doorway. She told them she was ordering abandon ship. She said it without pause or qualification. She said the life raft was serviced in March and the EPIRB was functional and the sea state, while severe, was survivable in an inflatable if they deployed on the lee side and kept the drogue out.

The first mate asked if she was coming. She said no. He did not ask why. I think he already knew. I think he had seen the letters too.

They went. The first mate, who was from Mindanao and had a wife and a son whose photo he kept taped inside his locker door. The engineer, who was older than Wen by ten years and had gray in his chest hair and a way of whistling through his teeth when the engines were running well. The deckhand whose name I am ashamed to say I cannot recall with certainty—Hsu, or Hsü, a quiet man who ate everything I cooked without comment, which in my profession is the highest praise. And the boy, whose name was Jia-hao and who had been aboard two months and was not yet able to eat in heavy weather without turning the color of uncooked dough.

The life raft deployed with a hiss and a sudden inflation that sounded like a gasp, a single enormous inhalation. It was orange. I watched from the hatch as it hit the water and the orange was wrong against that sea. The sea was not blue. Not gray. It was the color of something that has no interest in being described—the color of deep water in bad light, which is the color of the inside of a thing. And the raft's orange sat on it like a shout in a cathedral.

The first mate looked back. The deckhand, Hsu or Hsü, looked back. The engineer did not. The boy was already being sick over the raft's inflated gunwale.

I watched the orange shrink. The swells put it below the horizon line, then raised it, then swallowed it again, and each time it reappeared it was smaller, and then it did not reappear, or I could no longer distinguish it from the chaos.

I went below and washed the mugs they'd used at breakfast. There were four mugs. I washed each one and dried it and put it in the rack and closed the rack's retaining bar so they would not slide out.

Then I filled the kettle.

---

I do not know what time I went up to the chart room. The clock on the galley bulkhead had shaken loose and fallen and the glass face had broken and the hands were stopped at a time that was no longer the time. The light coming through the hatch was the dim amber of late day or heavy cloud or both. I climbed up because I had made rice and fried an egg and put them on a plate with pickled radish from the last unbroken jar, and I carried this plate the way I had carried every plate for eleven years: with my thumb on the rim and my fingers beneath, balanced against the roll.

Wen was in the chart room. She was sitting at the small table that folded down from the bulkhead. The cards were dealt.

Two hands, facedown, seven cards each. The remainder of the deck squared in the center with the top card turned over. A three of diamonds.

She had dealt before I arrived. She had laid out my hand at my seat, which was the seat with the rag stuffed under the short leg. She had expected me.

I set the plate beside her cards. I sat down. The ship heeled and the plate slid and I caught it and moved it to the shelf behind her.

"Eat first," I said.

"After," she said.

We played. Our game was a two-handed variant that I believe is called Cassino in English, though we did not call it that. We called it nothing. It was just the game. The rules involved building and capturing and I will not describe them because they do not matter. What matters is the sequence: the deal, the play, the counting of cards taken, the marking of score on the pad Wen kept in the chart table's top drawer, the one that didn't stick.

The hull groaned. It was a sound like a large animal trying to stand on a broken leg. I had not heard the ship make that sound before and I heard it in my sternum, not my ears.

A wave buried the starboard rail. I know because the porthole in the chart room went green—solid green, the green of water not the green of glass—and stayed green for three seconds, four, five, and then drained away and was gray again.

Wen laid down a queen of spades. She was building queens.

I looked at my hand. I could not remember what trumps were. There are no trumps in our game. I knew this. I had known it for years. But in that moment the rules slid away from me the way the cans had slid from the shelves—simply left, as if they had decided that the game no longer applied, that the weather had revoked the agreement between a card and its meaning.

I played a four. I do not remember if it was correct.

We finished the hand. Wen captured more cards. She won. She almost always won. I had never counted but I suspect the ratio was seven to one, perhaps higher. She did not win because I let her. She won because she saw the table the way she saw the water—as a text, as a pattern of pressures and absences, readable if you knew the language.

I did not mind losing. I had never minded. The losing was part of it, the way the flour is part of the bread. You do not resent the flour.

She picked up the egg and ate it in two bites, cold by now, the yolk set to a pale rubber. She ate the rice. She left the radish. She always left the radish. Eleven years and she always left the radish and I always put it on the plate.

"Tomorrow morning," she said.

She meant the game.

"Yes," I said.

I took the plate and went below.

---

The pumps failed sometime in the night, or what I understood as night. I was in my berth, not sleeping—the motion was beyond sleep, was a violence that made sleep a theoretical concept, like justice or dryness—when the sound changed.

A ship that is being pumped has a rhythm in its lower registers. A pulse. You stop hearing it the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat, but it is there, and it is the sound of the ship swallowing—taking in water and expelling it, taking in and expelling, the bronchial rhythm of a vessel keeping itself alive.

The sound stopped. Not gradually. It stopped the way a throat stops swallowing when the swallowing is no longer possible. One moment the pulse, then silence, then only the storm and the hull's groaning and the water—I could hear it now—the water moving in the hold below me with a new liberty, an exploration, filling spaces it had not been permitted to fill.

I got up. I put on my shoes, which were the rubber clogs I wore in the galley, because I had no others. I climbed up.

Wen was on the deck.

I saw her through the hatch. She had clipped her harness to the jackline and she was moving forward, toward the mast—not the wheel, the mast—in a sea state that I will describe only by saying that every fourth wave put the foredeck under. Not spray. Under. The deck became seafloor and then re-emerged, streaming, and Wen was on it.
