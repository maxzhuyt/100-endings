# Form 27-B: Application for Continued Existence

## I.

The filing room had once been Cargo Bay 7, back when the *Lethe* carried cargo instead of the surviving administrative record of an entire species. Seventeen years into the voyage, the bay held no trace of its original purpose. There were no crates, no loading mechanisms, no magnetic tie-downs. There were desks. There were 214,000 forms arranged in filing cabinets built by hand from decommissioned bulkhead panels. There were eleven hard drives containing the Auditor Procedural Index, a document whose table of contents alone ran to four hundred pages and whose appendices referenced appendices that referenced sub-appendices that referenced errata sheets correcting earlier appendices. And there was Pell.

Pell was twenty-three. She had been six when the *Lethe* launched, which meant she had no memory of Earth that wasn't also a memory of leaving it, and no context for what a "cargo bay" was supposed to feel like. To her it had always been this: a cold, bright room where people sat in rows and copied documents onto regulation-weight paper — 90 gsm, uncoated, acid-free, no watermarks — because the Auditors did not accept digital submissions. The Auditors did not accept laser-printed submissions. The Auditors did not accept ink that contained pigments derived from synthetic polymers. The Auditors accepted forms written or typed on paper of regulation weight, in inks derived from carbon or mineral sources, filed in triplicate, with original signatures on all three copies, and submitted through one of eleven billion intake beacons distributed throughout the local galactic volume. The *Lethe* carried Beacon 7,304,662,118. It was bolted to the outer hull near the port waste-reclamation exhaust, because that was the only compatible mounting point, and every time someone flushed a toilet the beacon hummed at a frequency that made the dentistry lab's equipment recalibrate.

Pell was not thinking about toilets. Pell was staring at the classification code on Form 1, the foundational document, the one that every subsequent form referenced, the one that 214,000 pieces of regulation-weight paper depended on like a family tree depends on its root ancestor not being a clerical ghost.

The code read 9-F.

She checked it against the Auditor Procedural Index. Classification 9-F: "Voluntary Dissolution of a Migratory Fauna." It covered species that were not sapient, not sessile, and not filing on their own behalf — species represented by a host planet or a patron civilization, species that had decided, through whatever process non-sapient migratory fauna used to decide things, that they would prefer to be dissolved. Archived. Processed. The word the Auditors used was "concluded."

Classification 27-B: "Application for Continued Existence of a Sapient Species." That was the one they needed. That was the one Captain Okafor-Reyes had announced they were filing, seventeen years ago, standing in this same cargo bay before it had desks, holding his infant daughter on his hip because his wife had been dead for six hours and there was no one else to hold her.

Pell checked the code again.

She checked it a third time.

She pulled up the cross-reference table. Classification 9-F and Classification 27-B were not adjacent in the index; they were not easy to confuse. 9-F was in Volume 3. 27-B was in Volume 11. They shared no subsection headers, no overlapping appendices, no common errata. Someone, seventeen years ago, had simply written the wrong number. Perhaps they had been exhausted. Perhaps they had been grief-stricken. Perhaps they had been holding a baby.

And then 214,000 forms had been copied, in triplicate, by hand, onto regulation-weight paper, by a rotating staff of volunteers who worked six-hour shifts in a converted cargo bay, and every single one of those forms had referenced Form 1, and every single one of them had carried its classification code forward like a gene, and every single one of them had been transmitted through Beacon 7,304,662,118 (which hummed when you flushed the toilet) to the Auditor processing center, where — and Pell did not know this, could not know it, but the record should note it — they sat in a subsurface pneumatic bin labeled AQUATIC APPLICANTS: PENDING REVIEW (OVERFLOW), because the queue for voluntary fauna dissolution was seventeen trillion entries long and not a priority for anyone.

Pell stood up.

She walked to the Captain's quarters. It was 0300. The corridor lights were at their nighttime output — ten percent, blue-shifted, meant to simulate a dusk that no one aboard younger than twenty had ever seen. She could hear the ship's recyclers humming. She could hear, faintly, the vibration of the beacon on the hull. She could hear her own breathing, which was faster than she wanted it to be.

She pressed the intercom on the Captain's door. It took him a long time to answer, and when he did, his voice had the flat, mechanical quality of a man who has learned to wake without confusion because confusion is a luxury available only to people whose species is not pending review.

"Pell?"

She held up the sheet of paper, though he couldn't see it through the intercom. Force of habit. Everything on the *Lethe* was documented; you held things up.

"Captain," she said. "We've been requesting permission to be fish."

## II.

The central atrium of the *Lethe* had been designed for six hundred people. This was optimistic. The ship carried three thousand, one hundred and twelve souls — three thousand, one hundred and fourteen, if you counted the Okeke twins, who had been born during the assembly and whose mother, Lieutenant Adaeze Okeke, had declined to leave her position in the crowd on the grounds that she had waited nine months for a status update on humanity's existential paperwork and she was not going to miss this one for something as routine as labor.

(The twins were delivered by a pediatric nurse named Yash, who was standing next to Lieutenant Okeke and who had not delivered a baby since residency, twenty-one years and one apocalypse ago. Both infants were healthy. They were named Emeka and Chiamaka, and they would grow up to be, respectively, an atmospheric chemist and the *Lethe*'s second-best clarinet player. But that is later.)

Captain Okafor-Reyes stood on the raised platform at the atrium's north end — a stage, technically, though no one had staged anything on it since the Year Three talent show, which had ended in a fistfight over whether "Danny Boy" was a legitimate expression of cultural heritage or a war crime against morale. He gripped the railing with both hands. He was fifty-four. His hair had gone grey at the temples in Year One and had spent the subsequent sixteen years completing the job with the methodical patience of a system that, once initiated, sees no reason to stop. He had the bearing of a man who has held three thousand people together through radiation storms, a failed hydroponics bay (Year Nine — two months of emergency rations, four suicides, one murder trial conducted under maritime law because no one could agree on which other law applied), and a near-mutiny in Year Six led by a faction that believed the Auditors were a collective hallucination and the real enemy was the navigation committee. He had held them together through all of this by maintaining, with the unwavering discipline of a man who understands that hope is a structural material and not a feeling, the fiction that the paperwork was working.

He told them.

He told them plainly, because the *Lethe*'s crew had been lied to by an indifferent universe for seventeen years and did not need to be lied to by their captain on top of it. He said: the foundational form was filed under the wrong classification code. He said: every subsequent form inherited the error. He said: for seventeen years, the Auditors' system had categorized them as a non-sapient migratory aquatic species requesting voluntary dissolution. He said: in effect, the human race had been发 asking for permission to die, phrased as if they were fish.

The silence lasted four seconds. Then someone in the back laughed. Then someone else. Then it moved through the crowd the way weather moves across an open plain — not all at once, but in bands, catching different people at different moments, some bending forward with it, some pressing their hands over their mouths, some laughing with their eyes closed and their heads tipped back as if receiving a blow from above. It was not happy laughter. It was the laughter of people who had survived the Processing of Earth — the six-hour bureaucratic event during which every mountain, ocean, city, and living thing on the planet had been "archived" with the cheerful efficiency of a clerk clearing a desk at the end of a workday — and who had spent seventeen years in a converted cargo bay copying forms by hand and who had just learned that all of it, every sheet of regulation-weight paper, every cramped hand, every night shift, every careful signature in mineral-based ink, had been an application to be concluded as a species of fish.

It was the laughter of people for whom absurdity and grief had become the same substance, the way water and ice are the same substance at different temperatures.

From the middle of the crowd, a hand went up.

It belonged to a woman standing between a structural engineer and a ten-year-old boy who was using her left hip as a leaning post. She was fifty-nine. She was not tall. Her hair was cut short and practical or, more precisely, cut short because she had been cutting it herself for seventeen years and did not regard hair as a project that warranted collaboration. She wore the standard-issue shipboard jumpsuit, which she had altered at the cuffs because the standard issue was designed for people who did not spend a significant portion of their day writing and the cuffs caught on things. There was a pen behind her left ear. There was always a pen behind her left ear. Some people carry pens as tools. Gloria Chen carried a pen the way a surgeon carries a scalpel — not as an accessory but as an expression of professional identity so fundamental that its absence would constitute a kind of undressing.

She had worked for thirty-one years at the Riverside County Department of Motor Vehicles, Branch 34, which was the branch on University Avenue with the water stain on the ceiling tile above Window 6 and the vending machine in the lobby that accepted dollar bills only if you fed them in face-down and slightly crumpled. She had processed driver's license renewals, vehicle registrations, identification cards, title transfers, disability placards, and, in one memorable instance, a formal complaint from a man who believed his vanity plate ("GODBLS") had been rejected on religious grounds when in fact it had been rejected because it was already assigned to a 2004 Honda Civic in Bakersfield. She had done this work without irony, without apology, and without any of the performative misery that popular culture assigned to DMV employees as a kind of class uniform. She had done it because she understood that the systems people complained about were also the systems people depended on, and that the difference between a person who could drive legally and a person who could not was often one form, filled out correctly, submitted to the right window.

She had not, in seventeen years aboard the *Lethe*, mentioned this background to anyone except during the initial skills census in Year One, where she had listed her occupation as "civil service, administrative" and her relevant skills as "document processing, regulatory compliance, queue management, conflict de-escalation." She had been assigned to the filing room. She had worked there for seventeen years without complaint. She had not filed the original Form 1. She had checked.

Her hand was up.

Captain Okafor-Reyes pointed to her. "Chen."

"Who issued the original classification guide?" she asked. Her voice carried. It was a voice that had been trained, over three decades, to project across a waiting room full of unhappy people without rising to a shout — a specific, professional volume, the volume of a person who needs to be heard by the person at the back of the room and understood by the person standing directly in front of her. "Is there an amendment cycle? And is there an appeals window or is it a grievance window, because those are different."

No one answered.

No one could answer. The Auditor Procedural Index was 4.8 million pages long. It was stored across eleven hard drives because no single hard drive aboard the *Lethe* had sufficient capacity. Its search function had been built by a volunteer programmer in Year Two and crashed if you used more than three Boolean operators in a single query. The people who worked in the filing room had mastered the sections relevant to their daily work — form formatting, submission protocols, ink requirements — the way a person living on the coast masters the tides without understanding orbital mechanics. No one had read the whole thing. It was not clear that any human being *could* read the whole thing while remaining a functional human being who also slept and ate and maintained relationships with other humans.

Gloria lowered her hand.

"I need to see the index," she said.

She stepped forward. The crowd parted for her. Not dramatically — not like the Red Sea, not like a scene in a film — but in the shuffling, awkward, ordinary way that a crowd of three thousand people in a space designed for six hundred makes room for a person who is moving with visible purpose. People shifted their weight. They turned sideways. A few pressed back against the people behind them. The ten-year-old boy who had been leaning on her hip found a new wall to lean on. Lieutenant Okeke, mid-contraction, stepped to the left.

No one voted. No one appointed her. No one said *let her through* or *she knows what she's doing.* But the crowd parted, and Gloria walked through it, and Captain Okafor-Reyes watched her approach the stage with the expression of a man who has been drowning for seventeen years and has just seen, not a lifeboat, but someone who appears to know how to fill out the requisition form for one.

This was her weather.

## III.

Beacon 7,304,662,118 could do more than transmit forms. It could, when activated in a specific sequence detailed on page 2,341,007 of the Auditor Procedural Index, open a transit corridor to the nearest Auditor intake facility. The sequence involved pressing the beacon's manual override button — a recessed square on its underside, accessible only during extravehicular operations — fourteen times in a pattern that Gloria described after three days of research as "a waltz in 7/8 time, if the waltz hated you." Chief Engineer Petrosyan performed the activation during a scheduled hull inspection, pressing the button with her gloved thumb while tethered to the ship's exterior at a velocity of 0.04c relative to nothing in particular, because the *Lethe* was in deep interstellar space and there was nothing nearby to be relative to.

The transit corridor appeared as a seam in the vacuum — not a hole, not a tunnel, but a visible *crease*, as if someone had folded space along a line and not bothered to iron it flat. It shimmered with a color that the human eye interpreted as grey-green but that was, according to the ship's spectrometer, no color at all — a wavelength that did not exist in the electromagnetic spectrum and that the spectrometer reported as an error code before shutting itself off in what Chief Engineer Petrosyan described as "a mechanical sulk."

The delegation was five people: Gloria, Captain Okafor-Reyes, Pell (because she had found the error and Gloria said it was important that the person who identified a problem be present for its resolution, a principle she had upheld at the DMV and saw no reason to abandon at the heat death of human civilization), Dr. Nneka Abara (the ship's chief medical officer, on the theory that someone should be able to certify that any documents the Auditors asked them to produce were being signed by living beings and not, for instance, fish), and a structural engineer named Tomás Ferreira-Vasquez, who had volunteered because his daughter had asked him to bring back a picture of what the place looked like and he could not think of a good reason to say no.

They walked through the crease.

The intake facility was a room.

It was beige. Not a warm beige, not an intentional beige, not the beige of a designer who had considered beige among the available options and selected it. It was the beige of absence — the beige that happens when no one has made a decision about color and the universe, left to its own devices, defaults to the path of least aesthetic commitment. The walls were beige.
