# The Thirty-Seven Letters of Monk Hideo, As Compiled by His Opponent

---

## Compiler's Preface

My name is Yamamoto Kenji. For most of my public life, I held the title of champion of the Tōhoku Regional Kendo Federation. I won this title at the age of twenty-six, in November of 1981, at the Sendai Civic Gymnasium, in a final-round match against Kuroda Hideo, who had been undefeated in sanctioned competition for three consecutive years. I held the title for nine more years before retiring from competitive fighting to teach full-time at my dōjō in Morioka. I have trained over four hundred students. Eleven have gone on to achieve fifth dan or higher. I state these facts not out of vanity but because they constitute the public record that gives this document its context. The reader is entitled to know who is speaking.

What the public record does not contain is the following: in March of 1982, four months after the championship match, I received a letter from Kuroda Hideo, now residing at a Rinzai Zen monastery in the mountains north of Yamagata. This was the first of thirty-seven letters, arriving at irregular intervals over a period of thirty-nine years. The last letter arrived in January of 2021. At the time of writing, I have received no thirty-eighth.

I never wrote back. This is not entirely true, and I will address the qualification later, in its proper place.

I am publishing these letters now for reasons I will attempt to state plainly. I am seventy years old. In September I was diagnosed with pancreatic cancer, which has since metastasized. My doctors speak of months in a tone that suggests they are being generous. I have no children to whom these letters might be entrusted, and my wife, Haruko, who tolerated their presence in our home for thirty-four years of marriage, has asked me not to burden her with decisions about them after I am gone. She said this kindly. She has said most things kindly for three decades, and I have not always deserved it.

I have organized the letters chronologically. Where dates were not included by the author, I have estimated based on postmarks, references to seasonal events, and the condition of the paper—the monastery, I learned over many years, used a heavier stock in winter when deliveries from their regular supplier in Yamagata were delayed by snow. I have noted the physical state of each letter where it seems relevant: the quality of the brushwork, evidence of revision, damage in transit or storage. In several cases I have added brief annotations for the purpose of context or correction. I have tried to keep these annotations factual. Where I have not succeeded, I trust the reader will understand that even a compiler is, in the end, a man with hands that are not always steady.

I do not know if Hideo is still alive. The monastery has no telephone, and my last inquiry by mail, sent in February of this year, has not been answered. It is possible he has died. It is possible he is sitting in that mountain hall at this very moment, legs folded beneath him, attending to his breathing with the same terrifying precision he once brought to a sword. I find I cannot tell which possibility disturbs me more.

I am not the hero of this document. I am its mechanism of delivery. If in the process of compiling I have occasionally confused the two roles, I ask the reader to consider that I have had forty years to make this mistake and only a few months to correct it.

— Yamamoto Kenji
Morioka, June 2021

## Letter 1

*Received March 1982. Standard monastery stationery, hand-milled. Brushwork formal, unhurried. No envelope — folded and sealed with plain wax.*

Yamamoto-san,

I hope this finds you in good health and that the winter has not been too severe in Morioka. I am told the north wind comes differently there than in Sendai, though I confess my experience of northern winters has been limited until now.

I write from a small room overlooking a courtyard where, this morning, the monks cleared the last of the snow from around the base of a very old pine. The tree has a name, though I have not yet learned it. I am new here and am given largely to observation. This suits me. The abbot, a man of considerable age and very few words, assigned me duties in the kitchen, where I am proving to be an indifferent cook but an excellent washer of radishes. There is no dishonor in this. A radish, properly washed, is a thing of clarity.

I trust that the championship has brought you the attention it deserves. You are a fine swordsman — this I have always known — and I hope the recognition sits well on you. I imagine your dōjō will grow. You have a gift for teaching that I, who could never explain what I did or why, have always envied.

I do not know yet what my life here will become. There is a schedule — zazen at four in the morning, work, study, more zazen — and within that schedule there is a kind of freedom I did not expect. The mind, stripped of opponents, turns to face itself. This is not always comfortable. But I suspect you know something of this already. A man who has fought as long as you have understands the silence after a match. This is that silence, extended.

I ask nothing in writing this. If a reply would be burdensome, please do not trouble yourself. I mean only to say that I remember you.

Kuroda Hideo

*Compiler's note: The championship match lasted approximately forty seconds. This is a matter of public record, confirmed by the official timekeeper's log, which I consulted in preparing this document. Hideo took his opening stance. I took mine. He studied me for what the timekeeper recorded as eleven seconds — though I would have said it was longer — and then stepped back, placed his shinai on the floor, bowed, and left the competition area. The chief judge consulted with the panel and awarded me the match by forfeit. I remember that the gymnasium was very quiet, except for one woman somewhere in the upper seats who began to clap and then stopped. The floor was cedar.*

## Letter 2

*Received June 1982. Same stationery. Brushwork slightly less formal, with a visible correction in the third line where a character was written and then struck through — unusual for Hideo, whose calligraphy was, even at this early stage, notably deliberate.*

Yamamoto-san,

Summer here arrives abruptly, as though the mountain has been persuaded against its will. The courtyard pine — its name, I have learned, is Ganki, after a monk who planted it two hundred years ago and about whom no one remembers anything else — is dense with new growth. I spend my mornings now in the garden rather than the kitchen. The abbot says my radish-washing has reached its natural limit and that the soil may benefit from my attention. I believe this is a polite way of saying I am not to be trusted with food.

I have been reading the *Heihō Kadensho* again, which I know you also studied as a younger man. Yagyū Munenori writes that the sword that kills is also the sword that gives life. I used to think I understood this. I am less certain now. Perhaps understanding is itself a kind of stance — one takes it up, holds it, and then one day discovers the weight has shifted and what felt like certainty is only habit.

Has the Tōhoku Federation announced the schedule for next season? I confess I still follow these things, the way a man who has left a city still reads its weather.

Hideo

*Compiler's note: The struck-through character in the third line appears to have been the first stroke of 体, "body," though this is difficult to confirm. It was replaced with 心, "mind" or "heart." The sentence, as completed, reads: "The heart adjusts slowly to new rhythms."*

## Letters 3 through 5

*Received between October 1982 and April 1983. I present these in summary, as they concern themselves primarily with the monastery's garden (the failure of a squash crop, a dispute among the monks about the correct pruning of azaleas), Hideo's developing meditation practice, and his reading — during this period he appears to have worked through the entirety of the Muromachi-era martial treatises and to have begun, somewhat incongruously, a study of Dutch botanical illustration. The brushwork improves steadily across these letters. By Letter 5, his calligraphy has achieved the quality it would maintain for the next two decades: clean, balanced, with a controlled energy in the downstrokes that any student of swordsmanship would recognize as the product of trained wrists.*

*In Letter 4, he asks whether I have changed my grip. I had not mentioned any change. He writes: "I have been thinking about the way the right hand tends over time to assert dominance over the left, and how this manifests at the top of the swing. It is a common evolution in fighters who favor men-strikes, and I wonder whether you have noticed it in yourself." The observation was correct. I had adjusted my grip that autumn at the suggestion of a visiting instructor. I did not write back.*

## Letter 6

*Received August 1983. A shorter letter, notable primarily for the first appearance of what would become a recurring motif.*

Yamamoto-san,

The heat is extraordinary. Even the monks who have lived here for decades say this summer is unusual. We sit in zazen and the sweat runs and we are instructed not to attend to it. There is a lesson in this, though I suspect the lesson is mostly about sweat.

I have been thinking about what a fighter owes his opponent. Not in the formal sense — the bow, the respect, the adherence to rules — but in the deeper sense, the sense that has no name in the manuals. Two men face each other. Each has studied for years. Each brings to the encounter everything he is: his training, his body, his fear, his desire. For a moment, they are more intimate than lovers, because a lover sees you only when you show yourself, but an opponent sees you when you are trying not to be seen. What, then, is owed? What does the one who sees owe to the one who is seen?

I do not have an answer. I present only the question, as one might place a stone in a garden — not because it resolves the landscape but because the landscape requires something heavy in that spot.

The pine is well. I am well. I hope you are well.

Hideo

*Compiler's note: No annotation necessary.*

## Letter 7

*Received January 1984. Heavy winter paper stock, as predicted.*

Yamamoto-san,

Snow. For days now, nothing but snow. The monastery has contracted to its smallest self — the main hall, the kitchen, the narrow path that the youngest monks shovel between the two. From my window the courtyard is a single white field interrupted only by Ganki, who wears the snow with the dignity of a very old man wearing a hat he did not choose.

I have been rereading my own earlier letters to you — I keep copies, a vanity perhaps, but I was trained as a scholar before I was trained as a fighter, and scholars are constitutionally unable to let words leave them without keeping a duplicate — and I notice something. I notice that I keep circling. Each letter begins with a season, moves to some manageable topic (the garden, a text), and then arrives, as though by accident, at a question about the nature of combat that is not, I suspect, as abstract as I pretend.

I will try to be more direct, though directness has never been my best technique. You will remember — or perhaps you will not, since your experience of that day is not mine — that in our match I had considerable time to study you before the engagement began. Eleven seconds, the record says. I am told this is what the record says. In my memory, it was much longer. In my memory, there was time enough to see a great deal.

But I am already circling again. Forgive me. Let me instead tell you about a text I have found in the monastery library, a Ming Dynasty treatise on the observation of structural weaknesses in fortress architecture, which I think you would find —

*[The letter continues for another full page on the subject of Ming Dynasty fortification analysis. I have omitted this portion, not because it lacks interest — it is, in fact, a lucid and original reading of the text in question — but because what matters in this letter is the paragraph above, and the reader should be allowed to sit with it as I have sat with it for thirty-seven years.]*

## Letter 8

*Received May 1984. A brief letter, the shortest in the collection. Reproduced in full.*

Yamamoto-san,

Spring. The plum tree by the south wall has blossomed early. The monks say this means a hot summer. The monks say many things about what weather means. I think weather means only itself.

I dreamt of the gymnasium last night. In the dream, the floor was cypress.

Hideo

*Compiler's note: It was cedar.*

## Letter 9

*Received November 1984. Standard paper. The brushwork in this letter is exceptional — each character placed with the kind of precision that suggests either great calm or great effort to simulate calm. I have studied this letter longer than any other. I have read it perhaps a hundred times. For many years it sat in the top drawer of my desk, unfolded, so that I would see it each morning when I reached for a pen. My wife asked me once what it said. I told her it was a technical essay. This was not a lie.*

Yamamoto-san,

I have been studying the classical stances again. Not practicing them — my body has lost its interest in such things, though I suspect the knowledge remains in the joints the way a word remains on the tongue even when one has decided not to speak it — but studying them as architecture. Each stance is a structure. Each structure has properties: load-bearing lines, points of equilibrium, vectors of intended force. And each structure, because it is assumed by a human body and not built from stone, contains the particular history of the body that assumes it.

I want to describe something I have come to understand about jōdan-no-kamae, the high guard, as it is typically deployed by right-handed fighters of above-average height who have trained primarily in the Hokushin Ittō-ryū tradition. I frame this generally. I am speaking of a type, not an individual.

In the classical high guard, the weight distribution at the moment of initiation favors the left foot, which serves as the pivot. The right foot, forward, accepts the commitment of the downward strike. Between these two moments — the pivot and the commitment — there is a transfer. It is brief. In a trained fighter, it is nearly instantaneous. But "nearly" is not "entirely," and in that fractional interval, the body passes through a state that is neither the old stance nor the new one. It is a kind of architectural zero. The structure, for an instant, is not a structure at all. It is a collection of limbs in transit.

Now: in most fighters, this interval is symmetrically vulnerable — the opening, such as it is, exists equally on both sides, and because it is equal, it is effectively nil. An attacker would have to choose a side, and the choice itself would consume the interval. The window closes before the hand can enter it.

But in certain fighters — those whose left knee has sustained particular kinds of stress over long training, or whose hip articulation carries a slight asymmetry that is invisible in static posture but becomes, in the moment of transfer, a measurable deviation from the center line — the interval is not symmetrical. The opening on the right side lasts perhaps a tenth of a second longer than the opening on the left. This is because the left leg, which is bearing the full weight of the pivot, compensates for its reduced lateral stability by extending the transfer phase — the body is, in effect, giving itself extra time to find its balance, but in doing so, it prolongs the window on the opposite side.

A tenth of a second is nothing. In ordinary combat, it is less than nothing. No fighter could identify it in real time, react to it, and execute a strike through it in the course of a single exchange.

Unless.

Unless the observer has spent years watching this particular body. Unless the observer has catalogued every transition, every weight shift, every micro-adjustment that the fighter himself does not know he makes. Unless the observer has built, in the architecture of his own mind, a model so complete that he can predict the exact moment the transfer will begin, the exact duration of the asymmetry, the exact line through which a strike — a perfectly timed tsuki, a thrust to the exposed right side of the throat — could pass without obstruction
