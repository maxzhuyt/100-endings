# The Last Watch of the *Mara Celeste*

---

I was the cook on the *Mara Celeste* for eleven years, and I am going to tell you about Captain Wen.

She drank Zhenghe jasmine, the kind that comes in the tin with the crane on it, not the one with the mountain. She'd had the same supplier in Fuzhou since before I came aboard, a man named Lao Pan who mailed the tins to whatever port we were headed for, and twice when the shipment was late she drank nothing at all rather than substitute. Her eggs she took poached, soft, in a bowl with no toast, and she'd break the yolk with the back of her spoon and eat the white first, always the white first, every morning I knew her.

The typhoon was called Meihua. It hit us forty-one nautical miles southeast of Bermuda on a Thursday.

But I was telling you about the tea.

---

The *Mara Celeste* was a forty-six-foot steel ketch, built in 1971 in a yard outside Kaohsiung that doesn't exist anymore. Her grandfather commissioned it — that's what Wen always said, *commissioned*, like the man had hired an architect, though from the photographs it was clear enough that he had stood in the yard himself and pointed at things until the welder did them his way.

You could see him everywhere in that ship if you knew where to look. The galley had a spice shelf set at a height no standard plan would call for — too high for me, just right for a man who must have been over six feet and who liked his star anise where he could reach it without bending. He'd replaced the original companionway hatch with one that had a lip on it, brass, and the brass had gone green decades ago and then been rubbed partway back to gold by every hand that ever gripped it going up and down in weather. There was a name carved into the beam above the chart table: 溫永海. Wen Yonghai. Eternal sea. I asked Wen once if that was a joke or a prayer and she said it was a name and went back to her charts.

The porthole above my galley sink let in a green light at about four in the afternoon when the sun was on the right quarter. Not blue-green, not teal — green, the color of a glass bottle held up to a flame. I never learned why. Something about the angle of the glass, Wen said, or the age of it. Her grandfather had installed that porthole. It was original. It had a crack in the seal that let in a whistle when the wind was from the northeast, and I'd stuffed a bit of cotton in it years ago, and the cotton was still there when we went down.

The drawer under the chart table stuck. You had to lift and pull at the same time, and even then it juddered out crooked. Wen could do it in one motion. I never could. She'd inherited the ship after her father died, which was after her grandfather died, which was after her mother left, which was a sequence of events Wen laid out for me once during a night watch in the same tone she used to list provisions. She had lived aboard in summers from the age of six. She had lived aboard continuously from the age of twenty. The *Mara Celeste* smelled, in its deep grain, of fifty years of sesame oil and diesel and salt and the particular mustiness of a hull that has been continuously inhabited by people who cook real food in a real kitchen and never quite get the smell out of the wood. I know that smell better than I know any house I've lived in. It's aboard now, or it was.

---

The letters started coming the year before the typhoon. They came from an outfit in Hamilton — I won't say the name, though I could — on heavy paper in envelopes with a window, the kind where your name shows through. The first few Wen opened at the chart table, standing, reading with her jaw set. She didn't discuss them with me. I saw them because I saw everything in that galley, and the chart table was six feet from my stove.

By March there were eleven of them. I know because I counted them one afternoon when Wen was on deck. They made a stack about the height of a tin of jasmine tea, and actually they were sitting next to the tin of jasmine tea, and the comparison struck me then as something, though I didn't know what.

She stopped opening them in April. The twelfth letter arrived in Road Town, and she picked it up from the harbor office and carried it aboard and set it on the stack without breaking the seal and used the previous envelope, the eleventh, as a bookmark in the pilot guide she was reading. After that they accumulated sealed. I watched the stack grow the way you watch a waterline rise on a pier — slowly, then one day you notice it's higher than it should be.

Wen mentioned the men once. We were in the galley, late, and I was cleaning the burners, and she said, "There are people from the bank in Bermuda waiting for us."

I asked what kind of people.

"The kind with a writ," she said. "A man named Garrett and a man from a marine recovery company. They want to take the boat."

She said *the boat* the way you'd say *the body.* The way you'd say *my daughter.*

I asked what she was going to do.

She picked up her tea — it was still hot that night, she was still drinking her tea that month — and she said, "I'm going to finish this cup," and she did, and she went to the wheelhouse, and we didn't speak about it again for two weeks. By then Meihua was on the weather charts and Wen had plotted a course that I understood, later, was not a course away from the storm.

The men were patient. I imagine they still are. I imagine they are standing on a dock in Hamilton in clean shoes, holding a clipboard, waiting for a ship that will not arrive, and the harbor water is flat, and they are checking their watches, and they have no idea that the ship they want is somewhere below them and to the south, in water so deep that the pressure would crush the documents they're carrying into a space no larger than a woman's fist.

Or perhaps they've filed the paperwork already. Perhaps *Mara Celeste* is listed somewhere as a loss, and the bank has written off the debt, and Wen's file has been closed and archived, and the men named Garrett have moved on to the next vessel, the next debtor, the next woman with an old boat and a dead grandfather and a stack of letters she stopped opening in April.

It doesn't matter. I was telling you about the storm.

---

Meihua arrived on Thursday and it arrived in my galley first.

I knew because the gimballed stove, which had tracked level through everything shy of a hurricane for eleven years, swung past its stops and the stockpot I'd left on the back burner slid into the bulkhead and split its seam and put a liter of chicken broth across the floor. That was at two in the afternoon. By three the burners wouldn't stay lit — the wind was finding its way through the ventilation cowl and snuffing the flame every time I got it going, so I stuffed a rag in the cowl and cooked in a galley that filled slowly with gas smell until I gave up and opened the cowl again and let the wind have its way.

I made tea. The water took a long time to boil because I had to hold the kettle on the burner with one hand and brace myself with the other and every few minutes the floor would tilt and I'd lose my grip on one or the other. I made it the way she liked it — the jasmine, three minutes, no more — and I put it in the heavy mug, the one with the chip on the rim that she wouldn't let me throw away because it was the mug her grandfather had used, and I carried it up to the wheelhouse with both hands and my elbows against the walls of the corridor.

Wen was at the wheel. She had her feet set wide and her hands at ten and two and she was looking through the glass at something I couldn't see because the glass was sheeted with water moving sideways. She looked like she always looked when she was working — still face, busy hands — except that the tendons in her forearms were standing up in a way I hadn't seen before.

I set the tea on the shelf beside the compass. She said, "Thank you, Mei." She did not pick it up.

I went back to the galley. I cleaned the broth off the floor. I wedged the other pots into the sink so they wouldn't slide. I held on to the counter edge as the floor tilted twenty degrees, thirty, and went back the other way.

Two hours later I went up again. The mug was where I'd left it. The tea was cold and had a skin on it, a thin membrane the color of weak caramel, and the ship's motion had made the liquid lean to one side of the mug so that the skin had cracked and re-formed and cracked again, and there was a faint brown ring where the surface had been before the tilting started.

I took the mug. I brought a fresh cup. She said, "Thank you, Mei." She did not pick it up.

This continued.

---

There were four crew besides Wen and me. I'll tell you about them because someone should.

Tomás had been aboard for three years and kept a photograph of a woman named Delia in a brass frame screwed to the bulkhead above his berth. He was from Angra dos Reis and he could splice wire rope faster than anyone I've ever seen. When Wen told him to go, he went to his berth first and came back with the photograph unscrewed from the frame — just the photograph, not the frame, because the frame was heavy and the lifeboat was small. He shook Wen's hand. He said, "Capitã." He went over the side.

Jun had been aboard for seven years, almost as long as me, and he was the one who'd taught me to play Big Two, which is the card game I'll tell you about later. He was from Quanzhou and he had a mole on his left eyelid that made him look like he was perpetually winking. He didn't shake Wen's hand. He stood in front of her for a moment and then bowed, a small bow, an old-fashioned bow, and said something I didn't hear over the wind. She nodded. He went over the side.

Piotr was twenty-two years old and from Gdańsk and had been aboard for four months and I don't think he fully understood what was happening until Wen told him to go, at which point he understood completely and went white and asked her to come with him. She said no. He asked again. She said, "Get in the boat, Piotr." He got in the boat. He was crying, but the rain was so heavy you couldn't be certain.

Sandy was the engineer and she was from Auckland and she'd been aboard for eighteen months and she was the one who'd kept the pumps running past the point where the pumps should have been running. She dropped into the lifeboat last. She looked up at Wen from below and said, "The starboard pump's got maybe six hours if you keep the intake clear." Wen said she would. Sandy nodded once and pulled the release.

The lifeboat fell away into the grey and I lost sight of it inside of forty seconds.

Wen stood at the rail for a moment, watching the place where it had been. Then she turned and saw me standing in the galley doorway.

"Mei," she said.

I said nothing.

"Get in the — " She stopped, because there was nothing to get into. The lifeboat was gone. There was no second one. She knew this. She'd known it when she gave the order.

"There's water for the tea," I said. "The kettle's still good."

She looked at me for a long time. The wind was pulling at her hair, which had come out of its tie hours ago and was streaming sideways in a way that should have been dramatic but wasn't. It was just wet hair in wind. She looked very tired.

"Okay," she said.

She went back to the wheelhouse. I went back to the galley.

I never told her why I stayed because she never asked and because I'm not sure the question has an answer that fits into words. I can tell you what I did next: I washed the mug with the chip on the rim, dried it, filled the kettle, and waited for the water to boil, which took a long time because the wind had found the cowl again and the flame kept going out.

---

On what I believe was the morning of the third day — it was difficult to tell morning from evening by then, but the clock said six and the light through the water on the wheelhouse glass had a quality I associated with mornings, a grey with the faintest yellow in it like a bruise going green — I brought the cards to the wheelhouse.

We'd been playing Big Two every morning for, I don't know, nine years. Jun had taught me and I had taught Wen or Wen had already known and let me think I was teaching her, which in retrospect seems more likely. It's a game for more players, ideally four, but we played it with two by modifying the rules in ways I won't describe because they wouldn't mean anything to you and because the modifications were ours and I find I want to keep something.

The deck was fifty-one cards. The nine of diamonds had gone overboard in a wind three years ago, off the Azores, and we'd replaced it with a rectangle of cardboard cut from a tea tin — the crane tin, the Zhenghe — on which Wen had drawn a remarkably precise nine of diamonds in ballpoint pen. The ink had smudged over the years and the cardboard had gone soft at the edges, but we both knew it by feel and neither of us had suggested replacing it with a proper card. The rest of the deck had the patina of ten thousand shuffles: corners rounded to ovals, faces faded to pastels, backs worn to a cotton softness that made them easy to hold and hard to snap down the way you're supposed to when you play a winning hand. We snapped them anyway. The softness made a muted sound, like a cloth being dropped on a table.

The hull was groaning.

I deal first when the clock says even, she deals when it says odd. The clock said six, so I dealt. The cards went out in their usual pattern: one to her, one to me, and again, and again. The ship tilted and three of her cards slid toward the edge of the console and she caught them with her elbow without looking.

"Pick up," I said, because that's what we said.

She picked up. She arranged her hand. The hull groaned — a long, low sound, not metal against metal but metal against the idea of holding together, a sound I had learned to distinguish from the ordinary sounds of a working ship the way you learn to distinguish a cough from a clearing of the throat. Somewhere below us and to the stern, water was audible, not the ocean outside but ocean that had gotten inside, that was sloshing in a compartment where it shouldn't be, moving with its own rhythm against the ship's rhythm, a syncopation.

Wen played the three of spades. I played the eight. She played the queen.

"You're holding the two," I said.

"I'm always holding the two," she said, which was the joke, because in Big Two the two is the highest card, and Wen had a way of saving it that drove Jun crazy when he was still aboard to be driven crazy.

She played the two. I passed. She led again.

The hull groaned a second time, longer, and the sound had a vibration in it I could feel in the soles of my feet, and the water below us shifted and I heard it find a new compartment — a gulping sound, then a settling, then a new pattern of slosh. I looked at Wen. She was looking at her cards.

"Your play," she said.

I played the six of clubs. She played the nine of diamonds — the real one would have been the nine of diamonds, but it was the piece of tea tin with the smudged ballpoint, and it made a different sound from the others when she laid it down, a flatter sound, almost a tap. We both heard it. We'd both been hearing it for three years.

"Your tea's cold," I said, because it was.

"I know," she said.

I picked up the mug when the hand was over. She'd won the hand. She usually won. I went below and made another cup, holding the kettle against the burner with my hip this time because I needed both hands to brace against the galley walls, which were now tilting at angles that made it difficult to believe the ship was still a ship and not a thing tumbling through water on its way to becoming wreckage. The green light was not coming through the porthole anymore. The sea had covered it, or the angle was wrong, or the afternoon had passed hours ago
