*[Recording begins. October 14, 1987. 11:38 PM.]*

*This is Margot Caldwell. Day 3.*

*[Silence, approximately four seconds.]*

*I want to describe what happened in the courtroom this morning as precisely as I can, because the transcript does not capture it, and I don't think the backup audio will either, fully. The technical record is complete. The sound—the experience of the sound—is what I need to set down.*

*Detective Royall was listing the titles recovered from the defendant's home. She had been motionless at the defense table all morning, same as every day, hands folded, cardigan, nothing. Then she stood. Not abruptly. She stood the way you'd stand to excuse yourself from a dinner table. Smooth. And she began to speak.*

*It wasn't shouting. It wasn't agitated. It was—this is where I'm going to sound imprecise, and I dislike that, but I've spent the last four hours trying to find the right comparison. It sounded like a record played at the wrong speed. Like someone speaking perfectly normally but through a machine that rearranges where the syllables land. The cadence was natural. The rhythm was conversational. If you were in another room, if you couldn't make out the words, you would think someone was calmly delivering a statement. But the phonemes—the individual sounds—they didn't coalesce into anything. Not English. Not any language I recognized. Not babble either. Babble is shapeless. This had shape.*

*I got it on the stenotype. I did my job. I rendered the nearest phonetic approximations and filed my Reporter's Note, and everything is technically in order.*

*I keep thinking about the phrase I used in the note: "did not conform to recognizable English phonemic structure." It's accurate. It's the right language for the filing. But it erases the thing that actually happened, which is that a woman stood up in a quiet courtroom and produced a sound that made the hair on my arms lift and did not stop making it for forty seconds, and in those forty seconds the room was the most silent room I have ever been in, because no one—not the judge, not the jury, not the prosecutor, not the gallery full of reporters and spectators—no one made a sound. Forty seconds. In a courtroom of seventy people. You can't get forty seconds of silence in a courtroom of seventy people.*

*The transcript records the judge saying "Order." It does not record that his voice, when he said it, was not the voice of a man restoring order. It was the voice of a man breaking a spell on himself.*

*[Pause.]*

*This is Margot Caldwell, court stenographer, Sutton County. End of entry.*

*[Recording ends.]*
