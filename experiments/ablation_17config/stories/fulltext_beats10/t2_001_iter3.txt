# The Reykjavik Frequency Heist

## I.

The studio smelled of dust and solder. These are not separate smells to anyone who has spent a life around equipment, but I mention both because the interviewer — your predecessor, the one from Danish public radio who came in 2014 — mentioned only dust, and I want the record corrected. Solder has a smell. It is the smell of two things being made into one thing, permanently, through heat. If you are going to sit here and record me, you should know what the room smells like.

I will tell this once. I will tell it completely. I will not abridge, and I will not be asked to repeat.

There is one condition. You do not ask about Elísabet until I reach her. You will want to. Everyone wants to begin with Elísabet because she is the spectacular part, and people have an appetite for the spectacular that I have never shared and do not intend to develop at seventy-four. You will wait. I will get there.

Here is where I begin.

At three in the morning, when you are alone in a relay station and you are twenty-six years old and you do not believe in anything you cannot measure with an oscilloscope, radio static sounds like the universe chewing. That is not a metaphor. I am not a man who uses metaphors. It is a description of a sound: broadband noise, stochastic, nonrepeating, the thermal agitation of electrons in the antenna circuit summed with atmospheric discharge and cosmic background radiation. Every frequency at once, no frequency dominant. If you listen to it long enough — and I did, five nights a week, forty-eight weeks a year — it begins to sound like a mouth working on something it cannot swallow.

I do not say this to be literary. I say it because this is the sound I was listening to on the night of March 14, 1971, and if you do not understand that sound, you cannot understand anything that follows.

Your microphone, by the way, is angled wrong. May I?

Yes. Like that. You want the diaphragm perpendicular to the source. Thirty years of broadcast work and people still point them like flashlights.

Now. 1971.

---

## II.

The Icelandic National Broadcasting Service maintained a longwave relay station eleven kilometers southeast of Akureyri, in a concrete building that had been a storage depot for the British occupation forces during the war and still smelled, in certain corners, of engine grease and tea. I worked the 2200-to-0600 shift because I was the most junior engineer and because I did not mind. I preferred the graveyard shift. The daytime staff talked. They talked about their wives, their cars, their opinions about the prime minister. I did not have a wife or a car or opinions about the prime minister. I had equipment, and equipment does not talk unless something is wrong, and when something is wrong it tells you with a specificity that human beings have never mastered.

On the night of March 14, at approximately 0247, I logged an anomalous interference pattern on 108.3 kilohertz, cutting into the standard longwave carrier signal from the Ríkisútvarpið transmitter at Eiðar. The interference was narrow-band, approximately 4 kilohertz wide, amplitude-modulated, with a carrier strength that should not have been possible from any source in the region at that frequency and that hour.

I ran the diagnostics.

Atmospheric skip: no. The ionospheric conditions that night were logged by the geomagnetic observatory at Leirvogur. F-layer critical frequency was 4.2 megahertz. There was no mechanism for skip propagation at 108.3 kilohertz under those conditions.

Soviet submarine communications: no. The Soviet Northern Fleet used extremely low frequency for submarine contact — 82 hertz, the ZEVS system — and even their surface-ship communications operated in bands far removed from 108.3 kilohertz. I knew the Soviet spectral signatures. Every engineer at our station did. We had been briefed, quietly, by a man from the Ministry of Communications who wore a tie with a stain on it and spoke as though he were reading aloud from a document he found very boring. The anomalous signal matched no Soviet profile.

Harmonic bleed from the NATO installation at Keflavík: no. I calculated the possible harmonic and intermodulation products from every known transmitter at the base — and we had a list, unofficial, compiled by my predecessor, a man named Gunnar who had been quietly requested to retire after making this list — and 108.3 kilohertz was not among them.

I rotated the directional antenna. The signal was strongest on a bearing of approximately 47 degrees magnetic. Northeast. Inland.

There was nothing inland. Northeast of Akureyri there is highland plateau, and then the Mývatn lake district, and then the Krafla volcanic system, and then more highland plateau, and then the Arctic Ocean. There were no registered transmitters. There was no population to speak of. There was rock and snow and geothermal steam and, below that, magma.

I wrote it up in the station log. I was meticulous about the station log. It was a hardbound A4 notebook, cloth-covered, with numbered pages, and I wrote in it with a 0.5-millimeter mechanical pencil because ink smears in humidity and the station was humid. The entry read:

> *14 March 1971, 0247 hrs. Anomalous signal detected, 108.3 kHz, est. BW 4 kHz, AM. Bearing 047°M. Duration 7 min 22 sec. Carrier S/N approx. 14 dB above noise floor. Waveform character: structured, possibly voice-band. Source unidentified. No correlation to known atmospheric, military, or commercial transmissions. Direction suggests inland origin, poss. geothermal EM artifact.*

I drew the waveform envelope below the entry. Three pencil lines: the rise, the plateau, the fall. Seven minutes and twenty-two seconds compressed into a shape no larger than my thumb.

I did not file a report. A report would have gone to the chief engineer, Halldór, who would have sent it to the Ministry of Communications, who would have sent it to the NATO liaison desk at Keflavík, who would have sent someone in a clean car to ask me questions I could not answer in a room with no windows. I did not want to sit in a room with no windows. I did not want to answer questions I could not answer. I wanted to understand the signal first, and I could not understand it yet, and I was not in the habit of speaking about things I did not understand.

Probably geothermal. That is what I wrote in the margin, in smaller letters, as an addendum to myself. An unexplained artifact, probably geothermal.

I went home. I slept. I dreamed about nothing. I have always dreamed about nothing, or at least nothing I retain, which amounts to the same thing.

---

The signal recurred on March 16, and again on March 19, and again on March 23. Same frequency, same bearing, varying durations. I logged each instance. Three pencil lines each time. I began keeping a separate sheet of graph paper with the dates, times, and durations plotted. There was a pattern, or the suggestion of a pattern — the intervals were lengthening, the durations were increasing — but the dataset was too small to fit a model, and I did not trust a model built on four data points.

Probably geothermal.

I believed this. I want you to understand that I believed this. I am not telling you this as a man who knew what was coming and pretended otherwise for dramatic effect. I am telling you this as a man who had four data points and a reasonable hypothesis and no reason — none — to suspect that the reasonable hypothesis was wrong in a way that would dismantle every other reasonable hypothesis he had ever held about what the world was and how it worked.

I went to work. I came home. I did not tell anyone. The signal was mine. Not in an emotional sense. In a professional sense: it was in my log, on my shift, and no one else had noticed it, and I would understand it, or I would not, but either way it would remain in the log, correctly documented, with three pencil lines.

Four weeks later Elísabet arrived.

I know. I said you were not to ask about her. You didn't. I'm the one who said her name. That is my prerogative. It is my story and I will disclose its contents in the order I choose, and if the order is imperfect it is because I am telling you something true, and true things do not organize themselves into clean sequences no matter how much you want them to.

---

## III.

She arrived on April 8, 1971, at approximately 0130, which means she drove from Reykjavik to Akureyri — six hours in those days, on roads that were not fully paved — in the dark, in early spring, when the roads north of Blönduós were still intermittently iced. I know this because I later calculated the timeline backward from her arrival and determined that she must have left the capital no later than 1930 the previous evening. She had not stopped for food. There was no evidence of food. There was evidence of coffee — she smelled of it the way a room smells of whatever has been in it for a long time.

She did not knock. The relay station door was not locked between 2200 and 0600 because there was no reason to lock it. No one came. No one had ever come. The station was eleven kilometers from the nearest occupied farm and the road to it was not marked.

The door opened and a woman walked in. She was — I am going to describe her as I perceived her at that moment, not as I came to know her, because the interviewer's job is to hear what was true at the time, not what became true later — she was approximately my age, perhaps a year or two older, with short dark hair that looked as though she had cut it herself and not recently. She wore a lamb's-wool coat that was at least two sizes too large; it reached nearly to her knees and the sleeves were rolled back in thick cuffs that gave her arms a truncated, mechanical appearance, as though she were a smaller mechanism housed inside a larger casing. She was not tall. She was not short. She moved through the station as though she had been there before, which she had not.

She was carrying two things. In her left hand, a manila folder, thick, with the edges of graph paper and photographic paper visible at the margins. Under her right arm, in a canvas case with Cyrillic stenciling on the flap, a spectrum analyzer. I recognized it — not the specific unit, but the type: a Soviet-manufactured SKCh-59, a swept-frequency analyzer used primarily in military signals intelligence. How she had obtained it I did not ask at that moment, though the answer, when I eventually received it, was both very simple and very illegal.

She walked past me to the monitoring desk. She looked at the equipment — the receivers, the patch bay, the strip-chart recorder — with the quick, inventorying gaze of someone evaluating whether a kitchen has the right knives. Then she picked up my station log. She picked up my station log without asking. She opened it to the entry for March 14.

She read it aloud. All of it. The frequency, the bandwidth, the bearing, the duration, the signal-to-noise estimate. She read my three-line waveform sketch aloud, which should not be possible, since it was a drawing, but she described it — "sharp onset, roughly trapezoidal plateau with minor amplitude flutter, exponential decay" — in terms more precise than I would have used myself.

Then she read the marginal note. "Probably geothermal," she said.

She laughed. Once. It was not a laugh that invited you to laugh with her. It was a short, percussive sound, like a door slamming in a house with too few doors. A laugh that had given up on finding anything funny and was now performing a different function entirely — a marker, a timestamp. *Here is where the wrong thing was said.*

"It is not geothermal," she said. She said this to the logbook, not to me, as though the logbook were the one who needed correcting.

I had not spoken. I realized I had not spoken since she entered the room. I had been — I will not say mesmerized, because I do not believe in mesmerism. I had been *processing*. There was a great deal of data arriving simultaneously: the woman, the coat, the analyzer, the folder, the laugh, her knowledge of my log entry. I had not yet assembled a response because I process sequentially and the inputs were parallel.

"That signal," she said, and now she looked at me, and I saw that her eyes were the color of nothing in particular, which I mention only because every account of Elísabet written by someone other than me describes her eyes as "piercing" or "intense" or some other adjective that belongs in a novel, and I want to correct the record — her eyes were eyes; they were light-colored; I did not at that moment care what color they were — "that signal is a temporally displaced carrier wave containing frequency-modulated audio content consistent with Icelandic-language broadcast radio. The content references events that have not yet occurred. I have interception records going back seven months. The displacement ranges from seventy-two hours to approximately six years, depending on conditions I have not yet been able to model. It is not geothermal. It is not Soviet, it is not NATO, and it is not atmospheric. It is a radio broadcast from the future, and it has been leaking through a fault in the Krafla caldera system since at least September 1970, and you and I are going to record it."

I looked at her for what I estimate was four seconds. Four seconds is a long time to look at a stranger in a relay station at one-thirty in the morning after she has told you that she has been receiving radio transmissions from the future through a volcano.

"I think you should leave," I said.

She did not leave. She opened the manila folder and placed it on the desk beside my logbook. Inside were fifty-three pages of signal intercepts, annotated in a hand even smaller and more precise than my own, with frequency plots, time-domain captures photographed directly from an oscilloscope screen, and a correlation table matching intercepted broadcast fragments — fishing-quota announcements, weather forecasts, parliamentary session summaries — with events that had subsequently occurred, dated, sourced, and footnoted.

She did not introduce herself. I know her name now, obviously — the whole world knows her name now, or the parts of the world that read the right journals and believe the right impossible things — but at that moment she did not offer it and I did not ask. I was reading the correlation table. I was reading it the way I read circuit diagrams: not for narrative, not for meaning, but for internal consistency. Were the connections sound? Did the signal paths close?

They closed.

"My name is Elísabet Jónsdóttir," she said, twenty minutes later, when she saw that I had reached the last page and had not yet asked her to leave again. She said it the way you state a catalog number. A designation. Something needed for filing purposes but otherwise not important.

She never thought her own name was important. She was wrong about this. She was wrong about very few things, but she was wrong about this.

## IV.

The problem, when Elísabet stated it, was elegant and terrible.

The signal existed. Her intercept records were, as far as I could verify with my available equipment, internally consistent. But the recordings she had made — on a portable reel-to-reel, using the SKCh-59 as a front-end receiver — were fragmentary, low-fidelity, and contaminated with so much atmospheric and receiver noise that the audio content was barely intelligible. To verify the temporal displacement hypothesis — I use the word "hypothesis" because I was still, at that stage, a rational man, or believed myself to be one — we needed a high-fidelity, full-bandwidth recording. We needed to capture enough audio to identify specific, verifiable claims about future events. We needed to record the signal cleanly enough that a third party could listen and confirm that the content was genuine Icelandic-language broadcast material.

This required equipment that did not exist in civilian Iceland.

The signal's characteristics — the 108.3 kilohertz carrier, the roughly 15-kilohertz audio bandwidth, the unusual modulation encoding that Elísabet had partially characterized in her notes — demanded a receiving system with sensitivity, selectivity, and dynamic range far beyond anything at my relay station or in Elísabet's canvas-cased Soviet analyzer. What we needed, in practical terms, was a purpose-built signals intelligence receiver, the kind of thing that sat inside the concrete buildings at the NATO base in Keflavík behind doors that required two keys turned simultaneously.

Elísabet's solution was Húsavík.
