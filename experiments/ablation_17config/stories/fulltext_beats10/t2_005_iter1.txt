# Form 27-B: Application for Continued Existence

---

## I.

The mistake was, in hindsight, alphabetical.

For seventeen years, four months, and eleven days—a period that encompassed the complete annihilation of human civilization on Earth, the jury-rigged launch of a colony ship carrying the last three thousand members of the species *Homo sapiens*, and the subsequent reorganization of those survivors into a functional if deeply eccentric micro-bureaucracy hurtling through interstellar space at four percent the speed of light—every form submitted by humanity to the Auditors of the Galactic Administrative Collective had been a Form 27-A: *Request for Posthumous Species Archival.*

They needed a Form 27-B: *Application for Continued Existence.*

The difference was a single letter, the smallest unit of alphabetical distance, the kind of discrepancy that in another context might cost you a parking ticket appeal or misdirect a piece of mail to your neighbor. In this context, the discrepancy meant that the governing body responsible for administering the known universe—an entity so vast and so procedurally dense that it had, at last estimate, more subcommittees than the Milky Way had stars, though the Auditors would note that this comparison was imprecise, as several of those subcommittees *were* stars—had spent nearly two decades filing humanity under "Extinct (Acknowledged, No Action Required)" rather than "Extant (Pending Review, Action Possible)."

This was discovered on a Tuesday.

Not a special Tuesday. Not an emergency Tuesday. A regular, scheduled, unremarkable Tuesday on the colony ship *Obligation*—a name selected by committee vote in Year 2, beating out *Hope* by a margin of four votes, because even in the immediate aftermath of planetary annihilation, seven hundred and twelve people felt it was important to be precise about what was keeping them alive, and it was not hope, it was obligation, a distinction the naming committee's report explored across eleven pages.

The discoverer was a clerk named Davi Souza-Perreira, thirty-one years old, formerly of Curitiba, which no longer existed, where he had been in his second year of library science studies at the Universidade Federal do Paraná, which also no longer existed. He had survived the Processing—the term universally adopted for the Auditors' systematic deconstruction of human civilization, a term borrowed from food manufacturing because it carried the right connotation of industrial indifference—by the accident of being on a student visa in Wellington, New Zealand, which shared an island with Christchurch, which was the last city processed, which was where the colony ship had been parked in a field outside the Riccarton Mall, engines running, a line stretching four kilometers into a light rain.

Davi had discovered the error the way errors in large systems are always discovered: not through genius, not through revelation, but through the miserable, eye-watering tedium of cross-referencing one document against another.

Three weeks earlier, a salvage team had recovered a fragment of Auditor documentation from a piece of debris the ship had passed through—a cloud of shattered material that had once been a relay station or a filing cabinet or possibly both. The fragment was small, dense, and largely illegible, its text printed in a script that reorganized itself each time you looked away, like a screen saver designed by an entity with no concept of screens. But Davi, who had spent seventeen years cataloguing and organizing the ship's accumulated knowledge of Auditor systems with the meticulous devotion of a man who believed, correctly, that taxonomy was a survival skill, managed to decode a single page. It was a table of contents. A directory of forms.

He found 27-A. He found 27-B. He read the descriptions.

He read them again.

He walked to the cafeteria where the Tuesday status meeting was already underway—twelve minutes in, agenda item three of nine, a discussion about whether the port-side hydroponic bay's lettuce output justified its water expenditure, a question that had been on the agenda in some form every Tuesday for six years—and he waited for the lettuce discussion to conclude, because to interrupt it would have been procedurally irregular, and Davi Souza-Perreira, trained by both library science and apocalypse, did not do procedurally irregular things.

When the floor was open, he stood.

"We've been filing the wrong form," he said.

He explained. He was concise. He used the fragment, which he had laminated.

The cafeteria held approximately eighty people that Tuesday, seated at bolted-down tables under fluorescent lighting that had been salvaged from a Christchurch government building in the eleven minutes before the Processing reached them—lighting that hummed at a frequency of 120 hertz, the same frequency as every fluorescent tube in every government building on the planet that no longer existed, which meant that the ambient sound of humanity's decision-making was identical to the ambient sound of the Curitiba DMV, the Nairobi permits office, the Beijing housing authority, and the passport control hall at Heathrow Terminal 3, all of which were now, in the Auditors' system, filed under "Processed (Complete, No Appeal Pending)."

Under that humming, Davi's announcement landed with a silence.

Not the silence of shock, which is loud in its own way—gasps, the sharp intake of breath, the clatter of things dropped. This was a different silence. This was the silence of eighty people performing the same calculation simultaneously: seventeen years times the number of forms filed times the emotional weight of each filing—the careful preparation, the proofreading, the cover sheets, the small optimism of pressing "transmit" on a machine that might or might not be reaching anyone—multiplied by the single-letter distance between "posthumous archival" and "continued existence," which was the distance between a death certificate and a petition to go on living, which was the distance between the universe looking at you and the universe looking *past* you at the next item in the queue.

It was the silence of people who had been doing something futile for almost two decades and had just been told so under fluorescent lighting on a Tuesday between the lettuce discussion and the scheduled vote on water-recycler maintenance shifts.

Someone coughed.

The cough echoed.

Then Priya Chandrasekaran, sitting in the third row with a binder open on her lap, said, "How many corrections will we need to issue?"

And somebody—it was never established who—laughed. And the laugh was like a crack in a dam, not because it let joy through but because it let through the specific pressure of the absurd, which had been building in the walls of the *Obligation* for seventeen years and which, it turned out, sounded exactly like a Tuesday-afternoon laugh in a government cafeteria, the kind that means *this is unbelievable* and *I believe it completely* and *please God let the fluorescent light stop humming.*

## II.

Priya Chandrasekaran was born in San Jose, California, on March 3, 1979, to parents who had emigrated from Chennai in 1972 and who ran, with the grim efficiency of people who understood that inefficiency was a luxury of the native-born, a dry-cleaning business on Bascom Avenue that closed in 2004 when the lease tripled. She attended San Jose State University. She majored in public administration, a field she chose the way some people choose a faith—not because it was beautiful but because it was *there*, solid and procedural and unlikely to betray her with ambiguity. She graduated with a 3.87 GPA. She applied to the California Department of Motor Vehicles.

This requires a brief aside.

The California DMV was, by most empirical measures, one of the most Byzantine bureaucratic institutions ever operated by a democratic government on Earth. Its forms numbered in the hundreds. Its wait times were legendary. Its employees occupied a unique psychic niche in American public life: they were simultaneously the most mocked and the most necessary civil servants in the state, gatekeepers to the document—the driver's license—that served as the *de facto* national identity card of a nation that refused, on principle, to issue one. To work at the DMV was to be the visible surface of a system so complex that no single human understood all of it, and to absorb, daily, the frustration of people who believed the system should be simple because *they* needed it to be simple, a belief the system had no interest in accommodating.

Priya thrived.

She was assigned to Office #7, in east San Jose, Window 3—the window nearest the restroom, which she requested during her first week because she had calculated, during a fifteen-minute break on Day 2, that proximity to the restroom shortened her break-time round trip by forty seconds, a savings that, compounded over a five-day work week across fifty weeks, yielded approximately 166 additional minutes per year, or 2.77 hours, or enough time to process between eleven and fourteen additional customers depending on complexity. She performed this calculation once. She never revisited it. It was simply *correct*, and correct things, in Priya's experience, stayed correct until the form changed, at which point you updated the form and the new thing was correct instead.

She worked at the DMV for nineteen years. She processed an estimated 94,000 transactions. She was employee of the quarter seven times, a distinction that came with a certificate and a $25 gift card to Applebee's, both of which she kept in a file at home labeled "Professional Recognition," which also contained her college diploma, her employee badge photos arranged chronologically, and a handwritten note from a customer in 2011 who wrote, *You are the only person in this building who looked at me like I was a human being,* which Priya had filed without comment but which she had also, it should be noted, never thrown away.

She survived the Processing because of her sister.

Her sister, Kavitha, lived in Christchurch, New Zealand, where she worked as a structural engineer and where she had been asking Priya to visit for six years with increasing desperation and decreasing subtlety. Priya went in October. The Processing began in November. It started in the Northern Hemisphere—some analysts later speculated this was arbitrary, others that it followed a grid pattern that resembled, troublingly, the layout of a spreadsheet—and moved south and east with the methodical patience of an entity that had no concept of urgency because it had no concept of time in any form that humans would recognize as time. San Jose was processed on November 9th. The DMV Office #7 was disassembled at the molecular level at approximately 2:15 PM Pacific Standard Time, which was, Priya later calculated, a Thursday, which meant that Window 3 had a line, because Thursdays always had lines, because that was when the previous week's online appointment slots expired and the walk-ins surged.

She was in Christchurch. Christchurch was last. This was either a function of geography, grid logic, or something else entirely, and the Auditors, who did not explain their sequencing, did not clarify.

Christchurch gave humanity eleven minutes.

Eleven minutes between the completion of the Processing in Queenstown, 480 kilometers to the southwest, and the arrival of the Processing wave in Christchurch itself. In those eleven minutes, approximately 4,200 people—of the 19,000 who had gathered near the colony ship in the Riccarton field, drawn by the radio broadcasts, the internet posts that still functioned in the Southern Hemisphere's final hour, and the ancient human instinct to move toward the largest nearby object when everything else is being taken away—were loaded into the *Obligation* through its three entry hatches.

Priya stood in that line. She carried a single bag. The bag contained: her passport, her sister's passport (Kavitha was already aboard, having been in the first thousand), a change of clothes, a travel toothbrush, and her DMV employee handbook, 2019 edition, which she had brought on the trip because she was updating its index for a departmental review upon her return to San Jose, a return that was no longer possible because San Jose was no longer there, a fact she had absorbed with the terrible efficiency of a woman whose professional life had trained her to process bad news at Window 3 without pausing, without crying, without doing anything other than saying, "I understand. Let me see what form we need."

She boarded. The hatch closed. The *Obligation* launched.

In Year 1, when the ship's surviving population of 3,012 organized itself—a process that took four months and involved a constitutional convention, three elections, two fistfights, and a 212-page founding charter drafted by a committee of eleven people, nine of whom were, in their previous lives, not constitutional scholars but middle managers, teachers, a dentist, a bus driver, and Priya—someone needed to be in charge of communicating with the Auditors. The logic was straightforward: the Auditors were a bureaucracy; communication with a bureaucracy required someone who understood bureaucracies; Priya had spent nineteen years at the California DMV. The appointment was unanimous. The title was Department of External Affairs, a name Priya chose herself because it was the kind of name that would appear on an organizational chart, and organizational charts, in her experience, were how you proved to other bureaucracies that you were a bureaucracy too, and being recognized as a bureaucracy was, she suspected, the first step to being recognized at all.

She set up her office in a repurposed storage room on Deck 4. She requisitioned a desk, a filing cabinet, and access to the ship's long-range communication array, which had been designed for navigation pings but which, with modifications she did not understand and did not need to understand, could transmit formatted documents into the void on frequencies the Auditors' systems were believed to monitor. She created templates. She established a filing protocol. She drafted a cover letter—formal, precise, structured according to the fragment of Auditor documentation guidelines recovered from the Processing—and she began to file.

Form 27-A. *Request for Posthumous Species Archival.*

She filed it in Year 1. She filed it again in Year 1, revised, because the first version had a formatting irregularity in Section 14(c) that she caught during a self-audit. She filed revised versions weekly through Year 2. She filed monthly updates through Years 3 through 8. She filed quarterly supplements with attached appendices through Years 9 through 17. She filed follow-ups, amendments, corrections to amendments, corrections to corrections, addenda, sub-addenda, cover letters for the addenda, and cover letters for the cover letters, because the Auditor documentation fragment specified that all substantive submissions required a cover letter, and Priya was not certain whether a cover letter was itself a substantive submission, so she covered the cover letters to be safe, and then covered those.

Four thousand, three hundred, and twelve forms.

4,312.

All of them wrong.

All of them 27-A—*Request for Posthumous Species Archival*—which, as the Auditors' own directory now made clear, was the form you filed when your species was already dead and you wanted it entered into the record, the cosmic equivalent of sending a death certificate to the county clerk. Not 27-B—*Application for Continued Existence*—which was the form you filed when your species was still alive, however tenuously, however absurdly, however much the available evidence suggested that three thousand people in a converted cargo ship with a failing digital projector and a lettuce-output crisis did not constitute a *species* in any sense the universe was likely to take seriously.

Priya had filed 4,312 requests to be posthumously archived.

She had been sending her own death certificate. For seventeen years. With cover sheets.

When Davi finished his announcement in the cafeteria on that Tuesday, Priya's face did something very specific. The omniscient record—which is to say, the account you are now reading, which has access to the interior of every moment on the *Obligation* and chooses what to disclose with the discretion of a clerk who understands that not all information is relevant to the current filing—can describe it precisely.

Her face did not collapse. Her face did not go blank. Her face performed the micro-expression known to career bureaucrats across every culture that has ever produced them: the brief tightening of the jaw, the single slow blink, the almost imperceptible nod that means *I see the scope of the error, I am calculating the corrections, and I will not waste time on the emotion because the emotion does not have a form number and therefore does not, at this moment, exist.*

She opened her binder. She uncapped her pen.

"How many corrections," she said, "will we need to issue?"

She was already making a list.
