# Perdition Gulch

## I

High noon, and the five of them sat on the porch of the old assay office like figures in a photograph nobody had commissioned. The building had been defunct since '82, when the last surveyor packed his theodolite and took the stage to Prescott, but the porch was wide and the overhang gave shade and the five had been settling disputes here long enough that people simply came, the way water finds a groove in stone even after the stone has forgotten what it was for.

Two ranchers stood in the street. Dolan, who ran sixty head on the eastern allotment, and Pace, who ran fewer and resented it. The matter was a well — the Hayward well, sunk in '76 on the property line between them — which had not given water in six weeks. The question was whether the water-rights agreement signed in that same year entitled Pace to dig a secondary shaft on Dolan's side, or whether the clause pertaining to auxiliary access had been nullified by Dolan's purchase of the adjacent parcel in '79.

Margaret Kindley sat in a cane-backed chair with her hands folded on her lap and her eyes on a fixed point above the ranchers' heads, as if reading text off a wall that wasn't there. She had not been asked to speak yet. But when Pace said, for the second time, that the agreement made no mention of auxiliary access, she said, without lowering her gaze:

"Section four, paragraph two. 'In the event of diminished flow, either party retains the right to pursue supplementary access within forty rods of the original shaft, provided written notice is delivered to the neighboring parcel holder no fewer than fourteen days prior to excavation.' The agreement was signed March eleventh, 1876, by Thomas Dolan — misspelled D-O-L-E-N in the original — and Francis Pace, witnessed by Judge Harold Henshaw. The auxiliary-access clause was not nullified by the '79 purchase. The '79 deed makes no reference to the Hayward agreement and therefore does not supersede it."

She said this the way a person reads aloud from a grocery list. Pace's mouth opened and did not close. Dolan looked at his boots.

On the steps below the porch, the boy sat whittling a piece of juniper into something that was not yet identifiable as anything. He was twelve, or looked it. He had looked twelve for some time. Without raising his eyes from the knife's work, he said, quietly, to Dolan: "He's not lying, Mr. Dolan. He just doesn't remember the fence post."

Dolan's face changed. The thing about the fence post — the conversation he'd had with Pace four years ago, the handshake agreement about the boundary marker, the promise Pace had made and, it turned out, simply lost track of — had not been spoken aloud by either man that morning. Dolan looked at the boy, and the boy went on whittling, and Dolan looked away.

On the far end of the porch, Luka and Nessa Valk sat in adjacent chairs with their shoulders nearly touching. They did not look at each other during the proceedings. They did not need to. When Pace shifted his weight to his left foot and his hand drifted toward his belt — not toward a weapon, just toward the comfort of a hand on leather, a man steadying himself — both twins turned their heads toward him at the same instant, the same degree of rotation, the same fractional narrowing of the eyes. It was not synchronized. Synchronization implies two separate actions brought into alignment. This was one action wearing two faces.

At the porch rail, Martin Ell sat with his boots up and his hat over his eyes, to all appearances asleep. On his left forearm, below the rolled cuff of his shirt, a knife scar ran from wrist to elbow — livid and ugly at dawn that morning, now pink, now paling, the flesh pursuing itself closed with the slow determination of a seam stitching itself in a dream. By the time Dolan and Pace shook hands and turned toward their respective horses, the scar was a white whisper. By the time the hooves faded, it would be nothing.

The ranchers left. They went without enthusiasm but without resistance, the way people had been going from this porch for eleven years — uneasy, obedient, unwilling to examine too closely the mechanism that had resolved their problem.

The porch was silent. Heat pressed down on the street like a palm on a table.

Luka said: "That's the fourth dispute this week."

Nessa finished: "They're not fighting about water."

Nobody answered, because nobody needed to. The well was dry. The creek was low. The stockpond behind the livery had gone to cracked mud. But the town of Perdition Gulch, Arizona Territory, had survived drought before, and drought alone did not make men reach for their belts when a property line was mentioned. Something else was draining. Something else was going dry.

The boy folded his knife and held up his whittling — a small, blunt shape, an animal or a person, impossible to tell. He turned it in the light. Then he put it in his pocket and walked off the porch and down the street, and the five were four, and then Martin Ell tipped his hat back and stood and said he had fence posts to sharpen, and the four were three, and the twins went inside the assay office where they kept their things, and Margaret Kindley sat alone on the porch with her hands folded and her eyes on that invisible wall, reading something the rest of the world had thrown away.

## II

A hot night in 1873. Not the dry heat of the territory in summer but a humid, clinging weight, a freak weather that came in from the south and sat on the town like a sick man's breath. The boarding house on Culver Street had twelve rooms and nine tenants, and its walls were pine planks joined with a faith more optimistic than structural, and a boy of seven crouched beneath a second-floor window with his hands clamped over his ears and his mouth open in a posture that was not quite a scream.

Inside the room, a man named Groder sat on his bed unlacing his boots. He was not speaking. His mouth was closed, his breathing even. But the inside of his skull was loud — louder than any voice the boy had ever heard — and what was in there was not words, exactly, but images shoved through language like meat through a grinder: the family camped at the creek, the woman's hair, the way firelight moves on canvas when people are sleeping inside a wagon, the hatchet in his saddlebag, the weight of it, what happens to a human jaw when you swing at the hinge, and after the man and the woman there would be time, there would be plenty of time, because the children—

The boy ran. Barefoot on packed dirt, nightshirt flapping, hands still pressed to his ears as if that had ever helped, as if the sound were sound and not something that bypassed his ears entirely and entered through the marrow of his skull. He ran down Culver Street and turned left on the main road and went past the saloon (a roar of overlapping thought so dense it was almost noise, almost merciful in its incoherence) and past the livery (the night man thinking about his dead sister, a loop of grief that played like a music-box melody, always the same bar) and around the back of the dry-goods store to the storeroom where the lantern was burning.

She was inside. She was always inside at this hour. Margaret Kindley, twenty-six years old, seated on an overturned crate, surrounded by crates, a pencil in her hand and a ledger on her knee, murmuring figures in a low monotone — "forty-two gross, iron nails, two-penny, lot dated June fourteenth; sixteen bolts, canvas, eight-ounce, undyed; nine tins, peaches, Del Monte, dented but sealed" — not reading from an inventory list but from the inventory itself, which she had glanced at once, six hours ago, and which now existed behind her eyes in perfect and permanent detail, down to the dented tins and the misspelling on a crate stamp.

He stood in the doorway until she looked up. His face told her something. Her pencil stopped.

"There's a man," the boy said. "On Culver Street. Second floor, third window from the stairs. He's going to hurt the family at the creek. Tonight. He's — I can hear him thinking about it. All of it. How he's going to do it."

She did not ask him how he knew. She did not ask him to describe what he had heard. She looked at him for a long moment, and her face did something complicated — a sequence of expressions so rapid and so precisely felt that it looked, from the outside, like no expression at all.

She said: "Who else knows what you can do?"

He said: "Nobody."

She said: "Keep it that way. Come with me."

They crossed the street. Not the main road but the back alley, where the shadows were good and the ground was soft. Margaret walked quickly and did not look back to see if the boy was following, because she had calculated his stride length and pace from the sound of his footsteps and knew he was four feet behind her and keeping up. They entered the back door of Mrs. Callahan's rooming house, which was not locked and never was, and went down a hallway that smelled of lye and bacon grease to a door at the end, beneath which lamplight showed.

She knocked twice. Paused. Knocked once more.

The door opened on a man sitting at a small table with his left forearm laid out before him like a piece of work on a bench. His right hand held a needle trailing catgut thread. The gash on his forearm was four inches long and deep enough to show the pale fatty layer beneath the skin, and it was already half-closed — not stitched half-closed but *growing* half-closed, the edges of the wound reaching for each other like lips forming a word, the flesh weaving itself together in a process that should have taken weeks and was taking minutes. He had been stitching to give himself something to do while it happened.

Martin Ell looked up. He looked at Margaret. He looked at the boy, whose face was still white, whose hands still trembled in small periodic convulsions, as if the thoughts he'd heard were a voltage that hadn't fully discharged.

Margaret said: "We have a problem."

Martin set down the needle. He looked at his forearm, where the wound was now a raised red seam, tightening. He let out a breath.

"Yeah," he said. "I figured it was a matter of time."

They went to Groder's room at one in the morning. Martin went first. No weapons. A man who could not be cut or broken or stopped by anything short of a cannonball did not need weapons. He opened the door, and Groder was asleep, and the hatchet was in his saddlebag as the boy had seen it in Groder's mind, and the family at the creek slept on, unknowing, and by dawn Groder was on a horse heading north with the understanding — conveyed by Martin Ell's hand on his shoulder and the particular quality of Martin Ell's gaze — that Perdition Gulch was not a place he should return to.

Nobody in town noticed. Nobody in the wagon at the creek ever knew. The boy slept the rest of that night in the storeroom, on a folded horse blanket, while Margaret Kindley sat on her crate and did not sleep and recited, very quietly, the terms of every contract she had ever read, as if the act of remembering everything else might crowd out the look on the child's face when he had stood in her doorway and tried to describe a thought that no seven-year-old should have had to carry.

## III

The fence post was juniper, dry and hard as iron, and the knife slipped on a knot and the blade caught the heel of Martin Ell's left hand — a nothing cut, the kind a man sustains four times a week and doesn't bother to notice. He wiped it on his trousers and kept working.

An hour later, stringing wire along the corral behind the livery, he felt the wetness in his glove. He pulled the glove off. The cut was still open. Not bleeding badly — a lazy seep, the kind of wound that in any ordinary man would have warranted a kerchief and a mild oath and no further thought. But Martin Ell was not any ordinary man, and in the fourteen years since he had first understood what he was, he had not once looked at his own blood for longer than the few seconds it took for his body to repossess it.

He wrapped it. He finished the wire. He went home.

His cabin sat at the eastern edge of town, a single room of pine and adobe with a stove, a bed, a chair he had carved himself, and on the wall above the bed a framed commendation from the town council — *For Distinguished and Continuous Service to the Community of Perdition Gulch, 1880* — signed by men who had no idea what the service actually was.

That night, by the light of a single candle, he unwrapped the kerchief.

The cut was a half-inch long and shallow. In a normal hand, on a normal man, it would be unremarkable. But the language of it was wrong. Every wound Martin Ell had ever sustained had spoken the same dialect: a brief red exclamation, then the swift syntax of closure, the flesh conjugating itself back to wholeness as if injury were merely a grammatical error the body could parse and correct. This cut spoke a different language. It sat open. The edges did not reach for each other. The seepage had thickened into a dark, sullen line, and beneath the parted skin he could see — for the first time, for the actual first time in his adult recollection — the whitish layer of tissue that exists beneath the dermis of every human being on earth but that he had never had occasion to look at, because he had never had occasion to remain wounded long enough for looking.
