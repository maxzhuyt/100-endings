## AUDIO DIARY — C. MOSLEY
## PERSONAL RECORDING, NON-EVIDENTIARY
## September 14, 1984. 9:47 PM.

This is Carol Mosley, certified shorthand reporter, license number 1147, state of Arkansas. I'm recording this on my Marantz PMD-220 portable cassette unit, which I purchased in May of this year for the purpose of maintaining a professional audio log of assignments. I use TDK SA-C90 high-bias tapes. I label and date each tape and store them in a fire-safe lockbox in my home office. This is not part of any official court record. This is mine.

Tomorrow I begin a capital assignment. *State v. Vance*. Murder in the first degree, three counts, which I am told are the deaths of three minor children, all foster children placed in the defendant's care. The defendant is Eleanor Marguerite Vance, age forty-one, formerly of Calico Rock. She has been held without bail since February.

I don't know anything else about the case and I don't need to. My job is the record.

I drove to Sutton County today to inspect the courtroom. The courthouse is on Highway 53, across the road from a Shell station and a Family Dollar. The courtroom is on the second floor. It has tall windows on the south wall and a dropped acoustical ceiling that was installed sometime in the 1970s, over what I suspect is the original pressed tin. The ceiling panels are water-stained. I clapped my hands twice at my station and counted the decay. About 1.8 seconds, which is long. A church would be 1.5. A good courtroom should be under one second. I'll compensate by positioning my backup microphone six inches closer to the witness box than I normally would and running my gain slightly hot. I prefer to solve problems before they begin.

My station is to the left of the bench, facing the gallery, which seats approximately sixty. My chair wobbles. I will bring a folded piece of cardboard tomorrow.

Parking is a problem. There are fourteen spaces behind the courthouse marked OFFICIAL USE ONLY and none of them are assigned to support staff. I was told by the bailiff, a Mr. Hardin, that I could park at the Shell station "if I bought something." I will park on the street.

I've been a court reporter for eleven years. I have covered thirty-seven jury trials, over two hundred depositions, and six years of family court in Pulaski County, which is its own kind of violence. I have never covered a capital case. I was told this one may last three to four weeks because of the defendant's—

[*pause*]

Because of the complexity. That's all. The complexity.

I'm going to test playback now.

[*click*]

[*playback: "—the complexity. That's all. The complexity. I'm going to test playback now."*]

Clear. Levels are fine. My voice on tape is higher than I expect it to be. It always is. Like hearing yourself on an answering machine and thinking: is that me? Is that really what I sound like to everyone else?

It's fine. Clarity is confirmed. End of entry.

## September 19, 1984. 8:15 PM.

Day three. The defendant made an outburst during the detective's testimony. Shouted over her own counsel. I hate that. It's the worst part of the job, trying to get a clean record when people won't stay in their lane. I noted it as unintelligible and included a phonetic approximation, which is standard practice when the speaker's words cannot be parsed into recognizable English. The sounds were clear enough. I got them down. But they don't map to anything, and it's not my job to interpret. My job is to capture.

The gallery was full again today. There's a group from—I don't know the denomination. Apostolic something. They've been in the first two rows since voir dire, maybe fifteen of them, holding laminated signs. One of the signs says SUFFER NOT A WITCH. Another says ELEANOR VANCE CHOSE SATAN OVER CHILDREN. The bailiff, Hardin, asked the judge to confiscate the signs and the judge said they could hold them as long as they held them quietly. They hold them quietly. They hold them like hymnals.

The defendant was calm after the recess. Didn't look at anyone. She has short gray hair and she sits very still. That's all I have to say about her.

## Direct Examination of Dr. Leland Hoff, Child Psychologist
## Examination by Mr. Colquitt (Prosecution)

**MR. COLQUITT:** Dr. Hoff, you were retained by the Sutton County Department of Human Services to evaluate the surviving foster children previously placed in the defendant's care, is that correct?

**DR. HOFF:** That's correct.

**MR. COLQUITT:** And as part of that evaluation, you asked the children to produce drawings?

**DR. HOFF:** Yes. Art therapy is a standard diagnostic tool with children aged four through—

**MR. COLQUITT:** I'd like to direct your attention to what's been marked as State's Exhibit 14. Can you describe this drawing for the jury?

**DR. HOFF:** This was produced by Child C, a six-year-old female. It shows a group of figures standing in a circle. The figures are drawn in brown crayon, which in my experience with traumatized children often correlates with—

**MR. ARNETT:** Objection. Foundation. The witness is speculating about the significance of crayon color.

**THE COURT:** Sustained. Dr. Hoff, describe what you see without interpretive overlay, please.

**DR. HOFF:** The figures are standing in a circle. Their mouths are open. In the center of the circle there is a smaller figure, which Child C identified as—

**MR. COLQUITT:** Without hearsay, Doctor. Just the drawing.

**DR. HOFF:** The smaller figure in the center is holding an object in its right hand. The object is elongated. It could be a stick or a pen or a candle. The smaller figure appears to be making contact with the ground with this object—dragging it, or writing with it.

**MR. COLQUITT:** And in your expert opinion, is this drawing consistent with what the clinical literature calls "ritualistic indicators" in the artwork of abused—

**MR. ARNETT:** Objection. Leading. And I'd ask the court to note that the "clinical literature" Mr. Colquitt refers to consists primarily of a single pamphlet distributed by the National Center for the—

**THE COURT:** The objection is sustained. Rephrase, Mr. Colquitt.

## AUDIO DIARY — C. MOSLEY
## September 25, 1984. 10:40 PM.

The prosecution showed the children's drawings today. They were projected on a screen set up next to the jury box, and I had a good sightline from my station.

I shouldn't be describing this. It's in the record. It's all in the record, every word Hoff said, every objection Arnett raised. I don't need to supplement.

But.

The drawing with the circle of figures. They were standing around the smaller one, and their mouths were open—wide open, like the child drew the mouths first and built the faces around them. Like the openness of the mouth was the relevant fact and the rest of the face was just housing. And the small figure in the center wasn't distressed. That's what Hoff didn't say and what the prosecution didn't ask about. The small figure was *working*. Focused. The object in its hand was touching the ground in a deliberate line, and even in crayon, even with a six-year-old's motor control, you could see the line had direction. It was going somewhere. It was recording something.

I don't know why I'm describing this. It's evidence, it's in the record.

## AUDIO DIARY — C. MOSLEY
## October 2, 1984. 11:52 PM.

I need to—okay. Okay. I want to say this clearly and then I want to evaluate whether to keep or erase this entry, and I will make that decision tomorrow when I've slept.

I keep backup recordings. Everyone who does what I do keeps backup recordings. It's standard practice. The Marantz runs a second line through a splitter to a Sony TCM that I keep under my station in a padded bag. This is insurance. If my primary machine fails, if a tape jams, if there's a power issue, I have a parallel record. Defense attorneys have tried to subpoena these backups before and I've successfully argued they're work product. They're mine. This is mine.

Tonight I pulled the backup tapes for the three outbursts I flagged. September 21st, 26th, and 28th. I isolated the segments and listened to them in sequence.

They sound like what they sound like. A woman shouting something that isn't English. Emotional. Guttural in places, almost singing in others. Gibberish. You would hear gibberish.

I was rewinding the September 28th segment—the short one, the three-second one—and my finger slipped on the shuttle control because the housing on the Sony is cracked and the button sticks, it's been sticking for months, I keep meaning to replace the—

When I rewound past the segment and hit play, I heard a few seconds of the reversed audio before I corrected.

Reversed, it—

It resolved. That's the only word. It resolved, the way your eyes adjust in a dark room and you realize the shape you've been staring at is a chair, a lamp, something you should have recognized all along. The sounds played backward were clear. Very clear. Like someone speaking calmly in a room with no echo.

I'm probably hearing things. The acoustics in that courtroom are strange. I noted that on day one. Dropped ceiling over pressed tin, that kind of layered architecture does unpredictable things to sound, creates phasing, standing waves. I've been in that room every day for three weeks. Eight hours a day of listening at high gain. Your ears start filling in patterns. It's like staring at static.

I'm going to re-record over this entry tomorrow. This is stress. This is a capital case and I've never done one before and the drawings got to me and I need to sleep.

I'm going to re-record over this.
