# The Divided Caretaker

## I.

The county services van backed into the loading dock at 2:47 p.m. on a Tuesday in March. Intake logged it as a miscellaneous equipment transfer, category 7-NP, which covered nonprofit placement of decommissioned assistive units. The driver stayed in the cab. A technician came around the passenger side holding a tablet in a waterproof case and asked for whoever signs for deliveries.

The night-shift supervisor, Marie, was still finishing her overlap. She came to the dock in her coat, already holding her car keys, and took the tablet. The technician pointed to two fields — receiving party and condition acknowledgment — and said, "It's a CR-4 series caretaker, eighteen months of residential use before the owner passed. County took possession, did a partial wipe — good enough for nonprofit placement." He said this the way one mentions a dent on a used appliance, already turning back toward the van's gate.

The android stood between two linen carts in the dock's staging area. It was roughly the shape and height of an average adult, though its proportions carried the uncanny smoothness of injection-molded paneling over a structural frame. Its surface was a matte off-white that had yellowed slightly along the forearm joints where, presumably, it had been most handled. A small diode at its left temple pulsed a slow, even amber. It did not move. It did not look at Marie or the technician or the dock or the stacked linens. It stood the way a coat rack stands — upright, patient, unoccupied by the fact of its own standing.

Marie signed both fields. The technician pulled the tablet back, checked something, tapped twice, and said, "You'll want to register it with your facilities manager for liability. There's a PDF." He walked back to the van. The van pulled away. Marie looked at the android for three seconds, then at her car keys, then at the android again.

"Follow me," she said.

It did.

They went through the service corridor past the laundry intake and the storage cages where surplus wheelchairs were kept in a row like collapsed umbrellas. The android's footfalls were nearly silent — a faint, rubbered compression on each step, engineered to not wake patients. Marie's keys jingled against her lanyard. She walked it to the charging station that had been set up in a converted linen closet off the east-wing nurses' station, showed it the outlet and the magnetic dock plate, and said, "Night shift starts at eleven. Someone will orient you." She said this with the same intonation she used to show new CNAs the breakroom microwave.

Across the corridor, forty feet away, Lem was replacing a mop head. The old one had frayed past the point where it cleaned rather than redistributed. He stood at his cart, threading the new head onto the clamp, and watched the android follow Marie around the corner. His hands kept working — aligning the cotton loops, tightening the wingnut — but his eyes stayed on the point where the android had disappeared for several seconds after it was gone.

Then the mop head was secure, and he went back to the east-wing bathroom.

## II.

His shift started at 10 p.m. and ended at 6 a.m. He had worked this shift for four years without requesting a change and without being asked if he wanted one. His routines were the architecture of those eight hours, and they did not vary.

At 10:15 he retrieved his cart from the maintenance closet: mop, bucket, two rags (one for surfaces, one for glass), a spray bottle of quaternary disinfectant, a spray bottle of glass cleaner, a putty knife for the things people left on floors, and a roll of trash bags. He filled the bucket at the utility sink, testing the water temperature with two fingers the way someone else might test a bath for an infant.

The east-wing corridor got stripped and re-waxed on Tuesday and Friday nights. He applied the stripper in long, overlapping passes, working backward from Room 14 to the nurses' station, then let it sit for exactly the time it took him to clean the shared bathroom. The bathroom had a sequence: mirror first, then fixtures — faucet handles, soap dispenser, towel bar — then bowl, then floor. He had done this in this order since his second week, when he had tried floor-first and learned that drips from the mirror cloth undid the work. He had not made that mistake again. The sequence was not preference. It was the geometry of cause and effect, and he respected it the way a mason respects plumb.

At 11:40 he broke for his sandwich. He brought it from home each night in a plastic bag, the same brand of bread, the same arrangement — turkey, one leaf of lettuce, no condiment. He cut it into four equal squares using a plastic knife from the breakroom drawer. He ate each quarter in order: top left, top right, bottom left, bottom right. He drank water from a paper cup he filled twice. The breakroom had a television mounted on a bracket near the ceiling; he never turned it on.

Back in the corridor, he finished the wax application. Then he moved through the west wing — trash collection from the small cans in patient rooms, done as quietly as a thing can be done. He had learned which trash bags crinkled least (the off-brand ones from the supply company's second catalog, thinner plastic, almost no sound). He had learned which door hinges needed the particular angle that kept them from clicking. These adjustments weren't noted anywhere. No one had asked him to make them.

It was on these rounds, passing Room 4 where Mr. Benitez slept with his mouth open and his TV on mute, that the cart's front left wheel caught the transition strip between hallway tile and patient-room carpet and began its noise — a rhythmic, high squeak, the sound of rubber on metal where the bearing had worn. Lem stopped. He reached down, gripped the cart's front rail, and lifted the left corner two inches off the ground. He held it there with one hand and pushed the cart forward with the other, past Room 4, past Room 5 where the woman whose name he knew but the story does not had been moved to comfort-measures-only that afternoon, past Room 6 where Doris slept with her mouth slightly open and her hand on the mattress edge as if waiting for something to be placed in it. He carried the wheel's weight for the full length of the hall, set it down gently past the last occupied room, and continued to the utility closet.

A nurse named Carla left a Post-it on the breakroom table: *Lem — ceiling stain above 7, is that a leak?* He found her at the nurses' station on his second pass. He pointed up and to the left, toward the approximate location of Room 7's ceiling. Then he held up two fingers.

"Two days?" Carla said.

He nodded.

"Thanks, Lem."

He returned to his cart. The wax in the east-wing corridor had dried. He buffed it in long, even strokes, and the floor repaid him with the dull, even shine that no patient or visitor would notice, which was how he knew it was right.

## III.

Four nights into the android's placement, Lem was buffing the floor outside Room 6 at 2:14 a.m.

He heard the door open — not heard it, exactly, but registered the change in corridor air pressure that meant a door had moved from mostly-closed to fully-open. The android passed him without acknowledgment or pause, entered Room 6, and stopped at the foot of Doris's bed.

Lem kept the buffer moving but shifted his weight so he could see through the half-open door. He did not decide to watch. Watching was not a decision. It was the thing his body did when there was something to see.

The android stood at the foot of the bed. Doris was asleep. Her breathing was the breathing of someone deep enough under that a hand on the shoulder would need to stay there for a while. The room's thermostat read seventy-four, which was what the day nurse always set it to, which was three degrees warmer than Doris's chart noted as her preference, because the day nurse set all rooms to seventy-four.

The android did not move.

One second. Two. Three.

Lem counted the way he counted drying times — not with numbers exactly but with the interior metronome of someone who has lived inside repetition long enough that his body keeps time the way a wall keeps plumb. Four. Five. Six. The android's amber diode pulsed. Seven. Eight. The room was still. Nine. Ten. Doris's hand twitched on the mattress edge, the kind of twitch that precedes conscious movement by minutes, not seconds. Eleven.

The android moved.

It stepped to the bedside table. It lifted the plastic pitcher — pale blue, the same model in every room, the kind with a hinged lid and a pour spout that always dripped — and poured water into the cup beside it. The pour was precise: it filled the cup to the point where the surface tension just began to dome, then stopped. It set the pitcher down. It adjusted the straw — a flexible straw, the kind with the accordion bend — approximately three degrees toward the side of the bed where Doris's hand rested. It positioned the cup so that the straw's tip was at the exact point where Doris's fingers, if extended, would meet it without reaching.

Then it crossed the room and adjusted the thermostat. Seventy-two. It paused for one second — Lem felt the pause as a kind of punctuation, a breath in the choreography — and set it to seventy-one.

Then the overhead light. The dimmer switch in Room 6 was the old rotary kind, and the android turned it with two fingers in a motion so controlled that the light dropped from whatever the day nurse had left it at — perhaps forty percent, the level you'd use to find a vein — down to something barely above darkness. Twelve percent. Maybe eleven. The room settled into the quality of light that exists just before dawn in a house where someone has left one lamp on in a far hallway: not dark, but no longer lit. A room in which a person could wake and not be startled by waking.

The android stepped back. It looked — or oriented its sensor array, or performed whatever mechanical operation substituted for looking — at the full arrangement: the water, the straw, the temperature, the light. It seemed, for a fraction of a second, to be reading the composition. Then it turned and exited through the door, passing Lem in the corridor with the same unhesitating, unlooking gait, and moved on toward Room 7.

Lem did not move.

In Room 6, the temperature settled. The air shifted from the dry warmth of seventy-four to something cooler that went to work on Doris's skin, perhaps reaching whatever sleeping nerve governs the difference between restless and still. She surfaced. It was not the startled surfacing of an alarm or a loud voice — it was the surfacing of someone drawn up gently from a depth, as if a hand beneath the water had cupped her shoulder and pressed. Her eyes opened. She did not look confused. She looked like a woman waking in a room that had been prepared for her waking.

Her hand moved across the mattress. Her fingers found the straw.

She drank. A long draw, then a short one. She set the cup down with the imprecise gentleness of someone still half asleep. She sighed — the sigh of a body receiving what it did not know it needed — and her eyes closed again.

"Thank you," she said.

The room was empty. The word went into the air the way a coin goes into a fountain — directed at nothing, carrying everything.

In the corridor, Lem had stopped the buffer. His hand rested on its handle. The motor was off, but he hadn't turned it off; he'd simply stopped applying the pressure that kept it running. His face, if anyone had been there to see it, carried no expression that could be easily named. He was watching the door of a room he could no longer see into, and what he was watching was the space where the android had been, which was now just air, just the hallway, just the ordinary gap between one room and the next.

He stood there for eight seconds.

Then he pressed down on the buffer and resumed.

## IV.

The breakroom at 3:15 a.m. had the quality of light found in parking garages and confessionals — overhead fluorescents that buzzed at a frequency just below awareness, turning every surface the same pale nothing. Lem sat at the table with his sandwich quarters arranged on a paper towel. Beside the paper towel, flat on the table and partially covered by his left hand, was a photograph.

The photograph was wallet-sized and had been laminated at some point, but the lamination had gone milky with handling — not cracked, not peeling, but clouded, the way glass fogs when you breathe on it and the fog never quite leaves. Whatever image it held existed now behind that fog, visible only to someone who already knew what was there. Lem looked at it the way one looks at a window rather than through it: not seeing the image but confirming the object's presence, the way you touch a pocket to check for keys.

He ate his first quarter. Top left.

Carla came in with a binder and a pen that she clicked open and closed as she read. She sat across from him, not because she wanted company — the breakroom had four chairs and they were all at the same table — but because that was where the table was. She wrote something. She turned a page. She clicked the pen.

"My kid's doing that speech thing at school," she said, not looking up. She turned another page. "You know, the debate thing. Two-person teams. She's terrified." Carla underlined something in the binder. "She threw up before her book report last year. Actual vomit. In the classroom." The pen clicked. "I keep telling her, once you start talking, the fear goes somewhere. You just have to start."

She looked up.

"You were a teacher, right? Before?"

Lem nodded. Once. A nod that carried the mass of a thing that had been true and was no longer true and could not be discussed in the space between those two truths.

Carla's pen hovered over the binder. She opened her mouth, and what came into it was a question — the shape of a question, the breath before a question — and then she remembered. The remembering crossed her face the way a cloud crosses a parking lot: a brief shadow, a recalculation of light. Her mouth closed. She looked down.

"Right," she said.

The word was small and careful and did the work of an apology and a retraction and a wall going up, all at once. She returned to her binder. The pen clicked twice.

Lem ate his second quarter. Top right. He chewed with the methodical, unhurried rhythm of someone for whom eating was maintenance, not pleasure. His jaw worked. The breakroom hummed. Carla turned a page. On the table, the photograph sat in its milky lamination, holding whatever it held — a face, faces, a place, a moment — in the unrecoverable past tense of all photographs, made more past by the fog between the image and the air.

He finished. He picked up the plastic wrap from around the sandwich and folded it. Once in half. Once again. And again, tucking the edges, pressing the creases flat with his thumbnail, until it was a tight square no larger than a postage stamp. He placed it in the trash can — not tossed, placed — and picked up the photograph and put it back inside his wallet, which went into his back right pocket, where it always was.

He stood. He pushed his chair in. He returned to the corridor, where the floor was waiting and the buffer was waiting and the next room's trash can was waiting, and the mute certainty of tasks performed in sequence was waiting, which was the only language left to him and the one he spoke most fluently.

## V.

Friday. Four nights later. Lem was in the supply closet at the end of the west wing, reorganizing the shelf where the disinfectant wipes were stored, because someone on the day shift had placed the half-used containers behind the full ones, which meant the half-used ones would expire, which meant waste, which was a problem Lem could solve with his hands, and so he did.

He heard the absence.

Not a sound — the subtraction of a sound. Room 11's cardiac monitor had a rhythm he knew the way you know the cadence of a dripping faucet in your own home: not by listening for it but by being so saturated in its presence that its disappearance registers as physical, a pressure change in the inner ear, the silence where a pulse should be.
