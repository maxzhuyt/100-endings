# The Fuel-Tin Cake

## 1.

The temperature was thirty-eight degrees. I know because I checked. Not from any medical imperative—there was no one left whose fever mattered—but because the thermometer was in my hand and my hand needed a task, and thirty-eight degrees was a fact that didn't ask anything of me.

I am going to tell this the way it comes, which is backward, because that's the direction memory faces. Memory doesn't start where the story started. It starts at the thing it can't get past and then walks away from it, and asks you to follow, and every step back is a step toward people who are still breathing. That's the bribe. That's why I go.

The lithium flats ran east for eleven kilometers to the processing sheds and west for longer than that to nothing. Wind came from the west. It moved the dust in low, purposeful lines, the way water moves over tile, and it erased bootprints at a rate I could calculate if I wanted to, but I didn't want to, and I noted this as the first thing I chose not to measure.

Seven bodies. I will not describe them as they were. I will tell you what I did.

I went to each one and I checked for a pulse. This is not heroic and it is not denial. It is protocol. You check because the body is not yours and the judgment that a body has died is medical and must be made by a medical person with fingers on the radial artery for a minimum of one full minute, and I had minutes, I had nothing but minutes now, so I gave each of them their full sixty seconds, pressing the place where the blood should answer, and the blood did not answer, and I moved to the next, and I gave them the same.

Seven times one minute. Seven minutes. I have spent longer waiting for water to boil.

Their names, in the order I reached them: Kess. Tomil. Pitar. Sule. Dak. Maro. Elin.

There were eleven rounds of ammunition remaining across all weapons. The medical kit contained four packets of hemostatic gauze, a suture set I'd opened and not used, two morphine syrettes—full, both of them, because at the end it had gone too fast for morphine—and a tourniquet that was still wound tight on Pitar's left leg, doing its job perfectly, which I want noted, which I need someone to know: the tourniquet held.

I closed their eyes when their eyes were open. Four of seven. Kess, Pitar, Dak, Elin. The others had closed their own, or the muscles had done it for them, or I am wrong about this and I closed all seven and only remember four, because memory is not a record—it is a draft, revised by guilt into something I can almost survive.

Wind snapped the supply tarp against the post where Sule had tied it that morning. That sound will outlast every other sound in my life. It was not meaningful. It was a tarp in wind. That is exactly why it will outlast everything: because it did not care, and I wanted so badly for something out there on that flat white expanse to care, and nothing did, and the tarp snapped, and the dust moved, and thirty-eight degrees.

By Elin's hand—not in it, but near it, as if she had set it down to do something else and simply not come back to finish the gesture—there was a fuel tin. Standard issue, olive drab, the kind you find in every supply dump from here to the capital. Someone had sheared off the top with a can opener and scorched the inside black over a camp stove. Inside: crumbs. Brown,ite, dense. A crust of something caramelized and burnt along one edge. A cake. Someone had baked a cake in a fuel tin, and there were crumbs left, and Elin had been near it when she died, and I looked at those crumbs for a long time.

I looked at them longer than I should tell you.

Then I went backward.

## 2.

Forty minutes, maybe fifty. I have never been able to fix it more precisely than that, and I was trained to be precise, so the failure sits in me like a stone I swallowed and never passed.

There were three of them still alive when the last contact started: Elin, Dak, and Maro. Dak was on the eastern berm with the rifle that had been Tomil's and then Sule's and was now Dak's by the law of who was still standing. Maro was eight meters south of me, behind the half-wall of stacked lithium crates, reloading, or trying to—her hands were doing something wrong with the magazine and I could hear her hitting it with her palm. Elin was next to me. Wounded. Left side, below the ribs. I had my hands in it.

I need to say this clearly: the wound was survivable. With a transport, with plasma, with twenty minutes and a table it was survivable. I want that on whatever record this becomes. I want it carved into the lithium itself. It was survivable. What was not survivable was the flat, the distance, the second burst that came from the north side where we had not been watching because we no longer had enough people to watch every side.

I was working. My hands were in the wound and I was packing gauze and I was speaking to Elin the way you speak to a patient, which is to say I was lying in a calm voice. "Pressure's good, you're okay, keep breathing, just keep—" She was looking at me and her eyes were clear and I have seen enough eyes go wrong to know: she was there. She was present and afraid and alive and looking at me for the specific thing a patient looks at a medic for, which is not salvation but permission. Permission to believe the lie. I gave it. I said, "You're fine," and she knew it was a lie, and she accepted the lie, and there was a contract between us, and I have honored that contract every day for thirty-one years by telling no one what her eyes looked like until now.

Dak was firing. Short bursts, controlled, the way Sule had taught him—Sule who was already dead by then, who'd been dead since the repositioning, Sule who I'll get to, I'll get to all of them—and between bursts Dak was shouting something I could not parse over the noise. I thought it was tactical. It wasn't. He was shouting at Maro about the magazine. "The *catch*, Maro, the *catch*, you have to—" He was teaching her to reload while shooting. He was seventeen.

Then Dak was not firing. The sound from the eastern berm changed from bursts to silence and the silence was a specific shape—I could feel it architecturally, the way you feel a wall removed from a house, suddenly there is wind where there was not wind.

I did not go to him. My hands were in Elin.

Maro got the magazine seated. I heard the click from eight meters away, or I have invented the click, but the result was real: she fired, and whatever she hit or didn't hit, the incoming paused, and in that pause she turned to look at the berm where Dak had been, and I saw the knowledge arrive in her.

She said nothing. She turned back and fired again.

Elin, under my hands, said: "Is Dak—"

"Don't talk."

"Is he—"

"*Breathe.*"

She breathed. Then she said something else. She said, "Tell Maro she still owes me—" and did not finish, and I waited for the rest, and the rest did not come, but she was still breathing, she was still *there*, and I said, "Tell her yourself, stay with me, tell her yourself," and she seemed to consider this, and then the second burst came from the north, and I lost Maro in the noise, and when the noise stopped I had lost Maro in the other way, the permanent way, and Elin's eyes were closed but she was breathing, she was still breathing, and I packed more gauze and I pressed, and the tarp snapped against the post, and I pressed, and she breathed, and then she breathed once more, and then the contract between us expired.

I checked the time because that is what you do. You note the time. You log it. It has been thirty-one years and I can tell you it was 1437. I can tell you the light was white and direct and that there were no clouds and that her blood on my hands was darker than you think arterial blood should be. I can tell you that Dak, when I finally went to him, was face-down on the berm with his cheek against the stock of Tomil's rifle and his mouth slightly open, as if he had been about to say one more thing about the catch on the magazine.

Maro was behind the crate wall. She had gotten off eleven rounds. I counted the casings because counting was the only language I had left. Eleven. She was sixteen.

I did not yet know that Elin had been carrying crumbs in her pocket. I did not yet know what they were from. I found them later, when I did what I had to do, which was to check the pockets for identification and personal effects, and from Elin's left breast pocket I shook out a handful of brown crumbs, and I did not understand, and then I looked at the fuel tin, and then I understood, and I did not have a container for it, so I set it down, the way you set down a weight that has beaten you, and I moved on to the next task, which was Dak's pockets.

I said I would go backward. I'm going.

## 3.

The day before. Or the night before—time had become approximate by then, a currency we spent without counting. Five of them alive: Elin, Dak, Maro, Sule, and Pitar. And me, though I did not count myself, because the medic is the instrument, not the patient, and an instrument does not include itself in inventory.

Sule died at 0600 during a water run.

I need to say that again because the sentence is so ordinary it does not hold what happened. Sule died at 0600 during a water run. She was carrying two canteens to the pump station ninety meters west of the camp. She was not in a hurry. There was no incoming. The line had been quiet for nine hours and the quiet had started to feel structural, like a house you could almost live in. Sule walked out with two canteens and a song she was humming—I could hear her humming from the medical station, I could not identify the song, and this is another failure I carry—and at ninety meters a single shot from the eastern ridge took her in the neck.

She fell immediately. She did not crawl or call out. The canteens hit the ground and I heard them ring, aluminum on lithium flat, a bright empty sound, and then Pitar was running and I was shouting for Pitar to stop and Dak was pulling Pitar down and Pitar was fighting Dak because Pitar and Sule had something between them—not love exactly, not at fifteen, but the thing that would have become love if time had been provided, and time was not provided—and Elin had the binoculars and was saying, "She's down, she's down, I can see—wait, she's—no. She's down."

The shot had opened the carotid. I know this because I reached her eventually, low-crawling with Dak providing cover, and by the time I pressed gauze to the place where her blood had been, her blood had left. Ninety seconds, maybe less. The human body holds five liters. The carotid, fully severed, empties a body in the time it takes to boil an egg. I have used this comparison in training and I am sickened by it and I continue to use it because it is accurate and accuracy is the only kindness I know how to perform.

I dragged her back. Dak helped. We dragged her by the jacket and her helmet came off and her hair—her terrible hair, the haircut Maro had given her two nights before with the medical scissors, the haircut that was so uneven that Kess had said she looked like a sheep that got in a fight with a lawnmower, the haircut that Sule wore like a crown because Maro had given it to her and anything Maro touched was, by Sule's private theology, consecrated—her hair dragged in the dust and I saw the dust catch in it, and I have been in this profession for forty years and I have seen what I have seen and the dust in her hair is the thing that wakes me.

We brought her inside the perimeter. Pitar sat next to her and did not speak for two hours. The rest of them rotated watch and dealt with it in their specific, insufficient, teenage ways, which is to say:

Dak cleaned his rifle. He had cleaned it four hours before. He cleaned it again. The bolt carrier was spotless and he cleaned it again and no one told him to stop because everyone understood what the cleaning was.

Maro sat against the supply crates and wrote something on the inside of her wrist with a ballpoint pen. She would not show anyone what it said. She is sixteen and dead and I will never know what it said and I am aware that this is a small thing measured against the larger loss and I am telling you: there is no larger loss. It is all this size. It is all wrist-writing and hummed songs and haircuts and crumbs.

Elin found a fighter from the other side.

This was later, after Sule was wrapped in the tarp and placed by the northern wall, after Pitar had finally stood up and taken his watch position and Dak had finally stopped cleaning the rifle. Elin was moving along the outer berm checking sight lines—her job, she had made it her job—and she called me over in a voice that was not urgent but was *careful*, the way you would call someone over to see an animal you didn't want to startle.

Behind the berm, in a shallow depression that might have been a foxhole or might have been just a place where the lithium had sunk, there was a body. A fighter from the other side. Young.
