# The Roster

---

## I.

The ink has to be ground fresh each morning now. The stick has worn to a sliver no wider than a chopstick, and the stone is chipped along one edge where it fell from the desk during the Nōbi earthquake three years ago—or was it four. The hand that holds the stick and turns it in slow circles against the wet stone is spotted and ridged along the tendons, and the man it belongs to does not recognize it most mornings, but the grinding is the same. Has always been the same. The motion is older than the Meiji government, older than the telegraph poles lining the Sapporo road outside his window, older than the name he uses now, which is not the name he used then. The grinding asks nothing. The grinding is the first discipline of the day, and Nagakura Shinpachi, aged sixty-one, formerly Captain of the Second Division of the Shinsengumi, has never in his life skipped the first discipline of the day.

The journals are spread before him on a writing desk that wobbles unless he folds a piece of cardboard beneath the northern leg. There are eleven of them. The earliest is water-damaged from the retreat through Aizu—the lower third of every page a brown watercolor of dissolution, the characters there visible only if he tilts the paper toward the window at a precise angle he discovered by accident fourteen years ago and has performed identically since. The middle journals are the most intact. The last two are fragments: pages torn out, pages used for kindling during the march to Hakodate, pages he cannot account for and suspects were taken by someone during the brief period of his imprisonment, though he has never determined by whom or for what purpose. It does not matter now. What remains is sufficient. What remains is, in fact, more than sufficient, in the way that a knife need only be so sharp before additional sharpness becomes a quality relevant only to the knife.

He opens the first journal to the roster page. He has copied this roster eleven times in eleven years, each time into a cleaner document, each time correcting an error of memory or adding a detail recovered from the silt. Today he will copy it a twelfth time, onto ministerial paper provided by a young historian from Tokyo Imperial University who visited last month with a Western-style recording device he did not use and a set of questions he did not finish asking. The young man wanted the story. Nagakura offered him the roster. The young man looked disappointed. Nagakura did not explain that they were the same thing.

He writes:

*Kondō Isami. Commander. Tama district, Musashi Province. Farmer's son adopted into the Kondō line. Tennen Rishin-ryū. Joined: founding. Departed: captured at Nagareyama, Fourth Month, First Year of Meiji. Beheaded at Itabashi. The head was displayed. I did not see it.*

*Hijikata Toshizō. Vice-Commander. Tama district, Musashi Province. Farmer's son, medicine peddler in youth. Tennen Rishin-ryū. Joined: founding. Departed: killed by gunfire at Hakodate, Fifth Month, Second Year of Meiji. I was fifty chō distant. I heard the volley but could not distinguish it from the others.*

*Okita Sōji. Captain, First Division. Shirakawa, Mutsu Province. Tennen Rishin-ryū, licensed master at fifteen. Joined: founding. Departed: died of tuberculosis in Edo, Thirtieth day of Fifth Month, Fourth Year of Keiō. He was twenty-five or twenty-seven depending on which record you trust. He was not present for any of it after Toba-Fushimi, and toward the end he could not grip a sword, and his page in this roster should be written in a different-colored ink because his death belonged to no battle and no code but only to his lungs, which were always his lungs, which were failing already at Ikedaya when I watched him fold in half mid-stroke and come up smiling with blood on his teeth, and I did not know then whether the blood was his or the other man's, and I did not ask, because there were things we had agreed not to name.*

He pauses. Re-reads. That is too much. In the clean copy he will trim it to two lines. Okita would have preferred two lines. Okita would have preferred a joke.

He continues.

*Yamanami Keisuke. General Secretary. Sendai, Mutsu Province. Hokushin Ittō-ryū. Joined: founding. Departed: ordered to commit seppuku for attempted desertion, Twenty-third day of Second Month, First Year of Keiō. He left a letter. The letter was not preserved.*

*Tōdō Heisuke. Captain, Eighth Division. Edo. Hokushin Ittō-ryū. Joined: founding. Departed: killed at Aburanokōji, Thirteenth day of Eleventh Month, Third Year of Keiō. See entry for Itō Kashitarō.*

He writes nineteen more names. Some take two lines. Some take only one. He records hometowns he is no longer certain of, marking these with a small circle in the margin—his notation for *approximate*. He records sword styles. He records dates of joining, though after the first forty men these become inexact, because the organization grew faster than the record-keeping, which was always Hijikata's responsibility, and Hijikata's hand was meticulous but Hijikata was also prosecuting a war, and wars do not wait for the brush to dry.

Beside each name, in a column as narrow and unyielding as a sword rack, he writes the manner of departure.

*Deserted.*

*Deserted.*

*Killed at Toba-Fushimi, Fourth day of First Month, Fourth Year of Keiō.*

*Executed by order for violations of Article Two.*

*Killed at Toba-Fushimi.*

*Died of tuberculosis, date uncertain.*

*Deserted.*

*Ordered to commit seppuku for visiting a brothel in the Shimabara district. He was nineteen.*

*Killed at Ueno.*

*Deserted.*

*Executed by order for violations of Article One.*

*Deserted. Joined Satsuma forces. Reported killed at Aizu but unconfirmed.*

*Killed at Toba-Fushimi.*

*Killed at Toba-Fushimi.*

*Killed at Toba-Fushimi.*

Three names in a row carrying the same four characters, and he writes them three times without variation, because the battle did not distinguish between the men and so the record should not either, even though one of them was left-handed and could split a practice dummy from shoulder to hip with a single diagonal stroke, and one of them kept a cat in the barracks in violation of no written rule but several unwritten ones, and one of them could not have been older than sixteen and had not yet, Nagakura suspects, been with a woman, though this is not a thing he recorded then or will record now.

The roster reaches one hundred and six names. He is certain he is missing at least eight. Possibly eleven. The missing names are worse than the dead ones, because the dead at least have their notation, their coordinate in the document, and the missing have nothing—not even the dignity of a line drawn through their entry. They are simply not here. They walked into the Shinsengumi and at some point walked out of it and then walked out of Nagakura Shinpachi's capacity to account for them, and the eleven journals hold no trace, and the memory holds no trace, and whatever those men became after—farmers, merchants, soldiers under the new government, corpses in unmarked ground—they became it without reference to this roster, which is the only monument, which is not enough, which was never going to be enough.

He reaches a name near the bottom third of the list and the brush stops.

He is aware of the ink pooling at the tip. He is aware that if he does not lift the brush or continue the stroke, the ink will fall and blot the character he has just completed—the family name, two characters, written correctly, the proportions exact. The given name has not yet been written. His hand, which has not shaken for any of the hundred and five names preceding this one, is not shaking now either. It is simply not moving. It is holding its position above the paper with the terrible stillness of a man in a fighting stance who has perceived, in the same instant, both the opening and the consequences of taking it.

The ink falls. A black sun on the page, obscuring the family name, rendering the entry unreadable.

He sets the brush on the rest. He sits with his hands on his knees. Outside, a horse cart passes on the Sapporo road, its wheels finding every stone. A child is calling to another child in the clipped vowels of Hokkaido dialect, which still sounds foreign to him after twenty years, which will always sound foreign, because his ears were tuned in Kyoto and they will die tuned to Kyoto, and the last thing he hears will probably be this Hokkaido wind and he will mistake it, in the final moment, for the wind that came down from the Eastern hills and through the streets of the capital and into the training hall at Mibu where everything was still whole.

He pulls a fresh sheet toward him. He will copy the roster again. He will reach that name again. He does not know yet what he will do when he reaches it.

But there are one hundred and five names before it, and each of them requires its notation, and the ink must be ground fresh, and the light from the window is good for another three hours, and the first discipline of the day is the first discipline of the day.

---

## II.

They woke at the same hour. This must be understood first—before the raids, before the politics, before all of it—that they woke at the same hour every morning, in the same order, with the same complaints, in a compound that smelled of woodsmoke and mildew and the particular staleness of thirty men's sleeping quarters in summer and fifty men's sleeping quarters in winter, because the Shinsengumi grew in the cold months when masterless samurai had nowhere else to go and food was a stronger recruiter than ideology.

Nagakura was always the third man awake. He does not know who was first, because whoever it was had already finished and left no trace, but the second was invariably Harada Sanosuke, Captain of the Tenth Division, a man from Iyo Province who had once attempted seppuku over an insult and botched it so badly that the scar across his belly resembled a child's drawing of a river, and who displayed this scar freely at drinking parties as a sort of comedy routine, and who laughed louder than any man Nagakura has known before or since, and who died at Ueno in the Fifth Month of the Fourth Year of Keiō, fighting for a government that had already ceased to exist, because Harada Sanosuke was incapable of arriving anywhere after the commitment had ended, a quality Nagakura listed as a virtue and the new era would have listed as stupidity.

Harada would be in the kitchen, starting a fire with the grim efficiency of a man who believed that tea delayed was a form of moral injury. Nagakura would join him. They did not speak during the fire-building. This was not an agreement. It was simply that the fire required attention and the morning required silence and both men understood this without discussion, which is a form of intimacy that does not have a name in any language Nagakura speaks.

By the time the tea was ready, the compound would be stirring. Feet on the wooden walkways. The scrape of sliding doors. The particular groan—Nagakura could identify every man in the compound by the sound of his morning groan, a skill he has never disclosed and which would be useless to disclose because there is no one alive who would recognize the names—and then the training hall, where the wooden swords waited in their racks like an argument no one had started yet.

Morning practice was mandatory. This was Article Three of the code, or rather it was the implication of Article Three, which stated: *It is forbidden to leave the Shinsengumi.* The logic, which Hijikata had constructed with the precision of a man building a box to hold something volatile, ran thus: the Shinsengumi existed to fight; fighting required daily practice; therefore practice was existence; therefore absence from practice was departure from the Shinsengumi; therefore absence from practice was a violation of Article Three; therefore absence from practice was punishable by seppuku. The chain of reasoning was unassailable in the same way that a sword stroke delivered with perfect form to the neck of a kneeling man is unassailable: the logic is in the result.

They practiced in pairs. The wooden swords clacked in rhythms that varied by style—the Tennen Rishin men with their heavy centered strikes, the Ittō-ryū practitioners with their single-point focus, the eclectic fighters like Nagakura himself who had trained in too many schools and settled, finally, on the principle that the correct style was the one that left you standing. The hall filled with sound in layers: the strikes, the breathing, the bare feet on polished wood, and beneath it all, nearly inaudible but present the way a river is present beneath a bridge, the coughing.

Okita always coughed.

Not dramatically. Not in the way the kabuki theaters would later portray it, with their pale-faced actors clutching handkerchiefs and sinking to their knees as cherry blossoms fell from concealed baskets in the rafters. Okita coughed the way a man with bad lungs coughs—frequently, involuntarily, between sentences, between strikes, with a slight hitch in the rhythm of whatever he was doing that he corrected so quickly you could almost believe you had imagined it. He coughed and continued the stroke. He coughed and finished the joke. He coughed and won the bout, because Okita Sōji had been the finest swordsman in the Shinsengumi from the day it was founded to the day his body physically could not lift the blade, and his lungs were a problem the way an earthquake is a problem—something happening below the surface that the surface refused to acknowledge.

"Nagakura-san." This was Okita's voice at morning practice on a day Nagakura does not assign a date to because it happened on many days and therefore no particular day, which is how memory works for the things that mattered most—they lose their dates and become weather. "Nagakura-san, your grip is too high again. You hold the sword like a man trying to keep a fish from escaping."

"My grip has killed eleven men."

"Your grip has killed eleven men *in spite of itself.* Imagine what a correct grip might do."

"I imagine it would feel unnatural and I would die in confusion."

"Ah. Then keep the fish grip. The Shinsengumi cannot afford to lose its second-best swordsman to self-improvement."

"Second-best."

"I am being generous. Saitō might dispute even that."

This was how Okita loved: through correction. Every adjustment he offered—the angle of a wrist, the placement of a foot, the height of a grip—was a sentence in a language he spoke more fluently than Japanese, and the sentence always meant the same thing: *I would like you to survive.* He corrected everyone. He corrected Nagakura's grip every morning for three years. Nagakura never changed his grip. This was his response in the same language, and it meant: *I hear you, and I am who I am, and we will proceed on that basis,* and Okita heard it, and the next morning he corrected the grip again, because that was the practice, and the practice was the bond, and the bond was the practice.

Hijikata inspected at the half-hour mark. He entered the training hall the way a blade enters a sheath—with absolute directional certainty and no wasted space. His eyes moved across the rows of practicing men like a brush across a ledger, noting, calculating, recording in the accounts of a bank whose only currency was discipline and whose interest rate was death. He did not praise. Praise, in Hijikata's economy, was the absence of criticism, and criticism was delivered not through words but through a quality of attention—a pause in the scanning, a fractional narrowing of the eyes, a silence directed at a specific man that was louder than any reprimand and more frightening because it contained no information about what the specific failure was, only the certainty that a failure existed and had been observed and would be remembered.
