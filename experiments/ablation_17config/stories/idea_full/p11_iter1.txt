# The Brand on the Throat

## I.

The bus drops her at Sasang, and the city enters through her skin before she can stop it.

Not through sight — she has been looking at Busan from the mountain road for the last forty minutes, watching the skyline clarify from haze to geometry, and that was manageable, that was still landscape. What breaks the decade open is the bus terminal's particular pressure: bodies at every angle, the hydraulic sigh of luggage compartments, a child screaming not in pain but in the formless complaints of overstimulation, and under everything the dense marine humidity that Busan wears in September like a second city laid over the first. She steps off the bus and her shoulders rise two inches. She catches them. Lowers them. Breathes from the floor of her diaphragm the way she has trained herself to breathe — four counts in, the air drawn not into the chest but into the bowl of the pelvis, held there, released through the teeth in a thin controlled stream that her master once compared to the sound of a door closing in an empty house.

She does not think *master*. She thinks *Kwon Seung-ho* and feels the name land where it always lands, in the space just below the xiphoid process, a fist-weight of obligation that has become so familiar she would miss it the way the tongue misses a pulled tooth.

The terminal crowd moves around her. A man with a roller bag clips her elbow and she is, for one half-second, in a stance — left foot withdrawn, right hand open, weight already transferred to the back leg — before the conscious mind catches the animal one and cancels it. She straightens. Adjusts the strap of her pack. No one noticed. That is the advantage of being a woman of moderate height with no visible weapons and the kind of face people's eyes move over without catching: the readiness lives beneath the skin, and the skin says nothing.

She has a list. Three names, three locations, gathered over fourteen months from the edges of a world she left ten years ago. The list is not written down. It sits in her memory the way forms sit, kinetic and sequential, each name opening into a geography: Jun-ho in Haeundae, Pilkyu somewhere in Gamcheon-dong, Miran near the port. She has repeated the list so many times during the mountain years that the names have worn smooth, abstracted from the people they belong to — have become more like coordinates than identities, waypoints on the clean line between where she is and where Dok-hyun will die.

She walks through the terminal. The fluorescent light is aggressive after the mountain's modulated grays. Her eyes adjust. Her hand rises to her throat, touches the ridge of tissue there — two inches of scar running diagonally from below the left ear toward the clavicle, healed white and hard and slightly raised, the texture of candle wax pressed flat. It is not a gesture of remembering. She does not need to remember; the scar remembers for her, waking her some nights with its phantom heat, a sensation less like pain than like the skin trying to speak. The touch is the same gesture as checking for a wallet, for keys. She touches it to confirm it is still there, still saying what it says.

*I was held down. A heated blade was drawn across my throat. The man who did it believed I would die in the mountains even if the burn did not kill me, and he was nearly right, and he was wrong.*

She finds the subway entrance. She has studied the transit map from a PC bang in Miryang, memorizing transfer points. She descends.

Underground, the crowd compresses. Rush hour is ending but its residue is dense — salarymen with loosened ties, high school girls with identical bags, an elderly woman with a wheeled cart full of scallions. Sae-jin positions herself at the end of the platform with her back to the tiled wall. She does not stand in the center of open spaces. She does not let strangers behind her. These are not choices; they are the architecture of a nervous system rebuilt in solitude, a body that learned to read wind patterns for threat and now applies the same grammar to subway platforms. The train arrives. She boards. She counts exits: two per car, plus the inter-car connectors, plus the emergency windows marked with their tiny red hammers. She sits.

The train moves. Busan scrolls past in flashes where the tunnel opens to elevated track — apartment towers stacked up the hillsides like offerings on an altar, cranes at the port swinging containers with the slow deliberation of giant birds feeding. She watches the city and feels, against her discipline, the hook of it. She was twenty-two when she left. She is thirty-two now. The decade between sits in her body as a specific weight, distributed primarily in the hands and the hips — the hands thickened from striking wood and stone, the hips carrying the dense tension of ten thousand hours of horse stance held past failure into a second country of endurance she did not know existed until the mountain showed it to her.

She gets off at Nampo. She walks six blocks, inventorying the street: which shops have back exits, which alleys connect to which, where the CCTV cameras perch on their poles like patient birds. She finds a *yeogwan* with a hand-painted sign and pays cash for a week. The room is small: vinyl floor, a folded blanket, a window that looks onto a wall. She sets her pack down. She sits on the floor in the posture she has sat in every day for ten years — seiza, hands on thighs, spine erect — and she studies the wall the way she once studied an opponent's center line, patiently, from a fixed position, looking for the opening.

The plan is clean. Find the three. Remind them of what they witnessed, what they owe, what they swore in the courtyard the morning after the funeral when the incense was still sweet in the rain. Then go together — four students, one debt — to find Dok-hyun and kill him. Not murder. Rectification. The code their master spent thirty years inscribing into their bodies has a provision for this, or so she has decided during the years when the only voice interpreting the code was hers. Dok-hyun violated the lineage. He killed the master. He scattered the school. He branded the one who tried to stop him, and left her for the mountain.

The math is simple. The justice is clear. The only thing between her and the completion of everything she has survived for is logistics.

She breathes. The wall offers nothing. She closes her eyes and runs the plan again, the way she runs a form — each movement following the last with the certainty of water finding the line of descent. It is clean. It is righteous. She would bet her life on it. She already has.

Outside the window, Busan hums its ten million voices, and none of them are hers.

## II.

She finds Jun-ho on the second day, because Jun-ho is not hiding.

The address led her to Haeundae, to a block where the old fish-smell neighborhood has been hollowed and rebuilt into cafés and fitness studios and a franchise chicken restaurant with a cartoon rooster flexing a human bicep. The gym occupies a corner storefront with full plate-glass windows — a deliberate transparency, an advertisement of the body's work. The sign reads **KWON LEGACY MMA** in English and Korean, the font modern, the logo a stylized fist enclosed in a circle that she recognizes, after a moment's delay that feels like falling, as a simplified version of the school's seal. The seal their master brushed with ink on mulberry paper. The seal that hung above the training hall's shrine.

She stands across the street, partially screened by a delivery truck, and watches.

Through the glass: a gym floor, matted, well-lit, the blue-white fluorescent light flattening everything into the textures of a catalog photograph. Heavy bags along the far wall. A cage in the back corner — an octagon, competition-sized, its chain-link catching the light. Along the near wall, a front desk, a water cooler, a rack of branded merchandise. And behind the desk, framed, the photograph.

She knows the photograph. She took it, or someone beside her took it, at the autumn demonstration fifteen years ago. Kwon Seung-ho stands in front of the school with his students arrayed behind him, his expression carrying the specific dignity of a man who has spent his life building a single thing and is showing it intact. In the photo his hands are at his sides. She knows without being able to see it from this distance that his right index finger is crooked from a break that never set properly, and that he used to joke it pointed toward the ground to remind him where everyone ends up.

Beside the photograph, a banner: **Rooted in Tradition. Built for the Ring.**

She watches.

Jun-ho emerges from the back office. He is thirty-four now and he looks it — not aged, but *established*, the lanky build she remembers filled out through the shoulders and neck, his hair cropped close, his movements carrying the easy authority of a man in his own space. He walks onto the gym floor and claps his hands once, and the class — a dozen men and women in compression shirts and shin guards — contracts around him like a school of fish responding to the flick of a lead body. He speaks. She cannot hear through the glass. He demonstrates a combination: jab, cross, low kick, the kick delivered with an L-step she recognizes because Kwon Seung-ho drilled it into them until the foot's path was involuntary, but the L-step is followed by something else, something borrowed — a Thai clinch entry, the hands catching an imaginary skull and pulling it down into the rising knee, and the combination that began in their master's lineage ends in another country's grammar entirely. The class copies him. The sound of shins hitting pads comes through the glass muffled and rhythmic, like someone beating a rug.

She watches for forty minutes. She should enter. She should cross the street, push through the glass door, and speak his name in the voice he will remember, the voice that called forms in the courtyard. She does not move. What holds her in place is not fear but something she does not yet have a name for — a growing pressure in the space below her ribs, the same space where the master's name lives, but this pressure is hot and shapeless and does not respond to breathing.

A woman comes through the front door. Sae-jin sees her from behind first: mid-height, dark hair pulled back, a canvas bag over one shoulder, moving with the unhurried gait of someone arriving at a place that is already hers. The woman sets her bag behind the front desk. She adjusts the thermostat on the wall — a small, proprietary gesture, the gesture of someone who knows this room's ideal temperature. She exchanges words with a trainer near the water cooler, and the trainer laughs, and the woman's posture carries the laugh forward into her next movement with the ease of a person who is, simply, comfortable.

She crosses the gym floor toward Jun-ho. She passes behind him as he corrects a student's guard, and as she passes she leans in and kisses him on the line of the jaw, a kiss that is brief and absent and utterly habitual, the kiss of someone who has kissed this particular jaw so many times that the gesture has migrated from intention to reflex. Jun-ho does not break his instruction. His hand finds the small of her back for one second, a reply in the same offhand language, and she continues past him toward the back office.

As she turns, Sae-jin sees her face.

She knows this face. It is ten years older and the proportions have subtly changed — the jaw has sharpened, the girlish roundness resolved into something definite — but the face is immediately, cellularly legible to Sae-jin because it is a variation on the face she has spent ten years learning to hate.

Dok-hyun's sister. Yeon-seo. Who was seventeen the last time Sae-jin saw her, standing in the courtyard with her hand over her mouth while the school burned and her brother stood over the master's body with the calm of a man who had planned this moment the way farmers plan for harvest.

Yeon-seo disappears into the back office. Through the glass, the class continues. Jun-ho adjusts a student's elbow angle with two fingers, precise and patient, and the gesture is their master's gesture, stolen and repurposed, kept alive in a body that has sold it.

Sae-jin crosses the street. Not toward the gym. Away.

She walks for twenty minutes before she realizes she is not navigating. Her body has chosen a direction and her legs are following and her hands are shaking. She stops in a convenience store doorway and breathes. Four counts in. Hold. Release through the teeth. The door closing in the empty house. But the house is not empty; the house is full of Jun-ho's confident voice muffled through plate glass, and the slap of feet on mats, and the photograph in its frame, and the small kiss on the jaw that landed on Sae-jin's chest like a brand finding the same skin twice.

Her hand is on her throat.

She lowers it.

She catalogs what she knows: Jun-ho is not in hiding. Jun-ho is not in grief. Jun-ho has taken their master's techniques and cut them apart and reassembled them inside a commercial cage, and hung the master's photograph on the wall like a certificate of provenance, and taken Dok-hyun's sister into his bed or his life or both, and this building — this glass-fronted, fluorescent-lit, protein-powder-smelling building with its franchise brightness and its branded hand wraps — is what their master's life has become. Not a memorial. A product.

She does not yet think: *perhaps Jun-ho found a way to keep the art alive in the only form the world will sustain.* She does not think: *perhaps Yeon-seo is not her brother.* She does not think: *perhaps the kiss on the jaw is something ordinary and decent, a woman touching the man she loves.*

What she thinks is: *it is worse than I knew.* And the plan, which was clean, acquires a new vector of dirt, and the dirt feels like confirmation.

## III.

Miran works at a bakery in Jung-gu, seven blocks from the harbor. Sae-jin finds it on the fourth day.

The bakery is narrow and bright, wedged between a stationery shop and a shuttered real estate office.
