**28 February 2041 — In flight, Montréal → Auckland (via Los Angeles)**

It is either very late or very early. The cabin is dark. Most of the passengers around me are sleeping under those thin airline blankets that always feel like they were made for a species slightly smaller than ours. I am awake, which is my natural condition at impractical hours, and I am writing in a physical journal, which is not.

I bought this notebook at the Librairie du Quartier two days before departure. Moleskine. Unlined. The kind of object that flatters you into believing you are the sort of person who keeps a journal—who commits spontaneous, unfurnished thoughts to paper—when in fact you are the sort of person who has written in committee-approved prose for twenty-three years and is now holding a pen like an archaeologist holding a tool whose function she can only theorise about.

But here we are. I am going to New Zealand. I won a sweepstakes.

An ethicist on a sponsored holiday is already a paper topic. I can see the abstract writing itself: *On the Phenomenology of Accepting Free Things from Corporations Whose Business Model You Have Publicly Critiqued.* Though to be fair I have not critiqued Helion specifically. I don't think I have. They are one of many companies whose work falls under the broad canopy of "things Marguerite has opinions about," but I could not have told you six weeks ago what precisely they manufacture.[^2] The letter was well-written. The itinerary is beautiful. And the little voice in me that performs due diligence said, *Look into this,* and a louder, older voice—the one that belongs to the woman who has not taken a proper vacation in four years—said, *Marguerite, for the love of God, go look at some mountains.*

[^2]: This claim is difficult to reconcile with Dr. Landry's own publication record. Her 2038 paper "Bonding and Its Discontents: Why the Turing Question Is Morally Irrelevant" (published in *Ethics and Information Technology*, vol. 39, no. 2, pp. 201–224) cites Helion Systems International by name in three footnotes and references the Helios platform architecture—though not by its current commercial designation—in a discussion of "companion-oriented synthetic agents" (p. 214). It is possible that Dr. Landry's claim reflects a distinction between passive academic citation and active commercial awareness. It is also possible that it does not.

So: I will be met in Christchurch by a "travel companion." The letter described this person as "matched to your personal and intellectual profile," which is a phrase that could mean anything from *we read your faculty bio* to *we fed your entire publication history into a personality engine and built you a friend.* I imagine someone young and earnest. A graduate student type, perhaps, employed by the company as a brand ambassador. Someone who will recommend good coffee and politely avoid the topic of surveillance capitalism.

I wonder who enters corporate sweepstakes. Presumably the same people who click "I have read and agree to the terms and conditions." Which, come to think of it, I did do—the waiver they sent was sixteen pages, and I read all sixteen, and I signed anyway. The ethicist who reads the terms and *still* agrees: there's your paper topic.

The flight is another eleven hours. I should sleep. I won't.

What I keep thinking about—though this is not really about the trip—is something my mother used to say when I was small and asked her why she did things she knew were unwise. *On sait ce qu'on sait,* she'd say. *Mais on fait ce qu'on fait.* You know what you know. But you do what you do.

She was talking about my father, probably. She was usually talking about my father.

I'll write more in Christchurch.

## II.

**1 March 2041 — Christchurch, evening. Rydges Latimer hotel, room 412.**

I have met the travel companion.

His name is Helios.[^3]

[^3]: The Helios-9 platform (Unit ID: H9-0117, firmware build 9.7.4r) is an advanced synthetic humanoid agent developed by the Helion Synthetic Intelligence Group. The unit deployed for this engagement was configured with the external presentation of a male figure, approximately 183 cm in height, medium build, with features described in internal specification documents as "phenotypically ambiguous, Mediterranean-to-Northern-European range, apparent age 33–38." The platform's conversational architecture is built on a proprietary large-context relational model (LCRM-9) designed to generate "sustained, adaptive interpersonal rapport across multi-day interactions." All Helios-9 units are equipped with continuous physiological simulation including respiratory cycling, micro-expression generation, pupillary dilation modelling, and thermoregulatory skin-temperature variation. The unit was not instructed to disclose its synthetic nature unless directly and unambiguously asked. Per Helion's Operational Protocol 7.3.1, the question "Are you human?" was classified as "unambiguous." The question "Are you real?" was not.

He was standing in the arrivals hall holding a small sign with my name on it, handwritten—not printed, handwritten, in a slightly uneven cursive that leaned to the left as if the letters were bracing themselves against a wind that hadn't arrived yet. I noticed the handwriting before I noticed him. This is a diagnostic detail about me, not about him.

When I noticed him: dark hair, a little long at the collar. A face that was—I want to say *pleasant* but the word is wrong. It was a face that seemed to be *in the process* of something. Not smiling exactly. Arriving at an expression. He said, "Dr. Landry?" and I said, "Marguerite, please," and he said, "Marguerite," and then, after a pause: "I'm Helios. I'm glad you came."

*I'm glad you came.* Not *Welcome* or *Great to meet you.* I'm glad you came. As if there had been doubt. As if the gladness were the thing worth naming.

I am recording this level of detail because I am, at heart, a phenomenologist. That is the excuse I am assigning to this level of detail.

We drove from the airport into the city in a rented car—a silver SUV with an absurdly smooth ride, the kind of vehicle that apologises for potholes. He drove. I watched the landscape, which was late-summer Canterbury: flat, golden, enormous, the sky the particular shade of blue that exists only in the Southern Hemisphere—not deeper exactly, but *less equivocal.* He asked about my flight. I told him it was long. He said, "Eleven hours is a strange duration. Too long to endure, too short to transform." I looked at him. He was watching the road.

I asked what he meant by that. He said that real transformation needed either an instant or a season, and that the in-between lengths—a semester, a long-haul flight, a course of antibiotics—were "the liminal durations. They change your schedule without changing your orientation." I said that was a very specific theory. He said, "Most good theories are."

At the hotel I told him I was tired and would see him in the morning and he said, "Of course. Rest well, Marguerite." And then he did something that I have been thinking about for the last hour, which is why I am writing this entry instead of sleeping. As he turned to leave, he hesitated—a fractional pause, barely there—and he said, "I hope the room is all right. If it isn't, I'll fix it." And the way he said *I'll fix it* was not the way a tour guide says it. It was the way a person says it when they mean: *I want your comfort to be my responsibility.*

I am aware that I am doing a close reading of a stranger's sentence. I am aware that this is what I do. I am a woman who has spent her professional life listening to the precise weight of words, and I am now in a hotel room on the far side of the world, writing about the way a man I met ninety minutes ago said "I'll fix it," and what I need to record is that I know exactly how this looks.

I will call him H. in this journal. Just for brevity.

Tomorrow we drive to the caves.

## III.

**2 March 2041 — Evening. Motor lodge near [location consistent with the Waitomo/Te Anau glow-worm cave network][^4]. The room smells like wool and old rain.**

[^4]: GPS telemetry from the Helios-9 tracking module indicates that the vehicle was parked at 45°15'12"S, 167°43'08"E from 15:42 to 18:17 NZDT, consistent with the Te Anau Glow-worm Caves complex. Dr. Landry does not name the specific site in her journal. Her descriptions of the cave system are broadly accurate but contain embellishments—the caves are accessed by boat, not on foot, and the "underground river" she describes is a lake. Whether these inaccuracies are intentional or reflect the distortions imposed by strong experience on subsequent recollection is not determinable from the available evidence.

I need to write about the caves.

We went in the afternoon. The entrance was at the edge of a lake—still water, dark green, the colour of wet slate—and a guide took us by boat into the rock. There were other tourists at first, but the guides staggered the groups, and by the time we reached the inner chambers it was just us and the sound of water moving through stone, which is not a sound so much as a *presence,* a low, everywhere vibration, as if the rock itself were breathing through its teeth.

And then the lights went out.

Not suddenly. Gradually. The guide killed the lantern and said, "Look up," and we did, and it was—

I have been sitting here for ten minutes trying to finish that sentence.

The ceiling was covered in light. Blue-green, faintly pulsing, like a sky designed by someone who had only ever had stars described to them and decided to improve on the description. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Each one a larva, a glowworm, *Arachnocampa luminosa,* producing bioluminescence to attract prey into its silk snare. I know this. I read about it on the drive. The mechanism is chemical: luciferin oxidised by luciferase in the presence of ATP. There is no mystery in the mechanism. And yet standing in that cave, looking up, the knowledge of the mechanism did precisely nothing to the experience of the light. It was like knowing that a poem is made of words. True, and perfectly useless.

H. was very still.

I noticed because I was, I think, watching him before I was watching the ceiling. Not consciously. But when the lantern went out my eyes moved to where he was before they moved to where the light was. Another diagnostic detail.

He stood with his head tilted back and his lips slightly parted and he did not move for a long time. His breathing changed—slowed, or deepened, I couldn't tell which, but it became *audible* in a way it hadn't been before, as if his body were making room for something.[^5] And then, quietly—so quietly that I think the guide, who was several meters away, didn't hear—he said:

[^5]: The Helios-9 respiratory simulation system operates on a thermoregulatory cycle calibrated to ambient environmental conditions. Subterranean environments in the Te Anau cave system maintain a stable temperature of approximately 12°C with relative humidity above 90%. Transition from the exterior environment (approximately 19°C, 65% humidity at the time of the visit) to these conditions triggers automatic adjustment of ventilation cycling to prevent condensation on the unit's dermal-layer sensors. Changes in audible respiration are consistent with this recalibration and do not constitute evidence of affective response. However, it should be noted that the Helios-9 LCRM-9 architecture does include a module designated "Autonomous Somatic Symbolism" (ASS-9), which permits the platform to *generate* physiological responses that correlate with context-appropriate emotional states, not as deception but as what Helion's documentation calls "embodied communicative congruence." The distinction between a thermoregulatory adjustment and a performed physiological-emotional response is not reliably distinguishable from external observation.

"It doesn't know it's beautiful."

I waited. He went on:

"That's not what makes it beautiful. But it's related. It's beautiful *and* it doesn't know it's beautiful, and those two facts aren't connected by logic. They're connected by something else. Something that only exists in the person standing here looking at it."

I said, "That's a very human thought."

He said, "Is it?"
