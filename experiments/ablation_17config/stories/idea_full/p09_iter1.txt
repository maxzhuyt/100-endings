# The Tide Pool

## I.

The house was correct.

That was the word Marta used later, when her cousin asked what the family was like, because the Spanish she needed — a word for a place that had been prepared for something the way a stage is prepared for a play no one has rehearsed — didn't come. *Correct.* Everything in its place. Everything new.

She arrived on a Monday in June, driving the borrowed Civic up the coast road with the address written on a gas receipt because her phone had died somewhere past Ventura. The house sat above the beach on a kind of shelf, modern and flat-roofed and the color of fog, with floor-to-ceiling windows that gave the impression you could see through it from the road to the ocean. You couldn't. But the impression was there.

Mrs. Kimura-Ross met her at the door. Claire. She'd said on the phone to call her Claire. She was tall, narrow-shouldered, thirty-four or thirty-five, with the kind of face that looked like it had been designed by someone who'd been asked to draw "kind" — wide-set eyes, freckled nose, a mouth that rested in a near-smile. She wore linen. She was holding a mug of something that smelled like turmeric, and she set it on the entryway console without a coaster, which Marta noticed because everything else in the entryway had been placed with visible intention: a ceramic bowl for keys that had no keys in it, a row of hooks with a single canvas tote bag hanging from the one farthest left, a framed print of a wave that matched the actual waves visible through the hallway window behind it.

"You found it," Claire said. "I'm so glad. The GPS sends people to the Hendersons' if you don't take the second fork."

Marta had not taken the second fork. She had driven into the Hendersons' driveway, reversed out, and found the correct fork by process of elimination. She said, "No trouble."

The tour took twenty minutes. Claire narrated the house in the manner of a museum docent — informative, practiced, slightly nervous about silences. The kitchen: white oak cabinets, a brushed-nickel faucet that dispensed filtered water at three temperatures, a refrigerator whose interior Marta would study later and find organized like a paint chart. A row of organic purées in small glass jars, arranged not alphabetically or by ingredient but by color — greens fading to yellows fading to oranges fading to the purple of a single jar of beet — so that the shelf, when you opened the refrigerator, looked like a sunset. On the refrigerator door, held by a magnet shaped like Catalina Island, a handwritten schedule in green ink on cream card stock, laminated. The lamination had been done professionally. The edges were smooth.

6:00 – Wake/nurse or bottle
6:30 – Tummy time (mat in nursery, NOT on bed)
7:00 – Breakfast (see purée guide in pantry)
7:30 – Walk (see stroller notes)
8:15–9:45 – Nap #1 (white noise ON, blackout curtains CLOSED)

It went on. Every half hour until 7:00 p.m., when the entry read simply: *Bedtime routine (see binder).* There was a binder. Marta would find it later on the nursery bookshelf, between *The Happiest Baby on the Block* and a Japanese parenting book whose title she couldn't read; the binder was three inches thick, tabbed and color-coded, with sections labeled SLEEP, FEEDING, DEVELOPMENTAL MILESTONES, EMERGENCY CONTACTS, and, curiously, PHILOSOPHY.

The nursery was upstairs, second door on the left. Claire opened it with the air of someone unveiling a renovation. The walls were a shade of green Marta had seen in magazines — not mint, not sage, something in between that had probably required four sample swatches. A crib with organic cotton sheets. A glider in cream boucle. A mobile of felt animals — handmade, possibly Etsy, each animal slightly different from the others in a way meant to look artisanal rather than incompetent. On the dresser: a sound machine, a Himalayan salt lamp, a baby monitor with a screen so crisp it looked like a small television, and a framed black-and-white photograph of a newborn foot that Marta would later realize was the foot of the baby she'd been hired to care for.

The baby monitor had a twin in the master bedroom, another in the living room, a third in the kitchen, and a fourth in the downstairs bathroom, this last one mounted on the wall beside the towel rack with a bracket that had been screwed into the tile, which meant someone had drilled into the bathroom wall rather than go to the toilet without surveillance of the crib.

In the mudroom by the back door, side by side: a stroller — complicated, European, with knobby tires and a bassinet attachment and a rain cover still in its packaging — and a second stroller, still in its shipping box. The box was large enough that it partially blocked the door to the backyard. Someone had written on it in Sharpie: *DON'T RECYCLE — returning? — ask C.* The handwriting was not Claire's.

"Owen wanted options," Claire said, noticing Marta's gaze. "My husband. He researched them for, honestly, longer than I want to admit. He has a spreadsheet."

She said this the way people say things about their partners that are meant to convey affection but convey something else — something closer to the expression you make when you're describing a meal you're still full from.

The baby was in the living room in a bouncer, the automatic kind that vibrated at intervals designed, according to the binder, to mimic the vestibular stimulation of the womb. Her name was Lucie, with an *ie*, which Claire mentioned twice during the tour — "Lucie with an *i-e*, the French spelling, Owen's grandmother was French, well, Québécoise" — as though the distinction mattered. She was four months old. She had dense black hair and a face that was still arriving at itself, the features not yet committed to a final arrangement. She was wearing a onesie printed with small avocados, which Marta would learn was one of nine onesies in regular rotation, three of which featured produce.

Marta picked her up. The baby was lighter than expected and heavier than she looked, the way all babies are, like deceptive parcels. Lucie turned her face into Marta's collarbone and left a trail of something clear and viscous on the fabric of her shirt, and Marta — who had cared for four babies before this one, who had started babysitting at fourteen in apartments nothing like this house, who was twenty-two years old and doing this work because it paid and because she was good at it and because the coastal family from the app listing had offered a room and she needed a room — Marta felt the baby's breath against her neck and cataloged it without sentiment: fast, shallow, warm.

Owen came home at five. He was shorter than Claire and louder. He had the energy of a man who has recently begun exercising with new seriousness — visible in his posture, in the protein shake he carried, in the way he entered the house like someone arriving at a place he wanted to be seen arriving at. He shook Marta's hand with both of his.

"You're already here. That's great. That's amazing. We're so glad you're here."

He said *we're* as though he'd confirmed this with Claire by some mechanism Marta had not observed.

They ate dinner together that first night — the three adults, the baby asleep upstairs, the monitor glowing green on the kitchen island between the salad and the grain bowl. The conversation was the kind Marta recognized from other first dinners with other families: questions about her experience delivered as casual interest, answers received with nods that were slightly too enthusiastic. She told them about the Orozco twins she'd cared for in Glendale, the single father in Mar Vista whose daughter had colic. They told her about their jobs — Claire was a brand strategist who worked from home three days a week; Owen did something with data for a startup whose function Marta immediately forgot.

At one point Owen stood to refill his water, paused behind Claire's chair, and placed his hand on her shoulder. Claire reached up and touched his hand. They held the pose for exactly the length of time it would take to photograph it, and then Owen continued to the refrigerator and Claire continued talking about the baby's sleep regression, and Marta, watching from across the island, registered the interaction the way she registered the laminated schedule and the second stroller in its box and the baby monitor bolted to the bathroom wall — as a feature of the household landscape, noted, not yet interpreted.

She moved into the guest room above the garage that night. The sheets were high-thread-count. The towels were folded into thirds. On the nightstand, a small succulent, a packet of earplugs still in cellophane, and a handwritten note on the same cream card stock as the schedule:

*Welcome, Marta! We're so happy you're here. Lucie is lucky to have you. — C & O*

The handwriting was Claire's. The message was plural.

Marta put the note in the nightstand drawer, plugged in her phone, and lay in the bed that was nicer than any bed she'd slept in, in a room that was nicer than any room she'd rented, in a house where everything was correct and nothing — she could not have said this yet, could not have assembled the observation into language — nothing was warm.

## II.

The first week was calibration. The second was pattern.

By the third week, Marta could predict which parent would enter which room at which time with the accuracy of someone who has memorized a bus schedule. Claire left for her office — a converted garden shed with a standing desk, visible from the kitchen window — at 8:15 every Monday, Wednesday, and Friday, carrying a laptop and the turmeric drink. Owen left for downtown at 7:40 every weekday, except he worked from home on Tuesdays, which meant he spent Tuesdays in the second bedroom he called his office, emerging at unpredictable intervals to do things with the baby that Marta came to recognize as *demonstrations.*

On a Tuesday in her third week, Marta was feeding Lucie in the kitchen — mashed sweet potato, actual, not from the jars — when Owen appeared in the doorway. He was holding his phone at an angle that Marta knew, from the Orozcos, was the angle of a person preparing to take a photograph of something they intended to appear candid.

"Hey, little bug," he said.

He crossed the kitchen. He took Lucie from the high chair — mid-bite, sweet potato on her chin, Marta's spoon in the air — and held her against his chest with one arm while reaching for something in the cabinet with the other. A pacifier. He put the pacifier in her mouth. Lucie, who had been eating, began to cry.

"Shh, shh, I know," Owen said. He bounced. He paced. He did the things fathers do on television, the sway-and-shush, the slow walk to the window and back, and Marta, standing with the spoon, watched him watch the doorway. He was waiting. The bouncing was oriented toward the hall. After a minute Claire's footsteps came down the stairs — she'd been in the master bedroom, on a call — and she appeared in the kitchen in her stocking feet, phone against her shoulder.

"Oh," Claire said. She mouthed *sorry* into the phone, then said aloud, covering the receiver: "Is she okay?"

"She's great," Owen said. He was holding Lucie with a competence that had materialized like weather. His posture had changed. "Just fussy. I've got her."

Claire smiled at him — genuine, or what looked like genuine, which Marta was beginning to understand might be a distinction without a useful difference in this house — and went back upstairs. Owen held the baby for another thirty seconds, then set her back in the high chair with the efficiency of someone checking a task off a list.

"She hates the pacifier," he told Marta. "Don't let her have it."

He went back to his office. Marta wiped the sweet potato off Lucie's chin and resumed the feeding, and Lucie ate with the sudden appetite of a baby who has been briefly, unnecessarily interrupted.

It happened again. It kept happening. The pattern was clearest on Tuesdays, when both parents were home, but it operated in diluted form throughout the week — in the evenings, on weekends, whenever the two of them occupied the same domestic space with the baby as a third point in the triangle. Owen picked Lucie up when Claire entered a room. He set her down when Claire left. Not always. Not mechanically. But often enough that Marta, by the fourth week, began tracking it the way she tracked nap durations and ounces consumed, as data that might eventually explain something.

The refrigerator notes were Claire's domain. Marta found the first one on a Wednesday morning, written on a square of paper torn from a small notepad printed with lemons:

*She LOVED the avocado! Two full servings!! 🥑*

Marta had fed Lucie avocado the previous evening. Lucie had taken one exploratory taste, compressed her face into an expression of philosophical betrayal, and ejected the avocado onto the wall behind the high chair with a force that seemed hydraulic. Marta had cleaned the wall with a sponge while Lucie ate banana instead. Claire had been in the kitchen for the first two minutes of this, sitting at the island answering emails on her laptop, before saying, "I think you've got this," and retreating to the living room. Marta had heard the television turn on.

The note was addressed to no one and signed by no one, but it was written for Owen, who opened the refrigerator for his protein shake every morning at 6:50, and who would see the note and its exclamation points and its avocado emoji, and who would absorb the information — *the baby loved the avocado* — and file it in whatever internal catalog he maintained of evidence that his household was functioning, that his daughter was thriving, that the organic purées arranged like a sunset on the second shelf were not decorative but consumed.

Marta read the note. She looked at the wall, which was clean. She said nothing.

The notes continued. They appeared two or three times a week, always in Claire's handwriting, always on the lemon paper, always narrating the baby's experience in language that performed both maternal attentiveness and effortless success:

*Lu slept 3 hours this afternoon — new record! Growing girl!*

(Lu had slept ninety minutes. Marta had been in the room for all of them, reading on her phone with one hand on the baby's back.)

*Tummy time was a HIT today. She almost rolled over!!*

(Tummy time had lasted four minutes before Lucie screamed. She had not almost rolled over. She had pressed her face into the mat and wept.)

*She smiled at the neighbor's dog! Think she might be a dog person 🐶*

(The dog had frightened her. Marta had carried her inside.)

Each note was a small fiction, and each fiction had the same subject: a baby who was doing well, a household where things were working, a mother who had been present for the moments that mattered. The notes were not lies, exactly. They were adjacent to what had happened, the way a portrait is adjacent to a face. They captured something real — Lucie existed, she ate food, she slept, she encountered dogs — and they edited everything else.
