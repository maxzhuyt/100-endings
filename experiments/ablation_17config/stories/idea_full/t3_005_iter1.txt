# The Grünsee Chronicle

### Being An Abridged Account of the Forty-Third Registered Aussetzung, Conducted Under the Authority of the Imperial Cartographic Society, Involving the Minor Surveyor-Candidate Liel Vásáry, Aged Twelve Years and Zero Days, and Comprising Observations on the Interior Terrain of the Grünsee Continent as Pertaining to the Subject's Subsequent Traverse, with Supplementary Field Notes Reproduced from the Recovered Journal (Archival Reference: GC-IV/2331/iii)

---

## I.

On the morning of the forty-third registered Aussetzung, which was also the morning of her twelfth naming day, the surveyor-candidate Liel Vásáry stood in the administrative courtyard of the imperial outpost at Südmessung and listened to a man she had never met read her future aloud from a leather-bound registry.

The official was a clerk of the Bureau of Interior Expeditions, Third Secretarial Division, and his name does not appear again in these records. He read the decree in the flattened cadence particular to his office, pausing at each sub-clause to allow the outpost notary — an elderly woman seated behind a folding campaign desk, her ink pot weighted against the coastal wind — to confirm the passage with a small nod. The decree noted that the candidate had been examined and found adequate; that her theoretical marks in triangulation, pace-count surveying, field sketching, and provisional cartography had met the threshold established by the Society's 1798 Expeditionary Standards (Third Revision); that her physical fitness had been assessed at no less than "serviceable" by the outpost physic; that her father, Senior Surveyor Second Class Márton Vásáry, had consented to the expedition in writing (form GS-17, countersigned); and that the continent known variously as the Grünsee, the Green Lake, the Interior, or (in older and now-suppressed vernacular usage) *der trauergrund* — the grief-ground — was prepared to receive her.

The morning was cold and clear. Liel stood at attention in the courtyard in the way she had been taught, feet together at a fifteen-degree splay, hands clasped behind her back with the right hand holding the left wrist. She wore the standard-issue expedition kit: grey canvas trousers hemmed three times to fit, a surveyor's jacket with pockets sewn at eleven points of access, leather boots one half-size too large to allow for growth. Her hair had been braided in a single plait down her back. Her father had done this himself, an hour before dawn, in the quarters they had shared since their assignment to Südmessung — the two-room apartment on the outpost's eastern terrace, overlooking the sounding pier and the channel markers. He had braided her hair with the same controlled, unhurried precision with which he calibrated his instruments, sectioning each portion with his fingers, maintaining even tension, tucking the ends beneath themselves so that nothing could work loose in weather. He had not spoken while he did this, and she had not asked him to.

(It is perhaps worth noting here, as a matter of contextual completeness, that the Grünsee continent — located some fourteen nautical miles offshore and visible from the outpost's upper terraces as a dark green continuity on the southern horizon — has been documented by the Society since the early surveys of 1743, though no expedition has successfully produced a stable map of its interior. The terrain is, in the clinical terminology adopted by the Bureau of Interior Expeditions following the Gärtner Commission of 1802, *affectively correspondent*: it reconfigures its own topography in continuous response to the interior psychic states of any human subject who enters it. The forest that Liel Vásáry is about to enter will arrange itself, with considerable and — the Commission's report concedes — distressing fidelity, into the precise landscape of her grief over her dead mother. This property of the terrain is indexed, archived, and, within the Society's operational framework, unremarkable. The candidate herself has not been informed. The Aussetzung protocols do not require disclosure. See Appendix C: "On the Pedagogical Value of Unmediated Encounter.")

Her father gave her his smallest compass — a brass pocket model with a cracked glass face, the hairline fracture running diagonally across the dial from west-northwest to east-southeast. He had carried it since his own apprenticeship. He had never repaired the glass because the crack did not affect the needle's movement, and because, as he had explained to Liel on multiple occasions when she had asked about it, a surveyor who required perfect equipment would produce nothing at all. He placed it in her right hand and closed her fingers around it. His hands were steady. His hands were always steady.

"Take bearings every two hundred paces," he said. "Record everything."

She said: "Yes, sir."

He said something else after that — or began to, the initial consonant audible to witnesses standing nearby — but the clerk, consulting his schedule, indicated that the gate time had arrived, and whatever Márton Vásáry intended to add to the record was replaced by the protocol's requirements.

Liel walked through the outpost's last measured gate, its stone posts carved with elevation markings and the Society's emblem — a theodolite superimposed upon a compass rose, circumscribed by the motto *Nihil Incognitum Patitur* ("It Suffers Nothing Unknown"). Past the gate, a gravel path descended forty meters to the shore, where a flat-bottomed survey launch waited to carry her across the channel. She descended. At the shoreline, just before the path gave way to shingle, she passed the final geodetic marker: a dressed-stone obelisk, waist-high, driven into the soil and stamped with its coordinate designation. Beyond it, there were no further markers. The measured world ended there, as cleanly as a ruled line on a page.

She did not cry. She did not look back.

This has been noted by subsequent commentators as evidence of uncommon resolve, or alternately of pathological dissociation, depending on the commentator's disciplinary commitments. The present account observes only that she boarded the launch without assistance and sat upright on the mid-bench with her rucksack between her knees and her father's compass already open in her palm, watching the needle swing and steady as the oarsman pulled them into the channel, toward the dark green treeline of the continent that was, even then, preparing for her arrival with the blind, procedural diligence of a body manufacturing a fever.

## II.

It is appropriate at this point, before the narrative enters the continent proper, to establish the relevant biographical data pertaining to the candidate.

Liel Vásáry was born in the administrative city of Oberhalt, the only child of Márton Vásáry (Senior Surveyor Second Class, Imperial Cartographic Society, assigned to the Coastal Geodetic Division) and Eszter Vásáry (née Halász, occupation: unregistered). The family was transferred to the outpost at Südmessung when Liel was six, following Márton's reassignment to the Grünsee coastal survey — a long-term project to establish fixed triangulation points along the continent's perimeter, a task that the Society regarded as both foundational and, given the continent's documented properties, essentially Sisyphean, though this latter assessment was confined to internal memoranda and not shared with field personnel.

Márton Vásáry was, by all personnel evaluations preserved in the Society's records, an exemplary surveyor: methodical, precise, reluctant to speculate beyond his data, and possessed of a capacity for repetitive fieldwork that his supervisors described variously as "invaluable," "remarkable," and, in one candid marginal note by his section chief, "faintly unnerving." He taught his daughter to read a compass before she could read a sentence. He showed her how to use a transit level at age seven. By eight she could pace-count distances to within four percent accuracy over uneven ground. By nine she could sketch a provisional field map of any room she entered, complete with scale bar and orientation arrow, in under three minutes. She loved his instruments with the passionate specificity that other children reserve for animals or particular adults: the satisfying mechanical resistance of the theodolite's tangent screw, the sun-warmed brass weight of the plumb bob, the surveyor's chain laid out in the morning grass with its hundred links gleaming like a ruled sentence. She copied maps in the outpost school with care that her tutor (himself a retired draftsman) described as "liturgical." She pressed so hard with her mapping pens that the nibs wore down at twice the expected rate, an expense her father absorbed without comment.

Her mother, Eszter, who was not a surveyor and not a member of the Society and who did not appear in any official capacity in any record the Society maintains, told Liel stories about the Grünsee's interior that contradicted every chart the outpost produced. She said, in the evenings, sitting on the apartment's narrow balcony with the green dark of the continent visible across the channel, that there were rivers inside that reversed their current depending on who was watching. She said there were forests where the trees migrated from hill to valley and back again over the course of a single night, leaving tracks in the soil like enormous slow animals. She said there were ruins that appeared only to people who were lost — not geographically lost but *lost* in the other sense, the sense that had no coordinates — and that these ruins were always, somehow, familiar, and that this was the most frightening thing about them, more frightening than if they had been strange. She said these things softly and with a quality of attention — her eyes focused on some distance that was not the distance to the continent's treeline — that suggested she was reporting, not inventing.

Liel did not like these stories. She did not say so directly but demonstrated her preference through the reliable mechanism of inattention, and later through correction: "Papa says rivers don't do that," or "That's not on any of the charts." Her mother received these corrections with a stillness that the present account cannot, at this remove, interpret with confidence — though it notes that Eszter Vásáry was not a woman given to argument, and that she stopped telling the stories approximately four months before she became ill, which was approximately eight months before she died.

The illness was a fever of uncertain etiology. The outpost physic treated it with quinine and cool compresses and, when these failed, with the honest admission that he was not equipped for whatever this was. Eszter Vásáry died in the smaller of the apartment's two rooms, in a bed positioned beneath a window that faced the continent. The date, duration of illness, and cause of death (listed as "pyretic failure, origin indeterminate") are preserved in the outpost's civil registry. Other details — the particular quality of the light in the room during the final days, the sound or absence of sound, what Liel did or did not say, what Márton did or did not do with his hands, which were always steady — are not.

Liel was ten. She continued her studies at the outpost school. Her cartographic work, her tutor noted, improved markedly in the months following the death — the lines cleaner, the annotations more precise, the scale bars ruled with a perfectionism that left no tolerance for error. She was entered into the Aussetzung candidacy registry four months later, on her father's signature.

Six months after Eszter's death, Liel stopped mentioning her. This cessation was not abrupt but procedural, a gradual reduction in frequency — as one might observe in a data set from which a variable has been removed — until the references fell to zero and held there. The present account speculates, with the caution appropriate to posthumous psychological inference, that this was not, as such, an act of grief. It was an act of *filing*. Eszter Vásáry could not be located on any chart. She could not be given coordinates, or a triangulated position, or a spot elevation. She could not be indexed. And within the system that Liel had adopted — her father's system, the Society's system, the system of the grid and the compass and the ruled line — that which could not be indexed could not, in any operational sense, be said to exist.

(She did exist. The present account, which is not in the habit of such interpolations and which wishes to note for the record its discomfort at the necessity, confirms this.)

## III.

The launch deposited Liel on a shingle beach on the continent's northern shore at 07:42. The oarsman — a contract laborer, not Society personnel — did not accompany her past the waterline. He told her to keep the shore at her back and walk south, which was the instruction he gave to all Aussetzung candidates, and which was, given the continent's properties, neither accurate nor inaccurate but simply irrelevant, in the way that telling someone to keep their childhood at their back and walk forward is both a navigable instruction and a profound misunderstanding of how memory works. Liel thanked him (she had been taught to thank service personnel; it was in the manual) and walked south.

She entered the forest at 08:15, by her own reckoning.

By 09:00, the trees had begun to grow architecture.

It was not immediate. The first indications were structural rhymes — a trunk that forked at a right angle, suggesting a doorframe; a root system that rose from the ground in a series of parallel ridges that resembled, if one were inclined to such resemblances (Liel was not), the rungs of a staircase. These features were, in isolation, explicable. Trees do fork. Roots do ridge. A surveyor trained in the discipline of objective observation would note the formations, record them, and assign them to the category of natural variation.

Liel noted the formations. She recorded them. She assigned them.

By 10:30, the formations had ceased to be assignable.

The trees were growing in the floor plan of the apartment at Südmessung — the one on the eastern terrace, the one with two rooms. She recognized this as one recognizes a word in a foreign language that resembles a word in one's own: with a jolt of almost-familiarity that the conscious mind immediately quarantines. The hallway between the two rooms was a corridor of birch trunks, pale and evenly spaced, their bark peeling in long vertical strips that hung like the edges of wallpaper in a room where someone has stopped caring for the walls. The larger room — the room where she and her father had eaten meals, where his instruments were stored in their cases along the western wall, where she had lain on the floor and traced maps by lamplight — was an open grove, roughly proportionate, its canopy forming a vaulted ceiling through which light fell in columns. The smaller room — her mother's room, at the end —

The smaller room was a hollow trunk. A single tree, enormously old, split open at its base to form a cavity approximately two meters deep and one and a half meters wide, filled with dark, still water. The water had the greenish-black opacity of water that has stood for a long time in an enclosed space. It smelled of tannin and slow rot. There was nothing else in the cavity. The bed was not there. The window was not there. The light that had come through the window in the final days was not there. There was only the standing water, opaque and still, and the faint, organic smell of something that had once been living wood and was now in the process of becoming something else.

Liel set down her rucksack. She opened her field journal to the first blank page. She took out her pencil — a standard-issue Society graphite, hexagonal, sharpened with a knife. She began to sketch.

The map she produced occupies pages 3 through 5 of the recovered journal (Archival Reference: GC-IV/2331/iii, pp. 3–5; see facing-page reproduction). It is competent, neat, and proportionate.
