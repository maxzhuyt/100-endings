# The Cottage

## I.

The drive from Wilmington to Fenwick Island took two hours and fourteen minutes in summer traffic and an hour forty-one without, and Meg Aldora knew this the way she knew the thread count of her childhood sheets and the exact pressure required to open the cottage's storm door, which was too much, always too much, so that you stumbled into the front room like you'd been shoved. She had made the drive on December 27th because she had told herself she was going to make the drive on December 27th, and Meg still did the things she told herself she was going to do. That was the scaffolding. You said the next thing and then you did the next thing and eventually it was night and you took an Ativan and eventually it was morning and you said the next thing.

Route 1 south of Dover was nearly empty. The gas stations had their Christmas lights still up, half of them burned out. She passed a seafood restaurant called The Crab Shack that had been called The Crab Shack for forty years and would be called The Crab Shack when they found the last clean water on Earth and tried to put a crab in it. She did not turn on the radio. She had stopped turning on the radio in October, or maybe September, because the act of choosing a station felt like the kind of decision that fed into other decisions — what to listen to, what to feel about it, whether the feelings were the right ones — and it was better, she had found, to drive in the sound the car made, which was just the car.

The Ativan was in her bag, in the amber bottle with the white cap, with her name on it. Meg Aldora, 0.5 mg, take as needed. She needed it the way she needed her license and her toothbrush, which is to say it was in the bag and the bag was on the passenger seat and the passenger seat was where a person used to sit. She did not think about the person. She thought about the bridge over the Indian River Inlet, which appeared precisely when it was supposed to, the water beneath it the color of pencil lead, and the cottage, which was four miles past the bridge, left on Coastal Highway, right on Sea Colony Drive, third house from the end, blue siding that had been blue once and was now the memory of blue.

She saw the car in the driveway and registered it.

A silver Honda Accord. Not hers. Hers was the one she was driving, a ten-year-old Subaru with a cracked dashboard and a inspection sticker she kept forgetting to renew. The Honda was parked close to the cottage's side door, tucked in as if trying to be unobtrusive, which was how you could tell it was trying to be unobtrusive. She did not recognize it. She told herself she did not recognize it.

She parked behind it.

The cottage was a three-bedroom rental her mother had been leasing since 1996, back when you could lease a shore place for the off-season at a rate that made her mother say "practically free" in the voice that meant she had negotiated someone into the ground. It sat on pilings like all the shore houses sat on pilings, as if the land itself could not be trusted, the underside exposed and full of lattice and beach chairs nobody ever unfolded. The blue siding had a crack near the kitchen window. The gutter on the east side had come loose in a storm three years ago and now hung at an angle that suggested exhaustion rather than damage, like a limb that had given up. There was a light on in the front room. She could see it through the curtain, which was the curtain with the small blue anchors on it, which had been the curtain with the small blue anchors on it since Meg was eleven.

She sat in the car for one minute, then two. The engine ticked as it cooled. The air smelled like salt and cold and the particular chemical sweetness that shore houses gave off in winter, some combination of pipe insulation and mouse deterrent and the memory of sunscreen in carpet fibers. She reached over and touched the bag. The bottle was where the bottle was.

She got out.

The storm door required exactly the amount of force she remembered, which was too much, and she stumbled into the front room like she'd been shoved, and the radiator in the corner was clanking.

It clanked. It had always clanked. It was a cast-iron single-pipe steam radiator from some decade that believed in permanence, and its clanking was not rhythmic so much as *insistent*, a sound that started and stopped according to laws Meg had never identified and her mother claimed to find "charming" in the way her mother found charming the things she could not fix which was the same way she found charming the things she chose not to fix. The sound was a dull metallic heartbeat. Or not a heartbeat. A knocking. Something trying to get out, or in.

Meg set her bag on the chair by the door. The chair was where it had always been. The room smelled like radiator heat and something else — coffee, fresh, which meant the pot in the kitchen had been used. Which meant the light she'd seen was not a timer.

She walked through the front room to the kitchen.

The coffee maker on the counter was half full. Two mugs sat in the dish rack: one she recognized as the cottage's, a white ceramic mug with a chip on the handle, and one she did not recognize, dark blue, no chip, the kind of mug a person brings with them because they cannot drink coffee from a mug that is not their mug. She knew people who brought their own mugs. She had been married to a person who brought his own mug.

She registered this and crossed to the counter and stood there.

The kitchen window looked out at the side yard, which was sand and beach grass and darkness. Her reflection was in the glass: a woman of forty-one in a coat she had bought in a state of either optimism or dissociation from a thrift store in Greenville, a good coat, a coat that suggested a person who was going somewhere on purpose. Her hair was pulled back. Her face was thin. She had lost twelve pounds she had not intended to lose and the loss had done something angular to her cheekbones that people sometimes complimented, which was the kind of compliment that was also a diagnostic question.

The hallway that led to the bedrooms was dark. She looked at it.

She could get in the car.

She could get in the car and drive the hour and forty-one minutes back to Wilmington, to the apartment she rented on Union Street that had good light and no memories and a lock on the bathroom that worked and a drawer where the Ativan lived next to a box of Earl Grey and a spoon she used for both, and she could sit in that apartment and do the things she had been doing since September, which were: existing, bluntly, one statement at a time.

The radiator clanked.

She opened the cabinet above the stove and took down a mug — not the chipped one, a different one, green, that she remembered from a summer when she was twenty-three and nothing mattered — and poured herself coffee that someone else had made.

She was still drinking it when the hallway light came on and he was there.

---

He looked the way men look when they have been sleeping in a house that is not their house and someone has woken them: confused, armored only in a T-shirt and flannel pants, his hair pushed to one side by a pillow. He was forty-four and he wore the forty-four well in a way that Meg did not begrudge because begrudging it would have required looking at him long enough to form an opinion, which she had stopped doing in June, or May.

"Meg," he said.

"Tom," she said.

He stood in the hallway in the flannel pants and the T-shirt and she stood in the kitchen with the green mug and between them was the narrow passage that in summer smelled like sunscreen and in winter smelled like the memory of sunscreen and the specific mildew of a house that breathes only four months a year.

"Your mother said — " he started.

"I know," Meg said, though she did not know. She did not know anything. She knew the radiator was clanking and the coffee was burnt and the man in the hallway was her husband in the legal sense and her ex-husband in every other sense and neither of those senses was the right sense because the right sense would require a word that had not been invented for the thing where you know exactly how someone stands in a doorway and exactly what their face does before they say the hard thing and you can't make any of that knowledge stop.

"She said you'd want to — she said it would be — "

"I know."

Tom rubbed the back of his neck. That was his gesture. That was the one he did. She had watched him do it in restaurants and in hospitals and in this kitchen and once in a Lowe's parking lot when they had just bought a ceiling fan and the marriage was already over but neither of them had said it yet and he rubbed the back of his neck and she thought, *I will remember this gesture when I can no longer remember why I married you,* and she had been right.

"I can leave," he said.

"It's fine."

"I can leave in the morning. I'll drive back to — "

"It's fine, Tom."

It was fine. She said it was fine and so it was fine. That was how the scaffolding worked. You said the thing and the thing became true enough to stand on and then you said the next thing. Fine. She was here and he was here and the coffee was burnt and the radiator clanked and nothing about any of this was a surprise because nothing was a surprise when you had stopped expecting the world to behave differently than it behaved.

She drank the coffee. He stood in the hallway. The distance between them was eight feet and fifteen years and Meg could have measured either with her eyes closed.

"How was — " he began.

"I'm going to bed," she said, and took her bag and her mug and went to the small bedroom at the end of the hall, the one with the single window that faced the ocean, and closed the door and sat on the bed and listened to the radiator clank through the wall and did not take an Ativan because she did not need to take an Ativan because she was fine and she would take it in the morning, she would take it tomorrow, and she put her bag on the floor beside the bed where she could reach it and did not sleep.

## II.

Her mother arrived at nine-fifteen. Meg knew this because she heard the car — she knew her mother's car the way a dog knows the sound of a particular engine from three blocks away, a biological fact, an inheritance in the bones — and she was standing at the kitchen counter when the storm door took its too-much force and Judith Aldora entered the cottage carrying two canvas tote bags, a Pyrex dish covered in foil, and the compressed energy of a woman who had been planning this moment since before Meg got in her car.

"Oh," her mother said. "You're up. Good."

She set the tote bags on the table and the Pyrex on the counter and took off her coat and hung it on the hook by the door, all of this in a single fluid sequence, the choreography of a person who had been entering this cottage for twenty-eight years and had solved its geometry long ago.

"I made the chicken thing," she said. "For tonight. And I brought sheets — the ones upstairs are probably musty."

"Mother."

"There's also half a coffee cake from Barbara, which I don't trust, but it was offered and I took it. Don't eat the raisins, they look aggressive."

"Mother."

Judith Aldora was sixty-seven and looked sixty-seven in a way she had decided to look sixty-seven: without apology, without effort to look otherwise, with her gray hair cut short and her reading glasses on a chain around her neck and her face arranged in the expression of a woman who was listening to you and had already finished the conversation in her head and was now managing the lag. She was five foot four. She filled any room she entered the way weather fills a room.

"You told him I'd be here," Meg said.

"I mentioned the cottage was available."

"To my husband."

"To Tom, yes. I didn't think he should be alone for the holidays. You know his mother passed in — "

"I know when his mother passed."

"Then you understand."

She did not understand. She understood in the way you understand the mechanism of a trap while your foot is in it — you see the spring, you see the hinge, you admire the engineering, and you are still caught.

"I came here to be alone," Meg said.

"You came here," her mother repeated, in a tone that omitted the rest of the sentence because Judith Aldora had never once in her life needed to finish a sentence to make her point. The rest of the sentence lived in the air between them, and it said: *You came here, which tells me everything I need to know about what you actually want, and what you actually want is not to be alone because people who want to be alone do not drive to the one place in the world where every surface is a memory of the person they are supposedly done with.*

Meg opened her mouth and closed it.

Her mother was unpacking the tote bags. Sheets, as promised — the good ones, from the linen closet at the house in Chadds Ford, not the cottage's own threadbare sets. A bag of clementines. A bottle of white wine. A paperback Meg didn't catch the title of. Two candles, off-white, unscented, the kind her mother kept in bulk because Judith Aldora did not trust the cottage's wiring and wanted backup light at all times, which was either practical preparedness or a metaphor for something Meg did not want to examine.

"Dennis is coming this afternoon," her mother said. "With Sandy."

Dennis was Tom's brother. Sandy was Dennis's wife. Of course they were coming. Of course her mother had constructed the full architecture of a family gathering from the materials of Meg's surrender, the way she had always constructed things — not by forcing, but by arranging, by making the alternative to compliance feel so effortful that compliance began to look like your own idea.

"Why," Meg said, but it was not a question and her mother treated it as such by not answering.

"The back bathroom is stuck again," her mother said. "I'll have Dennis look at it."

The back bathroom. Meg's bathroom. The one at the end of the hall, nearest the bedroom she'd slept in — or not slept in — with the sink that dripped and the window that didn't open and the medicine cabinet where she had put the Ativan at two in the morning because the medicine cabinet was where medicine went, a logic so domestic it was almost a joke.

"It's been sticking since October," her mother said. "The humidity warps the frame. You know how these old houses are."

"It was fine last night."

"It sticks."

Her mother was folding the canvas bags now, pressing them flat with the methodical patience of someone who had never in her life left a bag unfolded. She did not look at Meg while she did this. She looked at the bags.

"I want my car keys," Meg said. She did not know why she said this. Her car keys were in her coat pocket, or they were in her bag, or they were — she patted her jeans. She looked toward the hook by the door where she had not hung her coat because she had not taken off her coat last night, she had gone to bed in her coat, which meant the keys were—

"In the kitchen drawer," her mother said. "You left them on the counter last night. I put them in the drawer so they wouldn't get lost."

She opened the drawer. There were keys in it. There were several keys in it — cottage keys, a key she didn't recognize, a small brass key that might have been to a padlock. Her car key was not evident, but she was not looking carefully, because looking carefully would mean admitting she was looking for her car key, which would mean admitting that she felt she needed her car key, which would mean admitting something she was not going to admit in this kitchen with her mother folding canvas bags.

"There's coffee," her mother said.

"I can see there's coffee."

"Then have some."
