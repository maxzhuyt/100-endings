# Bellworth

## 1.

The third take was better but the pause was wrong.

Tess hit stop, dragged the playback head to 00:47, and listened to herself say "—and on the morning of November eleventh, 2004, when county workers entered the basement morgue of Bellworth Psychiatric Hospital, they found—" and there it was, the gap she'd left before the names, except it wasn't a gap, it was a hole, and holes didn't have shape. A pause needed shape. A pause needed to feel like a held breath in someone else's chest.

She hit record.

"—they found the bodies of three Bellworth staff members. Marcus Arnett. Head custodian. Nineteen years on the job. Linda Chalmas. Night-shift intake nurse. Eight years. And Ronald Pettigrew. Orderly. Fourteen months, most of it in the basement level, most of it alone."

She stopped. Played it back. The pause before "Marcus" was now a quarter-second too long—it tipped into theatrical, into a host who wanted you to know she'd practiced being haunted. She needed the pause to sit at exactly the length of an involuntary swallow. She needed it to sound like something the mouth did on its own.

Take four.

Her laptop was open on the kitchen counter beside a bowl of Grape-Nuts that had passed through every stage of sogginess into a state she would have described, in her podcast voice, as "structural collapse." The milk had turned the color of weak tea. She ate a spoonful between takes, not tasting it, the cereal landing in her stomach with the soft weight of wet paper.

On the screen behind the recording software, a browser tab showed the analytics dashboard for *Left at the Light*, season one—a show about missing persons along Interstate 10 that she'd produced entirely from this apartment between March and August of last year. The numbers were visible if she tilted the screen: 340 total downloads across nine episodes, 38 subscribers, a single five-star review that read "interesting concept but the host sounds like she's reading a book report." She had not closed this tab in four months. She did not know why. It sat behind every other window like a headstone.

"—they found the bodies of three Bellworth staff members."

Take five. The pause was right. She felt it land—that micro-catch, the swallowed thing. She kept rolling.

"The county coroner ruled all three deaths 'consistent with acute physical trauma of undetermined origin.' The police report noted that the basement door had been locked from the inside. There was no sign of forced entry. There was no weapon recovered. The case was never solved. The building was padlocked by order of the Grable County Commission in February 2005, and in the nineteen years since, no one—no journalist, no investigator, no family member—has been allowed inside. Until now."

She let the "now" hang, then clicked stop. Played it back. The "until now" was fine. Clean, minimal, none of the reverb-of-destiny problem she'd had on take three. But "no journalist, no investigator, no family member" needed recalibrating. The triad was good—triads were load-bearing structures in podcasting, they gave the ear a sense of acceleration—but "journalist" was wrong because it implied that real journalists had tried and failed, which cast her as their successor, which was a claim she hadn't earned. She changed it to "no reporter." Smaller word. Less institutional. Take six.

At 11:40 a.m. she made the first call.

Leonard Peake. Insurance adjuster, retired. Grable County Office of Risk Management, 1998–2011. He had signed the compliance certificate that deemed Bellworth structurally sound three months before the deaths. His number was in the county directory, still listed, either because nobody had thought to remove it or because nobody thought of Leonard Peake at all.

Tess heard herself shift. She could feel it in her soft palate—the voice moved up and forward, became lighter, more conspiratorial, the voice of a woman who was also figuring this out and needed your help.

"Mr. Peake, I appreciate you picking up. I know this is out of the blue. I'm working on a project about Bellworth—just trying to understand the institutional side, the paperwork trail—and your name kept coming up as someone who actually understood the building's operational history. I'm not looking to point fingers. I'm looking for the person who knew where the bodies were buried." She paused. "Figuratively speaking. Obviously."

Leonard Peake was quiet for a long time. Then he said, "Saturday?"

"Saturday would be perfect."

The second call: Priya Chandrasekaran. M.E., Grable County, 2001–2006. Terminated following a formal complaint she'd filed about procedural shortcuts in the Bellworth autopsies—specifically, that she had been instructed to duplicate language across the three death reports to expedite processing, and that when she refused, the reports were amended above her signature. She now worked at a veterinary pathology lab in Morgantown. Tess had found her through a Freedom of Information request that had taken nine weeks and arrived with so many redactions it looked like a crossword puzzle.

For Priya, the voice changed again—lower, more deferential, the voice of a student addressing an expert. "Dr. Chandrasekaran, I want to be upfront: I've read your complaint filing, and I think what happened to you was unconscionable. The work you tried to do on those reports was rigorous, and the county buried it. I'd like to give you a platform to set the record straight. On your terms."

Priya said: "I don't do interviews."

"I understand completely."

"I especially don't do them with—what is this, a podcast?"

"It's a documentary audio project."

"It's a podcast."

"It is, technically, yes, a podcast."

Priya was quiet. Then: "The reports were altered. I can show you where. Saturday?"

"Saturday."

The third call was the hardest, not because DeShawn Arnett was difficult to reach but because the register Tess needed was the most delicate—casually sympathetic, warm but not cloying, the voice of someone who happened to care and happened to have a microphone. DeShawn was twenty-six years old. He had been seven when his uncle Marcus was found dead in the Bellworth morgue. He had been nineteen when his family's petition for death-in-service benefits was denied by the county on the grounds that Marcus's presence in the basement during decommissioning hours constituted "unauthorized access to a restricted zone," despite the fact that Marcus had mopped that basement every weeknight for nearly two decades and the decommissioning crew had not yet changed the locks.

"DeShawn, I won't take much of your time. I just want to understand your uncle. Not the case—him. Who he was. What your family's been through with the county. If you're open to it, I'd love to have you walk through the building with me. Sometimes being in the space helps people remember things they didn't know they remembered."

DeShawn said, "Is this going to help with the pension?"

"I think public attention is the single most effective tool your family hasn't tried yet."

"That's not a yes."

"No," Tess said, "it's not a yes. It's a strategy."

He agreed. Saturday.

She set the phone down and opened the recording software again. The Grape-Nuts had entered their final form—a beige silt at the bottom of the bowl, vaguely geological, like a cross-section of something that had once been river. She pushed it aside and leaned into the microphone.

"I'm Tess Lark, and this is *The Bellworth Tapes*. If you have information about the events of November 2004, you can reach me at—"

She stopped. Played it back. "Tess Lark" sounded like a children's book character. She'd chosen it eight months ago when she'd decided that her actual name—Tess Larkowski—was too many consonants for a podcast host, too much friction in the mouth, the kind of name a listener would hear once and immediately let sink beneath the surface of whatever they were doing while they listened, which was usually driving or washing dishes or falling asleep.

She re-recorded the sign-off four times. On the last take she lowered her voice half a register and slowed her cadence by perhaps fifteen percent, and the name stopped sounding like a bird from a picture book and started sounding like a person you might trust to walk you through someone else's worst day. She played it back twice to be sure. The room was very quiet. Outside, a garbage truck was doing what garbage trucks do to a Tuesday morning, which is to say it placed a clear temporal boundary on either side of itself: everything before the truck was still and everything after it was still and the truck was loud and then gone.

The download counter on *Left at the Light* had not changed.

Tess closed the laptop, washed the bowl, and stood in her kitchen with her hands wet, thinking about the three dead people whose names she had now said upward of thirty times in various tones of performed solemnity, and about the one specific inflection—take five, the swallowed pause before "Marcus Arnett"—that had felt, for a quarter-second, like actual grief, and about whether that feeling had been real or whether she had simply gotten better at manufacturing the sound that real grief makes, which would mean the improvement was technical, not emotional, and which would mean—what? That she was good at her job? That she was becoming the kind of person for whom the distance between feeling something and broadcasting something had collapsed into a single gesture, like aeli folding a sandwich closed?

She dried her hands. She opened the laptop. She listened to take five one more time.

The pause was perfect. She exported the file.

## 2.

The county access waiver was printed on canary-yellow carbon paper that looked like it had been designed in 1974 and never revisited, and it bore, in the lower-left corner, a signature so aggressively illegible that it resembled a seismograph reading from a moderately alarming day. Leonard had obtained it through channels he declined to specify. "I still know which doors open," he said, and then he smiled, and the smile was the kind that had once meant something collegial and now meant something closer to a man reminding himself that he had once been a person who could get doors open.

They arrived at Bellworth at 7:20 p.m. on a Saturday in October. The light was doing what late light does to institutional architecture—finding the angles that daytime softens, turning window frames into eye sockets, making the building look like it had opinions about their arrival.

Tess was already recording.

"We're approaching the south entrance of Bellworth Psychiatric Hospital. Built 1951. Decommissioned 2004. Eight stories. Capacity at peak operation: three hundred and forty beds. Current capacity: us."

She liked that. She made a mental note to isolate it in post.

DeShawn was carrying one of the portable recorders—a Zoom H6 that Tess had bought refurbished from a seller in Akron whose online store was called, with either supreme confidence or total indifference, "Audio Good." Priya carried a hard-shell case containing what she'd described as "basic sampling equipment," by which she appeared to mean a forensic field kit that could have restocked a small crime lab. Leonard carried a flashlight and his own silence, which he wore like a coat he'd had for years and no longer noticed was heavy.

The padlock on the south entrance was newer than the door by approximately fifty years—a brushed-steel Master Lock clamped over a hasp that had been bolted through wood so old it had the texture of petrified fabric. Leonard produced a key. The key worked. The door opened inward with the sound of a seal breaking—not a lock-seal, but the hermetic kind, the sound of air that had been sitting in one place for a long time being introduced to air that had not.

"We're in," Tess said, for the recorder, and then, for herself, quieter: "We're in."

The lobby was large and its largeness made it worse.  That was the thing about institutional spaces after dark—scale was fine during the day, when a high ceiling just meant a high ceiling, but at night, with flashlights making the geometry move, a large room became a room that was larger than it needed to be for the number of people in it, which was a way of saying that the room had been designed for a population that was no longer there, and their absence had a square footage.

Tess narrated as she walked. She described the front desk, which was kidney-shaped and laminate and still held a Rolodex swollen with humidity to twice its natural girth. She described the floor tiles—green and cream in a checkerboard pattern, many cracked, some missing entirely, revealing a subfloor the color of old teeth. She described the mailboxes along the east wall, each one labeled with a room number in embossed plastic tape, the kind made by a label gun, each label now curling at the edges like a small scroll delivering very old news.

"DeShawn, can you hold your flashlight under your chin for a sec?"

"Why?"

"Just for the visual. I know it's audio, but I want to describe the lighting on your face. It helps listeners build—"

"I'm not doing that."

"Totally fine. Totally fine."

She turned to Priya. "Dr. Chandrasekaran, can you describe what decomposition at this humidity level would look like over a twenty-year period? For the listeners."

Priya looked at her. Priya had the kind of gaze that could core an apple. "Decomposition of what?"

"Generally. The environment. The—materials."

"You want me to describe how things rot."

"In clinical terms. It's—the audience responds to specificity."

Priya said, "The building is experiencing hydrolytic degradation consistent with two decades of moisture intrusion in an unventilated masonry structure. Calcium leaching from the concrete has produced visible efflorescence on most load-bearing surfaces. Several interior walls show advanced biofilm colonization. The humidity is between eighty and eighty-five percent, and the air contains measurable levels of mold spore consistent with—are you pointing the microphone at me?"

"You're doing amazing," Tess said.

They descended.

The stairwell to the basement was at the north end of the ground-floor corridor, past the dispensary and the records room, behind a fire door that was not locked because its latch mechanism had been colonized by a rust so thorough it had become almost organic—the metal had not merely corroded but *elaborated*, producing floral structures of iron oxide that looked, in the flashlight beam, like something that wanted to be seen.

The basement air was different. Tess felt it before she categorized it—a thickness, a presence-of-substance that was not quite smell and not quite temperature but occupied the same sensory neighborhood as both. It was the air of a room that had been breathing itself for two decades, recycling its own exhalations, becoming more concentrated with each pass. She smelled rust, mold, something faintly chemical that might have been old disinfectant, and beneath all of it, something else—something she didn't have a word for, so she found one: "oppressive."

"The air down here is oppressive," she said into the recorder, and she was satisfied with the word, and she was wrong about it. Oppressive was a word for weather. What the air was, the word she didn't reach for because it didn't live in the podcast lexicon, was *attentive*.

The basement morgue was the third door on the left. Priya identified it by the ventilation specifications stenciled on the frame—"This was a climate-controlled zone, see the duct gauge, this was designed for cold storage"—and Tess pushed the door open and they were inside.

The room was twelve by sixteen. Tess knew this from the building plans. The walls were institutional tile to waist height and painted plaster above, with the paint now hanging in strips like the building was molting. There were four stainless steel tables, still bolted to the floor, still bearing the shallow troughs and perforated drain insets of their function. There were cabinets along the west wall. There was a sink. There was a drain in the center of the floor, and the drain cover was missing, and the hole was dark in a way that darkness usually didn't bother being—dark like it had been working at it.

"This is it," Tess said. "This is where they were found. Marcus Arnett, Linda Chalmas, Ronald Pettigrew. November eleventh, 2004. Three people, one room, no explanation. We're standing in the silence that's been here for twenty years."

It was, she felt, a very good line. She would probably use it.
