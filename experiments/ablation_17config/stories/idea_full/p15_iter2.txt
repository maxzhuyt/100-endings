# The Fountainhead Gala

---

There are seven strangers in this room and not one of them is thinking.

You have to understand what that means to me. You have to understand it the way a woman who has lived her whole life beside a freeway would understand the freeway stopping — not quiet, not peaceful, but *wrong*, so wrong it wakes you up from a sleep you didn't know you were in, and you lie there in the dark and hear, for the first time, your own breathing.

That's where I am now. Standing in the wreckage of the Fountainhead Gala with gold dust settling on every surface like pollen from a flower the size of a cathedral, and I can hear my own breathing, and I don't know what to do with my hands.

My name is Mara. I hear thoughts. I have always heard thoughts — in grocery stores and in waiting rooms and through the walls of every apartment I have ever rented, which is why I have never rented an apartment with shared walls for longer than three months. I hear thoughts the way you hear traffic: a constant, layered, undifferentiated pour of noise that you can, with effort, separate into individual streams but mostly don't bother because what's the point, it's all going the same direction, it's all just people wanting things they won't say out loud.

I am a cocktail waitress. I am thirty-one. My feet hurt. I was supposed to clock out ninety minutes ago and the overtime is not going to materialize because the woman who was going to sign my timesheet is currently standing in the center of this demolished ballroom with her eyes closed and her arms at her sides and her mind — her *mind* — 

I'll get to her.

Let me tell you about the room first. Let me tell you about the seven strangers, because I need you to see them the way I see them — frozen, dusted in gold, utterly silent inside — so that when I explain how we got here, you'll understand what was lost and what was, somehow, built in its place.

The man by the overturned champagne tower is clutching a silver serving tray against his chest like a shield. His tie is gone. There is glass in his hair. He is the health inspector. Forty minutes ago he was the most important person in this room and he did not know it.

The woman half-crouched beside the second window — the one that still has glass in it — is covered in pollen. Not gold dust. Pollen. Orange and dense and real, the kind you'd find in a florist's workroom, coating her bare arms and the front of her green dress so that she looks like she's been rolling in a field of tiger lilies. She's the Finnish tourist. She is crying, but not the kind of crying that comes from sadness. She is crying the way you cry when you step off a roller coaster and your body hasn't caught up with the fact that you survived.

The dog groomer is sitting on the floor with his legs straight out in front of him like a child, staring at the space where the topiary used to be. His hands are scratched to ribbons. He is smiling.

There is a cellist without her cello. There is a teenager still holding a dessert fork. There is an old man in a linen suit who I think wandered in from the garden and simply never left. And there is a woman in dark red standing exactly where she stood when the last light came down, a woman whose mind, even now, in this silence, hums with a structure so foreign to me that hearing it is like pressing your ear to a wall and hearing, instead of voices, *architecture* — 

But I said I'd get to her.

Here is what you need to know: right now, in this room, no one is thinking. Not the absence of thought that comes with sleep or shock, not the white-noise blur of concussion, but a *clean* silence, the kind that has weight and texture, the kind you could rest inside. Every mind in this room is broadcasting the same stunned, luminous nothing, and I am standing in it the way a person born in a factory stands in a meadow for the first time and does not know the word for what she's hearing.

The word is silence. I have never heard it before.

I want to stay here. I want to stay in this frozen moment with the gold dust settling and nobody thinking and my feet aching and the overtime I won't get. But you want to know how we got here, and the only way I know how to tell it is the way it makes sense to me, which is backward, which is to say: I'm going to take this photograph and un-develop it, layer by layer, all the way back to blank film.

So let the dust rise.

---

Watch the gold dust rise.

It lifts from the tablecloths and the shattered stemware and the shoulders of the seven strangers and it swirls upward, catching the emergency lighting, and if you didn't know better you'd think it was beautiful. If you didn't know it was the residue of something designed to humiliate a woman in front of four hundred guests, you might even call it enchanting. That's the thing about spectacle. It doesn't care about its own intentions.

Let the chandeliers un-shatter. The one closest to the stage leaps back together first — crystal shards rising from the floor like startled birds, finding their places in the frame, going dark and then bright as the wiring reconnects. Then the second. Then the third. The room fills with light and the light is the color of champagne and everything the light touches looks expensive again.

Now un-break the windows. The glass slides up from the courtyard and seals itself like water healing over a stone, and the noise of the city goes away, and we are back inside the Gala, and the air smells like ozone and burnt sugar and something else — something alive — 

And there he is.

The protégé. Twenty-six years old, thin-wristed, standing on the mezzanine with his arms raised and his mouth open and light pouring from his fingers — 

God, it looked incredible. If you'd been standing where I was standing, behind the service bar with a broken bottle of Sancerre pooling at my feet, you would have seen a young man commanding a room. You would have seen the aurora-bolts arcing from his hands and connecting with the chandeliers, the conjured beasts materializing from the centerpieces, the topiary on the south terrace ripping itself from its pot and shouldering through the French doors with a sound like a green, wet scream. You would have seen four hundred guests in various stages of stampede, and you would have seen, amid that stampede, seven strangers who did not run. Seven strangers who stood their ground and fought back with whatever they had — a silver tray, a dessert fork, bare hands, a glass stag — and you would have thought: *those are the heroes of this story.*

You would have been wrong.

I was there. I heard what they were thinking. I heard all of it.

The health inspector — the man with the tray, the one who looked like he deflected an aurora-bolt with a calculated backhand, the one who will be described in three separate guest testimonies as "the man who saved my life with a serving platter" — he was not, at the moment of deflection, thinking about saving anyone. He was not thinking about the aurora-bolt. He did not see the aurora-bolt. What the health inspector was thinking, at the exact moment he raised the silver tray and caught a bolt of weaponized light on its convex surface and redirected it into the nearest chandelier, causing a chain-Loss of Crystal Integrity that would later be described by the insurance adjuster as "architecturally unprecedented," was this:

*Is that a cavity? Christ, is that— no. No, it's a sesame seed. From the tuna thing. Okay. But what if it's not? What if it IS a cavity and I can't get into Dr. Park until— oh GOD—*

He tripped. He tripped on a napkin. The tray came up because his arms went out for balance, and the bolt hit the tray because the bolt was already going there, and the chandelier exploded because that's what happens when you redirect an aurora-bolt into eighty pounds of Bohemian crystal, and the health inspector became a hero because he was worried about his teeth, and that is the truth, and the truth is not cinematic.

The cellist — the one without her cello, because she had used her cello as a battering ram against the conjured lynx that lunged for the teenager — she was not thinking about self-sacrifice either. I heard her mind the moment the lynx appeared, three feet away, all spectral fur and invented muscle, and what she was thinking was: *That is the THIRD instrument I've lost this year. Mom was right. I should have been a dental hygienist. A dental hygienist never has to beat a cat with her livelihood—*

And the teenager, the one who stabbed the dessert fork into the flank of something made of light and anger and held it there while it screamed and dissolved — she was thinking about whether the boy from her chemistry class had seen her do that, and whether it was hot, and whether "hot" was the right concept to apply to a moment of genuine life-threatening violence, and she decided yes, it was, it absolutely was — 

*If Austin doesn't ask me out after this I swear to GOD—*

I stood behind the service bar with Sancerre soaking into my shoes and I heard all of it. Every accidental heroism. Every private, ridiculous, fully human thought. And I watched the most spectacular thing I had ever seen dissolve, through sheer proximity to the truth, into what it actually was: a room full of frightened people who happened to be in the right place with the wrong intentions at the exact necessary moment.

It looked like a battle. It was a accident.

But if each of those accidents was unplanned, then how did each of those people end up in exactly the right position to have exactly the right accident? How did the health inspector get the tray? How did the cellist end up next to the teenager? How did any of them end up in this room at all?

Because none of them were on the guest list.

Wind it back further.

---

Let's take the Finnish tourist first, because hers is the image that nobody who was there will ever forget: a blonde woman in a green dress riding a glass stag through a banquet hall at what appeared to be a full gallop, her hair streaming behind her, her face set in an expression of transcendent Scandinavian determination, which the *Times* photographer captured in a shot that will, by morning, become the single most-shared image on every platform that still exists.

She looked like a Valkyrie. She looked like someone who had trained for this moment her entire life.

She was looking for the bathroom.

I need to explain. Lotta — I learned her name later, but I'll use it now — had arrived at the Gala forty minutes before the attack. She was not invited. She had wandered in from the hotel lobby because the doors were open and there was music and in Finland, she told me afterward, if a door is open and there is music, you go in. That is not actually a Finnish custom. Lotta simply wanted to see the magic.

She saw it. She drank two glasses of champagne. She ate four of the singing hors d'oeuvres, which she found "unsettling but delicious — like sushi that hums." And then she began looking for the restroom, which in a venue like the Fountainhead is never where you think it is — it's through a gallery and down a half-flight of stairs and past a door that looks like a wall, because wealthy people apparently believe that needing to use the bathroom is a moral failing best hidden through architecture.

Lotta did not find the restroom. What Lotta found, at minute sixteen of her increasingly desperate search, was a corridor off the east gallery that dead-ended in a room she should not have been able to enter, because the door was sealed with a ward that glowed faintly blue in the dark. But Lotta did not know what a ward was. She thought the blue glow was a bathroom nightlight. She pushed the door and the ward — which had been designed to repel anyone with magical intent — let her pass without resistance, because Lotta's intent was not magical. Lotta's intent was urological.

Inside the room she found the stag.

It was standing in the center of an otherwise empty chamber, about four feet tall at the shoulder, made entirely of glass — not decorative glass, not sculpture glass, but the kind of glass that happens when sand is struck by lightning, all frozen violence and captured light. It was the most beautiful object in the building, and the building was full of beautiful objects. Lotta looked at it. The stag looked at Lotta.

What she thought was: *Oh. This must be the Finnish thing.*

I don't know what she meant by this. I heard the thought clearly — it was louder than most, the way deeply confused thoughts often are — and I have turned it over in my mind many times since. I think Lotta, who had traveled alone to a foreign country and walked uninvited into a gala full of sorcery, had reached the point in her evening where anything she encountered was going to be processed as "the Finnish thing," which is to say, something she did not understand but assumed she was supposed to do.

She climbed on.

The stag moved. Not because Lotta told it to — she did not know it could move, and when it took its first step she made a sound like a door hinge — but because the stag had been waiting for someone to ride it, and Lotta was there, and sometimes that is how magic works. It is not always the worthy who are chosen. Sometimes it is the lost. Sometimes it is the person who needed the bathroom and ended up on a glass deer.

They came through the east gallery at speed. Lotta's thoughts at this point were a high clear stream of Finnish that I could not translate but whose emotional tenor I could read perfectly: a layered braid of terror and exhilaration and the absolute conviction that she was going to die and the separate, quieter conviction that this was, nonetheless, *the most Finnish thing* she had ever done.

She did not steer the stag. The stag wanted to go to the ballroom. Lotta wanted the stag to stop. They compromised: the stag went to the ballroom and Lotta screamed.

When she came through the double doors — antlers lowered, Lotta clinging to its neck, the green dress flying, the stag's glass hooves striking actual sparks from the marble — she plowed directly through the protégé's second wave of conjured beasts, shattering three of them on impact, and the room erupted in cheers, and Lotta thought, very clearly:

*I think this is the wrong room.*

And the stag, having done what it came to do, dissolved beneath her. She fell. The pollen — which had been blooming inside the conjured garden the protégé had thrown at the enchantress, and which was now filling the air like a terrible spring — settled on her skin and dress and hair, and she knelt there, covered in orange dust, and she began to cry, not because she was sad but because her body had feelings and they needed somewhere to go.

Now the dog groomer. His name was Edmund, and he was the only person in the building who had been hired to be there — not as a guest, but as a service provider, like me. The Gala featured, among its attractions, a "Pampered Familiar Station" where guests could bring their enchanted pets for grooming, and Edmund had been contracted to operate it. He had been told he would be grooming cats and small owls. He had not been told about the topiary.

When the topiary came through the French doors — a nine-foot yew sculpture in the shape of a bear, animated by the protégé's spite-hex, shedding dirt and leaves with every step — Edmund's first thought was not *monster* or *run* or *I am going to die.* Edmund's first thought, which I heard with the crystalline clarity that only professional indignation can produce, was:

*Who let that standard poodle get so overgrown?*

I know. I *know.* But you have to understand: Edmund had been grooming dogs for nineteen years. He had groomed standard poodles in conditions you would not believe — poodles matted to the point of structural failure, poodles felted into single continuous garments of fur, poodles whose owners had not brought them in for so long that the dog had essentially become a topiary. The shape was right. The size was — well, the size was wrong, but Edmund had once groomed a Leonberger that was functionally the size of a filing cabinet, so his sense of scale was compromised.
