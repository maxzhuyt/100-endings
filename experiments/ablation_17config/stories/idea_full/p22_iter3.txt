# The Oklahomans

## I.

Here is the image, and you will need it, so look:

Six people are tangled beneath an overturned four-person bobsled on the side of a Swiss alpine course in March, and if you are already asking why six people are involved with a four-person sled, congratulations, you have identified the least important of the many problems facing this group. The sled—a 1998 BTC Citius, purchased secondhand from a Latvian who called it "mostly cursed" and who later could not be reached for follow-up questions—is resting on its left rail at approximately the fourteen-hundred-meter mark of the St. Moritz–Celerina Olympic Bobrun, which is, for those who care about such things, the oldest bobsled course in the world, and for those who do not care about such things, a frozen ditch carved into a mountain by lunatics.

The temperature is eleven degrees below zero Celsius. That is twelve degrees Fahrenheit for the Oklahomans, not that any of them are doing conversions right now.

Dale Spurlock, thirty-nine, team captain, former assistant branch manager of a CrossFirst Bank in Broken Arrow, is on his back with the sled's left runner across his midsection. He is conscious. He is conscious in the particular way of a man who has been told by his own body that something has gone very wrong inside it but who has chosen, for the moment, to file that information under *deal with later*, the same mental drawer where he has kept his feelings about his ex-wife, his father's disappointment, and the certified letter from the IRS that arrived in January and that he put inside Hemingway's *The Sun Also Rises* on his nightstand because he is not, as a rule, someone who finishes books. His face is the color of library paste. His breathing is audible in a way that breathing should not be. He is telling Marisol Fitch, who is kneeling over him and pressing both hands to his abdomen, that the situation is "a setback."

"Stop talking," says Marisol.

"I'm just saying—"

"Dale. You are bleeding out of three places I can see, and probably a fourth place I can't. This is not a setback. A setback is when they put onions on your burger after you said no onions."

"That did happen to me recently," Dale says, and then he coughs, and the cough has a sound in it—a wet, interior, tectonic sound—that causes Marisol to lean harder on his abdomen and speak faster: "Hey. Hey. Stay with me. Tell me about the burger."

"It was at Freddy's."

"Okay."

"The frozen custard place."

"I know what Freddy's is, Dale."

"I just said no onions and they—" He closes his eyes. Something moves behind his lids. He opens them. "Are we on the radio?"

"Hector's trying."

We will get to Hector. We will get to all of them. But first you should know that Marisol Fitch is thirty-four years old, that she works as a dental hygienist in Bixby, Oklahoma, that she once threw a beer bottle at a television because a contestant on *The Bachelor* accepted a rose she considered unearned, and that she is, at this moment, more afraid than she has ever been in her life, though the fear is expressing itself—as fear does in certain kinds of people—as a sort of furious administrative competence. She is applying pressure. She is monitoring Dale's breathing. She is also telling him that his "captain voice"—the one he uses when he wants to sound calm and authoritative—sounds like a regional manager explaining a quarterly downturn to people who are about to be laid off.

"We're restructuring," she says, in a baritone. "We're pivoting. We're leveraging our core competencies toward a more streamlined—"

"I don't sound like that."

"You absolutely sound like that. You sounded like that when you told us we were going to qualify."

This is true. He did sound like that. But the qualifier is not the place to start, and neither is the moment when Dale told them they would qualify, and neither, really, is this crash, except that it is—because you need to see them here, broken and cold, pinned to this mountain in a country none of them had visited before February, in order to understand anything about what this was and why it happened and how six people from Tulsa, Oklahoma, ended up in a frozen ditch twelve time zones from home, in possession of a sled they could not properly steer, competing in a sport none of them had heard of in any context other than a movie about Jamaicans, which, by the way, every single one of them was asked about by every single person they told, until Terrance Okafor printed T-shirts that said COOL RUNNINGS ALREADY DID IT on the front and WE KNOW on the back.

Terrance—Big Terry, his friends called him, though he would have preferred they didn't—is fifteen feet uphill from the sled, sitting in the ice channel with his helmet cracked and his left hand shaking in a way that suggests either nerve damage or shock or both. He is very quiet. Big Terry is always very quiet. This is the thing about Big Terry that people always get wrong: they see a man who is six foot four and two hundred sixty pounds and played offensive line at Northeastern State and they assume a personality that Terry has never had. He is gentle. He is a reader. He reads mostly fantasy novels, big ones, the kind with maps in the front, and when he is afraid or in pain he goes to the maps in his mind, the ones he has memorized, and traces the routes of characters through invented countries, and right now he is following Frodo from the Shire to Bree, and his hand is shaking, and he cannot feel three of his fingers, and he is keeping very, very quiet because he learned a long time ago that being large and afraid makes other people more afraid, and so he has made himself into a person who processes fear internally, silently, like a boiler.

Lyla Beecham is the first of the two teammates who managed to get partially free of the wreck. She has pulled herself out from under the sled's nose cowling and is now trying to lift the hull off Dale, using her legs, her back, and a vocabulary that would, she has been told, make her late grandmother weep. Lyla is forty-one, a phlebotomist, and the only member of the team who had any previous experience on ice, which is to say she once went ice-skating in Wichita in 2011 and did not fall. She has cited this fact more than anyone wanted. Her arms are shaking. The sled will not move.

Connie Trask, fifty-three, the oldest member of the team by over a decade, is sitting on the ice with her back against the wall of the track, clutching her right wrist, which is broken. She knows it is broken because she has broken things before—her collarbone at seventeen, her ankle at twenty-six, two ribs in a car accident at thirty-one, and her promise to herself at thirty-nine that she would stop doing things like this, *this* being defined as: accepting invitations that arrive in the shape of someone else's enthusiasm and riding them until they crash. She is looking at Dale. She is not speaking. Connie Trask's silence is a different animal than Terry's silence; his is a kindness, hers is a calculation. She is running numbers. She is figuring out how long they have.

And then there is Hector Ruiz, twenty-six, the youngest, the one everyone calls the kid even though he is twenty-six and has a mortgage, who is forty meters down the track on his hands and knees, holding the team's radio above his head because the last time he got a signal it was at roughly this height and angle, and who is saying, into static, in high-school French that is making the situation arguably worse: "*Aidez-nous, s'il vous plaît, nous sommes crashés, nous sommes très crashés—*"

The radio crackles. Nothing.

"*Très, très crashés—*"

Nothing.

He switches to English. "Hello? This is the U.S. team? Oklahoma? We are on the track? We crashed? There's a guy hurt pretty bad?"

Static. A half-syllable in German. Static.

"Hello?"

The mountain does not answer. Mountains, generally, do not. This one in particular has been in the business of not answering for fourteen thousand years, and it is not about to change its policy for a dental hygienist, a phlebotomist, a retired lineman, a banker, a kid with a mortgage, or a fifty-three-year-old woman who should have known better, and who did know better, and who came anyway.

They are alone.

They are alone and they are in trouble and a helicopter is coming—it is; it will; the officials saw the crash on their monitors and the rescue protocol was initiated seventeen seconds after the sled left the track—but the helicopter is eleven minutes away, and eleven minutes is a very long time when you are bleeding internally, and because eleven minutes is a very long time, and because you need to understand how this happened, and because this narrator has no interest whatsoever in telling you things in order when telling them out of order is more true, we are going to leave them here.

We are going to leave Dale bleeding and Marisol pressing and Lyla cursing and Connie calculating and Terry tracing his map and Hector pleading into the static, and we are going to go back, eleven months and six thousand miles, to a place where the temperature is ninety-one degrees and the most dangerous thing in the room is an idea.

## II.

The bar was called Sliders, which is not important, except that when the story was eventually told on a podcast—and it was; it would be; we will get there—the host could not stop laughing at the name. "They decided to become bobsledders at a place called Sliders?" she said, and Marisol, who was the guest that week, said, "I know. It writes itself," and the host said, "It really does," and they were both wrong. It does not write itself. Nothing writes itself. That's why we're here.

It was April. It was a Tuesday. The six of them had just played their final rec-league softball game of the spring season—they lost 14–3 to a team sponsored by a podiatrist's office called Agony of De Feet, which tells you everything you need to know about the competitive landscape of Tulsa adult recreational athletics—and they were sitting in a U-shaped booth at Sliders watching the television above the bar, which was showing, for reasons no one could determine, a replay of the 2022 Beijing Winter Olympics. Specifically, it was showing the four-man bobsled event. Specifically, it was showing the German team, which moved in the way that German bobsled teams move: with the terrifying precision of people who have been preparing for this exact four seconds since they were children, in a country that considers bobsled a serious use of human potential.

The Germans slid. The Germans finished. The Germans posted a time.

And someone at the table said, "We could do that."

Now. Here is where the story fractures. There is no consensus. There has never been consensus. Dale says that Connie said it. Connie says that Dale said it and she laughed, which is not the same thing. Marisol says she's "ninety percent sure" it was Hector. Hector says it was Terry. Terry says it might have been Terry but that he meant it "philosophically." Lyla, when asked, says: "It doesn't matter who said it. What matters is that nobody said *no.*"

Lyla is correct. That is exactly what matters.

What someone said was: "We could do that."

And what someone else said was: "Bobsled is literally just sitting down fast."

And what someone else said was: "My truck hits ninety on the turnpike, and that's basically the same thing minus the ice."

And what Marisol said was: "It is not basically the same thing minus the ice."

And then there was a pause. The specific pause. You know the one. It happens at every table in every bar in every state where people who are not quite satisfied with their lives encounter an idea large enough to be mistaken for a door. It is the pause where the joke has not yet become a dare and the dare has not yet become a plan and the plan has not yet become a series of increasingly expensive and ill-considered decisions, and it is the most dangerous pause in the English language because on one side of it is laughter and forgetting and on the other side of it is *everything that happened next.*

The television showed the Germans. The Germans had won.

Dale Spurlock, who had been quiet, who was three beers into the kind of Tuesday-night sadness that followed his divorce the way a dog follows a mail truck—persistent, directionless, unable to stop—looked up from his glass and said:

"I'll look into it."

That is what he said. Those were the words. Not *let's do it.* Not *I'm in.* He said, "I'll look into it," which is the most Dale Spurlock sentence in the English language, because Dale Spurlock is a man who has spent his entire life looking into things as a way of not committing to them while still feeling the warmth of having gestured toward commitment: he looked into getting his real estate license, he looked into moving to Denver, he looked into marriage counseling, he looked into a kayak on Craigslist, and all of these lookings-into gave him three or four days of feeling like a man with a trajectory, before the looking-into revealed the doing to be difficult or expensive or frightening, and the trajectory collapsed back into the gentle downhill slope of his actual life.

But this time was different.

This time was different because Dale Spurlock went home to his apartment—a two-bedroom in Broken Arrow that he rented after the divorce and that still, seven months later, had the emotional temperature of a hotel room, because he had not hung anything on the walls, because hanging things on walls felt like admitting this was where he lived now—and he opened his laptop and he typed "how to start a bobsled team" into Google.

And Google, which is not a responsible advisor, which has no duty of care, which will answer any question with the same implacable algorithmic cheerfulness whether you are asking how to bake a cake or how to hurl yourself down a frozen mountain at ninety miles per hour in a glorified bathtub, told him that it was possible.

More than possible. Google told him it was *surprisingly accessible.* The United States Bobsled and Skeleton Federation, he learned, actively recruited athletes from nontraditional backgrounds. There was a development program. There were push championships—events where aspiring bobsledders simply pushed an empty sled on flat ice to prove they had the raw power—and the push championships were open to anyone. The sport needed fresh bodies because the sport ate bodies: athletes retired, burned out, blew their knees, and the pipeline was thin. Oklahoma had no ice, no tradition, no infrastructure, and no particular reason to be involved, but none of that was, technically, a disqualification.
