# The Deed

## I.

The gangplank was two pine boards laid over mud, and Nell Singleton crossed it the way a woman crosses a threshold she will not cross again—without looking back at what she was leaving, which was the deck of the *Pocahontas*, which was the smell of too many bodies in an enclosed space for six days, which was Missouri, which was the river, which was everything south of where her foot now fell. She stepped off the end of the plank and her boot sank three inches into Kansas.

Around her the landing at Wyandotte composed itself from noise. A white woman at a trestle table was ladling soup into cups and saying *Come now, come now* to no one in particular. A man with a church collar had a megaphone and was reading an address in Topeka where migrants could register for relief, and the word *relief* left the megaphone and hung over the landing like a thing no one wanted to claim. Children ran between legs. A family—the Burdens or the Burdens' cousins, Nell did not know them well enough to distinguish—sat on their single trunk and stared at the mud as though it might eventually resolve into instructions.

Three weeks. She had heard it from the deckhand, who had no reason to lie about other people's misery. Some of these families had been here three weeks. They had come from Tennessee, from Texas, from the particular cruelty of the Yazoo Delta, and they had arrived at this landing with its trestle table and its megaphone and they had stopped. They had no onward transport. They had no claim. They had no deed.

Nell had a deed.

She carried it in the interior pocket of her coat, which was itself too heavy for May but which she wore because it had an interior pocket. The deed was folded twice along creases that had softened to near-transparency, and when she pressed her arm against her ribs she could feel the document's particular resistance—stiffer than cloth, more pliant than wood, the specific material fact of a thing that was simultaneously paper and country. Two years' savings. Three hundred and twelve dollars paid to a labor agent in Topeka whose name was Garfield, or who said his name was Garfield. A receipt. A notarized description of a parcel: the southwest quarter of Section 14, Township 4 South, Range 19 West of the Sixth Principal Meridian, Graham County, Kansas. One hundred and sixty acres. A deed.

She pressed her arm tighter and felt its edge against her lowest rib and the sensation was the same sensation she imagined a person might feel touching the wall of a house they had built—confirmation that a solid thing exists and that you are the reason it exists and that it will remain solid tomorrow because the laws governing solidity do not expire overnight.

She did not stop at the trestle table. She did not listen to the address in Topeka. She passed the Burdens or the Burdens' cousins and did not let her eyes stay on the woman's face, which had achieved the particular blankness of someone who has been waiting long enough that waiting has replaced the thing she was waiting for.

Nell had a destination.

She walked west, away from the river, and the mud thinned under her boots, and the noise of the landing fell behind her like something dropped in water—a stone's influence radiating outward and then the surface closing over the place where the stone had been, and then the surface not remembering.

## II.

She knew where she was by singing.

This was not a thing she had been taught. It was a thing that had accumulated in her the way silt accumulates in a bend of the Yazoo—seasonally, without announcement, until one day you noticed the bank had moved and what had been water was now land and what had been land was now the place where water remembered it had once been. Her mother had hummed while hoeing the Singleton cotton—not the Singletons' cotton, the cotton that bore their name because they bore its owner's name, which was the first of many transactions in which humans and documents had failed to keep their agreements with each other. The hum had no words. It had a rhythm that matched the rise and fall of a hoe in thick Delta soil, and the rhythm divided the day into parcels that could be survived one at a time, the way you crossed a river by stepping on stones and looking only at the next stone, never at the far bank, because the far bank was a promise and promises were the business of the Lord and of white men and in her mother's experience these two worked in similar fashion, issuing largely and collecting ruthlessly.

Nell hummed as she walked. The sound organized distance. Two repetitions of the melody—she did not know its name; it may not have had a name; some things that are oldest have no names because they precede the naming—two repetitions equaled roughly the space between the Singleton cabin and the creek. Four repetitions equaled the walk to the gin. She had measured her entire life this way: not in miles, which were an abstraction that belonged to the people who owned the miles, but in phrases of music that belonged to the throat that made them. A phrase was a distance. A song was a journey. A silence was either rest or danger, and you learned the difference the way you learned which plants were food and which were poison, which was to say by watching who survived.

*Every place that the sole of your foot shall tread upon, that have I given unto you.*

Deuteronomy 11:24, or 1:3, or Joshua 1:3—the verses had merged in her memory into a single promise that attributed itself generally to the Lord and specifically to the book her mother had kept beneath the straw ticking, the book with the cracked spine whose pages were so thin you could see your thumb through them, brown and enormous as a shadow puppet behind a sheet. Her mother could not read. Nell could read, poorly, by the grace of a white child's castoff primer and the dangerous patience of a woman named Celia who had been sold downriver for the offense of teaching five children their letters. But her mother could *recite*. The verses had entered her through the ear and lived in her the way the hum lived in her, as vibration, as rhythm, as the deep structure beneath the words that the words merely decorated.

*Every place.* The sole of your foot. The language was so physical. Not every place your *eye* shall see, or your *mind* shall conceive, but your *foot*. The foot was the instrument of claiming. The body walked onto the land and the walking was the covenant. Moses saw the Promised Land from the mountain—Nell remembered this specifically, remembered Celia's voice describing the mountain, which Nell had imagined as the levee at Greenville because she had never seen a mountain, the levee being the highest thing in the world—Moses saw the land but could not enter, and the reason he could not enter was that his feet had sinned, or his hand had sinned by striking the rock, or his faith had sinned by faltering, the sin varied depending on who told it, but the punishment was always the same: *you may look but you may not walk there*. The foot was the instrument. The walking was the proof.

She walked now with this verse running beneath her steps the way a creek runs beneath ice, invisible but present, shaping everything above it. The deed in her coat was the documentary form of the verse. *Every place that the sole of your foot shall tread upon, that have I given unto you, as I said unto Moses.* The labor agent Garfield had given her a paper. The Lord had given Moses a promise. These transactions rhymed, and the rhyme was the structure that kept her moving, because a rhyme is a form of trust—the ear hears the first sound and *believes* the second sound will come.

She had been promised before.

She did not think about this. She had been promised before—by the word *emancipation*, which had arrived in the quarters one evening in the throat of a man on a mule and had sounded like a stone too large to pass through the throat that carried it, and the word had meant freedom the way a deed meant land, which was to say it translated an immensity into a document, and the document was either the immensity's container or its coffin, and you could not tell which until you tried to open it.

The landscape she moved through confirmed everything she knew. Wyandotte fell behind but the river bluffs held the land in place. There were fencerows. There were hedgerows of Osage orange, their branches braided tight as wickerwork. There were property lines visible as the edges between plowed land and unplowed, between one man's winter wheat and another man's weedy fallow. The world had *seams*, and the seams told you where one thing ended and another began, and this was the fundamental information required for navigation, for ownership, for sanity. A field was a field because it had edges. A farm was a farm because a fence said so. A self was a self because a skin contained it, and a life was a life because a birth and a death enclosed it, and everything Nell had ever known about being alive was built on the principle that things had *boundaries* and that boundaries could be known.

Even the sky. In Mississippi the sky was something you saw in pieces—between branches, above the corridor of a road, in the circle directly overhead when you lay on your back in a clearing and let the trees hold the blue like water in a bowl. The sky had edges because the trees gave it edges. The trees were the frame and the sky was the painting inside the frame, and a painting without a frame was just color with no instruction for the eye.

She walked. She hummed. The fencerows continued. The sky stayed framed by cottonwoods that followed the creeks, and the creeks followed the land's subtle folds, and the folds gave the land a grammar—*here* is a bottom, *here* is a rise, *here* is a place where water gathers and things green, *here* is a place where the wind strips the soil and things do not grow. She could read this grammar. It was the grammar of Mississippi translated into a slightly different dialect, and she was fluent, and the fluency was a kind of wealth that no labor agent could sell and no deed could describe because it lived in the body and the body was the one document that could not be forged.

Or so she had believed, not knowing that belief was another document.

## III.

The trees ended.

They did not thin. They did not gradually surrender to brush and then to scrub and then to grass in the way a river narrows toward its source. They simply stopped, the way a sentence stops when the speaker is shot—a last cottonwood, and then its shadow, and then the shadow's edge, and then.

Nell stood at the place where the shadow ended and the grass began and the grass did not end.

She had been told about the prairie. A man on the steamboat—a man who had been to Kansas before and returned to Mississippi for his family and was now going back, a fact that struck her as suspicious, why would a man in paradise return to hell unless paradise had fine print—this man had said *There is nothing out there for days* and she had heard the words and understood them but understanding was not the same as—

It was not flat. That was the first wrong thing. She had expected flat. She had steeled herself for flat. But the prairie was not flat, it was *level*, which was a different terror entirely. It had rises and it had dips and the grass moved over them in waves that imitated water so faithfully that her inner ear, the ancient mechanism that knew the difference between solid and liquid, sent up a confusion she felt as nausea. The ground beneath her feet was firm but the world atop the ground was *flowing*, and the flowing had no bank, no shore, no dock, no blessed interruption of any kind between where she stood and the place where the land became the sky, and the place where the land became the sky was not a line but a *smear*, a failure of conviction on the part of the horizon, as though the earth and the air had begun a negotiation about where one ended and the other began and had abandoned the negotiation and gone home and left the boundary undecided, permanently, a question no one would ever answer because the question was the answer.

She stepped forward. The grass received her. It was waist-high and it closed behind her and there was no path because path was a concept that required a *between*—between the trees, between the fences, between here and there—and there was no between. There was only *through*. She pushed through the grass and the grass pushed back with the idiot persistence of a thing that had been pushing back for ten thousand years and would push back for ten thousand more regardless of who entered it, regardless of whether anyone entered it, the grass having no knowledge of or interest in the distinction between a woman with a deed and a woman without one, the grass being the one thing in Kansas unburdened by the concept of property.

She hummed. The hum left her mouth and went into the air and the air took it and gave nothing back. No echo. No return. In Mississippi she had hummed into corridors of trees and the sound had come back to her changed—dampened, delayed, contoured by the surfaces it struck—and the return was a kind of conversation, the landscape confirming that it had heard her, that she existed in relation to it, that her sound and its shape were in dialogue. Here the sound traveled outward in every direction simultaneously and met nothing. She was humming into a mouth that had no teeth, no tongue, no palate—a mouth that was only throat, only openness, only the undifferentiated capacity to swallow.

She stopped humming.

The silence that replaced the hum was not the silence of Mississippi, which was a silence made of small sounds—insects, birdcall, the creak of a branch adjusting its weight, the tick of earth cooling after sundown—a silence that was actually a choir performing at a volume the ear called quiet. This silence was structural. It was the silence of a vast room empty of furniture, a silence you heard not with the ear but with the skin, a silence that was the acoustic signature of *nothing between you and the end of the world*.

She walked. She did not know how far she had walked. This was new. In Mississippi, distance had been a knowable thing—her body knew the distance to the creek the way her tongue knew the distance to her teeth, by long habitation, by the muscle's memory of repeated traversal. Here her body knew nothing. Each step was the same as the last step and would be the same as the next step and the sameness was not comforting but annihilating, because repetition without variation was not rhythm—it was erasure, the difference between a drumbeat and a stopped clock, which both make the same sound at the same interval but one is music and the other is the end of time.

*Every place that the sole of your foot shall tread upon.*

But the verse needed *place*. Place required *differentiation*—this place and not that place, here and not there. The sole of your foot shall tread upon—upon *what*? Upon land that could be distinguished from other land, upon a parcel, upon a bounded and named and surveyed rectangle of earth that had been cut from the general fabric of the world and given a number and a boundary and an owner, and if the earth refused the cutting? If the earth was this—this—this thing she was walking through that had no seam, no joint, no place where a surveyor could drive a stake and say *here is the edge of yours and the beginning of someone else's*? If the earth was continuous the way water was continuous, the way air was continuous, the way grief was continuous? Then the verse was not a promise. The verse was a description of a place that did not exist, a land of bounded parcels and distinguishable places, a land that was not *this* land, and if the verse was not a promise then Deuteronomy was not a map and the deed was not a key and she was not walking toward something, she was walking *into* something, and the something had no far side.

She pulled the deed from her pocket. She did not decide to do this. Her hand decided.
