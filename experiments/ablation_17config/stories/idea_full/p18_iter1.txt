### Wednesday, August 11 — Arrival / Initial Assessment

Arrived [REDACTED] at approximately 2:15 PM via personal vehicle. Drove up from the city with ANTHONY GALLANTE (hereafter "Tony Ga-Ga," street name used by all associates) and PATSY DELUCCA in Tony Ga-Ga's 1978 Lincoln Continental Mark V, white, with red interior that smells like Brut cologne and calzone grease. Three-hour drive. Tony Ga-Ga drove forty-five the whole way and got furious when anyone passed him, which was everyone.

Town is in Ulster County, roughly forty minutes west of the Thruway. No direct highway access. You come in on a two-lane state road through farmland that eventually narrows into a county road flanked by white pine. The pines go on for approximately six miles and then stop, all at once, as though someone drew a line, and the town begins.

First impression: clean. I don't mean tidy. I mean *clean* the way a model home is clean, like no one has ever actually eaten dinner or tracked mud across a floor. The main street — Conforte Avenue — runs roughly north-south for eight blocks. Storefronts have fresh paint. Green awnings. Window boxes. Sidewalks look like they were poured last week. But the signage on some of the shops is wrong in a way I can't pin down. A barbershop called "Barbershop." A hardware store called "Hardware & Supply." Names that aren't names. I noticed this but did not have time to document further because Tony Ga-Ga was giving me a tour and I needed to maintain engagement.

Population appears to be maybe two, three thousand. Tony Ga-Ga says it swells to eight or ten during the festival. "The Feast of Our Lady of the Assumption," four days, Thursday through Sunday, the biggest thing this town does all year. The crew has been running it for eleven years. Before that, apparently, it was run by a civic committee of local merchants. Tony Ga-Ga says the old committee "asked for help" and "volunteered to step aside." He says this without irony.

The festival infrastructure is already partially erected. Vendor stalls line Conforte Avenue on both sides — wooden-frame structures with canvas roofs, electrical hookups, plumbing for the food stands. Bandstand at the south end. Archway at the north with painted plaster columns and a banner frame. Strings of light bulbs crisscross overhead between the buildings, hundreds of them, none lit yet but all in place. What I will note is that the infrastructure looks permanent. Not temporary stalls put up and taken down each year. Permanent. Bolted to the sidewalk. Wired into junction boxes set into the ground. The canvas is weathered. The wood shows layered paint. I would estimate some of these structures have been standing for years, possibly since the festival began. Either the town doesn't bother dismantling them or sees no reason to. Municipal laziness. In any case, not relevant to the case. I note it only for completeness.

**Crew orientation re: my role.** Tony Ga-Ga introduced me to the rest of the crew on-site as "Donnie, from Mulberry Street, a friend of ours who's gonna help us with the festival." My assignment for the duration: I am to manage the vendor payments. Cash collection from each stall operator, daily, plus the up-front "participation fee" each vendor pays for the privilege of operating during the feast. This is the primary skim — the money that flows upstairs to the Bonanno family hierarchy rather than into the festival fund. My position gives me direct access to the financial structure of the operation. This is exactly the kind of documentation that will support a RICO prosecution. I will record all cash amounts, payers, and recipients.

Tony Ga-Ga also told me I'd "report to the sheet," by which he means a logistics printout that is generated by the municipal mainframe and used as the operational bible for the festival. More on this below.

**Re: the municipal mainframe.** Mentioned in passing by PATSY DELUCCA while we were sorting out parking assignments behind the town hall. Patsy was complaining that his designated parking spot — the mainframe assigns numbered spots to crew members — was behind a dumpster. "That fuckin' machine put me behind the dumpster again," direct quote. I asked what machine. Patsy indicated the basement of the town hall, a two-story Colonial Revival building on Conforte Avenue. He said the town has a mainframe computer that handles "all the festival stuff" — scheduling, logistics, assignments. A town clerk retrieves printouts and distributes them. I asked who programmed it. Patsy looked at me the way you'd look at someone who asked who programmed the weather. "It's a computer," he said. "It does computer things."

I walked past the town hall basement door later that evening. Locked. No window. But you can hear it — a low, continuous hum, electrical, resonant, the kind of sound that seems to come from the walls rather than from any single source. Like standing near a transformer station, except transformers cycle and this sound is constant. No variation in pitch or volume. I logged the observation because the mainframe is government property and may be relevant if the crew is using municipal resources for criminal purposes (potential fraud/misuse-of-public-funds charges). But the machine itself is a side issue. The money is what matters.

**Lodging.** I am staying at a motor lodge on the northern edge of town. Room 6. The room is adequate. Single bed, bathroom, window unit. The wallpaper is a repeating pattern of small flowers — blue, on cream — and it is in excellent condition, which is unusual for a motor lodge. No peeling, no stains, no water damage. It looks as though it was applied yesterday but the style is thirty years out of date.

One navigational note: the street grid in this town is not logical. Conforte Avenue is straightforward enough, but the side streets branch at inconsistent angles, and on two occasions today I attempted to walk to a location I could see — the church steeple, two blocks east — and found myself arriving from the wrong direction, as though the street curved without my noticing. I attribute this to unfamiliarity with the layout. I will obtain a map tomorrow, if one exists.

### Thursday, August 12 — Crew Profiles / Pre-Festival Preparations

Spent the day working setup with the crew. Six members of the Gallante street crew are present for the festival operation. I will profile each for the record, as their roles and personalities are relevant to understanding the criminal hierarchy and will be useful in future testimony.

**1. ANTHONY "TONY GA-GA" GALLANTE.** Crew captain. Age approximately fifty-five. Five-ten, two-twenty, bald on top but cultivates long side hair that he combs across the dome with a level of architectural ambition that has to be seen to be appreciated. Tony Ga-Ga earned his street name from his habit of making grand, operatic speeches to his crew at the slightest provocation. He will declaim about the old neighborhood, about respect, about the meaning of the feast, about his mother's sacrificing, about the sacred duty of putting on a beautiful festival "for the people, Donnie, for the *people*" — and then, three minutes in, the tears come. Real tears. Streaming. He pulls out a cloth handkerchief — monogrammed, AGG — and presses it to his face, and his men stand around like pallbearers waiting for the body, staring at their shoes, hands clasped, faces blank, while Tony sobs his way through the peroration.

This happened twice today. Once during a morning meeting about electrical permits. Once while assigning sausage-stand positions.

What I will note for the record is that Tony Ga-Ga's speeches, while obviously performative in the sense that he enjoys delivering them, do not appear to be *voluntary* in the usual sense. When I watched him this afternoon — the sausage-stand incident — I noticed that the speech began mid-sentence, as though a switch had been thrown. He was discussing propane tank placement and then, without transition, he was eulogizing his dead uncle Carmine and weeping about "the blood we carry in our veins, the soil we carry in our hearts." His eyes during the speech were focused on something approximately six inches in front of his face. When it was over, he resumed the propane discussion at the exact syllable where he'd left off. His men did not remark on the interruption. I found this odd. I also found it unexpectedly affecting — for approximately two seconds during the dead-uncle segment, I felt a thickness in my own throat that I attribute to the heat.

**2. SALVATORE "SALLY SLEEVES" PETROCELLI.** Enforcer. Age approximately forty. Six-two, two-fifty, arms like bridge cables, which he displays by rolling his shirtsleeves to the bicep at all times, hence the name. Sally Sleeves has a documented history of aggravated assault — three arrests, no convictions — and is the crew's primary instrument of physical persuasion. I have personally witnessed him lift a man off the ground by the belt in a dispute over a parking space in Bensonhurst.

Sally Sleeves is terrified of the festival's live poultry.

This requires explanation. The Feast of Our Lady of the Assumption includes, on its second day, a Blessing of the Animals ceremony conducted by the parish priest in the church parking lot. Local residents bring pets. The festival supplies a ceremonial component: crates of live chickens, doves, a rooster, and two rabbits, arranged in a semicircle around a small altar. The animals are blessed, the crowd applauds, the priest sprinkles holy water, the animals are taken away. Standard folk-Catholic pageantry, nothing unusual.

Sally Sleeves will not go near it. Today, while helping erect the bandstand, he realized the animal crates were being stored in a shed thirty feet from his work area. He went pale. Rigid. Put down his hammer and walked to the other side of the street, where he stood with his arms folded and his back to the shed and refused to return until Patsy moved the crates. Patsy, to his credit, did not mock him. Nobody did. They moved the crates the way you'd clear a path for someone in a wheelchair — efficiently, without comment, as though this were an accommodation they had made many times.

I asked Patsy about it later. "Sally don't like the birds," he said.

"Since when?"

"Since last year's feast. The blessing thing." Patsy shrugged. "Something happened. He don't talk about it. You don't ask."

I pressed: what happened?

Patsy looked at me for a long moment, then said: "The birds did something."

I asked what.

"I don't know. I wasn't there. Nobody was there. But Sally was there and he don't like the birds now." He walked away.

I am logging this because Petrocelli's psychological state may be relevant to his reliability as a witness or subject in future proceedings. Beyond that, I have no explanation.

**3. RAYMOND "RAYMIE CLEANFINGERS" SCOTTO.** Hitman. Age approximately forty-five. Slight, fastidious, wears pressed slacks and a polo shirt with the collar up even in August heat. Raymie Cleanfingers has been linked by Bureau intelligence to at least three contract killings in the Bonanno family's internal wars, though no charges have been brought. In person, he is quiet, meticulous, and profoundly germophobic.

He wipes everything. Doorknobs. Chair backs. Silverware. Glasses. He produces a white cotton handkerchief from his pocket — he appears to carry several — and polishes every surface before contact. Today I watched him spend three full minutes cleaning a cannoli before eating it. Shell, filling, powdered sugar — all wiped. He held the cannoli with the handkerchief like a man handling evidence.

He does not shake hands. When introduced to a festival vendor this afternoon — a woman selling fried dough from a stand on the south block — Raymie offered a nod, placed his hands behind his back, and said, "Pleasure." The woman extended her hand. He looked at it as though she were offering him a dead rat and said, "I don't do that." She was confused. Tony Ga-Ga intervened: "Raymie's got a thing, sweetheart, don't take it personal."

I watched Raymie throughout the afternoon. His wiping is constant and systematic. Not nervous — purposeful. He cleans in a specific order: surface center first, then edges, then underside if accessible. Always the same pattern. The handkerchiefs go into a plastic bag in his jacket when soiled. He replaces them from a supply in his car. I counted: he went through eleven handkerchiefs between noon and six PM.

What I will note: the wiping intensified after he spent twenty minutes inside the town hall, where he'd gone to use the restroom. When he came out, he was wiping his hands continuously — not the purposeful, systematic cleaning, but a rapid, repetitive motion, rubbing the handkerchief across his palms and between his fingers as though trying to remove something he could feel but not see. He did this for the next hour. I asked if he was all right. "This town," he said. "It gets on you." I took this as a reference to the heat and humidity.

I have noticed that I am washing my hands more frequently since arriving. The motor lodge soap is adequate.

**4. PATSY DELUCCA.** Tony Ga-Ga's driver and general-purpose assistant. Age approximately thirty. Stocky, amiable, low-level. Patsy is useful to me because he talks freely and has no apparent awareness that anything he says might be incriminating. He handles day-to-day logistics and acts as liaison between the crew and the town administration. His chief complaint in life is his parking spot.

**5. DOMINICK "BIG DOM" ROSSANO.** Bookmaker. Age approximately sixty. Large, sedentary, operates from a folding chair he positions outside the festival's main entrance and does not move from for the duration of each day. Big Dom takes action on horse races via a phone in the bakery across the street but is otherwise uninvolved in festival operations. He was sent by the family to "keep an eye," which he does by sitting, eating, and occasionally commenting on passersby. His chair is in the same location, with the same orientation, that the mainframe's printout specifies. He did not choose it. He was assigned it. He shows no awareness that this is unusual.

**6. MICHAEL "MIKEY POCKETS" SPERANZA.** Numbers runner. Age approximately twenty-five. Youngest member of the crew. Thin, nervous, smokes constantly. Mikey Pockets runs messages and small errands. He is the one who collects the mainframe printout from the town clerk each morning and brings it to Tony Ga-Ga. I will observe his routine closely, as it gives me a natural reason to examine the printouts.

---

Festival preparations continued through the day. Weather hot. Streets quiet aside from the crew and a handful of townspeople moving through Conforte Avenue on what appeared to be routine errands. Foot traffic is remarkably consistent. I observed approximately the same number of pedestrians on the street at 10 AM, 1 PM, and 4 PM. A slow, steady flow, like a current.

I attempted to obtain a map of the town from the motor lodge office. The clerk — a woman, maybe sixty, pleasant, unhurried — said she didn't think they had one. "People just kind of know where things are," she said. I said I didn't, being from out of town. She smiled and said, "You will."

No map acquired. I will draw my own from observation tomorrow.
