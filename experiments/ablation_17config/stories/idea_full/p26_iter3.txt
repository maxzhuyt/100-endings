# The Garden of Forking Paths (Pheromonal Variant)

## As Narrated by Deputy Roy Puckett, Pottawatomie County Sheriff's Department (Ret.)

---

## I.

For the record, I want to state that the following account represents my recollection of events to the best of my ability and that I have not been compensated, coerced, or pheromonally influenced in the production of this document. I want to further state that I am providing it voluntarily, in the interest of preserving an accurate record, and that I have organized it chronologically where possible and thematically where chronology has ceased to be a meaningful framework. If anyone from the Oklahoma Bureau of Investigation, or any surviving federal entity, requires this account in a different format, I am willing to restructure it per their specifications, assuming they can furnish me with the relevant forms. I have not been able to locate the relevant forms. I have not, as of this writing, been able to locate the Oklahoma Bureau of Investigation.

My name is Roy Dean Puckett. I served as deputy with the Pottawatomie County Sheriff's Department for eleven years under Sheriff Dale Robert Mackey, and for two years before that under Sheriff Bill Dunlap, who retired due to a back condition and an unwillingness to deal with the new computerized dispatch system. I was not the senior deputy. That was Mike Barroso, who had two years on me and a certificate in crime-scene processing from a weekend seminar in Tulsa. I was second-most senior. I mention this because it is a matter of record and because matters of record are what I have left, and because the question of why I was in the vehicle and not Mike Barroso is one I expect someone, eventually, to ask. The answer is that Mike Barroso was on vacation in Branson, Missouri, during the week of the initial outbreak, and by the time he returned, the situation had developed past the point where vacation schedules applied. But I was the one in the vehicle. Put that down. I was the one in the passenger seat.

Pottawatomie County comprises eleven hundred and thirty-two square miles of eastern Oklahoma, population sixty-three thousand and change, county seat Shawnee. We had, in the period leading up to the events I am describing, a department of fourteen sworn officers inclusive of the sheriff, three civilian dispatchers, and a part-time records clerk named Donna who came in Tuesdays and Thursdays and who maintained a filing system that I will say, without exaggeration, was the envy of three adjacent counties. Donna had a color-coded method of organizing case files that she had developed on her own time, using principles she adapted from a book about Japanese kitchen organization. Every active case was in a blue folder. Every closed case was in a green folder. Every case that was open but inactive — meaning no leads, no new information, but not formally closed — was in a yellow folder. Donna's system was consistent, retrievable, and universally respected within the department, and I mention it because by the end of what I am about to describe, every blue and yellow folder in her system had been transferred to the back seat of a 2019 Ford Police Interceptor Utility and was heading west on Interstate 40 at ninety-one miles per hour with the sirens off and the sheriff at the wheel and me riding next to him saying nothing because there was nothing procedurally appropriate to say.

But I will get to that.

The point I want to establish is what the department was before. What the county was. I have heard people, in the aftermath, describe places like Pottawatomie County the way you'd describe a small, yapping dog — "quaint" is the word they use, or "close-knit," as though we were a sweater and not a functioning jurisdiction. I will grant you that our most common calls were noise complaints, livestock on the roadway, and the occasional methamphetamine situation in the rural sections east of Tecumseh. I will grant you that the week before the outbreak, the most complex matter before the department was a zoning dispute between a Church of Christ congregation and a neighboring landowner who had installed a commercial-grade inflatable water slide on his property and was, depending on whom you asked, either operating an unlicensed recreational facility or simply enjoying his God-given right as an American to own a large water slide. I will grant you that Dale Mackey spent the better part of three days on that water-slide matter, visiting the site twice, measuring setback distances from the property line, and producing a four-page memorandum that cited both the county zoning ordinance and what he described as "the general principle that a man's property is his dominion up to and not exceeding the point at which runoff affects his neighbor's drainage."

Dale wrote that. Dale Mackey, who people later talked about like he was some kind of tragic figure, or a lunatic, or a joke. Dale Mackey, who had run unopposed in two elections and semi-opposed in a third, the "semi" being a libertarian candidate who showed up to one debate and spent his allotted time discussing chemtrails. Dale Mackey, who pressed his uniforms. Who wore his duty belt at the correct angle, not canted the way some of the younger deputies wore theirs because they'd seen it in a movie. Who arrived at the department every morning at 6:45, fifteen minutes before his shift, and read through the overnight reports with a cup of black coffee and a yellow legal pad on which he wrote questions. Not observations. Questions. "What was the lighting at the scene?" "Was the complainant's dog present at the time of the incident?" "Has anyone confirmed the serial number on the catalytic converter?"

This was a man who believed, in a way I think most people are not capable of believing, that the apparatus worked. That if you followed the procedure, documented the evidence, filed the correct forms in the correct sequence, the truth of any situation would become available to you, the way a vein of ore becomes available to a miner who digs in the right spot. Dallas could believe that. I don't mean to say he was naive. He had worked narcotics interdiction on the interstate for two years before making sheriff, and he had seen the things that come out of people when they are cornered or high or both. He had once talked a man out of a mobile home in Wanette who had a sawed-off shotgun and three warrants and a pit bull that was, per Dale's later report, "agitated but ultimately responsive to calm verbal instruction." Dale was not a man unfamiliar with chaos. He was a man who had met chaos and concluded that it could be administered.

I admired him. I want that on the record too. I admired Dale Mackey and I got in the car.

## II.

The first report came in on a Tuesday. I know it was Tuesday because Donna was in the office, and because Donna logged it, and because Donna's log — which I later personally transferred to the vehicle and which I have in my possession now, stained with coffee and something I believe to be Red Bull, in a folder that used to be blue and is now something closer to the color of a week-old bruise — reads as follows:

*10:47 a.m. — Call from Earline Jessup, 1847 Rural Route 3, Earlsboro. Reports her neighbor, Deacon Wayne Cluff (First Baptist, Earlsboro), found in the feed store in a state of undress and "carrying on," her words, with two individuals she could not identify. Jessup states Cluff was "not himself." Requests welfare check. Dispatched Unit 4 (Dep. Harmon).*

I was at my desk when Harmon came back. Harmon was one of the younger deputies, twenty-four, still in the phase where every call was either boring or the most exciting thing that had ever happened to him. This one, based on the expression on his face, was the latter.

"Puckett," he said. He was very pale. "I need you to — can you come look at my body camera footage?"

I did not ask him to rephrase this. I went and looked at the footage. What it showed was Deacon Wayne Cluff, sixty-three years old, lifetime member of the Earlsboro Masonic Lodge, father of four, in the seed-and-supplement aisle of McAllister's Feed & Farm Supply, engaged in an activity I will describe as sexual congress with two individuals who were later identified as a long-haul trucker from Arkadelphia, Arkansas, and a graduate student from the University of Oklahoma studying soil microbiology. None of the three appeared to be in distress. In fact — and Harmon pointed this out, his finger trembling slightly over the pause button — all three appeared to be in a state of considerable enthusiasm, oblivious to Harmon's presence, his verbal commands, and the ambient soundtrack of the feed store's PA system, which was playing "Islands in the Stream" by Kenny Rogers and Dolly Parton.

Harmon had eventually retreated to his cruiser and called for backup. By the time the backup arrived, Cluff and his companions had concluded their activities and wandered off in separate directions, each looking, per the backup deputy's report, "dazed but not unhappy, similar to post-anesthesia." Cluff was located forty minutes later in the parking lot of the Earlsboro post office, fully dressed, with no memory of the preceding two hours and a strong and apparently new conviction that "everything is going to be fine, really fine, just real fine." He repeated this several times. He did not want to press charges. He did not want to seek medical attention. He wanted to go home and — these were his words — "think about all the ways things could be."

I brought the body-camera footage to Dale.

Dale watched it twice. He leaned forward during the second viewing, not in the way a man leans forward when he is titillated but in the way a man leans forward when he is reading small print. He paused it at one point and asked me what brand of poultry supplement was visible on the shelf behind Cluff's left shoulder, and whether McAllister's carried that brand regularly or if it was a seasonal item. I told him I did not know. He wrote a question on his legal pad.

"Roy," he said, "I want a case number on this."

I said, "Dale, it's indecent exposure at most. It's Wayne Cluff. He teaches Sunday school."

"Case number," Dale said. "And I want Harmon's report on my desk by end of shift." He tapped his pen on the pad. "Something happened to that man, Roy. Wayne Cluff does not do what that video shows Wayne Cluff doing. Not without a cause. I want to find the cause."

I gave him a case number. 2024-00347. I remember it. I remember all the numbers. Donna and I, we filed it blue.

The second report came in six hours later. A married couple, the Paynters — Joe and Linda, both schoolteachers, both regular churchgoers, both in their fifties — found by a teenager in the Dairy Queen parking lot on Harrison Street in Shawnee, engaged in sexual activity with a man none of their acquaintances could identify, a man who, when eventually separated from the Paynters by EMS, spoke in a language no one present recognized and emitted, per the paramedic's report, "a smell like ozone and warm bread." Linda Paynter, wrapped in a shock blanket in the ambulance, told the paramedic she felt "so open, just so open, like a window that's been painted shut for thirty years and someone finally — " She did not finish the sentence. She began to cry. She was not crying from shame. The paramedic noted that she was smiling while crying, and that the tears appeared to be "tears of a grateful nature."

Case number 2024-00348.

The third report was the PTA treasurer. The fourth was a veterinarian and his receptionist. The fifth was a county roads employee found on the lawn of the First Presbyterian Church in a state I will decline to detail, except to say that the church's outdoor nativity set, which had not been taken down since Christmas due to a volunteer shortage, was involved in a way that required the department to photograph it from several angles for the file, and that Dale studied those photographs with the same grave attention he had given the water-slide zoning dispute three days before.

By Thursday we had eleven case numbers. By Saturday, nineteen. By the following Wednesday, the number had exceeded Donna's ability to file in real time, and she came in on a non-scheduled day — a Friday — and I could see from the set of her jaw and the way she opened a new box of colored folders that she understood this was no longer a series of isolated incidents. Donna was, in her own way, as much a proceduralist as Dale. She did not say the word *outbreak*. She simply began a new organizational subsection within her system, labeled it "Unclassified Behavioral," and bought more blue folders.

The state sent an advisory. I will reproduce it here in its entirety, because its entirety was three sentences: "The Oklahoma Department of Health has received reports of atypical behavioral episodes in several eastern counties. Residents are advised to avoid prolonged exposure to unfamiliar atmospheric conditions. Further guidance is forthcoming." The word *forthcoming* was doing a lot of heavy lifting in that sentence. No further guidance came. I am not sure the Oklahoma Department of Health is still issuing guidance. I am not sure the Oklahoma Department of Health exists in a form that would recognize the concept of guidance.

Dale read the advisory, pinned it to the corkboard behind his desk, and circled the word *atypical* in red ink. Underneath it he wrote: *Insufficient.*

Then he requisitioned a case board — the kind with the corkboard and the rolling wheels, the kind you see on television procedurals when the detectives are tracking a serial killer. We had one of these in storage. It had last been used for a string of catalytic converter thefts in 2021. Dale wheeled it into his office, cleared the old thumbtack holes, and began pinning up the case files, the photographs, the paramedic reports, the body-camera stills. He ran string between the pins. He used different colors of string for different categories: red for incidents involving clergy, blue for incidents involving public employees, green for incidents on public property, yellow for incidents involving more than three participants. After three days the board looked like something between a spider web and a transit map, and Dale would stand in front of it with his arms crossed and his reading glasses low on his nose, moving his lips slightly, as though the string were a sentence that would parse if he just found the verb.

"Roy," he said on the fourth day, "I want you to look at this board and tell me what you see."

I looked at the board. I saw a lot of string. I saw nineteen case files and forty-some photographs and a map of Pottawatomie County with colored pins marking each incident location, and I saw that the pins were clustered in patterns that were either meaningful or random, depending on whether you were the kind of person who saw patterns in clusters, which Dale was and I was not, or at least I tried not to be.

"I see the case files," I said.

"You see the *distribution*," Dale said. He tapped the map. "Earlsboro first. Then Shawnee. Then Tecumseh. Then Maud. It's moving northeast to southwest. There's a vector, Roy. This thing has a vector, and a vector has an origin point, and an origin point means there's a source, and a source is something you can find."

"The state says it's atmospheric."

"The state says *atypical*. The state has a thesaurus. I have a case board." He pulled a pin from his teeth and stuck it into the map, south of Maud, in an area where no incident had yet been reported. "I'm going to put a unit here. If my vector is right, the next incident will be in this zone within seventy-two hours."

The next incident occurred sixty-one hours later, four miles from his pin. Two ranchers and a mail carrier, in a barn, in a manner involving baling twine. Dale, when informed, allowed himself one small, tight nod — less triumphant than confirmatory — and drew a new line on his map.

It was around this time that the national news began covering what they were alternately calling "the heartland episodes," "the compulsion phenomenon," and, on one cable network that did not survive the first month, "America's mass pheromone event." The pheromone aspect had been identified by a CDC team in Tulsa, who released a preliminary report confirming the presence of an airborne biochemical agent "of unknown provenance" that appeared to act on the human limbic system, suppressing inhibition and dramatically amplifying sexual response.
