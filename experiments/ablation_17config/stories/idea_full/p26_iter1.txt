# The Waterboy's Testimony

---

## I.

The doctor's office smelled like a wrestling room.

I was sitting in one of those vinyl chairs they bolt to the wall, the kind with chrome armrests that hold the cold no matter how long you've been gripping them, and the nurse had opened something—Betadine, maybe, or whatever they use now—and the smell reached me before the reason for it did. Iodine. Sharp, medical, brown-orange. And under it, the ghost of rubber mats, of mildew growing in the cracks between rolled-up sections nobody ever cleaned properly, and of something else I can only describe as the mineral residue of exertion—sweat that has soaked into vinyl so many times and over so many years that the material itself becomes a record. A repository. A witness.

My hands were in my lap. My granddaughter was somewhere down the hall getting an X-ray of her wrist, which she'd fractured doing something on the monkey bars that her mother had warned her not to do, and I was supposed to be filling out an insurance form on a clipboard, and instead I was sitting in that bolted-down chair with a ballpoint pen in my fingers and the iodine in my nose and I was gone. I was back. I was seventeen and I was kneeling at the edge of a mat in a gymnasium in Placer, Kentucky, in the autumn and winter of 1986, and I was holding a plastic squeeze bottle of water in each hand and I was watching something I have never been able to stop watching, not really, not in thirty-seven years.

My name doesn't matter. It didn't matter then. That is the first thing you need to understand, or you will misread everything I'm about to tell you. I was the waterboy. The towel-fetcher. The kneel-down boy. I had asthma that turned my lungs into whistling bags of wet paper every time I tried to run a full practice, and I was built wrong for every weight class—too tall for 119, too narrow for 145, too brittle everywhere. Coach Skaggs kept me on because my mother asked him to, and because someone had to fill the bottles and stack the headgear and mop the warm-up area between bouts, and because I was willing to do all of it for the privilege of being two feet from the mat when the whistle blew. I was there. I was so close I could hear the breathing. I could hear the small, desperate sounds that boys made when they were being turned, sounds that an audience, even an audience six rows back, could never detect under the noise of its own want.

This is a story about Judah Combs. I have tried to tell it many times. In my head, mostly. Once or twice out loud—to my wife, before she was my wife, and to a man I sat next to at a bar in Lexington in 1999 who I never saw again and chose specifically because I would never see him again. Each time, the telling breaks down at the same point. Not at the end, where you'd expect. At the beginning. At the part where I have to describe what he was, and my words go sideways, because what he was does not fit inside one sentence. He was—

He was the best thing that ever happened to that town, and he was—

No.

He came to us. I'll start there. He came to us in September. And what I'm about to tell you is what I saw from two feet away, from my knees, with a bottle in each hand, which is the only vantage I've ever had on anything in my life. You'll have to decide if it's enough.

---

## II.

You have to understand Placer, or none of this means what it means.

Placer, Kentucky. Pike County. Population 2,300 the year I'm talking about, down from 3,100 the year I was born, which was itself down from 4,600 the year my father was born, which is to say the town was a thing in the process of becoming a smaller thing, the way a bar of soap is still a bar of soap when it's half the size it started but you can see where it's going. The Eastover mine had cut its third shift in '82 and its second in '84, and the men who'd worked those shifts were still in town because where would they go and with what, and they sat on porches that needed painting and drove trucks that needed manifolds and they watched the high school sports schedule the way I imagine men on lifeboats watch the horizon. Not for entertainment. For evidence. Evidence that the world still had a spot marked for them in it.

Main Street was five blocks long. Three of those blocks had storefronts with plywood in the windows, and the plywood had been there long enough to warp and stain, which meant it had passed from temporary to permanent without anyone holding a ceremony for the transition. The diner was open. Gainer's Hardware was open but only in the way that a mouth is open on a dead man—the shape was right but nothing was moving through it. The Piggly Wiggly held on. The high school held on. The First Baptist Church of Placer not only held on but swelled, because when the material economy collapses, the spiritual economy inflates to fill the vacancy, and Pastor Kern was shrewd enough to understand that what people needed was not theology but proximity to other people who were enduring the same slow diminishment, so that the diminishment could be recategorized as "fellowship" and therefore made survivable.

Wrestling. I need to explain wrestling.

In most of Kentucky, in most of Appalachia, football is the language and basketball is the religion. But Placer was too small for football—we couldn't field the numbers, hadn't been able to since the seventies—and our basketball team was a recurring act of municipal embarrassment, five gangly boys launching bricks at a rim in a gymnasium so old the backboard bolts wept rust every spring. Wrestling was what we had. It was ours by default, and then by choice, and then by something closer to identity, the way a man who inherits an ugly house and can't afford to leave it will, given enough years, begin to believe the house is beautiful.

It was the right sport for the place. I understood this even then, even before I had the words for it. Football requires land, equipment, numbers, money. Basketball requires height, or at least the illusion of height in the form of leaping. Wrestling requires a body and a willingness to use it against another body, and it takes place in a space the size of a living room, and the smallest boy in the school can do it if he has enough want in him, and our boys had want. God, they had want. Three generations of it. Want bred into the bone by grandfathers who'd gone into the mountain and fathers who'd gone into the mountain and a mountain that had, one day, decided it was finished with all of them.

But we lost. That is the thing I have to tell you alongside the other thing, and they are the same thing. We lost. Year after year, season after season, the Placer Rams wrestling team drove its rented van to gymnasiums across the eastern part of the state and wrestled with a fury that was beautiful and useless and came home with another season below .500. The trophy case outside the main office had glass shelves and fluorescent backlighting installed in 1974 by a booster club that had, at the time, expected there would be things to illuminate, and by 1986 it held: one third-place regional plaque from 1979, a photograph of a boy named Darren Castle who'd made the state quarterfinals in 1981 and then blown out his knee and now worked at the Piggly Wiggly, and a hand-lettered sign that read GO RAMS in green and gold that someone had propped in the corner and that had faded, over the years, into a color that was neither green nor gold but the specific color of abandoned optimism.

Coach Skaggs. I loved Coach Skaggs. I want to say that clearly, because what I'm about to tell you might make it sound like I'm blaming him, and I'm not. Or if I am, I'm blaming him the way you blame a man for being exactly what he is and no more. Arley Skaggs had wrestled at Placer, class of '68, 138 pounds, with a record of 14-11 his senior year that was and remained the best individual season in school history. He coached because no one else would. He coached the way he'd wrestled, with a decency and a doggedness and a fundamental lack of imagination that were, in retrospect, the qualities that made the town love him and the team lose. He believed in conditioning, in repetition, in the small number of moves he himself had mastered twenty years prior. He did not believe in adaptation, because adaptation implies that what you are is not enough, and Arley Skaggs had built his entire life on the premise that what Placer was, was enough. Had to be.

The gymnasium seated four hundred. On match nights, five hundred came. They stood along the walls. They sat on the floor behind the scorer's table. They brought cushions and thermoses and hand-painted signs, and they shouted with a hoarse, full-throated sincerity that I have never heard replicated at any professional sporting event I have since attended, because professional cheering is about preference and this was about survival. When our boys won a bout—any bout, even an exhibition—the gymnasium produced a sound that was less a cheer than a detonation of accumulated need, a collective exhalation that said: *See? See? We're still here. We can still make something happen.* And when they lost, which was more often, the silence had a texture. It settled over the bleachers like ash. People drove home quiet. The town absorbed it. The town had become expert at absorbing it.

That was Placer.

That was the town Judah Combs walked into.

## III.

He enrolled the second week of September. This I know because I was in the main office returning a mop when Mrs. Dalton, the registrar, was processing his paperwork, and I saw his name on the top of a Manila folder and did not think about it again until practice the following Monday, when Coach Skaggs introduced him to the team.

"Boys, this is Judah Combs. Just moved here with his mama. He's going to work out with us and we're going to make him feel welcome."

That was the entire introduction. Judah stood at the edge of the mat in a plain gray T-shirt and gym shorts that looked new, and he had his hands at his sides, and he nodded and said, "Thank you, Coach," and that was the first time I heard his voice, which was soft and without identifiable accent, which in Placer was itself a kind of accent—the accent of nowhere, or of somewhere deliberately scrubbed.

He was not physically remarkable. I want to be precise about this because the mythology that later built up around him—and it was a mythology, complete and self-reinforcing and absolutely lethal—tended to retrofit his body into something it was not. He was five foot nine. Maybe 155 pounds. Lean but not cut. He had dark hair that he kept short, and a face that was pleasant without being handsome, and eyes that were brown and quiet and that did something I noticed the first day and then persuaded myself I hadn't noticed, which was: they moved independently of his expression. His mouth smiled. His posture was open, deferential, correct. His eyes traveled the room like they were taking measurements.

Coach Skaggs paired him with DeWayne Sloan for a live go in practice. DeWayne was our 152, a senior, the closest thing we had to a threat—a stocky, low-centered boy who'd gone 19-14 the year before and who wrestled the way he did everything, which was with a relentless, grinding, an almost peristaltic forward pressure that wore opponents down through sheer insistence. DeWayne was good. DeWayne was our best.

They shook hands. They took their stances. Coach blew the whistle.

DeWayne shot immediately—a double-leg, his bread and butter, the move he'd drilled ten thousand times. It was a clean shot. Fast. Low. His hands hit the backs of Judah's knees and his head drove forward into Judah's hip and the whole thing should have ended with Judah on his back or at least sprawled and scrambling.

Judah did not sprawl. He did not scramble. What he did was—

I've tried to describe this. I've tried for decades. The closest I can come is to say that he *agreed* with DeWayne's shot. He let it happen. He absorbed the forward drive the way a net absorbs a ball—giving way, yielding, letting the energy transfer—and in the yielding, something shifted. DeWayne's momentum, which should have been his weapon, became his exposure. Judah's hips dropped and turned, and his hands found positions on DeWayne's body that I couldn't track—wrist, shoulder, somewhere near the neck—and then DeWayne was rotating, not violently, not with the wrenching force I was used to seeing, but slowly, almost carefully, the way you'd turn a child in your arms so he could see something you wanted to show him. And DeWayne was on his back. And Judah's chest was on DeWayne's chest, and the weight was distributed with an evenness that looked effortless and was, I now understand, the product of a technique so advanced that no one in that gymnasium had the vocabulary to identify it.

Three seconds. Maybe four. From whistle to pin.

The gym did not erupt. The gym went silent. I know because I was kneeling four feet away with a bottle of water in my right hand and a towel over my left shoulder, and the silence was so complete that I could hear the fluorescent lights buzzing in their casings and the idling of a truck in the parking lot outside.

Coach Skaggs was standing with his whistle still in his mouth. He had not blown it for the pin. He was just standing there.

DeWayne was on his back looking at the ceiling with an expression I recognized from experience, if not from wrestling—the expression of someone who has just been shown that the world contains a category of thing he did not know existed and that this knowledge has rearranged him in some way he cannot yet articulate. Judah removed his weight. Judah extended his hand. DeWayne took it. Judah pulled him up and said something that I, from four feet away in a silent gym, could not hear. I don't think anyone heard it. He said it directly into the space between his mouth and DeWayne's ear, and then he released DeWayne's hand and turned and walked to the edge of the mat.

This is where I saw it.

He was walking toward me—toward the water bottles, toward the bench—and his face was still in transition. The expression he'd had during the match, which was a kind of focused blankness, was dissolving, and the expression he would wear for the remaining five months I knew him—humility, gratitude, the quiet deference of a boy who knew he had a gift and didn't want to make anyone uncomfortable with it—was not yet in place. There was a gap. A half second, maybe less, when neither mask was on and what was underneath was visible, and I saw it because I was kneeling directly in his path and I was the waterboy and I was nothing and his face had not yet dressed itself for company.

I did not know what the expression was. I have thought about it for thirty-seven years. The closest I have come, and it is not close enough, is that it was the expression of a person who has *confirmed* something. Not discovered. Confirmed. As though the match with DeWayne had been not a contest but a calibration, and the instrument had returned the reading he expected, and he was satisfied in the way that a builder is satisfied when a load-bearing wall holds the weight he calculated it would hold.

He reached me. The expression was gone. In its place was a shy half-smile, slight and warm and perfectly boyish.

"Could I have some water?" he asked.

I handed him the bottle.

"Thank you," he said. And then: "What's your name?"

Nobody asked me my name.

I told him.

"Good to meet you," Judah Combs said.
