# The Greenhouse on the Roof

---

## I.

I have told this story four times. Once to a licensing board, where it mattered. Once to a colleague over drinks in a hotel bar in San Antonio, where it didn't. Once to my husband, who listened the way he listens to everything, which is to say with his hands around a mug and his eyes on me and a patience I have never deserved. And now to you, whoever you are, because I am seventy-one and Maren has been gone from Detroit for six years and I still keep her intake form in the drawer of my desk beside the pencil sharpener, and I have begun to suspect that the only way to put a thing down is to give it to someone else to hold.

I am a retired clinical psychologist. Thirty-eight years of practice, the last twelve in supervision, which means I spent them teaching younger therapists how to sit with other people's pain without either drowning in it or building a boat. I was good at this. I want you to know that because I need you to understand that what I did, I did with a trained hand. Whether the hand was steady is another question.

Maren Gale came to me as a supervision referral in the spring of 2016. She was twenty-six. Her application was handwritten on the back of a water bill — this was my first indication that she was either charmingly eccentric or clinically disorganized, and I have learned that the distance between those two things is about six months. Under "therapeutic orientation" she had written *listening, mostly.* Under "population served" she had written *people who want love and are wrong about what that means.* Under "office address" she had written a street number I did not recognize, followed by the word *roof.*

I should have asked more questions. I asked her to come in for coffee instead.

She was small, sharp-featured, prone to wearing men's flannels with the sleeves rolled past her elbows, and she had a quality I can only describe as attention so focused it felt like heat. When she sat across from me at my kitchen table, she looked at me the way a thoughtful child looks at a clock — not at the face, but at the place where the ticking comes from. I liked her immediately. I also recognized her immediately, which is to say I recognized the particular brightness of a person who has built her whole life around a gift she has not yet examined, and I thought: *I can help her.* I thought: *This is what I'm for.*

She told me, that first afternoon, that she could hear what people were thinking.

She said it the way you'd say you were double-jointed or mildly lactose intolerant — a fact of the body, neither boast nor complaint. I asked her to elaborate. She said it wasn't like the movies. She didn't hear full sentences narrated in people's voices. She heard — and here she paused, and picked at the cuff of her flannel, and looked past me toward the window — she heard the *underneath.* The thing below the thing someone was saying. The want beneath the stated want. The fear propping up the preference.

I asked her how long this had been the case.

"Always," she said. "But I didn't know other people couldn't do it until I was maybe twelve."

I asked her if she'd seen anyone about it.

She laughed. It was a good laugh, sudden and unguarded. "You mean a therapist? Dr. Kessler, I *am* a therapist. I'm just not a very credentialed one."

This was true. She had no degree, no license, no malpractice insurance. What she had was a greenhouse on a parking structure roof in Corktown, a growing list of clients who had found her through word of mouth, and what I can only call a vocation — meaning a thing she did not choose but could not stop doing, a thing that used her as much as she used it. She heard too much and she listened too little. By which I mean: she listened to everyone except herself, and she did it so skillfully that it looked like wisdom rather than what it was.

I agreed to supervise her. I set up biweekly phone calls. I asked her to keep notes.

I did not yet understand what I was agreeing to supervise, or what it would cost us both.

---

## II.

The greenhouse was on top of the Arnault Street parking structure, a seven-story concrete block that had serviced a department store that closed in 2003. By the time Maren found it, the upper levels had been chained off for years. The greenhouse itself was a relic — built in the 1970s by someone whose name the city had forgotten, a glass-and-steel frame meant for a rooftop garden that a series of ownership transfers had rendered no one's responsibility. It sat at the northwest corner of the roof, roughly twelve by twenty feet, with half its panes missing, a drain clogged with leaves and pigeon feathers, and a volunteer colony of tomato plants that had somehow rooted in the crumbling grout between the floor tiles.

I never saw it in person until later. But Maren described it to me during our calls with the specificity of someone describing a cathedral, and later I read about it in the notes she pinned to the glass — notes I eventually held in my hands, their ink blurred by condensation, their edges curled from sun. She wrote to the greenhouse. Not about it. *To* it. As though the building were a confidant she trusted more than any person, which — I understand now — it was.

What she told me was this: that at street level, Detroit was unbearable. Not the city itself — she loved Detroit, loved it with the fierce loyalty of someone who had chosen it rather than been born to it — but the *noise.* Thousands of minds layered over each other, wanting, grieving, raging, hungering. She described it as standing inside a crowd that was also somehow inside her. She wore headphones that played nothing. She took temp jobs in warehouses with skeleton crews. She kept her apartment on the ground floor of a duplex in Mexicantown because her downstairs neighbor was very old and very quiet and thought mostly about his garden.

She found the greenhouse by accident. She'd gone to the parking structure to be alone — height helped, she'd discovered, the way thinning a forest thins its sound. But when she pushed through the rusted door to the rooftop and stepped inside the glass frame, something changed. The noise didn't vanish. It dropped. Like someone had turned the volume from ten to two. She could hear the city still, but distantly, the way you hear traffic from inside a house. And for the first time in what she described as years, she could hear her own thoughts without other people's thoughts crowding against them.

One of the notes pinned to the eastern pane, written in her small, slanted hand, read:

<p style="text-align: center; font-style: italic; margin: 1.5em 0;">Up here I am only one person.</p>

I found that note beautiful when she read it to me over the phone. I find it beautiful still. But I hear it differently now. *Up here I am only one person* does not mean *up here I am finally myself.* It means *up here I am not overwhelmed by others.* Those are not the same thing. One is presence. The other is absence. Maren mistook relief for wholeness, the way a person with chronic pain mistakes the first hour without it for health rather than remission.

She cleaned the greenhouse out over a series of weekends. She replaced four of the broken panes with plastic sheeting. She swept the floor, unclogged the drain, repotted the tomatoes into salvaged buckets. She brought in two mismatched chairs and a low table she'd found on the curb on Michigan Avenue. She hung a wind chime from a nail in the frame — copper tubes, green with patina, that rang in fourths when the wind came from the west.

It was a real place. I want to be clear about that. It was not a symbol. It was glass and rust and pigeon shit and the particular quality of light that comes through dirty panes in southeast Michigan in the afternoon — not golden, not white, but a color I'd call *tired silver,* the light of a city that has been through something and is still here. The tomatoes were real. The wind chime was real. The peace Maren felt was real.

But a crutch that works is still a crutch, and the comfort of a quiet room can become the walls of one if you mistake the room for the cure.

---

## III.

The practice began, as most callings do, with one person telling another person, who told a third.

Maren's first real client was a friend of her neighbor's grandson — a woman named Debra, fifty-three, a payroll administrator at a small manufacturing firm in Hamtramck, recently divorced and certain that what she needed was "a man who has his act together." This was what she wrote on the intake form, which Maren had designed herself: a single index card with three prompts.

**Name:**
**What you think you want:**
**What you're afraid of:**

Debra filled in the first two. She left the third blank.

Maren told me about this session during our second supervision call. She said she'd sat across from Debra in the greenhouse with the afternoon light coming through the plastic sheeting, and she'd listened — listened the way she listened, which was not the way I'd been trained and not the way I trained others, but which I could not, in honesty, call anything other than extraordinary.

What Maren heard, beneath Debra's stated desire for a competent and financially stable man, was this:

*somebody who won't need me to perform being okay*
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*I am so tired of smiling when I come home*
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*Doug never once asked me how I was and meant it, not once in twenty-one years*
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*I don't want ambition I want someone who sits down*
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*I want to be ugly on a Tuesday and have someone stay in the room*

Maren told me she'd sat with this for a long moment while Debra talked about salary ranges and fitness habits. Then Maren asked: "What if what you actually want is someone who can see you on your worst day and not flinch?"

Debra cried. Not dramatically — just a sudden, startled leaking of tears, the way a pipe cries when the pressure shifts. She said: "How did you know that?"

Maren said: "You told me. You just didn't use those words."

I remember sitting at my desk, phone pressed to my ear, thinking: *She is doing something I cannot do.* I remember the complicated feeling of that — pride, envy, unease, a mentor's particular joy at being surpassed. I asked Maren how she planned to find a match for Debra. She said she'd wait. She said the right person would come through the door eventually. She said she'd know by the underneath.

Over the following weeks, I heard about more clients. Each one arrived with an index card and a stated want. Each one carried, beneath the stated want, a secret architecture of need they could not articulate and in some cases did not consciously know.

A man in his thirties, a high school track coach, wrote that he wanted "someone adventurous, someone who keeps things interesting." Maren heard:

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*I am boring I know I'm boring*
*my father said it every day, you're the most boring kid I've ever met*
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*I don't need adventure I need someone*
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*who finds me interesting*
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*just me, just sitting there, just enough*

A woman, barely twenty, a line cook with a shaved head and a septum ring, wrote that she was afraid of "settling." Maren heard:

*afraid I'll end up like my mom*
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*afraid I'll love someone more than they love me*
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*afraid I already do that*
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*with everyone*
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*every single time*
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*god I do it at work, I do it when I order coffee, I am always*
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*trying to make people love me and I don't know how to stop*

Maren matched the track coach with a woman who thought in images rather than words — a ceramicist from Eastern Market whose interior life, Maren told me, was a slow, warm current, like a river in August. She thought he was interesting. She genuinely thought so. She found his steadiness beautiful. And he, sensing that — not telepathically, just humanly — began to believe it.

Maren matched the line cook with a man ten years older, a librarian, whose underneath was the calmest Maren had ever heard — not because he had no fears, but because he had already examined them, slowly, patiently, the way he examined everything. His love, when it came, would not need to be earned through performance. It would simply arrive, like a book returned to the right shelf.

These were good matches. I want to be clear: they were *good.* Some of them lasted. Some of them, I have reason to believe, are lasting still. What Maren did in that greenhouse was not a trick and not a con. She saw people with a depth of perception I have never encountered in four decades of clinical work, and she used that perception to build connections that were genuine and often life-changing.

But here is what I noticed, and what I noted in my supervision files, with a growing unease I did not act on quickly enough:

Maren's own thoughts were never part of the record.

In the sessions she described to me, in the notes she pinned to the glass, in the index cards and the cascading interiors of everyone who sat across from her — Maren was everywhere and nowhere. She was the lens. She was the medium through which other people's hidden selves became visible. But her own interior remained unpresented, unspoken, unexamined. I never heard her cascade. In all the time I supervised her, she never once told me what her own underneath sounded like.

I should have pressed harder. I should have pressed at all. Instead, I told myself she was young, she was gifted, she would come to it in time. These are the lies supervisors tell when the person they're supervising makes them feel like they're witnessing something sacred. I didn't want to interrupt the miracle. I wanted to watch.

---

## IV.

By the fall of 2017, Maren had become the kind of open secret that Detroit is good at keeping — known to everyone who needed her, invisible to everyone who didn't. A writer for the *Metro Times* ran a half-page profile under the headline "The Matchmaker of Corktown," which described Maren's greenhouse practice in terms that were affectionate and only slightly incredulous. The writer had been a client. She did not say which match was hers, but the warmth in her prose was specific rather than general, the warmth of someone who had been accurately seen.

After the article, the index cards multiplied. Maren told me — in one of our increasingly brief and infrequent calls — that she was seeing eight, ten, twelve people a week. People drove from Ann Arbor. People drove from Dearborn, from Ypsilanti, from Toledo. A couple from Grand Rapids made the three-hour trip because they were failing at loving each other and someone had told them about a woman in a greenhouse who could hear what you actually meant.
