# Love & Liability: The Collapse of the Magnificent Ludo Gang

---

## I.

The cast goes from my left shoulder to the bottom of my ribs and then around behind my back in a way that Dr. Altmeyer says is "architecturally necessary," which is a word I wish doctors would not use around me anymore. I can hold a pen. I can hold a cigarette, though Dr. Altmeyer would prefer I didn't. I can hold a clipboard, and since October that's been about the extent of what anyone's needed from me.

My name is Francie Delk. I've been a licensed lookout in the tristate area since 1964, union-certified class C with an optical endorsement, meaning I'm rated for night work and telescopic surveillance up to four hundred meters. I'm currently on modified desk duty, processing the dissolution paperwork for the crew I've worked with for the last eleven years. The crew was called the Magnificent Ludo Gang, which was Ludo's idea. Nobody else voted on it. There's a form for that — IRS Schedule K-1(CR), Line 14, "Trade Name, DBA, and Associated Branding Costs" — and Ludo filed it without a group consultation, which I could have told you then was a sign of things to come if anyone had thought to ask the lookout. Nobody asks the lookout. That's not a complaint. That's a job description.

The Magnificent Ludo Gang no longer exists. What I'm putting on the record here is how it came apart. Not why — I think "why" is above my pay grade and possibly above everyone's — but how, which is the kind of question a lookout is suited to answer. I've spent eleven years on rooftops, in parked cars, on fire escapes, on one occasion in a dumbwaiter that turned out to be non-operational, watching things happen to other people. I have been present for every significant event in this crew's history and I have participated in none of them. My operational role has been to observe, report, and, if necessary, start the car. The only reason I'm in this cast is that observation, in this line of work, does not protect you from falling debris.

I should tell you a few things about how this works, because people outside the industry have strange ideas. We file quarterly. We pay Social Security on gross haul minus approved deductions — equipment, explosives, bribes to local officials where local ordinance permits, union dues, mileage. The IRS Criminal Revenue Division audits us like they audit anyone: inconsistently and with prejudice toward the self-employed. If you underreport, you go to revenue jail, which is different from regular jail in that the food is slightly better, the sentences are shorter, and you come out owing more than when you went in. There's no drama in it. You go, you sit, you fill out amended returns, and you come home. It's like jury duty that lasts three years.

Ludo Paak's quarterly filings were late every single quarter I worked with him. Not delinquent. Not fraudulent — that came later and was a separate issue. Just late. Three days, four days, once an entire month. He'd hold the envelope in his left hand and his car keys in his right and stand in the doorway of whatever motel we were staging from and say, "Francie, what's the date," and I'd tell him, and he'd say, "Is that a postmark date or a sincerely-held-belief date," and I would not laugh because it wasn't funny the first time and it certainly wasn't funny the forty-seventh time, and he'd mail it four days late and then act mortally wounded when the penalty notice came. The man could open a Harding & Block triple-tumbler delayed-relock vault in under nine minutes in the dark with nothing but a field stethoscope and a glass of something he shouldn't have been drinking on the job. He could not mail an envelope on time.

Everyone thinks the crew fell apart because of Bess. That's almost right. The crew fell apart because Ludo and Bess were still the best two-person safecracking team in the tristate area, and everybody standing near them knew it.

I'll start from when Ludo got out.

---

## II.

Revenue jail, upstate. The Schenectady Minimum-Revenue Correctional Facility, which the inmates call the Receipt Drawer. Ludo did three years for filing fraudulent deductions on explosive materials. The specifics were that he'd been writing off det cord as "office supplies — miscellaneous adhesive products," which the auditor flagged because the deductions exceeded eleven thousand dollars in a single fiscal year and because det cord is not, by any accepted definition, adhesive. The auditor who caught it was Glen Moray, GS-13, Federal Theft Auditor, Criminal Revenue Division, Newark field office. I mention this because it becomes important later, though I suspect most of you already know that.

Ludo came out in April. I drove up to get him because no one else wanted to and because the crew, such as it remained, had taken a vote and determined that my car was the least likely to upset him. This was a considered decision. Wheels Petrosky offered his Cadillac, which was the obvious choice for picking up a crew chief from a corrections facility, but Dolly Lhotka pointed out that the Cadillac's trunk still had the broken drill press from the Wilmington job in it and the symbolism of picking up a man from jail in a car full of defunct equipment was, in Dolly's words, "not ideal from a morale standpoint." So they sent me in the Chevelle, which was beige and dented and said nothing about anything, which I've always thought was appropriate for a lookout vehicle and which turned out to be appropriate for this, too.

He came out with a box. The box had his personal effects from intake plus three years of amended tax returns he'd completed during incarceration, organized in manila folders and labeled in his handwriting, which was meticulous. Ludo's handwriting was always meticulous. If you saw his handwriting on a form you'd think: here is a man with his life in order. You'd be wrong, but you'd think it.

He put the box in the back seat and sat up front and said, "How's Bess."

I said, "You should probably hear this from someone other than me."

He said, "Francie, you're the only person I trust to give me information without editorializing."

This was true. It was also the reason no one else had volunteered to drive.

I told him Bess had filed for divorce fourteen months into his sentence, that the divorce was finalized six months later, and that she had married Glen Moray the following spring. I told him the wedding was small, tasteful, and held at the Marriott off Route 17, which several crew members had attended out of loyalty to Bess and about which they all still felt guilty and confused. I told him Glen Moray was, by all accounts, a decent man who filed on time, kept his shoes clean, had never detonated anything in his life, and loved Bess with the sincere and organized devotion of a man who alphabetizes his spice rack. I told him Bess and Glen were now operating a licensed crew — Moray & Paak Associates, she'd kept her professional name — with Glen's regulatory connections providing institutional access and Bess's safecracking providing the actual service. They were doing well. They had a dental plan.

Ludo was quiet for about forty-five seconds, which, if you knew Ludo, was the equivalent of a civilian man being quiet for about six months.

Then he said, "She can't use the Paak name for a competing operation. That's a non-compete violation. That's in the partnership filing."

I said, "Ludo."

He said, "I need to see the original partnership agreement. There's a clause — Section 8, subsection D — "

"Ludo."

"A federal auditor running a crew. Do you understand what that is, Francie? That's a conflict of interest so blatant it should trigger congressional review. He *audited* us. He audited our last three jobs. He saw our methods, our margins, our supplier list — "

"Ludo."

" — and now he's *married to my safecracker* and running jobs on *our territory* with *our techniques* and filing them under — "

"Ludo, she's not your safecracker."

That stopped him. Not because he agreed with me. Because the sentence contained a word he wasn't ready to hear used in that configuration. He looked out the window at the highway for a while. We were passing a billboard for a personal injury attorney that said HURT ON THE JOB? and I thought about how in our line of work that question had about fourteen more layers of complexity than the billboard's creators intended.

"How's the crew," he said, eventually.

The crew was not great. I told him so. In the three years he'd been inside, we'd freelanced — picking up subcontract work from other licensed operations, filling in on jobs that needed extra hands. Low-margin stuff. Grocery store safes, small-town credit unions with single-dial vaults you could open with a stiff look. Dolly Lhotka had been handling demolitions, which was technically Ludo's role, and she was competent but uninspired. Wheels was restless. Our newest member, a kid named Perry Keach who'd joined as a runner six months before Ludo went in, had been promoted to interior work by necessity and was developing a nervous blink that I attributed to professional anxiety and that Dr. Altmeyer would later attribute to a scratched cornea from plaster dust, which amounted to the same thing. We were making enough to cover union dues and quarterly filings and not much else. The crew's W-2 equivalents for the past three years would depress you.

We pulled into the motel lot where the crew was waiting. Ludo got out of the car and stood there with his box of amended returns and looked at the four people who were left of his operation. Dolly was sitting on the hood of the van, smoking. Wheels was leaning against the ice machine. Perry was blinking. They looked at Ludo the way employees look at an unstable boss returning from a leave of absence: with hope they didn't trust and anxiety they couldn't hide.

Ludo said, "I want a job by Thursday."

Perry blinked.

I stayed in the car, engine running, because the engine was already running and because that's what I do, and I watched through the windshield as Ludo shook hands with each of them and said things I couldn't hear, and I noted in my log — I keep a log; union regs require the lookout to maintain a contemporaneous job diary — that the crew chief had been retrieved, that he appeared to be in adequate physical condition, and that his first stated priority upon release was operational rather than personal, which I recorded as a factual observation without editorial comment, per my role.

What I did not write, because it was not a factual observation but a professional intuition developed over fourteen years of watching things from across the street, was that the way Ludo had said "my safecracker" — possessive, automatic, like a man referring to his own left hand — was going to be the thing that broke us.

---

## III.

The job was a savings-and-loan in Bethlehem, Pennsylvania. The Lehigh Valley Mutual Savings & Loan, a three-story limestone building on East Broad Street, built in 1921 and last renovated in 1954 in a manner that the Bethlehem Building Code Enforcement Office had described in a 1971 inspection report as "structurally adequate with notable deferred maintenance in sub-floor supports, mezzanine level." I know this because I pull building permits and code inspections as part of my pre-job research. The lookout does more than look. The lookout *reads*.

The vault was a Mosler class F, which was well within our capability. Ludo prepped the job in two days, which was fast even for him, and which I noted in my diary as "timeline compressed — possible motivational factors beyond professional scope." He ran the briefing in the motel room with a hand-drawn floor plan taped to the bathroom mirror and a level of intensity that was familiar from his best work but which now carried a frequency I hadn't heard before: higher-pitched, more brittle. Like a tuning fork struck too hard.

What he did not know, because he had not asked and because I had not yet completed my pre-job surveillance, was that Moray & Paak Associates had been contracted for a federal audit-recovery operation on the same institution that same week. The Lehigh Valley Mutual Savings & Loan was under IRS review for unreported interest income, and Glen Moray's crew had a federal warrant to access the vault, inspect the contents, and document any discrepancies for the Criminal Revenue Division's institutional fraud desk. They were coming in through the front door, with paperwork. We were coming in through a steam tunnel that connected to the basement. The bureaucratic overlap was, within the world's rules, entirely legal: a licensed crew could operate on any target not currently under active federal hold, and the audit-recovery warrant technically applied only to the institution's records, not to the physical contents of the vault, meaning both operations could proceed simultaneously in the same building without either one being in violation, provided they filed the appropriate concurrent-access forms, which neither crew had done because nobody ever does.

I found out about Moray & Paak's involvement at 11:40 p.m. on the night of the job, while sitting in the Chevelle on East Broad Street, when I saw Bess Paak — Bess Moray, I should say, though I've never been able to make my mouth do it — walk into the building through the service entrance on the east side.

I picked up the radio. "Ludo, you have a situation."

"Define situation."

"Concurrent operation. East entrance. It's Bess."

There was a silence. Not forty-five seconds. Six seconds, maybe seven. In demolitions and safecracking, six seconds is a very long time to be silent. It is long enough for a det cord fuse to burn, for a tumbler sequence to reset, for a man to make a decision that will cost four people their skeletal integrity.

"We proceed," Ludo said.

Dolly, on the demo channel: "Ludo, if there's a federal team in the building — "

"We proceed. We're licensed. We filed. Our operation is legally distinct."

This was technically correct. I wrote "crew chief invokes legal distinction — tone suggests emotional rather than jurisdictional basis" in my diary and did not broadcast it.

What happened inside the Lehigh Valley Mutual Savings & Loan over the next forty minutes has been reconstructed from five sources: my external surveillance log, Dolly's post-incident report to the union, Perry Keach's workers' compensation claim (filed in triplicate, as required), the Bethlehem Police Department's structural damage assessment, and Bess's own account, which she gave three weeks later to the IRS mediator and which I obtained through channels I am not obligated to disclose.

Ludo's crew entered through the steam tunnel and reached the basement at 12:05 a.m. Bess's crew, consisting of Bess, Glen, and two contract employees whose names I don't have, entered through the service door and reached the mezzanine at approximately 12:02 a.m. For three minutes, both crews operated in the same building without awareness of each other's precise location. At 12:08 a.m., Ludo, ascending the basement stairs to the main floor with a field stethoscope around his neck and a satchel of tools over his shoulder, opened the stairwell door and found himself twelve feet from Bess, who was kneeling in front of the vault door with her ear to the steel and her fingers on the dial.

According to Dolly, who was ten feet behind Ludo in the stairwell, he stopped.

Bess did not stop. Bess was listening to the third tumbler in a five-tumbler sequence and she didn't look up when the stairwell door opened because a safecracker mid-sequence does not look up for anything. This is professional discipline. This is also, when the person who has opened the door is your ex-husband, something else — something that occupies a space between discipline and statement that I am not qualified to diagram.

What Ludo did next is the subject of some dispute. His account, given to the union mediator, was that he "advanced to assess the competing operation's methodology and ensure compliance with concurrent-access protocols." Dolly's account was that he "walked straight at her like a man who's forgotten how doors work." Both of these are probably true.
