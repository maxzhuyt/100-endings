## I. Cappadocia

The capsule hotel burrows into the cliff face like a bivalve, each suite a geologic parenthesis carved by wind and water over millennia before being outfitted, in 2019, with heated floors and a minibar. At dawn the fairy chimneys cast shadows that lengthen across the Göreme Valley like — no, delete that, you can't say "like fingers," absolutely not, this is a professional manuscript and the word *finger* in the context of an erotic travel guide is napalm, it's a liability, it's Marco's index finger tracing the rim of a wine glass at dinner and me watching it and everybody else watching me watch it and —

Start again.

*At dawn the Göreme Valley is a study in geological patience. The tuff formations — popularly called fairy chimneys — are the product of volcanic ash compressed over sixty million years and then sculpted, unhurriedly, by erosion. To sleep inside one is to be reminded that the earth, too, has an interior life.*

Good. That's good. "The earth, too, has an interior life." That's the kind of sentence that will make the Condé Nast excerpt. That's the kind of sentence that will make people forget they're reading a book about where to fuck.

The capsule is seventeen square meters. The bed fills eleven of them. The remaining six are devoted to a wet room carved from the living rock, a niche that holds a single beeswax candle the diameter of a fist, and a recessed speaker system playing, at present, something ambient and Turkish that sounds like an oud falling down a well. Marco is in the wet room, running the water. The water here has a mineral smell — sulfur and old iron — and I should write that down, I should be writing all of this down, because this is what the advance was for, this is what the team is for, this is what the —

His phone buzzed twenty minutes ago and it was Gianluca. My husband. Marco's brother. I know this because Marco's screen was on the nightstand and the notification said **Gianluca: Hey little bro are you still** and then it cut off, the way notifications do, the way everything in my life cuts off before the part that matters.

*The capsule suites at Göreme Eros were designed by the Turkish-Finnish architecture collective Sıcak, whose founding principle — that erotic space must feel both ancient and unprecedented — finds its purest expression here. The rooms do not reject modernity; they absorb it. The heated floor is invisible beneath the original stone. The lighting responds to movement. The walls, smoothed by hand over a period of —*

I'm going to be honest. I'm going to tell you what the walls do. What the walls do is hold sound. Fourteen capsules bored into the same cliff face, which means fourteen couples sleeping inside the same rock, which means — and this is not in the brochure but probably should be — you can hear everything. Not words. Not even really voices. More like the rock itself is breathing, tidal, and if you lie very still you can feel the pulse of other people's choices pressing in on you like sediment. Like sixty million years of compressed heat. Like the weight of what you've done settling into geological time and becoming, eventually, just part of the landscape.

Marco comes out of the wet room. His hair is wet. He's thirty-eight. He was twenty-six at the wedding.

My phone lights up in the dark. Not a buzz — I turned the ringer off hours ago — just the screen, spectral, and then Gianluca's voice, because I forgot to turn off voicemail playback, because I am a woman who has mastered the logistics of infidelity across fourteen countries and four continents but cannot remember to change one setting:

"Hey love, just checking in, I know you're probably somewhere without signal — Marco said you guys were heading to some cave hotel? Sounds wild. Sounds like something you'd find." A laugh. A warm, easy, unhurried laugh. "Anyway, don't forget to eat something that isn't hotel food. I love you. I'm making that thing with the lentils tonight and it's going to be — well, it's going to be lentils. Okay. Call me when you surface. Love you."

*When you surface.*

Marco sits on the edge of the bed. He hasn't said anything. He doesn't need to. We've been doing this for twelve years.

Twelve.

Let me say that again in a different register, in the professional voice, in the voice I use when I'm drafting copy for a book that seventeen people are depending on for their professional reputations and one person is depending on for a reason that has nothing to do with professional reputation:

*The Atlas of Erotic Destinations* is a collaborative survey of the world's most architecturally and sensorially significant spaces designed for romantic encounter. It is the product of a multidisciplinary team including specialists in tantric spatial theory, hospitality science, neuroaesthetics, and biochemistry. The project has been in development for —

Twelve years.

The same twelve years.

His hand was on the doorframe to the coatroom. I have to get this right because I've gotten it wrong before, in my own head, I've placed it on the back of a chair, on the banister, on my waist. But it was the doorframe. We were three hours married — Gianluca and I — and the reception was at the villa outside Lucca with the terracotta floors and the jasmine that smelled like it was being paid to smell, and I was twenty-nine and I had published exactly one book, a slim guide to romantic hotels in Umbria that sold nine thousand copies and was blurbed by a woman at the *Telegraph* who called it "sensuous without being sticky, rigorous without being cold," which is a description I have aspired to ever since and which could also, now that I think about it, describe the quality of detachment necessary to sleep with your husband's brother three hours into your marriage and then build a career on the architecture of that specific wound.

But I'm getting ahead. The doorframe. His hand.

No. Further back. The courtyard, the dinner, the dancing, Gianluca's speech that was too long and too sincere and that made his mother cry, and Marco — younger, thinner, with a jaw that hadn't yet settled into the architecture of his thirties — Marco across the table looking at me with an expression I have spent twelve years trying to translate and have rendered variously as *hunger*, *recognition*, *challenge*, *boredom*, *tenderness*, and most recently, in the volcanic capsule suite at Göreme while the oud fell down its well, *habit*.

But it wasn't habit then. Then it was the first time. And the doorframe —

*The entry points of erotic spaces deserve particular architectural attention. A threshold is not merely a boundary between public and private; it is a somatic event. The body registers the transition — the change in temperature, acoustics, light — before the mind articulates it. The finest erotic venues understand this and design their doorways accordingly: narrowed, dimmed, requiring a slight physical adjustment that —*

— that mimics the mechanics of choice. I wrote that. That's my prose. The doorframe to the coatroom at the Villa Castellani was not designed for eroticism. It was a sixteenth-century doorframe in a house built for storing olives. But Marco's hand was on it, and his body was half-turned, and the coatroom was dark and smelled of wool and someone's perfume — not mine, I wasn't wearing perfume, another detail I've edited and re-edited — and he said something. What did he say?

He said, "You look like you need to not be out there for a minute."

And I said.

And I said —

I don't remember what I said. I remember going through the doorframe. I remember the wool smell and the hangers rattling and the sound of the band, still playing, on the other side of the wall that was suddenly — and I need this word exactly — *sufficient*. The wall was sufficient. It was enough wall. It was a wall that could hold the weight of what was about to happen on one side while the other side kept dancing.

I've never written that in the atlas. But every wall I've described since — every capsule suite carved from tuff, every lighthouse lamp room with its glass perimeter, every ryokan screen that slides between *here* and *there* — is that wall. The coatroom wall. The wall that was enough.

## II. Patagonia

*The Refugio Austral sits at the southern edge of the Grey Glacier, where the moraine gives way to an alluvial plain scattered with erratics — boulders deposited by ancient ice flows and then abandoned, monumental and purposeless, in a landscape that seems designed to humble every human structure placed within it. The Refugio's designers have embraced this humility: the camp consists of eight geodesic domes, each containing a king-sized sleeping platform, a woodburning stove, and an uninterrupted sightline to the glacier's calving face. The erotic proposition is austere: here, there is no architecture to mediate the encounter. There is only the ice, the wind, and the improbable warmth of another body.*

Very nice. Very nice. Absolute horseshit. The erotic proposition of the Refugio Austral, as experienced by the on-site assessment team of *The Atlas of Erotic Destinations* on the fourteenth of March in a year I won't specify, is as follows: it is minus eleven degrees Celsius, the wind is rendering speech theoretical, the geodesic domes smell like a combination of damp nylon and the chemical toilet that services the eastern cluster, and the king-sized sleeping platform is, upon closer inspection, two camping cots pushed together with a memory-foam topper that remembers nothing, least of all comfort.

No one could have sex here. I want to be precise about this. No one *has* had sex here. The Refugio Austral's guest book — which Tomás, the retired concierge, inspected with the forensic melancholy of a man who once managed the Hotel Negresco's front desk and is now standing in a nylon dome reading entries that say things like "Incredible glacier!! Saw a condor. Nigel had altitude sickness" — the guest book contains no evidence that any guest has ever removed a single layer of clothing for any purpose other than changing into a different, drier layer of clothing.

And yet.

We're in the camp's common dome — the large one, the one with the propane heater that produces just enough warmth to make you aware of how cold you actually are — and Tomás has presented his site assessment, and Kenji has presented his spatial analysis, and Yael has presented her atmospheric chemistry readings, and I have read aloud my draft copy, and now we are voting.

Kenji, the tantric architect, votes first. He says, "The potential is extraordinary." He says, "The spatial austerity creates a kind of erotic vacuum — the body must generate its own warmth, its own enclosure." He says this while his notebook, open on his lap, displays not a spatial diagram but a sketch of a house — a real house, a house in Nagano — that I know from context is the house his ex-wife lives in with their two daughters, the daughters he hasn't seen in fourteen months because he accepted a position on a project that requires him to travel to a different improbable location every six weeks and evaluate its sexual architecture. He votes yes. He votes yes with enthusiasm. He says, "We should include this. This should be in the book."

Tomás votes next. Tomás, who is sixty-seven and whose posture was forged in the lobbies of grand hotels and has not degraded despite seventeen months of bush planes and glacier camps. Tomás, who smiles like he's welcoming you to check-in. Tomás, who told me once, in the bar of the Kakslauttanen Arctic Resort in Finnish Lapland while we evaluated glass-roofed igloos for two-person occupancy, that when he retired, his wife said to him, "Now what will you do?" and he heard in it not tenderness but a door closing. Tomás says the Refugio has potential. He says the wind is actually an asset — "adversity heightens physical awareness, the body becomes its own hearth." He smiles. He votes yes.

Yael adjusts her glasses. Dr. Yael Kauffman. The pheromone chemist. The woman who studies the molecular grammar of desire for a living and who is, in this particular dome at the end of the world, the person I am most afraid of. Yael says her readings indicate low ambient pheromone persistence due to the temperature and airflow — "essentially, the wind carries away every chemical signal before it can register" — but that this might itself be "an interesting datum for the chapter." She votes yes. She does not smile. She does not look at me. She does not look at Marco. The precision of her not-looking is, as always, immaculate.

Marco — my collaborator, my co-traveler, the brother of my husband, who is at this moment eleven time zones away probably making the thing with lentils — Marco says the glacier is "visceral" and that the destination has "an honesty the urban sites lack." He says this and I think, *honesty.* I think, *you are using the word honesty while sitting four feet from the woman you are sleeping with in a project designed to facilitate the sleeping with.* But his face offers nothing. His face is the face of a competent logistics coordinator with good bone structure and a clear professional interest in the atlas's completion. He votes yes.

I vote yes.

I vote yes because the advance has been spent, because the publisher expects twenty-four destinations and we have completed eleven, because seventeen people are on a mailing list that receives my monthly progress updates and at least three of those people are literary agents and at least one is a film scout, and because — and this is the part I'd like to skip but can't, because the monologue doesn't let me skip, the monologue is the one honest thing — because if the project ends, the travel ends. If the travel ends, the proximity to Marco ends. If the proximity ends —

What? What happens?

I don't know. I've never tested it. That's the whole point. The project is the structure that prevents the test.

The vote is unanimous. We disperse to our respective domes. The wind screams across the moraine. I can hear Kenji's pencil through the nylon — even here, even at the edge of a glacier, the man is drawing that house.

*The Refugio Austral reminds us that eroticism is not a product of luxury but of limitation. Strip away the marble, the silk, the carefully calibrated lighting, and what remains is the oldest erotic architecture of all: the shelter. Two bodies, a barrier against the cold, and the mutual decision to stay.*

The mutual decision to stay.

That one might actually be true.

## III. The Norwegian Lighthouse

*Falkenberg Fyr stands on a skerry two hundred meters off the Lofoten coast, accessible only by tender during the May-to-September season when the midnight sun reduces darkness to a rumor.
