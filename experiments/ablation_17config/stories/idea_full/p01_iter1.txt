# The Soft One

## I.

The east bunkhouse fell in September. Not all at once — first the ridgepole bowed under a wet snow that came too early and stayed too long, and then the tar paper split along the southern face where the wind works hardest, and then one night the whole thing folded in on itself like a dog curling to sleep. I heard it go. A sound like bones in a jaw.

I had already moved us out of there.

You learn the architecture of collapse if you live inside it long enough. The way a doorframe torques before it buckles. The particular sag of a ceiling that means *next storm* versus *three storms from now.* The generator sheds are still good — low-slung, steel-framed, their walls bolted to a concrete pad that keeps the damp from climbing. Claw marks on the doorframes, deep ones, grooved in sets of four where something tried to peel back the aluminum sheeting and failed. Whatever left those marks was bigger than me and couldn't get in. I find this reassuring. The marks say: the doors held.

The supply crates near the main building are mostly gnawed open, the contents scattered and frozen and gnawed again. Some rice in the bottom of a steel drum that nothing could breach. A few cans that smell like fish when the sun warms them but won't open for teeth. The humans left jackets on nails inside the main building — three of them, one torn at the shoulder, one missing an arm, one still whole but stiff with frost now in the mornings. They left in the warm season, and they left fast. You can read the speed of an evacuation by what gets abandoned: jackets mean autumn wasn't in the plan. The overturned table near the rear door means the last one through was moving too quickly to step around furniture. The dark stain on the floor near the table has been there longer than I have. I walk around it. Everyone walks around it.

The camp is laid out like a hand with its fingers spread. Main building is the palm — largest, most enclosed, but too many windows and two doors that don't latch properly anymore. Five structures radiate outward: the two bunkhouses (one standing, one not), the generator sheds (two, connected by a covered walkway), and the storage building at the far north end where the tree line comes closest. I don't use the storage building. The tree line comes closest there.

Between the main building and the lake, there's an open stretch of sixty paces — gravel and dead grass and the remains of a boat rack. No cover. A killing field in both directions: nothing can approach from the lake without being seen, but nothing can retreat to the main building without being exposed. I cross it fast. I cross it only when I must. In the mornings, when the light is flat and the thermals rise off the water and carry scent, I stand in the doorway of the west generator shed and read the air. Moose to the south. Rot from the collapsed bunkhouse — old rot, structural, not meat. Pine. Ice. And on certain mornings, when the wind swings north, the other smell. The one like wet chalk and copper that means the corridor along the eastern fence line is not safe today.

There are seven of us. We sleep in the west generator shed, which is large enough if everyone observes the protocol, which is this: I sleep nearest the door. Bull and the gray bitch whose name I don't know sleep against the far wall near the fuel drums. The three yearlings — littermates, dumb as stones but fast — sleep in a pile between the workbench and the tool rack. The new one sleeps wherever she wants because she has not learned the protocol and I have not yet decided if she will be here long enough to need to.

Seven is a workable number. Seven is enough to hold sound across the camp at night — enough ears distributed across enough space that a approach from any corridor produces a warning chain. Seven is enough to lose one or two and still function. I have been counting since the spring. The count was eleven in the spring.

The water in the drainage ditch behind the main building froze last night for the first time and did not thaw by midday. This is the clock I watch. Not the sun, which is useless — it makes promises with its angle that the air does not keep. The water is honest. When the drainage ditch freezes and holds, there are perhaps three weeks before the lake begins to skin over at the edges, and when the lake skins over at the edges, the geography changes. The wolves can come from the north then. Right now the lake is a wall at our back. Soon it will be a road.

I have opinions about all of this. I have been here long enough to have opinions.

## II.

The new one was standing in the open.

I saw her first from the west generator shed, early, the light still gray and the frost thick on the gravel. She was between the main building and the lake — the sixty-pace stretch I have just described, the one I cross fast or not at all — and she was not crossing it. She was standing in the middle of it. Still. Tail up. Ears forward. Facing the main building's rear door as though it had spoken to her.

Her coat was wrong. It was golden, and it was clean — not lake-washed clean but *maintained* clean, the kind of clean that requires someone else's hands. I could see the symmetry of a recent grooming in the way the fur lay along her shoulders. She had been brushed. She had been brushed *recently.* Which meant she had not been here long, or she had found some method of self-maintenance that I could not fathom, or the world had stopped making sense.

She was wearing a collar. Blue nylon. A metal tag that caught the new light and threw it.

I watched her from the doorway for a long time. She did not move. She did not scan. She did not do any of the things that a dog standing in the open in the early morning should do, which is to say: she did not act as though anything in the world might want to kill her. She stood with the guileless immobility of a rock or a stump, something that had never had to develop an opinion about its own survival.

Then she wagged her tail.

There was nothing to wag at. The door was closed. The gravel was empty. The only moving thing in her sight line was the frost-steam lifting off the lake, and I can assure you the frost-steam was not interested in her. But her tail moved — not the quick, anxious wag of appeasement, not the stiff metronome of warning, but the wide, sweeping, full-bodied wag that moves the whole back end, the kind of wag that is not a communication but a *condition,* a state of being, as though joy were a weather system and she was permanently inside it.

I thought: brain-sick.

I thought: she will be dead in two days.

She turned and saw me then, across the sixty paces, and everything about her changed — or rather, everything *intensified.* The tail went faster. The ears came up higher. She dropped her front end to the ground, rear in the air, and held the position, her mouth open, her tongue out, her eyes locked on mine with an expression I can only describe as *deranged expectancy.* She looked like she was waiting for me to throw something.

I did not have anything to throw. I did not understand the request.

She broke the stance and came toward me — not cautiously, not in the angled, indirect approach that says *I am not a threat, I am making myself small, I am showing you my flank so you can see I carry no weapons in my geometry.* She came straight. Straight and fast and *bouncing,* her front legs doing something unnecessary in the air, her whole body a series of exclamation marks, and when she reached me she dropped a piece of wood at my feet.

A stick. Stripped of bark, chewed white at one end. She dropped it on the frozen ground between us and looked up at me and her whole body said *now you.*

I looked at the stick. I looked at her. I looked at the sixty paces of open ground she had just covered as though it were a hallway in a house.

Soft in the belly. No muscle definition in the jaw. Nails blunt — not worn blunt from rock and gravel, but *clipped* blunt, maintained, shaped by someone with a tool. She flinched when a gust knocked a sheet of tin loose from the collapsed bunkhouse — flinched hard, full-body, as though a sound were a thing that could bite. But she had not flinched at the open ground. She had not flinched at me.

She flinched at the wrong things. She did not flinch at the right things.

I picked up the stick in my jaws and set it aside because it was in my path, and she practically vibrated apart with what I can only call *enthusiasm,* as though I had performed some tremendous act, as though the relocation of a piece of debris were a feat worthy of celebration, and then she ran out and came back with a chunk of frozen canvas and dropped that at my feet too.

I stepped over it and went to check the eastern fence line. She followed me. She followed me at a distance of approximately three paces, which is the distance at which a dog becomes a liability in a corridor — too far to coordinate with, too close to maneuver around in a reversal. I stopped twice. She stopped twice. I accelerated. She accelerated. I could hear her panting. It was not the panting of exertion. It was the panting of *happiness.*

She had no idea where she was.

That is not a spatial statement. She was at the eastern fence line. She could see the tree line, the bent posts, the gap at the northeast corner where something large had pushed through and the wire had never been repaired. She could see these things, and she could smell what I could smell — the chalk-and-copper undertow that said someone had used this corridor recently, someone with big feet and no interest in our continued existence — and none of it registered. She looked at the gap in the fence the way she looked at everything: with bright, bovine interest, as though the world were a sequence of novel stimuli and none of them had consequences.

The humans did this to her. That's what I decided, walking back. Whatever process produced this animal — this soft-bellied, blunt-nailed, collar-wearing, tail-wagging, stick-retrieving, open-ground-standing, corridor-ignorant, fence-gap-indifferent animal — it was a human process, and it had removed from her the fundamental equipment required to live inside the actual world. She had been *completed* by humans the way the buildings had been completed by humans: made functional only within a set of conditions that no longer existed. The buildings were collapsing because the humans who maintained them were gone. She would collapse for the same reason.

Two days, I thought. Maybe three.

Bull came around the corner of the main building and saw her and his hackles went up and he showed her his teeth — not a serious display, but a clear one: *identify yourself, state your rank, explain your presence in this corridor at this hour.* Standard protocol. Any dog that has survived a week in this camp can read it.

She wagged at him.

She wagged at him and play-bowed and made a high, thin sound in her throat, a sound I later learned was her *greeting,* her all-purpose announcement of affection and goodwill, the sound she made at everything that moved and half the things that didn't, and Bull stood there with his teeth out and his hackles up and a look on his face that I have never seen on a dog before or since, a look that I would describe, if forced, as *philosophical bewilderment.*

He looked at me. I had no explanation to offer.

She retrieved a frozen glove from somewhere and brought it to him.

## III.

The yearling — the smallest of the three, the one with the torn ear who slept on the outside of the pile — did it to herself, is how I see it.

There's a rule about the walkway between the two generator sheds. The walkway is covered, roofed in corrugated steel, open on both sides but narrow enough that you can see through it end to end. It's a good corridor during the day. It connects the sleeping shed to the secondary shed where the fuel drums and the remaining rice are stored. I use it often. Everyone uses it.

But not at night. At night the walkway becomes a different structure — not architecturally, which hasn't changed, but *legibly.* The openings on either side, which during the day function as sight lines (you can see what's coming), at night function as entry points (something can come in). The corrugated roof blocks moonlight and starlight, so the walkway is darker than the open ground on either side, which means you are visible at the threshold — backlit on entry, backlit on exit — and invisible in the middle, where you cannot see what's waiting.

I don't use the walkway at night. I have said this. I have demonstrated this. Anyone paying attention knows this.

The yearling with the torn ear went into the walkway sometime between the deep cold of early morning and the first gray. I don't know why. Possibly she heard something in the secondary shed — a rat, maybe, or the sound of a can shifting in the cold. Possibly she needed to move, the way young dogs sometimes need to move, and she picked the wrong corridor because she was young and had not yet developed the understanding that every corridor has a daytime identity and a nighttime identity and they are not the same.

I heard it from the sleeping shed. Not the kill — I want to be precise. I heard the *mistake.* The click of her nails on the concrete pad at the walkway's entrance. A pause. Then the sound that meant she had reached the middle, the darkest part, and discovered that she was not alone there.

One bark. Short. Not an alarm bark, not the chain-sound we pass along the perimeter to move information. Just one bark — surprise, or protest, or the beginning of something that didn't get to become what it was going to become.

Then the sound of a large body moving fast in a confined space, and then a different sound, which is not one I will describe, and then quiet.

I waited in the sleeping shed with Bull and the gray bitch and the two remaining yearlings. We waited with the patience that is not courage and is not cowardice but is the specific discipline of knowing that the walkway is thirty paces long and the sleeping shed door is steel and the sounds on the other side of that door are sounds that have already finished meaning anything you can change.

In the morning I checked the walkway. There was blood on the concrete and fur caught in the rough edge of a bolt where the wall meets the roof. The yearling was gone — not dead in the walkway, but *taken,* dragged out through the west opening and into the tree line, which meant the wolf (I believe it was one, from the prints, though one is enough) had come in from the east side and gone out the west with its mouth full and had known, had *known,* that the west opening led to cover faster than the east.

They are learning the camp.

I adjusted the sleeping protocol. The two remaining yearlings now sleep against the door, which I don't love, but their hearing is better than Bull's and the door is the point of failure. I sleep behind them. Bull and the gray bitch keep the far wall. The fuel drums provide a choke point that anything entering would have to navigate, and the navigation would make noise, and the noise would give us time, and time is the only currency that matters here.

Six.

The soft one — I should tell you what she did. When the bark came, the single bark, the one that was all the yearling got to say, the soft one stood up in the sleeping shed and went to the door. She went *to* the door. Toward the sound. Not in the way Bull went to the door, which was to position himself between the door and the rest of us with his weight forward and his head low — a defensive posture, a doorway-holding posture, a posture that says *nothing enters here.* The soft one went to the door the way she goes to every sound, which is to say: with *interest.* With concern. Tail half-up, ears forward, whining, her nails clicking on the concrete, and if I had not blocked her with my body she would have pushed through and gone into the walkway and I would be telling you about two dead dogs instead of one.

She pushed against me. She whined. She tried to get past me toward the sound of something dying, because — I have no framework for this — because she *wanted to help.* That is the only reading that makes behavioral sense, and it makes no sense at all. The yearling was being killed thirty paces away and the soft one wanted to go to her, the way she goes to every distress sound, the way she went to Bull when he cut his pad on the ice and lay licking it — she went and lay beside him and put her chin on his shoulder and he was so startled he forgot to bite her.
