# The Swamp Clown

## I.

The boot is the beginning.

Clyde Beaumont's left boot, a Red Wing Irish Setter, size twelve, mud-caked from four hours walking the family's eight hundred acres of Basin bottomland, catches the port-side fuel line of the Bell 206 JetRanger at the precise point where the flexible rubber hose meets the aluminum Quick-Turn coupling. The coupling is a QT-44, manufactured in 2019 by a company in Wichita, and it is rated for fourteen hundred pounds of lateral shear force, which is more than sufficient for any plausible scenario involving turbulence, hard landings, or normal passenger movement. What it is not rated for is a two-hundred-and-twenty-pound man whose heel catches the hose at a forty-degree angle while his upper body is still rotating from the act of turning to look out the left-side window at the canopy below, generating a torque vector that doesn't push the coupling so much as peel it, the way a thumb peels the ring off a soda can, not with force but with a precise angular meanness that happens to be the one thing the coupling cannot withstand.

The hose separates.

Jet-A fuel, which is essentially kerosene with discipline, begins to mist from the severed line. The mist rate is approximately two ounces per second. This is not, in itself, catastrophic. The JetRanger carries roughly ninety-one gallons and can fly for two and a half hours on a full tank, so the loss rate is trivial, and the pilot—whose name is Hal Greer, who has two daughters and a Labrador and fourteen thousand flight hours and who will be dead in ninety seconds—has time to identify the leak, adjust, autorotate, put the aircraft down on any of a dozen open patches of water below. Hal has done this before. Hal is good.

What Hal does not know is that the mist is being drawn aft by the JetRanger's internal ventilation pattern—a subtle current created by the asymmetry between the open left vent and the closed right vent, the right vent being closed because Clyde, before takeoff, asked if it could be shut, he was cold. The mist travels eleven inches aft and encounters the avionics cooling exhaust, which is not hot enough to ignite Jet-A in liquid form but is precisely hot enough—four hundred thirty-seven degrees Fahrenheit at the exhaust port—to ignite Jet-A in aerosolized form.

The flash is small. A petal of blue flame, almost pretty, blooming where no flame should be.

Hal sees it in the overhead mirror. His hands move before his thoughts do. Fourteen thousand hours live in those hands. They are already reaching for the fuel shutoff and the collective when the secondary ignition occurs—the mist has been accumulating for eleven seconds in the hollow behind the rear bulkhead, and when it catches, it catches all at once, and the concussion blows the tail rotor driveshaft housing open like a man sneezing out his teeth.

The JetRanger begins to spin.

Clyde does not understand what is happening, which is its own kind of mercy. He sees the horizon rotating through the windshield. He sees Hal's mouth open, a word in there somewhere but the rotational forces pressing it back down his throat. He feels the harness cutting into his shoulders and the centrifugal press of his own blood pooling in the left side of his body. He feels, specifically and with lunatic clarity, the rectangular outline of his cell phone in his breast pocket as it pushes against his sternum. He thinks: I should have called Claudia before we took off. This is the last thought he has before the water.

The impact is not what movies have taught him to expect. It is not an explosion. It is a compression, as though the entire swamp has inhaled and he is what it swallowed. The JetRanger hits the surface of a shallow bay at an angle that would be fatal at highway speed but is merely annihilating at the aircraft's reduced terminal velocity—Hal, even dying, managed to dump enough collective to slow the descent, the last competent act of his fourteen thousand hours, a gift Hal gave a stranger. The cabin deforms. The windshield buckles inward and admits the Basin in a single filthy gulp. The instrument panel collapses toward Clyde's knees and stops three inches short because the seat rail, which torqued during impact, has jammed against the pedal assembly in a configuration so geometrically specific it will later take a salvage team four hours to reconstruct, and they will call it a miracle, which it is, the same way it is a miracle when a bullet passes through a crowd and hits only one person—someone's physics worked out, and someone else's didn't.

Hal's did not.

Clyde releases his harness. The water is at his waist. It is warm—eighty-one degrees, bathwater, amniotic—and it smells like the world before concrete: sulfur and tannin and the sweet underbelly of rot. He pushes through the shattered door frame and stands. The water is at his waist. The wreckage of the JetRanger is around him in a corona of debris—insulation, wiring, a clipboard, a thermos trailing coffee like a wound. Somewhere inside the cabin, Hal Greer is not standing.

Clyde does not look.

The silence is the thing. It is not the absence of sound—the swamp is never silent, is always tuning its million-piece orchestra of frogs and insects and bird complaints—but it is the absence of mechanical sound, of engine and rotor and the reassuring hum of a species that has conquered the air. That hum is gone. What remains is the Basin, which was here before the Beaumonts and will be here after, and Clyde, who stands hip-deep in the water of his family's land with a cut above his left eye and a ringing in his ears and the knowledge, which is not a thought but a weather system moving through his whole body, that he has done this. His boot. His cold. His weight shifting at the wrong moment in the wrong direction.

He stands there. A great blue heron, displaced by the crash, resettles on a stump forty yards out and regards him with one sulfur eye.

He is the last Beaumont. He is alive. The math of the universe briefly, spectacularly failed to kill him, the way it has briefly, spectacularly failed to kill every Beaumont until the next time, when the math collects.

## II.

He builds it well.

This is the thing that must be understood about Clyde Beaumont: he is not a soft man. The logging dynasty ended two generations ago, but the body remembers what the bank accounts have forgotten. His grandfather taught his father to work green wood with a hand axe, and his father taught him, and the knowledge lives in the tendons of his forearms and the spacing of his grip. He can look at a piece of tupelo and know where it wants to break. He can read the grain the way some men read stock tickers.

The lean-to takes him two hours. He salvages aluminum sheeting from the JetRanger's tail section, bends it with his hands and a rock into a serviceable roof panel. He cuts saplings with a knife from the emergency kit—the kit that survived because it was stowed in the one compartment that didn't flood, which is so perfectly consistent with everything that Clyde does not even register it as luck. The saplings are water tupelo, straight-grained, cooperative. He notches them with joints his hands remember but his conscious mind does not, lashing the cross-members with wire stripped from the helicopter's electrical system. The ridge pole is cypress, a branch he finds floating, already seasoned, almost suspiciously perfect. He leans the structure against a hummock, angles the roof for drainage, leaves one end open for ventilation and firelight.

It is good. It is genuinely good. A person trained in wilderness architecture would look at it and nod. The angles are correct. The lashing is tight. The drainage will work. Clyde steps back and looks at the lean-to, and for a span of perhaps forty seconds, he is just a man in the wilderness who has built a shelter, and the narrative of his life is the narrative of human competence triumphing over hostile conditions, which is the oldest story there is, older than language, older than fire, and for those forty seconds, Clyde is inside that story, and the story holds.

Then he builds the signal fire.

He builds it on the hummock, the highest ground available, maybe three feet above the water line. He selects his fuel the way his grandfather would have: a base of dried palmetto fronds, a midsection of pencil-thin cypress twigs, an upper structure of thicker branches arranged in a tepee configuration that will channel the draft upward and inward. He nests the green wood—fresh-cut tupelo, leaves still attached—around the outside, because green wood makes smoke, and smoke is the point. Smoke is the message. Smoke says: *I am here. Come get me.*

He strikes the fire with the helicopter's emergency flare kit, which contains waterproof matches that light on the first strike. The palmetto catches. The twigs catch. The flames negotiate the gap between thin wood and thick wood the way they are supposed to, climbing the tepee's interior scaffolding, heating the green wood's exterior moisture into steam that will become the tall white column he needs.

The fire is working. Clyde feeds it another handful of fronds and crouches back on his heels and watches the smoke begin to rise—a pale rope uncoiling itself into the last hour of daylight—and the fire is working, it is working, and then the pine knot detonates.

It is not pine. It is water tupelo, which is not supposed to contain resin pockets, except that this particular piece of tupelo grew around a knot where a branch once joined the trunk, and in the hollow space where the branch rotted away, moisture accumulated over decades and was sealed in by subsequent growth, creating a pressurized pocket of steam and volatile organic compounds that, at four hundred degrees, does what any pressurized vessel does at failure point: it finds the path of least resistance and expels its contents at the speed of sound.

The path of least resistance is a channel in the grain that runs at a thirty-seven-degree angle from the horizontal.

Thirty-seven degrees is the exact angle of a mortar trajectory optimized for range.

The knot blows. A chunk of burning tupelo, roughly the size and shape of a man's fist, launches from the fire at a velocity that would be impressive if it were intentional. It is not intentional. It is physics, which does not have intentions, which is not cruel, which is just the universe's arithmetic solving for X, and in this particular equation X equals the ridgepole of Clyde's lean-to.

The burning chunk hits the ridgepole where the lashing crosses, and the lashing is wire, which is metal, which conducts heat with cheerful efficiency, and the heat transfers to the cypress ridgepole, which is dry, which is seasoned, which was almost suspiciously perfect, and which ignites.

The lean-to burns.

It burns the way a well-built thing burns: structurally, completely, each element feeding the next because they were joined with care and skill, because the ventilation was correct, because the angles were sound. A poorly built lean-to might have smoldered. This one, because it was good, because Clyde built it with knowledge bred into his hands, becomes a pyre.

He stands in the firelight. The signal fire burns to his left. His shelter burns to his right. Between them, lit by both, he stands with his arms slightly away from his body and his chin slightly down, and his posture is not surprise. It is not outrage or despair. It is a slackness, a subsidence, as though a muscle he'd been holding taut for years has simply released. His shoulders know this feeling. His knees know it. Somewhere in the architecture of his body, in whatever place the spine stores what the mind refuses to inventory, there is a drawer, and the drawer is full, and the label on the drawer reads: *Of course.*

He watches the lean-to collapse into its own embers, and as the ridgepole folds and the aluminum sheeting warps in the heat with a sound like a moan, something moves through him that is not quite a memory. It is older than that. It is the muscle-echo of a man he never met—a great-uncle, maybe, or a great-great-uncle, a man who had the same hands, who built something once that was good, who watched it come apart. The echo is a single flash, half a second: hands like his hands, a structure falling, the same slackness in the same shoulders.

Then it's gone, and Clyde is alone in the swamp, unsheltered, two fires burning, the signal smoke still rising into a sky that no one is watching.

He sits down in the mud. He does not rebuild.

## III.

Night comes on like a bruise—first yellow, then purple, then black.

Clyde cannot move. This is not metaphorical. His right leg, during the scramble away from the burning lean-to, punched through a layer of root mat into the mud beneath, and the mud has closed around his calf like a fist, and each attempt to extract it only deepens the seal. He has tried. He has tried the slow twist, the steady pull, the sudden wrench. The mud holds him with the patience of something that has been holding things for ten thousand years.

So he sits. His right leg entombed to the knee, his left leg folded beneath him, his back against a cypress knee that presses into his spine with the intimacy of a pew. The signal fire has burned down to a sullen glow. The swamp sounds have returned—the industrial thrum of frogs, the mosquitoes' thin drill, somewhere out in the dark the heavy plash of something large shifting its weight in water. The sky above the canopy is moonless and flagrant with stars, the kind of sky that only exists where humans have failed to build.

He is so tired. He is tired in his marrow.

And the deaths come.

---

They come the way inherited things come—not summoned, not chosen, but simply *there*, the way the shape of his jaw is there, the way the length of his fingers is there. He does not decide to remember them. They arrive, and he is too depleted to turn them away.

The first Beaumont to die in the Basin was Augustin Beaumont, in 1891, and his death had the quality of folklore, which is to say it was almost certainly embellished in the retelling but the underlying architecture was sound.

Augustin was a cutter. He ran a crew of thirty men along the eastern edge of the Atchafalaya, harvesting virgin bald cypress that had been growing since before the Europeans came, trees so large that a man could stand inside the buttressed roots and not see daylight. Augustin was good. He read a tree the way a surgeon reads the body—he knew where the weight sat, knew which direction the crown would pull, could calculate the hinge-cut to drop two hundred tons of wood into a gap between stumps with the precision of a woman threading a needle.

On the morning of March 14, 1891, Augustin selected a cypress that his foreman, a man named Delacroix, had marked two days prior. The tree was sound. The notch was clean. The back-cut proceeded exactly as Augustin's forty years of experience predicted it would, and the tree began its slow, committed lean toward the northeast, exactly where Augustin had aimed it, and the crew retreated along the escape routes Augustin had cleared, and the tree fell.

It fell correctly.

It hit the ground where it was supposed to hit the ground. The impact was enormous—the percussion traveled through the saturated soil like a second heartbeat—and the tree settled, and the crew began to breathe, and Augustin turned to Delacroix and said something, the content of which is lost because what happened next erased all other memory from the witnesses' minds.

The tree bounced.
