# The Pedestrians

## I.

Thing about sawdust is you can't just throw it down. People think you can just throw it down. I have watched grown men — men employed by the Garden, men who presumably applied for their positions and were selected above other candidates — dump a bag of sawdust on a banked turn like they're salting a stoop in February. And then they look at you. They look at you like you're the one who's lost his mind when you tell them the grain's wrong, the depth's wrong, they've got it piled a quarter-inch thick on the straight and bare board on the curve where a man's going to be putting ninety hours of lateral torque on an ankle that's already been taped twice and prayed over once.

I told the head official — Garretty, the one with the mustache that looks like something died on his lip and he's decided to honor it — I told him, you need a rake. A garden rake and a man who understands what a foot does on the sixth pass versus the six-hundredth pass. Sawdust compacts. It migrates. By Day Three your track is a geological survey. You've got ridges. You've got valleys. You've got a bald patch on the far turn that might as well be a sheet of lake ice, and you've got a man with seven thousand dollars in wagers riding on him about to set his heel on it at three in the morning when the only people watching are bookmakers and the kind of women who find suffering attractive in a man, provided he's suffering at a distance.

Garretty told me to mind my walkers and leave the track to the track men.

I have been minding walkers for eleven years. I have taped the feet of men who walked from one end of human endurance to the other and came back with nothing to show for it except toenails that looked like they'd been put on wrong by a man working in the dark. I have mixed tinctures that are not, strictly speaking, legal in any state where the pharmacist has a conscience. I have held a man's head in my lap at four in the morning while he wept about a woman in Pittsburgh and then I have gotten him up and I have pointed him at the track and I have said *walk*, because that is the job. The job is to keep them walking.

But I could not, apparently, be trusted with an opinion about sawdust depth.

Fine.

The morning of the race — February the ninth, 1879, a Monday, cold enough that the vendors' breath made them look like they were on fire — I had my two men in the tent and my hands full. Literally full. I had Declan Shea's left foot in my right hand and a roll of zinc-oxide tape in my left, and I was wrapping his arch with the focus of a man defusing ordnance, because Declan's arches were the kind of architectural problem that kept you up at night. Flat. Not flat like some feet are flat. Flat like God had started to make an arch, gotten distracted, and just left the whole underside of the foot as a rough draft. You could set a spirit level on Declan Shea's instep and the bubble would not move.

He sat there and let me work, the way he did everything — like a man on a dock waiting for the next crate to come down the chain. Hands on his knees. Eyes somewhere middle-distance. He'd been stretching when I arrived at six that morning. Not the way athletes stretch, with purpose and attention. The way a man straightens up from unloading ballast and twists his back because his back hurts and there's more ballast coming. Declan did not have a philosophy of preparation. Declan had a body that he used until it told him to stop, and then he did something else with it.

He was twenty-six years old and he looked forty, and I mean that as a compliment. Forty in the way that means the soft stuff has been scraped off and what's left is structural.

"How's the left heel?" I asked him.

"Still there," he said. Which was the most I'd gotten out of him in three days of preparation.

Now—

Harlan. Harlan "The Colossus" Pryce arrived at Gilmore's Garden at half past nine, which was two and a half hours before the starting gun and approximately three hours later than I'd told him. He arrived the way Harlan arrived everywhere: like the building had been built for the specific purpose of having him walk into it. He had a sash. He had — I need you to understand this — he had an *embroidered sash* that someone's wife had made him, purple with gold thread, and it said THE COLOSSUS in letters you could read from the gallery. He wore it diagonally across his chest like he was the mayor of walking. He was waving to the early crowd — maybe two thousand at that point, the real devotees, the ones who come for the warm-ups and the sawdust and sit through the opening-gun ceremony with the solemnity of men attending a baptism — and they loved him. They rose. They rose for an embroidered sash, and I want to say that was ridiculous, and it was, but I also want to say there was a man inside that sash and the man could walk, and the two things were not as separate as I wished they were.

"Burt!" He had a voice that carried the way cannon fire carries. "Burt, tell me you've got the burgundy shoes."

I had the burgundy shoes. I had four pairs for each man, broken in over six weeks, each pair calibrated for a different stage of swelling. Days One and Two, you want a snug heel and a generous toe box. Day Three, you want room everywhere because the foot has become a different foot — it has swelled, it has blistered, it has begun to entertain the possibility that it is no longer a foot at all but some kind of marine organism. Days Four through Six, God help you, you want whatever shoe the foot will still tolerate, and sometimes that is no shoe at all.

The burgundy pair was Harlan's Day One shoe. Calfskin upper, cork sole, beautiful things. More expensive than a good suit. Ruined in seventy-two hours.

I had them. I always had them. I had been doing this for Harlan for three years and I had never once not had the shoes.

"I've got the burgundy shoes," I said.

"You're a prince," he said, and clapped me on the shoulder the way you clap a man you consider a supporting character in your own life, and went out to wave some more.

The Garden filled. Ten thousand people, Garretty said, although Garretty also said the sawdust was fine, so I'd take his numbers the way I took his track management — with skepticism and a backup plan. But it was a lot. It was a lot of human beings packed into tiered seats around a dirt-and-sawdust oval, brass band playing Sousa in the gallery with a cornet player who was either very drunk or very avant-garde, and the whole place smelled like cigar smoke and orange peel and sawdust and the particular human electricity of ten thousand people who have come to watch men *walk*.

I need to say something about that. About the walking.

I have tried to explain pedestrianism to people who don't follow it. I have sat in bars and at dinner tables and tried to convey, to essentially normal people, the parameters of this sport, and I have watched their faces go through stages — confusion first, then amusement, then a kind of profound concern for me personally. The rules are simple. You walk. You walk around a track for six days. You may rest in your tent, you may eat and sleep as strategy dictates, but the clock does not stop. After six days, whoever has walked the most laps wins. First prize is six thousand dollars. Some men walk over four hundred and fifty miles. No, they do not run. Running is a disqualification. One foot must be on the ground at all times. Yes, people pay to watch this. Yes, they *scream.*

I don't explain it anymore. It's like explaining why you love a person. The reasons aren't the point. The reasons come after, assembled to justify a decision your body made before your mind was consulted. Ten thousand people did not come to Gilmore's Garden because competitive walking is rational entertainment. They came because at some point they'd seen a man walk himself past the boundary of what a body should do, and something inside them had opened a door, and now they lived in the room behind that door, and the room did not have a name but it was theirs.

That's enough about that.

The starting gun fired at noon.

Eighteen men in a staggered line — the field had been twenty-two but four had scratched, two with influenza and two with what I'll call better judgment — and the gun went off and they began to walk, and the crowd erupted the way crowds do, and the band hit a march, and Declan Shea put his flat, wrecked, beautiful feet on the track and began to shuffle, and Harlan Pryce raised his arms to the crowd and swung into his stride like a man born for the express purpose of being watched while moving at four miles per hour, and the race was on, and I retreated to the handler's tent with my bottles and my tape and my journal and the sense, which I did not yet understand, that I had set something in motion that was going to cost more than I was prepared to pay.

But that part comes later.

---

## II.

Let me tell you about two men walking, because this is a story about two men walking, and if I can't make you see the difference between how they walked, then I have failed at the only thing I have ever been any good at, which is the reading of a human gait.

A gait is a signature. That's not a metaphor. A man's walk is as distinctive as his handwriting, and it tells you more than his handwriting ever will, because a man can lie with a pen but he cannot lie with his feet. Not for long. Not under load. You put a man on a track and you make him walk for six hours and his feet will tell you everything — his injuries, his history, his confidence, what he's protecting, what he's abandoned, whether he was raised to be seen or raised to be useful.

Declan Shea walked from the hips. Low center of gravity. His stride was short — twenty-eight inches, I measured it with a length of string and a piece of chalk on Day One, because that is the kind of man I am — and he landed heel-first with no roll to speak of, just a flat, deliberate stamp, like a man setting paving stones. His arms hung. His shoulders were forward, not with effort but with the permanent posture of a person who has spent years leaning into physical work. He walked like walking was a job, and he'd been assigned this particular job, and he would do it the way he'd done every other job, which was without complaint or adornment until the whistle blew or his body quit, whichever came first.

He was ugly to watch. I don't say that with malice. I say it with the professional detachment of a man who has observed hundreds of walkers and who understands that beauty is not correlated with effectiveness. Some of the finest pedestrians I have ever handled walked like they were apologizing to the ground. Declan walked like the ground owed him money and he was collecting step by step.

But here's the thing — and I wrote this in my journal at the four-hour mark, in the cramped hand of a man trying to write while mixing a mustard plaster: *Shea's variance is nil.* Four hours. Not a single lap deviated by more than two seconds from his average. I timed him with a stopwatch I'd bought from a retired horse trainer in Saratoga, a beautiful piece, Swiss movement, and I sat there clicking the stem and recording numbers and the numbers were the same. The same. A minute forty-two. A minute forty-three. A minute forty-two. A minute forty-one. A minute forty-two. Like a metronome. Like a tide. Like something that had nothing to do with human will and everything to do with physics — a body that had found its economy and saw no reason, no reason at all, to deviate.

I have handled athletes. I have handled men who trained with coaches and physicians and nutritionists and men who kept logs of their heart rates and their urine color and the barometric pressure on race day. Not one of them could hold a pace like Declan Shea, who had no coach, who had no plan, who when I asked him on Day One what his strategy was looked at me like I'd asked him the strategy for breathing.

Now. Harlan.

Harlan Pryce walked from the chest. Chin up — always, *always* chin up, even at hour sixty when his knees were sending up signals of distress like a sinking ship — chin up and eyes forward and arms swinging in these great theatrical arcs that the crowd adored. Each stride was long, thirty-four inches, and he landed on the ball of the foot with a kind of springing action that added what I can only describe as *bounce*. He walked like a man entering a ballroom. He walked like a man who believed, genuinely believed, that the act of walking was a form of performance, and that a performance without an audience was not a performance at all, and that a life without performance was — and here I'm speculating, but I've earned the right to speculate, I have earned it with three years and eleven races and I don't know how many rolls of tape — a life not worth the body it was living in.

The crowd loved him. I want to be precise here. They did not merely cheer for him. They *organized around him.* By the second hour, there was a section of the gallery — maybe two hundred people, mostly men, a few women, some children on shoulders — that chanted his name every time he passed the quarter mark. PRYCE. PRYCE. PRYCE. And when they chanted, Harlan's arms pumped higher. And when his arms pumped higher, his stride lengthened. And when his stride lengthened, his lap time dropped by — I measured this — approximately two seconds.

Two seconds faster per lap when the crowd chanted his name. Two seconds per lap, which over the course of eighty circuits is a hundred and sixty seconds, which is two and a half minutes, which at Harlan's pace is nearly an extra lap. The crowd was literally *adding distance to his total.* He had found a way to convert adoration into mileage.

But those two seconds? Those two seconds cost him something. The arm-pumping — I want to be precise — the arm-pumping was biomechanically expensive. The human body, when walking efficiently, keeps the arms low, lets them swing as passive counterbalances to the leg stride. Pendulums. That's what arms are, during walking. Pendulums. You don't power them; they power themselves. Harlan was *powering* his. He was lifting his fists to shoulder height on each backswing, engaging his deltoids, his upper trapezius, his rhomboids. He was, on every single stride, doing work that produced zero forward motion and a tremendous amount of visual spectacle.

That's a choice. Most people would call it a bad one. I would have called it a bad one, once, when I was a young handler and still believed that efficiency was the point.

But Harlan pumped his arms, and the crowd screamed, and his lap times dropped by two seconds, and for the first twelve hours he led the field by a comfortable margin, and the society ladies sent oranges and cold chicken and notes to his rest tent, and one note that I read said *Your strength makes poetry of suffering* — I remember it because I read it while lancing a blister on Declan's right heel, and the blister made a sound that was not poetic in any sense I understood — and Harlan was Harlan, and the Garden was his, and everything was exactly as it had been in every race I'd handled him.

Declan was in sixth place at the twelve-hour mark. He was in sixth place the way a river is in sixth place when you line up rivers by how fast they reach the sea — technically accurate, categorically irrelevant. His pace had not changed. His expression had not changed. I brought him into the tent for his first rest at hour eleven and he sat down and ate a bowl of beef broth and four soda crackers and looked at neither the lap board nor the crowd nor Harlan nor anything at all, really, and after twenty-two minutes he stood up and walked back out.

"How are your feet?" I asked him, before he left.

"Still there," he said.
