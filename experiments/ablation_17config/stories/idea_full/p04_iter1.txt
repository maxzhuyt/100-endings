# The Sun-Eater

---

## I.

You'll do. You'll have to.

I don't say that to be unkind. I say it because the corridor behind me just sealed—a membrane of calcified bone-rock grinding shut like a throat swallowing in reverse—and I have perhaps four minutes before the next one closes, and I had hoped for someone with more context, but you're here, you're listening, and listen: that is enough. That is the only credential I require of you. You are the one who showed up. In my profession, we call that sufficiency.

My name is Lenne Vasic-Ohala. Chief Geometer of the Heliological Cabinet, which no longer exists. Holder of the Dûl Professorship in Applied Stellar Compression, which no longer exists either. Fellow of the Archive of Angles. Member, emeritus, of the Last Light Consortium. These titles sound like nonsense to you. I can hear it in the way you're listening—the particular silence of someone too polite to say *I don't know what any of those words mean*. That's fine. Politeness is underrated. Politeness will keep you with me a few more sentences, and a few more sentences is all I've ever needed to make someone understand.

I fold suns. That's the plain version.

Or: I *folded* suns. The tense matters now, apparently. Everything is in the past tense when you're running.

Here is what sun-folding is. Imagine you have a star—not a healthy star, not a fat bright confident thing in the middle of its sequence, but a star at the end. A star that has spent itself. Its hydrogen is a memory; its helium is going; it has begun the long, humiliating process of becoming a cinder. Now imagine you could take the geometry of that star—not its matter, not its fuel, but the *shape* of how it burns—and fold it. The way you'd fold a sheet of paper to make it smaller but no less present. You compress the angles of combustion. You reduce the dimensionality of the radiative zone. You take a dying star and you make it *more efficient*—not by giving it new fuel, but by teaching it to burn in fewer directions at once.

A folded sun doesn't burn brighter. It burns *tighter*. It takes light that would have dispersed into nothing across eight dimensions and forces it through three. The warmth becomes specific. Directed. A folded sun is a sun that has learned to aim.

I was good at this. I was the best at this. I say that not out of vanity but out of a precision I owe you, because you've agreed to be my witness and a witness deserves accurate testimony. There were nine of us who could perform a tertiary fold, and of those nine, three could achieve a quaternary, and of those three, only I could fold past the fifth inflection—the point at which the geometry of light begins to resist its own compression and you must, essentially, *persuade* the star. You must show it a shape it has never been that is more economical than the shape it is, and you must make that shape so elegant, so irrefutably beautiful, that the star *chooses* it. Stars are not conscious. But they are aesthetic. They recognize parsimony the way water recognizes downhill. Finding the fold is mathematics. Holding the fold is something else—something closer to singing a child to sleep, something that requires you to be, for a moment, more patient than fire.

I had an apprentice. She was brilliant.

I'll tell you about her. I'll tell you everything about her, because she's the reason the corridors are closing, the reason the walls are herding me like a blood cell through a vein, the reason I'm talking to you at all instead of sitting in my study with a cup of thermal broth and a star-chart, which is what I should be doing, which is what I was *owed* after forty years of keeping this world lit.

Her name is Kael Yenneth, and she has told the remaining population of this planet—all of them, every last one—that I am a sun-eater. That the art I practiced, the art I *taught her*, was not preservation but consumption. That every fold I made was a bite. That I have been swallowing the star for decades, metabolizing it, taking the world's last warmth into myself like a body absorbing a meal, and that the reason we are all dying in the dark is not entropy, not physics, not the natural senescence of a ten-billion-year-old stellar object, but *me*. My hunger. My mouth.

She's wrong. I want to be clear about that before we go further. She is wrong, and she was my best student, and the fact that those two things can be simultaneously true is the kind of cruelty that makes me tired of being precise.

The corridor ahead splits. Left passage, right passage. The lichen on the walls here—you can't see it, but I'll describe it, because I'm thorough and because you need to understand the world you've entered—the lichen is bioluminescent. Engineered. It feeds on residual stellar radiation and converts it into a low amber glow, enough to navigate by, enough to keep the architecture from becoming a tomb. It has been growing dimmer for years, which is consistent with a dying sun, which is consistent with everything I've been saying, which is—

The left passage is slightly darker. I notice this because I notice everything about light, because that is my profession, because that is who I am. The lichen dimmed just a bit when I entered this junction. That's the architecture—when the corridors reconfigure, the lichen loses coherence temporarily. It's a well-documented phenomenon. Structural mycologists published a paper on it before the university collapsed. It means nothing.

I'll go right.

I'll keep talking. You'll keep listening. And I'll tell you how I taught Kael Yenneth to fold the sun, and you'll understand why her accusation is not just false but *obscene*—a desecration of the most beautiful thing I've ever done—and then, maybe, when I've given you everything, you'll know what I am. You'll name me correctly. Because that's what I need from you. Not rescue. Not pity.

A correct name.

## II.

Once, I had a student.

I tell it to you like that—*once*—because that's how it felt, even at the time. Like something out of a tale. Like the kind of story that begins with a woman alone in a high room and ends with the room in flames, and you know it will end in flames from the first sentence but you listen anyway because the woman's voice is warm and the room is full of interesting things and you think, maybe this time, maybe for me, the fire won't come.

She arrived on a translation-day—a day when the planet's rotation stuttered and the shadows went wrong for about an hour, all of them pointing toward the sun instead of away. Translation-days happened more frequently as the star decayed. An omen, if you believed in omens. I didn't. I believed in geometry.

Kael was nineteen. Tall. Hands like a pianist's, if you know what a pianist is—I don't know what your world has. Long-fingered, is what I mean. Precise. She stood in the door of my study and said, "I want to learn sun-folding," the way someone else might say, "I want to learn to breathe." Not a desire. A diagnosis. She was identifying a condition she already had and asking me to make it functional.

I said, "Why?"

She said, "Because the sun is dying and I want to learn how to argue with it."

Argue. Not *fight*. Not *fix*. Argue. As if the sun were a position that could be reasoned with, a premise that could be complicated. I loved her for that word. I don't say that lightly. I mean I loved her for it the way you love a proof that arrives fully formed—with the shocked gratitude of someone who has been shown that the universe is more structured than they feared.

The first lesson.

I took her to the Aphelion Chamber—a room at the planet's outer crust, vaulted with engineered crystal that allowed unfiltered starlight to enter in controlled beams. The light was thin even then. Ochre. The color of something remembering what gold looked like. I positioned her at the primary axis and I said, "Watch."

I unfolded my hands—the gesture is literal; sun-folding begins in the body, in the specific configuration of the fingers, because the geometry must be *held* before it can be projected—and I folded a beam of light.

A single beam. The simplest fold: a bilateral compression along the radiative axis. You take a column of light and you show it that it can be a sheet. That three dimensions of travel can become two. That the energy is conserved—nothing is lost, nothing—but the *directionality* changes. A beam that illuminated a circle can, when folded, illuminate a line with twice the intensity. You sacrifice breadth for depth. You teach the light to be specific.

The beam compressed. The room, which had been evenly amber, became bifurcated: one strip of blazing gold where the folded light landed, and shadow everywhere else. The gold was warm. Not metaphorically. The fold concentrates thermal radiation along with the visible spectrum. You could feel it on your skin—a line of summer across your forearm in a room that was otherwise the temperature of a shallow grave.

Kael's face. I remember her face.

She looked at the folded beam the way you look at something that has solved a problem you didn't know you had. Hunger, but the good kind—the kind that means you want to understand. The kind that I recognized because I had felt it once, standing in the same room, watching my own teacher fold light for the first time, and I had thought: *I will do this for the rest of my life, and the rest of my life will be justified by the doing of it.*

I taught her the bilateral fold that day. Then the trilateral. Within a week, the quaternary. She was faster than I had been. Her hands found the geometries the way roots find water—not by calculation but by something older, some proprioceptive sense of where the angles *wanted* to go. I would show her a fold and she would replicate it and then, almost immediately, begin to vary it—testing the edges, pushing the compression, finding the point where the light resisted and then easing back, coaxing it, the way you coax a knot loose by pulling in the direction it *isn't* stuck.

I began to teach her the fifth inflection. This is where sun-folding stops being engineering and becomes—I don't have a word. Art is too small. Prayer implies a listener. It becomes the thing it is, which is the practice of standing in front of a dying star and finding, in the geometry of its collapse, a shape that is more beautiful than death.

The fifth inflection requires you to fold the star along an axis that doesn't exist yet. You must *propose* the axis. You must show the star a dimension it hasn't tried and make the case—through the precision of your gestures, through the symmetry of your proposed geometry—that this new dimension is where it should put its remaining energy. You are asking a star to change the shape of its dying. You are saying: *Not like this. Like this.* And you are holding up a shape with your hands, and the shape must be so right, so inevitable, that the star agrees.

Kael couldn't do it at first. No one can. The axis kept collapsing—the star would reach for the new dimension and then flinch back, like a hand touching a hot surface, and the fold would unspool and the light would scatter, uncompressed, wasteful, dying in all directions at once.

I stood behind her. I put my hands over hers—the only time I ever touched her, the only time the pedagogy required it—and I guided her fingers into the configuration. And I said, "Don't show it where to go. Show it where it *already is* and doesn't know yet." And she adjusted. And the light folded. And for a moment, standing there with my hands over hers, I felt the star *lean* into the new shape the way a tired body leans into a chair, and the room filled with a warmth that had no right to exist, a warmth that was, by every thermodynamic model, impossible—concentrated, specific, inexhaustible within its fold.

I took my hands away. The fold held. Kael was breathing fast and her eyes were wet and she said, "It's like it was waiting for someone to ask."

Yes. That's exactly what it's like. I told her so. I told her she understood.

One of the archivists was there that day—a woman named Prenn, who maintained the records of stellar output, who catalogued each fold's effect on the star's luminosity budget. She stood at the edge of the Aphelion Chamber with her measurement instruments and she watched me guide Kael's hands and she said, afterward, when Kael had gone: "It looked less like you were folding the light and more like you were inhaling it."

Archivists are always dramatic. Their entire profession is the cataloguing of loss; it makes them see diminishment everywhere. I told Prenn that she was projecting, and she made a note in her instrument-log, and I went home and I sat in my study and I felt the warmth of the lesson still in my fingertips and I was, for that evening, as happy as I have ever been.

Prenn's measurements did show a slight discrepancy that day. The fold's output was fractionally less than the input. These things happen. Compression is imperfect. Energy is lost in the translation between geometries—every sun-folder knows this; the residual is dissipated as subharmonic heat, absorbed by the local architecture, converted to the low entropic hum that keeps the corridors warm enough to walk through. The numbers were within tolerance. I noted the variance and I filed it.

I mention this because I am being thorough. I mention this because you are my witness and I am giving you everything, every datum, every detail, even the ones that, from a certain angle, in a certain light, might look like something they are not.

I trained Kael for seven years. She surpassed me in manual precision by year four. By year six, she could achieve the fifth inflection alone—without my hands, without my guidance, her own fingers finding the axis with a speed that made me feel old and grateful in equal measure. By year seven, she was as good as I was.

She was my legacy. She was the proof that what I knew could survive me—that the art would outlast my hands, my life, my particular relationship with the angles of dying light. She was the shape I had folded my knowledge into: tighter, more efficient, aimed at the future.

I say this so you understand what she was to me. I say this so you understand what it meant when she stood in front of nine hundred people and called me a mouth.

## III.

There are, as of the last census that anyone bothered to take, nine hundred and thirteen people alive on this world.

I'll let that number sit with you. Nine hundred and thirteen. The planet once held—the records are fragmented, but the Archive of Angles maintained demographic overlays keyed to energy consumption patterns—approximately four billion. The die-off took three centuries. It was not dramatic. It was not a cataclysm. It was a slow, systemic cooling: crops failing at the edges first, then the centers; habitable zones contracting like a pupil in bright light, except the light was what was leaving; cities going quiet one by one, not with screams but with the particular silence of a place where the last person has closed the last door. For a long time, people moved inward, toward the planet's core, where geothermal veins kept the corridors warm enough. For a long time, my folding kept the remaining sunlight concentrated enough to grow food in the crystal-houses and run the bioreactors and maintain the lichen. For a long time, what I did was called *saving*.

Nine hundred and thirteen people. In corridors that were designed for millions. The architecture of this world is vast and mostly empty—a body with almost no blood left, the veins still intact but carrying nothing. The corridors stretch for thousands of kilometers, branching and reconnecting, and most of them are dark now, the lichen dead or dormant, the crystal-houses shattered, the air still breathable only because the atmospheric processors were overengineered by people who planned for scarcity but not for this. Not for almost-nothing.

Kael Yenneth stood in the Central Atrium—
