# The Frost Ball

## I.

The van smelled like old ranch dressing and a moral failing. Lionel—my father, though I sometimes introduced him as my business associate because it made him laugh in a way that showed all his teeth—had rented it from a man named Gareth who operated out of a storage facility in Tukwila and who had, I noticed, taped a photograph of Jesus to the dashboard with blue painter's tape. Not a nice photograph. The kind they sell at gas stations in Louisiana where Jesus looks like he just found out his credit score. Lionel had paid five hundred of the seven-hundred-dollar deposit and promised the rest "by the twenty-seventh, absolutely, no question," which in Lionel-speak meant the twenty-seventh was a theoretical date, a suggestion the calendar had made that he was willing to consider.

The heater worked in the sense that it produced a sound. A whirring, consumptive rattle, like a cat dying inside a filing cabinet. No heat accompanied this sound. The sound was the whole show. I'd stuffed hand warmers into my coat pockets before we left, and I sat with my fists balled against my thighs as we drove north on I-5, past the Costco, past the auto mall where all the inflatable tube men danced with the dead-eyed ecstasy of middle management at a company retreat, and then off the highway onto a road that didn't have a name so much as a title. Bellingham Heights. Which was not in Bellingham—that was the first lie—and was barely on a height, unless you counted the slight geological smugness of its hills, which rose just enough above the surrounding neighborhood to ensure that everyone in the Houses could look down, literally, on everyone in the houses.

We had: forty-two individual beef Wellingtons wrapped in Lionel's puff pastry, which was genuinely his one great art, his Sistine ceiling, each one golden and architectural; a roasted beet salad with chevre and candied pecans that I'd helped assemble at four that morning, my fingers still faintly purple; six dozen profiteroles; a cambro full of butternut squash soup; a bread basket situation involving three kinds of rolls; and, because the client had requested them specifically and Lionel never said no to a client, thirty-six bacon-wrapped dates stuffed with Gorgonzola, which I personally thought tasted like a very elegant mistake but which rich people consumed with the mechanical devotion of ants dismantling a carcass.

We also had: sixteen rented champagne flutes, which I had hand-washed twice because two of them had arrived from the rental company with a cloudy, spectral residue that made them look haunted; Lionel's knives in their rolled leather case, which was the only actually nice thing he'd ever bought himself; his white chef's coat, which he'd bleached until it practically hummed; and his apron—pressed, creased, squared at the corners—which he had ironed that morning on the kitchen counter because we didn't own an ironing board. He'd laid down a towel and gone at it with the kind of attention to detail that, in another life, with another set of starting coordinates, might have made him a surgeon. Instead it made him a man who catered for homeowners' associations at eleven hundred dollars a job, minus food costs, minus the van, minus the flute rental, minus the gas, which came out—I'd done the math because someone needed to do the goddamn math—to roughly four hundred and ten dollars of actual profit for nineteen hours of prep and six hours of service on Christmas Eve. Forty-one dollars an hour if you were feeling optimistic. Sixteen dollars an hour if you counted my labor, which Lionel did not, because counting my labor would have meant admitting that his fourteen-year-old daughter was, functionally, his sous chef, and admitting that would have cracked something in him that I could not afford to let crack.

The road into Bellingham Heights had a gate. Not a manned gate—we weren't at that level of fortification—but a stone-and-wrought-iron arrangement with a keypad and a little camera, the universal symbol for "we know you don't live here and we'd like to record that fact." Lionel punched in the code the client had emailed him. The gate swung open with the slow, hydraulic courtesy of a system that had all the time in the world.

Inside: lawns. In December. Green lawns in December in western Washington, which meant someone was paying for sod or paying for paint or paying for the kind of irrigation system that treated the water table like a personal checking account. Christmas lights, yes, but not the lights of our neighborhood—not the tangled, half-dead strings stapled to gutters with the grim optimism of people decorating a sinking ship. These were *designed*. Coordinated. Whole rooflines traced in warm white, icicle lights descending from eaves with the mathematical regularity of piano keys. An inflatable reindeer the size of an actual reindeer stood on one lawn, which I found conceptually troubling—if you could afford a six-foot inflatable reindeer, why not just get a reindeer?—and next to it a wire-frame sleigh outlined in lights so bright it probably showed up on satellite imagery.

Lionel was humming. He hummed when he drove, always had—fragments of Motown, mostly, or sometimes hymns from the church he'd stopped attending two years ago when Sundays became prep days. Tonight it was "My Girl," which he hummed the way other people breathe, unconsciously, from some deep reserve of irrational gladness that I had never once been able to locate in myself. He was wearing his good shirt under his coat. He'd shaved. He looked, in the dashboard light, like a man driving toward something he believed in, which was the most frightening thing about him: the sheer, stupid, gorgeous durability of his hope. Lionel believed in the basic sequence—you work hard, you do it right, people see that, the check clears. He believed this the way some people believed in astrology or trickle-down economics: not because the evidence supported it but because the alternative was a cliff.

I did not believe in the sequence. I believed in the check, but only in the way you believe in a bus that is already eleven minutes late—it might come, or the system might simply not be designed for you.

"Looks nice," Lionel said, glancing at the houses.

"It looks like money," I said.

"Same thing," he said, and smiled, and I let him have it because it was Christmas Eve and in two hours he was going to hand-plate forty-two beef Wellingtons for people who would not learn his name.

## II.

The Frost Ball was held at the Bellingham Heights Clubhouse, which was the kind of building that had been designed to communicate the word "tasteful" so aggressively that it looped back around to assault. Stone facade. Cedar beams. A portico with actual columns, Doric or Ionic or whichever one meant "we read a book about Greece once and missed the point." The front entrance was a set of double doors flanked by potted topiaries shaped like spirals, which struck me as a metaphor for something I'd work out later.

We did not use the front entrance. We used the service entrance, which was around the back, past the dumpsters, down a concrete ramp that smelled like grease trap and resignation. The door was propped open with a rubber wedge. No topiary. The hallway beyond was fluorescent-lit and floored in gray linoleum, and it led to a kitchen that was large, institutional, and equipped with exactly the kind of commercial range Lionel gazed at in restaurant supply catalogs with an expression I can only describe as horny. He lit up. He actually straightened his posture.

"Nice setup," he said, running a hand along the stainless steel counter.

"Don't bond with it," I said. "It won't call you."

He laughed—the big one, the one that came from his chest and made him seem, momentarily, like a man with no problems—and started unpacking. The cambros. The sheet trays. The Wellingtons, arranged in rows of seven, each one a small perfect architecture. This was the part where Lionel became his best self. In the kitchen, with the timing and the heat and the choreography of getting twelve things to the right temperature at the right moment, he was not a man whose ex-wife lived in Reno with a dental hygienist named Todd. He was not a man who'd missed rent in October and lied about it in November. He was a man making puff pastry do exactly what he told it to do, and the pastry listened, and that was a kind of authority the rest of his life did not grant him.

I polished flutes. Sixteen of them, each one held to the overhead light and examined for streaks I then removed with a microfiber cloth and a level of care I did not extend to any other area of my life. When the flutes were done, I arranged the garnish station—lemon twists, rosemary sprigs, the edible flowers the client had requested, which were pansies, purple and yellow, perched in their little clamshell like tiny, startled faces.

Then I ferried the bread baskets out to the ballroom, and I saw the Frost Ball for the first time.

Here is what I want you to understand about the Bellingham Heights Frost Ball: these people had committed to a theme with the zeal of the recently converted. The theme was "Winter Wonderland," which I know sounds like the default setting for every suburban holiday event since 1953, but the Bellingham Heights Homeowners' Association had approached the concept with a budget and a ferocity that elevated it from cliché to clinical condition. The ceiling—the entire ceiling—was draped in white chiffon, gathered at the center into a massive fabric rosette from which descended a chandelier made of actual crystal that had been augmented, somehow, with additional crystals, as though the original chandelier had been found insufficiently crystalline and sentenced to more. White birch branches stood in tall glass vases on every table. Fake snow dusted the windowsills. A fog machine—a *fog machine*—crouched in the corner near the DJ booth, periodically exhaling a low carpet of mist across the dance floor, which gave the whole room the look of an expensive funeral for a cloud.

Tables were set with white linen, silver chargers, and napkins folded into shapes I eventually identified as swans. Forty-two swans. Someone had folded forty-two napkin swans. I wanted to find that person and ask them, sincerely, if they were okay.

The guests had begun arriving, and I cataloged them from behind the bread station with the quiet devotion of a field biologist documenting a threatened species—threatened not by extinction but by an excess of resources that had led to unusual adaptive behaviors.

Mrs. Hargrave. First through the door. Late sixties. Silver hair sculpted into a shape that could survive a wind tunnel. She wore a white fur stole—real fur, the kind that had once been a living thing with opinions—and walked with the posture of someone who had been told, perhaps at a young age, that her skeleton was better than other people's skeletons. Her husband followed three paces behind, the standard trailing distance of a man whose primary marital function was to hold things—a clutch purse, a coat, a grudge so old it had become architectural.

The Kemps. He: tan, February tan, which in December in Washington state meant a tanning bed or Cabo, both of which suggested a man at war with his own latitude. Golf shoulders. Teeth that had been whitened past the point of biological plausibility, teeth that existed in a shade of white not found in the natural world, the white of a star dying. She: blond, the kind of blond that is a full-time job, her hair arriving in the room slightly before she did. A dress that was red in the way that ambulances are red—you were meant to look. She laughed before anyone said anything funny. She would laugh many more times that night, at things that were not funny, and it would always sound like someone testing a smoke detector.

Dr. Whitfield-Raines. Hyphenated. A woman in her fifties who had, at some point, committed to a pair of architectural glasses—huge, red-framed—that dominated her face like a building that had been granted a zoning variance no one understood. She served on the HOA board. She approached the auction table with the air of a museum docent returning to her post.

Because there was an auction table. This was, nominally, a charity event—the Frost Ball's stated purpose was to raise funds for something called the Bellingham Heights Community Betterment Fund, which, based on the neighborhood I'd driven through, could only mean bettering a community that was already so bettered it had lapped the field. The auction table held the usual assortment: gift baskets, spa packages, a wine collection donated by someone who wanted you to know they had a wine collection. But the centerpiece—literally centered, on a raised display stand, under a small spotlight that had been installed for this specific purpose—was a necklace.

The necklace sat on a black velvet bust, the kind jewelers use, headless and armless, which gave it the look of a tiny, glamorous crime scene. The necklace itself was a pendant—a single large stone, pear-shaped, set in a halo of smaller stones, on a chain so delicate it looked like it had been engineered by spiders with an advanced degree. A placard beside it read: **"Diamond Pendant Necklace — 2.3 ct center stone, 18k white gold setting — Valued at $45,000 — Generously donated by Association President Margaux Villard."**

Margaux Villard. There she was. I had seen her photograph in the email chain Lionel had shown me—she'd organized the event, booked us, sent the menu specifications with the crisp authority of a woman who had opinions about font choice—but in person she was more. She was a small woman, fine-boned, mid-forties, with the kind of dark bobbed hair that looked like it had been cut with a laser. She wore a silver dress that fit her like a verdict. She moved through the room the way a director moves through a theater before curtain—checking sight lines, adjusting placements, touching the edge of a tablecloth here, nudging a vase three millimeters to the left there. When she passed the necklace display, she paused. She didn't look at the necklace directly. She looked at the space around it—the lighting, the angle of the placard—the way you check a trap you've set, making sure the mechanism is sound.

That detail. I filed it then, in the part of my brain where I kept things that didn't yet have a category.

I brought the bread baskets to their stations. I returned to the kitchen. Lionel was plating the beet salads, each one a small composition—a disc of roasted beet, a crumble of chevre, three candied pecans placed at what he considered the optimal angles, which were angles he could have defended in court. His hands moved with the economy of practice. He was fast and he was precise and he was, in this moment, inarguable. A man making twelve identical salads that each looked like a painting. I wanted someone from the ballroom to see this. I wanted Mrs. Hargrave to stand in this kitchen and watch my father's hands and understand that she was about to eat something that had been *made*, with intelligence and care, by a person who existed.

No one came to the kitchen. The kitchen was not a place guests came. The kitchen was backstage, and backstage, by definition, is the place you are not supposed to know about.

I made four more trips. Salads out. Soup tureens positioned. Bread replenished. Each trip took me through the ballroom, and each trip I was constructing the map—where the exits were, where the DJ was set up, which way the auction table faced, who was standing near it and who wasn't. Not because I suspected anything. I didn't suspect anything yet. I was just a person who noticed things, which is what happens when you grow up watching your father not notice things: you develop the complementary mutation. He was the optimism. I was the sonar.

On my third trip through, I passed close to the necklace display. The velvet bust. The spotlight. The pendant, catching light, rotating slowly on its chain—someone had arranged it so the stone faced the room. I was close enough to see the setting clearly, the thin prongs holding the center stone, the halo of smaller stones around it.
