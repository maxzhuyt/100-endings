# The Check

## I.

The jacket had a grease stain on the left cuff that he'd stopped noticing three shifts ago, and the collar was soft with wear in the way that only cotton beaten through a hundred industrial wash cycles gets soft, which is to say not soft at all but merely surrendered, and he wore it to the dinner because he had come from work and because he did not yet own the kind of clothes you change into. Anthony Bourdain sat down across from Éric Ripert at a table in the middle of a dining room on East Fifty-First Street on a Tuesday in November 1998, and the first thing he registered was the man's hands. They were not a cook's hands. A cook's hands — Bourdain's hands, for instance — were landscapes of old burns and nicks, the topography of ten thousand tickets, and they moved with a kind of controlled recklessness, always reaching, always a little too fast. Ripert's hands rested on the tablecloth as though the tablecloth had been laid for the specific purpose of receiving them. They were precise and almost entirely still. They belonged to a surgeon, or a monk, or a man who had learned to control every gesture because the alternative was to reveal something he did not wish to reveal. Bourdain didn't know any of that yet. He saw the hands and thought: *This man is serious about things I am not yet serious about.*

Someone had introduced them. A mutual friend — José, or maybe it was Michael; the name was lost even before the bread arrived— had said, *You two need to eat together,* in the way people in the industry said it, which meant either *you'll love each other* or *this will be entertaining for me to hear about later.* It didn't matter. What mattered was the menu.

Ripert picked it up. He held it at a slight angle, as though he were reading a letter from someone he respected but did not entirely trust, and his eyes moved down the page with the particular velocity of a man who could extrapolate an entire kitchen from three menu descriptions. He set it down. He looked at Bourdain with an expression that contained, in its several layers, amusement and challenge and the faintest flicker of generosity, and he said: "I'll order for you."

Bourdain, who had been planning to order the cassoulet because he always ordered the cassoulet because cassoulet was the dish against which he measured all restaurants and found most of them wanting, looked at Ripert and understood immediately that a game was being proposed, and that the game had rules, and that the rules would never be spoken aloud. He picked up his own menu. He read it. He'd already read it. He knew what Ripert should eat because Ripert was a fish cook, the best fish cook alive, and a fish cook in a restaurant like this would either be bored or disgusted or, in the rarest case, silently impressed, and Bourdain wanted to produce the third outcome with his selection and the first with his commentary. He set the menu down.

"I'll order for you," he said.

Neither of them explained.

Ripert ordered for Bourdain a vitello tonnato to start and a roasted guinea hen with sauce Albuféra, which was a choice so precise it constituted a form of mind-reading, because the Albuféra — cream, foie gras, meat glaze, a reduction that was an argument about French technique condensed into a single spoonful — was the kind of dish Bourdain would have killed for and been too proud to order for himself in front of a four-star chef. Bourdain ordered for Ripert the tartare and the black bass with artichokes, which was a choice designed to seem obvious and was in fact a trap: the bass was either going to be the best thing on the menu or a minor embarrassment, and either way it would give him information about what Ripert's face did when he encountered another cook's fish.

The napkins were unfolded. Bourdain unfolded his with a single motion, the way you clear a station — functional, unhesitant, the cloth snapped once against the air before landing across his left thigh. Ripert unfolded his as though he were unwrapping something that might contain a small, sleeping animal, each fold turned back with care, the final rectangle smoothed across his lap with one pass of his palm. These were not affectations. These were the honest gestures of two men whose hands had been trained by different kitchens and who had never had reason to reconsider how they handled cloth.

Wine was poured. Wine was swirled. Bourdain swirled his burgundy with three quick rotations, the glass gripped too far down the stem in a way that would have made a sommelier wince, and he put his nose into the glass with the frankness of a man smelling a stockpot to check if someone had scorched the fond. Ripert lifted his glass and turned it once, slowly, and the wine climbed the interior surface and fell back in legs so even they could have been used to calibrate an instrument, and he inhaled with his eyes closed, and when he opened them, he said nothing, which meant the wine was good.

Bourdain drank. He set the glass down.

"Your palate," he said, "is monastic."

He said it the way he said everything at that time in his life — with the timing of a man who had learned that if you made people laugh, they gave you things: shifts, drugs, second chances, the benefit of the doubt. He said it as an insult and meant it as a question. What he was asking was: *How do you taste things so quietly? How do you stand in the middle of all that noise and refinement and not lose your mind? What is it like to be that controlled and that good at the same time?*

Ripert looked at him. The look took perhaps two seconds and contained a complete assessment.

"Your palate," Ripert said, "is juvenile."

And Bourdain laughed — a real laugh, not the performed bark that he would later deploy on camera like a concussion grenade, but an actual surprised release of air from the lungs of a man who had just been accurately diagnosed by a stranger and found the diagnosis delightful. Juvenile. Yes. His palate was juvenile. It wanted what it wanted with the uncomplicated greed of a fourteen-year-old, and it wanted pork fat and fish sauce and the char on the bottom of a clay pot and the last spoonful of ramen broth at four in the morning, and it did not want to be refined, and it did not want to be educated, and it wanted, above all, to be *hungry*, to stay hungry, because hunger was the engine and the engine was everything.

Ripert's palate was a different machine. Ripert tasted the way certain people listened to music — not for the melody but for the architecture underneath, the intervals, the places where the structure breathed. His palate was discretion made physical. He could detect a quarter-teaspoon variance in a court-bouillon across a kitchen the size of a tennis court. He could taste whether a commis had salted the water before or after the boil, and he considered this not a talent but a basic professional obligation, and he held everyone around him to the same standard with a gentleness that was more devastating than rage. Bourdain understood none of this in 1998. He understood only that the man across from him was operating at a frequency he could hear but not yet produce, and that the recognition of this gap — the precise distance between his own capacity and Ripert's — was not humiliating but thrilling, the way it is thrilling to stand at the edge of deep water and understand for the first time how far down it goes.

They ate.

The vitello tonnato arrived and Bourdain ate it in four bites, which was too fast but was also exactly the right speed because the dish was about temperature and the surrendering tenderness of the veal against the tuna-and-caper sauce, and four bites kept the temperature right and let the flavors do their work across the whole palate simultaneously rather than in the studied, analytical progression that a four-star chef would have preferred. Ripert watched him eat. There was nothing clinical in the watching. He watched the way a man watches another man eat when he wants to know if the other man is real.

The bass arrived for Ripert. He cut into it, and the flesh opened white and clean. He ate the first bite with his eyes down, then looked up, and his face did the thing that Bourdain had been waiting for: absolutely nothing. Blank. Composed. The fish was good. The fish was not as good as his fish. This information passed across Ripert's features like a cloud shadow crossing a field — there and gone, visible only if you were already watching the field.

"Not bad," Ripert said.

"Don't be generous," Bourdain said. "It makes you look Venezuelan."

This was meaningless. It was not a joke that could be decoded. It was the kind of thing Bourdain said when he was happy and wanted to see if the person across from him could tolerate nonsense, because the tolerance of nonsense was, for Bourdain, one of the load-bearing requirements of love.

Ripert's mouth moved in a way that was not quite a smile.

"Venezuelan," he repeated, tasting the word the way he tasted everything, with complete attention. "I don't know what this means."

"Nobody does," Bourdain said. "That's the point."

They talked. They talked about food first — about the things everyone in the industry talked about: who was doing what, which kitchens were falling apart, the impossible economics, the thirteen-hour days, the way a perfect service felt in the body afterward like the afterglow of violence. Then they talked about other things. Bourdain talked about books. He talked about Conrad and Orwell and Graham Greene and a Patrick O'Brian novel he was reading for the third time. He talked the way he would later write — in long, controlled, syntactically adventurous sentences that took detours through metaphor and analogy and low comedy before arriving, unexpectedly, at something true. Ripert listened. He listened the way he tasted. He asked questions that were not the questions Bourdain expected. He asked about the O'Brian novel as though it mattered to him that the rigging details were accurate, and Bourdain realized with a start that Ripert had *read the book*, that Ripert read everything, that behind the technical perfection and the monastic palate was a man who consumed the world in his own way, privately, without display, and who was now, across this table, displaying it — just slightly, just enough — as an offering.

The guinea hen arrived and it was magnificent. The sauce Albuféra was the color of old gold and had the viscosity of something that had been reduced past the point of politeness into the territory of pure desire. Bourdain ate it and felt the cream and the foie gras and the demi-glace register on his palate the way a chord registers in the chest, as a physical event, and he looked at Ripert and said: "You bastard. You knew about this sauce."

"I know about all the sauces," Ripert said, and this was not a boast but a statement of fact delivered with such serene confidence that Bourdain laughed again, and the laugh was warm, and the warmth was real, and the evening had tipped over into the territory where time changes speed and the restaurant empties around you without your noticing and the waitstaff begins to glance at each other with the particular patience of professionals who understand that the table by the window is not going anywhere tonight.

They ordered more wine. They drank it. Ripert told a story about Joël Robuchon that involved a saucepan and a silence so terrifying it had restructured his understanding of authority. Bourdain told a story about a prep cook in Provincetown who had once deep-fried a rat on a dare and served it to a health inspector as "squab." The stories were not equivalent, and the inequivalence was part of the pleasure, and Bourdain understood this — understood that the contrast between Ripert's world and his own was not a gap to be bridged but a space to be inhabited, a room they were building between them out of differences.

At some point — eleven-thirty, midnight, later — the check arrived. Bourdain reached for it. Ripert reached for it. Their hands arrived at the same time. There was a brief, friendly negotiation that was also a minor physical comedy, two men's fingers on opposite edges of a leather folio, neither quite willing to let go. Ripert won, or Bourdain let him win, and paid.

"Next time," Bourdain said.

"Next time," Ripert said.

Bourdain walked home. He walked south on Second Avenue in the raw cold of a November that had not yet committed to winter, and his jacket was insufficient, and his hands were in his pockets, and the city was doing the thing the city does after midnight when you are in the right mood, which is to say it was performing its own existence with a clarity and specificity that felt like a private communication, every lit window a sentence, every cab a comma, every gust of wind off the river a parenthetical containing information he could almost decode. He was a forty-two-year-old line cook who had written a book that might or might not be published and who worked in a kitchen that was not the best kitchen and who had, for most of his adult life, maintained the controlled catastrophe of a functioning addict, and he was walking home from a dinner with a man he had met three hours ago and feeling, inside his chest, in the precise location where the warmth of the burgundy overlapped with the warmth of the evening, a sensation he did not have a name for.

It was not admiration, though he admired Ripert. It was not envy, though there were things about Ripert he envied. It was not gratitude, though he was grateful, obscurely, for the guinea hen and the sauce and the Robuchon story and the way Ripert had said "juvenile" with an accent that made the word sound like a diagnosis and a benediction simultaneously.

It was the pleasure of being *seen*. Not seen the way a cook is seen by a critic, which is the experience of being translated into someone else's vocabulary and found either adequate or wanting. Not seen the way a man is seen by a woman he desires, which is the experience of being selected from a field of options and presented with the bill later. Seen the way a musician is seen by another musician: the full apprehension of what you are doing and how you are doing it and *why* you are doing it, rendered not in language but in the quality of attention.
