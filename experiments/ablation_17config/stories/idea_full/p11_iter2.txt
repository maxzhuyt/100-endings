# Every Time We Lost Kovacs

## I.

The pudding cups were the hill Torrez was willing to die on.

Not the war — he'd already done that, given it both legs below the knee and the ability to sleep through the night and whatever part of a man's brain is responsible for tolerating small talk at cookouts. Not the card game, though I'd beaten him six hands running and he owed me three cigarettes he didn't have and a favor to be named later. The pudding cups. Butterscotch. Twelve of them, which Deborah on night shift had left in the dayroom refrigerator with a sticky note that said **FOR RESIDENTS — 1 EACH** in handwriting so aggressive it should have required a prescription.

"One each," Torrez said, like he was reading from Deuteronomy.

"One each is a suggestion."

"One each is a *rule*, Earl."

"Rules are suggestions with better posture."

He had six of the pudding cups stacked in his lap and both stumps planted on the wheelchair's footrests like he was daring me to come get them. I had six in a pillowcase knotted at the top. We had been arguing for twenty minutes. It was the best night I'd had in weeks.

Let me tell you about the ward and then I'll tell you about Kovacs and then I'll tell you about the deer, because all three things happened in the same forty-eight hours and I want to get them right while I can still get things right, which is not a guarantee I'm able to make in perpetuity, as Dr. Reyes likes to remind me every Tuesday with her little flashlight and her little clock-drawing test and her face like she's watching a chandelier come loose from the ceiling in slow motion.

Building 7, Ward C, VA Medical Center, Prescott, Arizona. Fourteen beds, twelve occupied. Fluorescent lights that buzzed at a frequency specifically designed to make you remember every bad decision you ever made. Linoleum the color of yellowed piano keys. A dayroom with a television bolted to the wall that only picked up three channels, all of which seemed to be showing the same program about people selling storage units. An alarm system that went off at least once a day because the automatic doors on the east corridor had a sensor malfunction that nobody had fixed since the Clinton administration — it'd just start screaming whenever a draft blew through or a bird got too close or God hiccupped, and everyone would look up like they'd been personally accused of something, and then Deborah or whoever was on shift would punch the code and we'd all go back to the business of dying at government-approved speed.

Room 4 had Meacham, the stroke patient, who hadn't spoken since July. He communicated by knocking his plastic cup against the side rail of his bed — one knock for yes, two for no, and a long rattling drumroll that meant he either needed the bathroom or was lodging a philosophical objection to his circumstances. In rooms 5 through 7 you had the rotating cast of short-timers who came in with pneumonia or infected wounds and left in a week looking relieved. Room 8 had Kovacs.

Kovacs. Let me do Kovacs.

Hungarian, or his people were, with a face like a bag of wrenches and hands that still looked strong even though the rest of him was shutting down in no particular order — kidneys first, then the lungs, then the eyes, then some days the kidneys would come back online and the lungs would quit and the whole system would rearrange itself like it was playing musical chairs with his organs. He'd been dying since I got there in April. He was magnificent at it. He died the way some men play poker: slowly, with great theater, making you think he was about to fold every hand and then turning over a card that kept him in the game for another round. He'd code at 2 a.m. and the crash cart would come and the whole ward would go electric and still, the way the air goes before a storm, and then Kovacs would be back by breakfast, eating eggs, looking offended that anyone had been concerned.

"You know what's wrong with this place?" he told me once, three days before the forty-eight hours I'm telling you about. He had an oxygen cannula and an IV and a blood-pressure cuff that inflated automatically every fifteen minutes like a hand squeezing his arm to see if he was still there. "Nobody here is going anywhere. That's what's wrong. In a real hospital, people leave. Here we just — " He made a gesture with his wrenched-up hand, a kind of slow rotation, like a wheel turning in place.

He was right. We just.

Now Torrez. Torrez was in the bed three feet from mine, which meant I knew the sound of his breathing better than I knew my own, which meant some nights I'd lie there in the dark and listen to him exhale and think, *That's what safety sounds like,* and I didn't know why I thought that, only that I did, and that it was connected to something older than this bed, this room, this version of myself.

The night of the pudding cups, Torrez and I played gin rummy until 2300 — I'm going to use military time because it's what's in my head and I have been advised by Dr. Reyes to use what's in my head even when what's in my head is not what's in the room, which is a piece of therapeutic advice that seems to me to have some significant design flaws, but fine.

We played gin rummy. Torrez shuffled by splitting the deck into two halves and riffling them together with his thumbs, precise as a bank teller, which is what he'd been for nineteen years before his diabetes took the first leg and the VA took the second. As a card player, he was what I'd call optimistically incompetent — he held every hand like it was about to become something better and could never bring himself to discard a face card, which meant his hand was always full of royalty and empty of strategy. I loved playing cards with Torrez. I loved beating Torrez. The beating was an act of friendship. He understood this.

"Gin," I said, and laid down my hand.

"That's not gin."

"I miscounted. But it's close enough to gin that I think we should honor the spirit of the thing."

"The spirit of gin is that you actually have gin."

"The spirit of gin is that I have more than you."

He looked at his hand. He looked at mine. He put down his cards and picked up a pudding cup and peeled back the foil with the slow, deliberate care of a man defusing a bomb.

"Cookout's Saturday," he said.

He said this every night. The Veterans Day cookout at the American Legion post across town, which Torrez had been to every year for eleven years and which he considered to be the only event on the calendar that mattered, more important than Christmas, Thanksgiving, his own birthday, or the second coming of Christ, which he said he also planned to attend but only if it was catered.

"Cookout's Saturday. Martindale's driving. We sign out after breakfast, we're back by lights-out, nobody gets flagged."

"I'm aware of the plan."

"I'm *repeating* the plan, Earl, because plans require repetition for people who—" He stopped. He looked at his pudding cup. "For everyone," he said. "Plans require repetition for everyone."

I knew what he'd stopped himself from saying. It was kind of him to stop. It was also unnecessary. I knew about the tests. I knew about the scores. I knew that Dr. Reyes had written something in my chart two weeks ago that made the morning nurse look at me differently, the way you look at a carton of milk when you're not sure about the date. I knew that "assessment" was a word that had begun appearing with the regularity of a season, and that the season it heralded was not spring.

But that night — that night I was sharp. I want you to know that. The cards were good and the pudding was cold and sweet and Torrez was cursing under his breath about his hand and the alarm went off at 2317 because the east-corridor doors had sensed the passage of a phantom and somewhere down the hall Meacham was rattling his cup against his rail like a man sending code from a long way off and I was *here.* I was exactly here. Every object in the room had its right name and every name was where I left it.

The alarm cut out. Deborah came through on her round, counted heads, confiscated two of my pudding cups with a look that could curdle concrete, and told us lights were in twenty minutes. She had arms like a middleweight and a voice from Detroit and on her good nights she'd sit on the edge of somebody's bed and tell stories about her son who was deployed in some desert I kept losing the name of.

"Lights in twenty," she said.

"Copy," I said.

She looked at me.

"I mean — sure. Fine. Twenty minutes."

She moved on. Torrez was watching me. He had a way of watching that wasn't watching — he'd be looking at his cards or his stumps or the ceiling, but the quality of his attention would change, and I'd feel it the way you feel someone aiming at you, which is another thought I did not know the source of.

"You good?" he said.

"I'm good."

"Because you said 'copy.'"

"Some people say copy."

"Some people who were in the Army in 1968 say copy. Some people in a hospital in 2019 say 'sure, fine, twenty minutes.'"

"I said both. I contain multitudes."

He snorted. But he was still watching-not-watching. He finished his pudding. He placed the empty cup on his bedside table with the exactness that was the signature of everything he did — a man who had lost two legs and compensated by making every remaining act of his body deliberate, certain, placed.

Somewhere down the hall, in Room 8, the machines around Kovacs were doing their quiet work, and I could hear them if I listened, which I did, the way you listen for a heartbeat through a wall, which is the same as listening for a footstep in the dark, which is the same as — 

We slept. I dreamed of nothing I could name when I woke, only a color, which was green, and a sound, which was rotors, and a feeling, which was that I had left someone behind.

---

Kovacs died at 0340.

I know because the machines changed their music — from the steady beeping that was the baseline hum of the ward to a single sustained note that every man on that hallway recognized even in sleep, even through the chemical fog of nighttime meds, even through whatever private country each of us had retreated to in the dark. The note said *boundary.* The note said *someone has crossed to where you cannot follow.* The note said *subtract one from the number you are.*

The crash cart came. The team came. The sound of shoes on linoleum at speed, which is different from the sound of shoes on linoleum at walking pace — faster, obviously, but also more committed, as though the floor itself understood the difference and changed its acoustic properties accordingly. Torrez was awake. I was awake. We lay in our beds and listened to Room 8 the way a congregation listens to a sermon being delivered in a language they only partially understand.

It took eleven minutes. I know because I watched the clock on the wall, the institutional clock with its steady second hand that moved with the bureaucratic patience of a form being filled out in triplicate. Eleven minutes of voices and movement and the particular controlled urgency that is not panic but is panic's first cousin, and then the tone stopped, and the voices changed register — from imperative to past tense, from *doing* to *having done* — and I knew.

Torrez said, "Kovacs?"

I said, "Kovacs."

He was quiet for a while. Then he said, "That man was dying for six months and every time I thought he was going to hold on for one more hand."

"He did hold on for one more hand. He ran out of hands."

"That's not how it works."

"That's exactly how it works."

We listened to the aftermath: the lowered voices, the paperwork sounds, the phone call to whoever you call at 0351 to tell them that a man named Kovacs is not a man named Kovacs anymore but a body that used to carry that name, a room that needs to be cleaned, a bed that will be filled by Thursday because the waitlist is long and the living are impatient.

Deborah came to our door. She was silhouetted against the hallway light, and for a moment she was just a shape — broad-shouldered, solid, backlit into featurelessness — and my heart did something I did not authorize it to do, which was to lurch, heavily, with a recognition that did not belong to this hallway.

"You boys okay?"

"We heard," Torrez said.

"I know you heard. That's why I'm asking."

"We're okay."

She stood there a moment longer. Then she said, "I'll leave the hallway light on," which was against regulation and which she did every time someone on the ward died, which told you something about the frequency of death in Building 7 and something about Deborah and something about the difference between rules and kindness, which is that kindness mostly happens in the space where rules decide to look the other way.

The hallway light stayed on. Torrez and I did not sleep. We lay in the low glow and we did not talk about Kovacs and we did not talk about the cookout on Saturday and we did not talk about the assessment Dr. Reyes had scheduled for me on Monday, which was the reason Torrez wanted us both out of the building by Saturday morning — because he knew, though he'd never said it, that Monday's assessment might result in a transfer, and a transfer would mean a different ward, a different building, a locked unit where the doors did not malfunction because the doors did not open at all.

At 0600 the day shift came on and the ward resumed its rhythm, and Kovacs's room was cleaned and the bed was stripped and the machines were wheeled out and the room stood empty in that particular way that rooms stand empty when they have recently held a specific person — not vacant but *evacuated,* still shaped by the presence that had left it.

---
