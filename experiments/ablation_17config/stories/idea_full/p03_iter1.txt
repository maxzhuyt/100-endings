# The Meridian League

## I.

Vince Morrow, who had been Valiant, who had once held a suspension bridge's severed cable in each fist while eleven hundred commuters crossed to safety in the rain and who afterward checked his reflection in a hubcap to be certain the angle of his jaw had photographed well, was in the waiting room of Dr. Cheryl Kazmierski, D.D.S., reading a three-month-old copy of *Better Homes & Gardens* with studied disinterest when the television in the upper corner—muted, captioned, tuned to Channel 4—showed his son's yearbook photo above a red-bannered crawl that read MISSING TEEN LINKED TO FOUR OTHERS IN APPARENT ABDUCTION, and he stood, or his brain sent the ancient hero-signal to stand, the full floodlit imperative of it, every nerve ending craning upward toward the posture that had once been the last thing a hundred villains saw before unconsciousness, but his left knee—Loss Adjuster for the Great Indemnity of Aging—cashed in its chips at the apex of the surge and he went down, the herofall of him, all two hundred and thirty-one pounds of gone-to-thickened-middle ex-magnificent, one hand catching the magazine rack and taking it with him in a clattering avalanche of *Highlights for Children* and dental hygiene pamphlets, the other hand reaching not toward the screen but toward the pocket where his phone was, as though the phone were the child.

The receptionist stood. "Mr. Morrow? *Mr. Morrow?*"

On the screen, captioned: *—authorities have not confirmed whether the disappearances are connected but sources close to—*

Kyle's face. Seventeen. Vince's own forehead, which he had always secretly liked, the broad regal architecture of it, replicated in miniature on this boy who had last spoken to him on the phone six weeks ago to ask for money and had said *thanks* the way one thanks a vending machine. That forehead now inside a red rectangle, the universal signifier of someone who has been made into news.

He tried to stand again. The knee popped—*gristlecrack*—not the clean heroic crack of knuckles before a fight but the sound of a body bargaining with itself and losing. He used the chair. He made it upright. The receptionist was coming around the partition with her hands out, the gesture people make toward the elderly and the very drunk.

"I'm fine," said the man who was not fine, who had been Valiant, whose body had once been a territory of absolute obedience and was now a province in open revolt. "I need to—I'm fine. I need to make a call."

He called Marcus first because that was the old order, because twenty years dissolves everything except muscle memory and the chain of command. It rang four times and went to voicemail
and Marcus's voice, recorded however many years ago, said only "Leave a message," in that level, measuring, maddening way he had of compressing all available authority into the fewest possible syllables, and Vince heard himself say: "Marc. It's Vince. Turn on the news. It's Kyle. It's—you need to turn on the news. I think it's all of them. Marc, I think it's all of our—" and his voice did something it had not done since the christening, since the thing none of them could agree had happened the way any of them remembered it, and he hung up before the voicemail could record whatever came after.

He stood in the parking lot of Kazmierski Dental, Dayton, Ohio, squinting in October sun that felt like an accusation. His left knee was a hot, liquid, wrongful thing. His phone showed one missed call from a number he didn't recognize, and a text from the same number, no words, just a photograph: five teenagers seated in a row on a concrete floor against a cinderblock wall, wrists zip-tied, mouths not gagged, expressions not terrified but *bored*, the way teenagers look bored at everything, as though kidnapping were simply another adult imposition they were enduring with contempt.

Kyle. Second from the left. Wearing headphones around his neck. Still wearing headphones.

Beneath the photo, three words from the unknown number:

**You remember me.**

And he did and he did not, and he stood there in the parking lot with the strip mall behind him (Kazmierski Dental, Dollar General, a place that sold vape cartridges and phone cases), and his knee screamed in its small domestic language, and the sky was very blue and extremely ordinary and his son's face was on his phone as a hostage and on the television inside as a statistic and in his memory as a baby someone held at a christening twenty years ago, someone held and then everything—

But he couldn't think about the christening. Not yet. Not standing. Not with this knee.

He got in his car and he drove toward Columbus because Columbus was the center, Columbus was always where they went to ground, though no one had called the assembly and probably no one would because the assembly was a dead thing, a dissolved thing, the Meridian League had not existed in any operational sense since that afternoon in the church when Diana had held the baby—Kyle, his Kyle—and said the word *provisional* and meant by it, or had seemed to mean by it, or had not meant by it at all—

Vince drove. The highway unfurled. His knee throbbed in 4/4 time.

## II.

What happened at the christening:

Diana would tell you she said nothing wrong. She would tell you she held the baby—Vince's boy, Kyle, the first child born to any of them, a fact that felt historic because they had been historic people—and she looked at the gathered team in that church in Columbus (St. Joseph's, the one on High Street with the water stain on the ceiling that looked like a question mark if you tilted your head) and she said the word *provisional* in reference to something liturgical, the provisional nature of naming, how a name was a kind of hope and not a fact, and she had meant it philosophically, or theologically, or perhaps she had not fully known what she meant, and the word had landed in the room and Marcus had taken it one way and Helen had taken it another way and Vince, holding his son back after the ritual, had felt the word soak into the blanket like a stain and heard in it something about the whole enterprise—the League, the years, the costumes, the purpose—being *provisional*, being a phase through which one passed on the way to something realer, and he could not forgive this because forgiving it would mean admitting he had heard it correctly.

Marcus would tell you the christening was irrelevant. A pretext. The League was already fracturing along structural lines—Helen's drinking, Ray's increasing reluctance to engage physically in combat after the Waterton incident, Diana's barely concealed contempt for the publicity apparatus Gerald Peet had constructed around them, Vince's vanity, his own (Marcus would admit this) rigidity. The christening merely provided the detonation point. The explosives were already wired. Anyone who narrated it as a single event caused by a single word was engaged in the fundamental human error of mistaking a proximate cause for a sufficient one.

Helen would tell you she didn't remember.

She would tell you this while looking away, and the quality of the looking-away would tell you that she remembered everything but had decided, through whatever calculus governs the stories we most desperately need not to tell ourselves, that the not-remembering was more survivable than the alternative. Her version, if she had one, existed somewhere behind the vodka-and-seltzers she no longer drank but could still taste in moments of acute stress, a phantom limb of flavor on the back palate, the ghost of the thing she had used to dissolve her memory of the afternoon when she—

But she didn't remember.

Ray would tell you about Marcus's face. That was all he would tell you. The look on Marcus's face when Diana said *provisional*. How Marcus's expression did something Ray had never seen it do in a decade of combat—it *opened*, briefly, like a door swinging inward onto a dark room, and Ray saw in that opening something about how Marcus understood himself, how the League was not a thing Marcus *led* but a thing Marcus *was*, and the word *provisional* applied to the League was the word *provisional* applied to Marcus himself, and the door closed, and Marcus's face returned to its usual granite administration, and two hours later they were all in the parking lot having the conversation that ended everything, and Ray—who had said almost nothing during the argument, who had stood next to the rented minivan with his hand on the roof feeling the heat of the metal—Ray had looked at Diana and wanted to say something and had not said it, and for twenty years he had thought in Diana's cadences, in Diana's metaphors, in the luminous color-saturated vocabulary of a woman who perceived the electromagnetic spectrum seventeen octaves beyond human range and who described the world in language that sounded like synesthesia because for her it was simply accuracy.

Gerald Peet would tell you nothing. Gerald Peet had not been invited to the christening. This was the sort of detail none of them remembered, because none of them had thought of Gerald Peet as the sort of person one remembered to invite or not invite. He was the publicist. He was adjacent. He wrote the press releases and crafted the talking points and developed the brand architecture and negotiated the licensing agreements—lunchboxes, Saturday morning cartoons, a bewildering range of shampoos—and he understood, because understanding was his profession, that the Meridian League's power lived not in their abilities but in their *narrative*, the story the public told itself about what these five people meant, and he had written that story, and he had not been invited to the christening because the story did not include him except as a footnote, and the story was all anyone ever saw.

Gerald Peet was not at the christening but he heard about it within the hour, because Helen called him—Helen, the one who would later tell you she didn't remember—from her car in the church parking lot, crying in a way he had heard her cry only once before, after Waterton, and she told him what Diana had said, or what she believed Diana had said, or what she had felt Diana mean, and Gerald listened and took notes because that was what Gerald did, he listened and took notes, and after Helen hung up he wrote on a legal pad: *The Meridian League is over. Question: what do I do with what I know about them?*

He had not answered this question for seventeen years. Then his body began to fail him, differently from how their bodies would fail them, more dramatically, in ways he decided to answer with surgery rather than endurance, and the answer to the question on the legal pad began to take its present shape.

## III.

Diana drove in from Portland. Two days on the interstate, every mile a color, every color a temperature, every temperature a frequency her retinas decoded into information she could not stop receiving and had long ago stopped trying to describe to people who would hear it as metaphor. Crossing into Ohio the sky shifted—cerulean flatlined into a washed-out zinc, the specific gray of industrial Midwest October that she thought of as *griefpalette*, the color temperature of used dishwater, and the billboards along I-70 pulsed in their garish advertising frequencies and she tracked them all involuntarily, the infrared heat signatures of their halogen lamps fading in the afternoon like the body temperatures of the recently dead—

She was thinking about Lily. Her daughter. Nineteen. A sophomore at Ohio State, which was in Columbus, which was where they were all going, which was where it had all ended, and Diana had let Lily attend college in Columbus knowing this and feeling about it a complicated thing she had not yet successfully portmanteau'd into a single word, though the closest she had come was *returnguilt*—the guilt of having made a place radioactive through your own history and then watching your child choose to live there anyway, as though the radiation were yours alone and did not travel down the line.

Lily had not called her in eleven days before the disappearance. This was not unusual. Lily communicated in bursts, three days of manic texting followed by silences that lasted weeks, and Diana had learned to tolerate the silences the way she tolerated the zinc-gray Ohio sky: by reminding herself that her sensory range was not the standard and that other people lived happily in spectrums she would find unbearable. But now the silence had a shape. The silence was a zip-tied girl on a concrete floor—she had received the same photograph Vince had, from the same number—and Diana looked at her daughter's face in the photograph and saw that Lily's expression was not bored, the way the other children's expressions were bored. Lily's expression was *doing something with its wavelengths*, something in the micro-muscular frequencies around the jaw that Diana's hyper-perceptive eyes could decode even in a low-resolution photograph, and what Lily's face was doing was:

Being brave in a way she had learned from someone. Being brave in a way she had seen modeled. Being brave in a specific practiced way that Diana recognized because it was hers—her own face, her own wavelength, the face she had worn at Waterton and at the Pinnacle Tower and at a hundred smaller catastrophes—and Diana discovered, doing eighty-three on I-70 outside Springfield, that a person could cry at seventeen octaves simultaneously and that each octave was a different species of loss.

*You remember me*, the text had said.

She remembered everyone. Her eyes forgot nothing. The visual field was total and permanent, every face she had ever perceived stored at full resolution in the eidetic architecture of her perception. She could close her eyes and see the christening—Vince's ridiculous church, the water stain on the ceiling, Marcus's face *opening*, Ray standing by the minivan afterward with his hand on the hot metal and his mouth shaping a word he did not say—and she could see all of it in the full electromagnetic spectrum, every infrared bloom of emotion on every face, every ultraviolet flicker of adrenaline, every micro-expression catalogued and preserved, and this was her particular curse: she could not misremember. The others had the luxury of revision. The others could smooth the christening into a narrative that protected them. Diana was stuck with what she had seen, and what she had seen was:

Everyone. At their worst. In high definition. Forever.

She pulled into Columbus at four in the afternoon. The city was a ruin of itself—or no, that was her palette talking, her tendency to saturate perception with mood. Columbus was fine.
