# The Dunnhaven Ladies' Curling Club

---

I should say first that I am the third alternate, which means I am the woman who replaces the woman who replaces the woman who replaces one of the women who actually play. In forty years I have never delivered a stone in competitive play. I have held stones. I have carried stones. I have, on one occasion in 1997, warmed a stone against my body in a car park outside Dumfries because Eileen had read somewhere that temperature variation affects the curl and the boot of the minibus had a draft that came in through the rust, which itself came in through the salt, which was Phyllis's fault for storing road grit beside the equipment bag, though to be fair to Phyllis the grit was there because Janet had refused to let anyone track slush into the Dunfermline Travelodge and someone had to put it somewhere and Phyllis is, if nothing else, a woman who solves problems by absorbing them into her personal vicinity, which is how she ended up captaining the team in the first place, and also how she ended up with sciatica, two storage units, and a freezer full of casseroles she made for other people's crises and then forgot to deliver.

My name is Maureen Kettleworth. I keep the scorecards. I launder the jerseys. I know that Phyllis takes her tea with one sugar and a splash of milk added *after* the sugar has dissolved, not before, and that this distinction matters to Phyllis in a way she will never explain but which I suspect has something to do with her mother, because everything about Phyllis that cannot be explained by logic can be explained by her mother, who was a tremendous woman in the way that a weather system is tremendous, meaning you survived her or you didn't, and Phyllis survived her, barely, and has spent the rest of her life making sure every small thing is done in the right order as if order itself could be a form of shelter.

I know that Janet takes hers black. I know that Eileen won't drink tea at all before a match but will drink precisely half a cup afterward, and that if you fill it past the halfway mark she will stare at it with the expression of someone who has been personally affronted by crockery. I know that Dot takes three sugars and has been told by her GP to take none and so takes two when Phyllis is watching and three when she isn't, and I know this because I am always watching, which is what happens when you spend forty years on a bench behind a barrier next to the bag with the knee braces.

You see more from where I sit, which is to say, from where I've always sat, which is just there, behind the barrier, next to the bag with the knee braces. The knee braces are mine. Well—some of them are mine. The beige elastic one with the metal stays is Dot's, and the neoprene sleeve is Janet's, though Janet insists she's never worn it and it must belong to someone else, meaning it belongs to Janet, because Janet is the only person I've ever met who lies exclusively about her own joints.

I should tell you what happened. The Dunnhaven Ladies' Curling Club reached the semifinal of the 2019 World Senior Curling Championships, which were held in Stavanger, Norway, where the arena had the kind of fluorescent lighting that makes everyone look like they've already died and the coffee was served from a machine that produced a liquid bearing the same relationship to coffee that a photocopy of a painting bears to the painting, which is to say it was technically the same shape and contained some of the same information but was missing whatever quality makes you glad to be alive, and Janet said the biscuits were "Continental," by which she did not mean French or Italian or in any way sophisticated but meant, specifically, that they were flat, dry, and tasted of nothing, which is how Janet has used the word "Continental" since at least 1979 when she and Phyllis went on a coach tour of Belgium, a trip about which Janet has never volunteered a single positive detail and which, I believe, inoculated her permanently against anything that occurred east of the North Sea—and in the semifinal, in that arena, under those lights, with that coffee, the Dunnhaven Ladies' Curling Club lost.

They lost to the Swedish national team, 7-3, in eight ends.

And every single one of them was glad.

---

It started, as these things do, because of a leaflet. Or rather it started because Phyllis Taggart could not find her reading glasses—her *other* reading glasses, the ones she kept in her handbag rather than on the chain around her neck, the chain being reserved for what she called her "official" glasses, which she wore for driving, church, and arguing with the council, the handbag pair being for everything else, meaning everything that Phyllis considered to be occurring in a lower register of formality—and without them, the notice pinned to the board in the Dunnhaven post office was nothing more than a promising blur.

What Phyllis saw, or believed she saw, was a signup sheet for a community social evening at the ice rink. What it actually was, was an entry form for the 1979-80 East Central Scotland Ladies' Curling League, Division Four.

This was 1979. Phyllis was forty-one. Janet was thirty-nine. Eileen was thirty-seven and newly separated from Gordon, which no one spoke about directly but which had settled over the village like weather, everyone adjusting their indoor arrangements accordingly. Dot was forty-three and had just been made redundant from the woolen mill. I was thirty-six and lived alone and had always lived alone and would continue to live alone, which I mention not for sympathy—I have never wanted sympathy; sympathy requires a wound, and the absence of certain things is not a wound but a topography, a way the land happens to lie—but because it explains why Phyllis's phone call found me at home on a Tuesday evening with nothing in particular to refuse.

"Thursday," Phyllis said. "The rink. It'll be a social. Bring something."

I brought shortbread, which turned out to be a mistake, because Janet also brought shortbread, and this was my first encounter with a fact that would govern the next four decades of my life: there is room in the Dunnhaven Ladies' Curling Club for only one shortbread, and it is Janet's. My tin was eaten. It was never mentioned again. I learned my place. I have since brought only cling film, plasters, and a thermos of tea with the correct number of sugars for each woman pre-dissolved in individual portions, which I keep in labelled jars in a small insulated bag, and about which no one has ever commented, not once, in forty years, which is either a compliment or an indictment and I have chosen to receive it as the former because the alternative would require a bitterness I do not have the energy to maintain.

The rink was not much. A low breeze-block building on the Kirkhallam road with a corrugated roof that leaked when it rained, which in that part of Scotland meant it leaked. The ice was maintained by a man called Angus Breck who also maintained the village cricket pitch in summer, which tells you everything you need to know about the quality of both surfaces. The changing rooms smelled of damp and ancient rubber and there was a hand-written sign above the door that said MIND YOUR HEAD, the final D partially obscured by a piece of tape on which someone had written, in different handwriting, SERIOUSLY.

We arrived on a Thursday evening. It was not a social. It was the first round of the East Central Division Four League. Our opponents were Kirkhallam B.

Phyllis, who had by now found her other glasses and read the entry form and understood the situation, said nothing. Phyllis is a woman for whom backing down from a public commitment, even a commitment entered into by accident, even a commitment entered into by optometric failure, is not possible. I have watched her eat an entire plate of kidneys at a Burns Night supper because she'd told the hostess she loved offal—this was a lie; Phyllis does not love offal; Phyllis does not *like* offal; Phyllis, I believe, regards offal with the same cold suspicion most people reserve for foreign policy—but she'd said it and so she ate it, every piece, chewing steadily, the way you might chew through a moral obligation. She showed up at the rink with her stones and her brush and her team of bewildered women and she said, "Right then."

Kirkhallam B fielded three players. A stomach flu had taken their lead and their second, and their skip, a tall woman named Morag whose expression suggested that the flu was the latest in a long sequence of personal betrayals, spent much of the match delivering stones with one hand while clutching her abdomen with the other. Their remaining player, whose name I never learned but who wore glasses thicker than mine, appeared uncertain about which end of the rink they were meant to be aiming for and at one point swept vigorously in the wrong direction.

We won.

Janet's first competitive delivery—her first ever, because she had never held a curling stone before that evening, or indeed expressed any interest in curling, or in sport, or in activity of any kind that could not be performed while seated near a kettle—left her hand at an angle that I can only describe as diagonal to intention. The stone traveled. It did not travel where Janet had aimed it, though where Janet had aimed it was itself unclear, since her delivery stance resembled someone trying to return a borrowed suitcase to a shelf slightly too high to reach. The stone crossed the ice in a wide, grieving arc, struck the first of Kirkhallam's placed stones at its outer edge, which redirected it into a second stone, which nudged a third, and when everything stopped moving three opposing stones had been removed from the house and Janet's stone sat alone in the centre, perfectly placed, spinning gently, as if it had always meant to be there.

Morag from Kirkhallam stared at the ice for a long time. Then she stared at Janet. Then she was sick in a bin.

Nobody celebrated. Phyllis asked if we could stop at the chippy on the way home. We could. We did. I had a fish supper and sat in the back of Phyllis's Cortina and the windows steamed up and we ate in near-silence except for Eileen, who said, "That was all right, actually," and Dot, who said, "My knees are going to regret this," and Phyllis, who said nothing but who, I noticed—because I am always noticing, because noticing is what I do instead of the other things people do—was sitting very still with a chip in her hand and a look on her face, and the look was not triumph and was not embarrassment but was something more complicated, something I didn't have a word for then but which I would learn, over the next four decades, to recognize in all of them, in different light, at different angles, a look that meant: *this is not my life, and yet here I am in it, and it is not altogether terrible.*

We had a team. We had not meant to have a team. Nobody had asked for one. The leaflet was wrong and Phyllis's glasses were wrong and Janet's delivery was—by any biomechanical standard—wrong, and none of it mattered because the thing about a wrong turn is that it takes you somewhere, and somewhere is different from where you were, and where we all were, in 1979, on Thursday evenings, was in our houses, and our houses were—

Well. Maureen, stop. That's not your story to tell. The houses were their own. The houses were fine. The houses were simply places that five women needed, on Thursdays, not to be.

---

The years did what years do, which is to say they accrued.

Every Thursday. Every Thursday the five of them—or four, officially, and me behind the barrier—would arrive at the rink and something would happen on the ice and something else, something different and more true, would happen in the spaces between the ice: in the changing room, in the car park, in the back of whichever vehicle was ferrying us to whichever draughty hall in whichever village had the misfortune of hosting that week's fixture.

I could tell you about the matches. I could tell you that we won the East Central Division Four title in 1980 and the Division Three title in 1982 and that Phyllis was nominated for the Regional Skip of the Year in 1984, an award she did not win but which came with a certificate that she kept in a drawer beneath her tea towels, neither displayed nor discarded, existing in the liminal state Phyllis reserves for compliments. I could tell you about the county championship in 1986—held in a rink in Forfar where the radiators were so powerful that the ice developed a thin film of melt by the fourth end and all the stones ran long and Dot said it was like playing curling in a bath—and I could tell you about the national ranking in 1994 and the special mention in the Scottish Curling Association's annual report, which used the phrase "remarkable consistency" in a way that could have meant either "they are very good" or "they have been doing this for a bewilderingly long time," and knowing the Scottish Curling Association, it meant both.

But that is not what I remember. What I remember—what is sharp, what is individuated, what rises from the long blur with the clarity of something that has happened not once but is happening still—is who made the tea.

The tea rotation was Phyllis's invention, which marks it as simultaneously logical and unassailable. The system was this: on ordinary practice nights, the person who had arrived first made the tea. On match days, the person who had played worst in the previous match made the tea, unless that person was Janet, in which case Eileen made it, because Janet's response to having played badly was to produce shortbread, and you could not have one woman performing two catering functions simultaneously without undermining the entire domestic economy of the team. On days when there had been an argument—and there were arguments; of course there were arguments; five women in close proximity for forty years generate arguments the way a river generates silt, slowly, continuously, depositing layers of grievance that harden into the landscape—on those days, the tea was made by the person who was *not* involved in the argument, which was almost always me, so in practice I made the tea after arguments, which means, in practice, I made a great deal of tea.

There was also the matter of the minibus.

The minibus came later—1990, I think, or 1991, because it was the year Dot's car failed its MOT and Eileen refused to drive any vehicle manufactured before 1985 on the grounds that pre-1985 seatbelts were "decorative." Phyllis drove. Phyllis always drove. This was not discussed; it was *understood,* the way plate tectonics are understood, as a fundamental condition of the earth. Janet rode shotgun, except when she and Phyllis were not speaking, in which case Janet sat in the second row and Eileen sat in the front and the radio was turned to Radio 2 rather than Radio 4, because Radio 4 was Phyllis's preference and Janet's silent exile from the front seat required a corresponding exile from the airwaves, a punishment so precise in its pettiness that I rather admired it, though I would never have said so, because the third alternate does not have opinions about seating arrangements any more than the furniture has opinions about the room.

When Janet and Phyllis were not speaking, Dot sat in the third row with me. Dot would say, "How long this time, do you think?" and I would say, "Hard to say," and Dot would say, "Shortbread by Wednesday," and she was always right. The shortbread was always the ceasefire. It would appear—Janet's tin, the blue one with the Highland cattle on the lid, which she'd had since at least 1983 and which had achieved, through sheer repetition of service, the status of a regimental colour—and it would be offered without comment. Phyllis would take a piece. She would eat it. She would not say thank you, because to say thank you would be to acknowledge that the shortbread was a gesture, and to acknowledge the gesture would be to acknowledge the argument, and to acknowledge the argument would be to introduce into their relationship the catastrophic possibility that things between them could be, and had been, wrong. So Phyllis would eat her shortbread and Janet would close the tin and the minibus would continue to its destination and the radio would return to Radio 4 and the world would be, for the moment, re-established.

The shortbread appeared at other times. It appeared after Dot's mother died, in 1995, offered in the car park of the crematorium without a word.
