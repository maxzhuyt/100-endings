# The Ward of Forking Frequencies

## I.

The hospital thought about dying the way old people did—constantly, involuntarily, in the middle of doing something else.

Mirela Vasile pressed her forehead against the window of the transport van and received the building before she saw it. Three floors of poured concrete cantilevered over a basalt headland, the Oregon coast unraveling in grey ribbons behind it. Seaview Memorial Experimental Care Facility—that was the name on her transfer paperwork. What the building called itself, in the corroded language of its own electrical systems and the cumulative psychic residue of everyone who had suffered inside it, was something closer to *still here, still here, still here*, stuttered like a fluorescent tube that wouldn't fully catch.

She was still two hundred meters out when the chorus hit.

It arrived the way it always did: not as sound but as pressure, a sudden inflation behind her sternum, as though her lungs had been requisitioned to breathe for strangers. The night-shift nurse in the east stairwell was calculating whether she could afford both her daughter's inhaler and the electric bill, and the math wasn't working, and the failure of the math had a taste—copper and dread. A janitor on the second floor was composing, with the slow recursive fury of the deeply wounded, the speech he would give his brother-in-law at Thanksgiving, a speech that had been under construction for eleven years and was now so encrusted with revision that the original offense had been entirely replaced by the pleasure of hating. Two orderlies were having sex in a supply closet, and their thoughts were the simple, stupid heat of two bodies that had found a use for each other in a place where bodies mostly just broke down. An ancient patient on a breathing machine was dreaming about a dog she'd had in 1974, and the dream was more vivid than anything that had happened to her while awake in the last decade, and the dog's name was Brother, and Brother was running.

Mirela took it all in because she had never learned to do otherwise.

Twenty-seven years of involuntary reception. She'd been three years old when her mother realized the child wasn't making things up—wasn't pretending to know what the mailman thought about his wife, wasn't guessing that the preschool teacher cried every day during the eleven-to-eleven-fifteen bathroom break. She'd been seven when the headaches started, the ones that lived behind her left eye like a nail being driven at recreational speed. She'd been fourteen when she stopped going to school because the cafeteria was a kill zone, three hundred adolescent minds broadcasting their anguish at full volume through skulls that hadn't yet learned to muffle anything. By nineteen she'd been diagnosed, misdiagnosed, rediagnosed, medicated into a pleasant fog, unmedicated into a screaming clarity, and finally referred—through the quiet network of physicians who had seen things they couldn't publish about—to the experimental ward at Seaview.

The van stopped. The driver—*four more hours then SportsCenter and a beer, God let there be no traffic on 101*—opened her door.

Mirela stepped out and the ocean was everywhere, not just audible but *felt*, a bass vibration that came up through the cracked asphalt and into her knees. The building's west wall was streaked with salt rime. A section of rain gutter dangled from the roofline like a dislocated finger. Somewhere inside, a machine was beeping its two-note cardiac tattoo, and somewhere else a different machine was humming at a frequency just below the threshold of hearing, and the two sounds were not the same thing, but from where Mirela stood they merged into a single pulse that she filed, without examination, under *building noise*.

This was her first mistake, though she would not recognize it as such for three days.

Inside, the receiving nurse—Helen, fifty-four, hip pain she was ignoring, a mind like a filing cabinet that had been left open in a rainstorm—walked her through the intake procedures with the efficiency of someone who had processed unusual admissions before and had learned that the fastest route through strangeness was bureaucracy.

"Ward C is on the third floor. Four other patients currently in residence. You'll have the bed by the window."

"How much do they know about—"

"They know you're a telepath. They know you can't turn it off. Two of them think that's interesting, one thinks it's invasive, and one hasn't expressed an opinion." Helen glanced at her clipboard. "The one who thinks it's invasive is Mr. Okoro. He'll warm up. He's like that with everyone."

They took the elevator because the east stairwell was still colonized by the nurse with the inhaler math. The elevator smelled of bleach and old electricity. Its cables sang a metallic note that Mirela's inner ear translated into something almost linguistic—a long vowel, sustained and patient.

*Still here. Still here.*

Ward C was a long room with five beds separated by curtains that had once been green and were now the color of resignation. The window at the far end faced the ocean, and the glass was smeared enough to turn the view into an impressionist study: grey sky, grey water, the faintest suggestion of a horizon where the two greys argued about which was which. The overhead lights were the type that buzzed at 60 hertz, and three of the five fixtures had developed independent flickers, so the room's illumination had an arrhythmic, cardiac quality—*bright bright dim bright dim dim bright*—that made Mirela feel as though the ward itself had a pulse.

Four beds were occupied.

The nearest mind hit her like a wall of summer air: dense, shimmering, hot. Not thoughts exactly but *thermal potential*, as though the brain generating the signal was running at a temperature that would cook a normal neural architecture. Beneath the heat, held down by what Mirela recognized as years of practiced suppression, emotions moved like magma under a crust—slow, massive, and capable of surfacing without warning. The body attached to this mind was a lean, dark-skinned woman in her mid-thirties, sitting cross-legged on her bed with a deck of cards and an expression of cultivated disinterest that Mirela could now see was as functional as a blast shield.

The second mind was a corridor.

There was no other way to describe it. Where the pyrokinetic's thoughts were volcanic and contained, this person's consciousness extended *backward* through time like a hallway with no end wall, door after door after door opening onto rooms that were not memories in any personal sense but *inherited cognitions*—the sensory experiences of ancestors, layered and catalogued, stretching through centuries in a chain of lived moments that no single human life could have accumulated. Mirela caught flashes: a woman gutting fish on a shore she didn't recognize, the year approximately 1630 based on the clothing; a man reading Torah by candlelight, the parchment old, his lips moving; a child watching a city burn, the city possibly Thessaloniki, the child's grief so vast and old it had become geological. The body generating this impossible archive was a slight, grey-haired man in his sixties, sitting up in bed with a book—an actual physical book, a paperback history of the Ottoman Empire—and the irony of that choice radiated from his mind with the dry, self-aware humor of someone who had read the primary sources through his grandmother's grandmother's eyes.

The third mind was *fast*.

Not intelligent—or not only intelligent—but temporally compressed, as though the brain producing the signal was running its clock cycle at ten or twenty times the normal rate. Thoughts arrived in clusters, half-formed, already superseded by the next cluster before the first had resolved. Intentions trailed behind decisions like exhaust behind a jet. The effect, to Mirela's telepathy, was of a mind perpetually outrunning itself, living thirty seconds ahead of its own body, thoughts arriving pre-thought in bursts of fizzing, carbonated cognition. The body was young—early twenties, male, Black, with a face that should have been twenty-two but kept slipping, its features subtly rearranging themselves as though aging and de-aging were happening in alternating frames. He was eating an orange. He was already thinking about the next orange.

The fourth mind was a closed door.

Not silent—Mirela had never encountered true silence—but *self-referential* in a way that made her telepathy slide off it like water off waxed fabric. This person's thoughts were about their *own* thoughts, which were about their own body, which was about its own cells, which were about their own integrity. A recursive loop of self-monitoring so complete that it left almost nothing for an external telepath to grasp. It was, Mirela realized with a chill of professional respect, the cognitive signature of a regenerator—someone whose biological gift was so total that even their consciousness had turned inward, becoming a closed system dedicated entirely to self-maintenance. The body was unremarkable: a pale, heavyset person of indeterminate age and gender, lying on their side, watching Mirela with eyes that gave nothing and took everything in.

Four extraordinary minds. Four flavors of loneliness.

And beneath them all, *through* them all, the way a carrier wave threads through the music it transmits—

Warmth.

Not emotional warmth. Not the warmth of the pyrokinetic's banked fire. Something lower, more fundamental, like the background radiation of the room itself, a hum so deeply embedded in the ward's ambient frequency that it registered not as a signal but as the medium through which all other signals traveled. It was in the fluorescent flicker. It was in the sixty-cycle buzz of the lights. It was in the elevator cables and the rain gutter and the gaps where the window caulking had failed and the salt air came through. Mirela felt it the way you feel the temperature of water you've been swimming in for too long—absolutely, invisibly, as the condition of existence rather than a quality of it.

She assumed it was hope. That was logical. A hospital ward full of frightened people with incurable conditions, hoping to be cured—that would generate a background frequency. A warmth. She had felt versions of it in every hospital she'd ever entered.

She set her bag on the bed by the window and began to unpack.

The ocean was loud. The lights were flickering. The building was saying *still here still here still here*, and so was something else, and Mirela, who heard everything, did not hear the difference.

## II.

The pyrokinetic's name was Adaeze Okoro—no relation to the Mr. Okoro that the intake nurse had mentioned, because there was no Mr. Okoro; Helen had simply assumed that the person who found telepathy invasive must be male, and Adaeze's private fury about this assumption was a small, hot jewel that Mirela discovered on the first morning and learned not to mention.

"You're staring," Adaeze said, without looking up from her cards. She was playing solitaire. She was cheating.

"I wasn't."

"Your eyes do this thing. They go flat. Like you're listening to something I can't hear." Adaeze laid down a red seven on a black eight. Her thoughts said: *She's already in my head. Everyone's always already in my head. The last telepath I met cried when she touched me. Said it felt like standing next to a furnace. Good. Stay out of the furnace.*

"I can't help it," Mirela said.

"I know." Adaeze placed a black six on the red seven. Her thoughts said: *That's what makes it invasive.* Then, immediately, layered over the resentment like butter on burned toast: *She looks tired. She looks like she hasn't slept right in years. I know what that's like. Don't soften. Don't you dare.*

It was always like this. The gap between the spoken and the thought, the performed and the felt. Mirela had spent her entire life in that gap. It was the loneliest place in the world—not because people were dishonest, but because they were *multiple*. Everyone contained at least two people: the one they showed and the one they narrated to themselves. Mirela had access to both, which meant she had access to neither, because the truth about a person was not in the curated exterior or the private interior but in the *tension between them*, and that tension was a place no amount of telepathy could reach.

"Breakfast is at seven," said the ancestral-memory carrier from the next bed. His name was Lev Mendelsohn, and his mind was, at this particular moment, running a background process that cross-referenced the hospital's breakfast menu with the dietary practices of his maternal line going back eight generations. "Ten years ago the eggs here were adequate. Now they are powered."

"Powdered," the accelerated-metabolism patient corrected from across the room. His name was Dion Jeffries, and he had already eaten his breakfast tray, Lev's untouched toast, and a banana he'd smuggled from somewhere. His thoughts were moving too fast for Mirela to track individual words—instead she got *impressions*, like trying to read a book being riffled past her eyes: hunger, hunger, boredom, hunger, a flash of something that might have been fear, hunger again. He looked roughly nineteen this morning. His intake photo, pinned to the clipboard at the foot of his bed, showed a twenty-four-year-old. "Powdered. Not powered."

"I know the word," Lev said, with the exquisite patience of a man who remembered—through the body of a great-great-uncle—being corrected on his Yiddish by a six-year-old in Vilnius in 1898. "I was making a joke about the quality."

Dion's thoughts, fizzing: *Was that a joke? Scan. Process. It was. The old man is funny. File that. What's for second breakfast? Is second breakfast a thing they do here? It should be.*

"It wasn't a very good joke," said the regenerator.

Everyone turned. The regenerator almost never spoke. Their name was Sam Acheson, and they lay on their side with the stillness of someone who had long ago realized that the most efficient use of energy was no use at all. Mirela had spent the first six hours trying to read them and had gotten nothing but that maddening recursive loop: *cells checking cells checking cells*. Now, for the first time, a thought surfaced that wasn't self-referential:

*If I can make them laugh, they'll stop looking at me like I'm already dead.*

"It was an excellent joke," Lev said mildly. "In 1922, my grandfather told it to a grocer in Odessa and the man laughed so hard he gave him a free herring."

"That didn't happen," Adaeze said.

Lev's mind unfurled like a scroll: the grocer's face, the smell of brine and sawdust, the herring wrapped in newspaper, the specific quality of winter light through a shop window in a city that no longer existed in the form his grandfather had known it. "It did. I have the receipt."

"You have a *memory* of a receipt."

"In my family, that is the same thing."

The argument that followed—about the epistemological status of inherited memory, which Dion attempted to adjudicate by eating another banana, which Adaeze ended by accurately observing that none of them were qualified to discuss epistemology before coffee—was the first moment Mirela felt something other than the low, constant ache of reception. She felt, improbably, the desire to stay.

It didn't last. On the ward's second day at full occupancy, the friction of proximity replaced the warmth of novelty.
