# The Portrait of Frau Gergen

## I.

The building at Heumühlgasse 14 was dying in the manner of all Viennese apartment buildings, which is to say: with dignity, with mold, and with an absolute refusal to admit that anything was wrong. The plaster along the eastern stairwell had been separating from the lath since the autumn of 1927, and by the winter of 1931 the separation had achieved the character of a philosophical position — the plaster was not falling, it was *withdrawing*, curling away from the wall in long, languid scrolls that hung like the pages of a book no one had been meant to read. Behind the plaster, the lath was dark with moisture. Behind the lath, the bricks were cold. Behind the bricks was another flat, another life, another set of problems indistinguishable from the first.

This was the building's essential quality: its *thinness*. Four stories of load-bearing walls that had been, in the optimism of 1863, advertised as solid masonry, and which had since proved to be something less — not hollow, exactly, but porous, the way a lung is porous, so that whatever moved through one chamber inevitably seeped into the next. Sound traveled through Heumühlgasse 14 without regard for lease agreements. The cough that belonged to Herr Löwenthal on the second floor — a thick, chesty thing, a cough with real architecture — arrived nightly in the bedroom of Frau Dennler across the hall, who received it with the grim intimacy of a woman who had long ago stopped being surprised by the proximity of other people's bodies. The arguments between the Pavelkas on the ground floor rose through the building's skeleton like sap, so that by the time they reached the third floor they had been stripped of their words and reduced to a pure, thin vibration — a tuning fork of domestic misery that resonated in the floorboards and set the water trembling in a glass that sat on the nightstand of Herr Brandstätter, a retired civil servant who noticed the trembling sometimes and sometimes did not.

The stairwell smelled of cabbage. It had smelled of cabbage since a date no living tenant could specify, and the smell had achieved the permanence of an architectural feature — it was not *in* the building, it was *of* the building, in the way that lime was of the mortar and iron was of the railings. Layered beneath the cabbage, or perhaps woven through it, were the lesser scents — the turpentine that drifted down from the top floor, the boiled wool that rose from the basement where Frau Dennler sometimes washed blankets in a copper tub, the particular sweet-sick smell of Herr Löwenthal's cough drops, which were made with an extract of horehound that he purchased, eight at a time, from the pharmacy on Margaretenstraße. The stairwell held these scents the way a riverbed holds the memory of many waters, each indistinguishable from the next, all flowing toward the same drain.

The tenants.

On the ground floor: the Pavelkas, Silesian by origin, four children, the oldest of whom was twelve and already carried himself with the exhausted authority of a man who has been asked to manage something far beyond his competence. Herr Pavelka repaired shoes. Frau Pavelka repaired Herr Pavelka. The arrangement was sustainable but joyless, and their arguments — which were about money, which is to say about everything — had, over five years, worn a groove in the building's consciousness so deep that when they were silent the other tenants felt not relief but unease, the way one feels uneasy when a clock that has been ticking stops.

Across from the Pavelkas, the ground-floor flat on the left had been vacant since September, when its previous occupant, an elderly tailor named Wiesinger, had died in his chair with a needle still pinched between his thumb and forefinger, a detail that the building had absorbed and would not release — it circulated through the tenants' conversations like a coin through an economy, gaining value with each exchange, until the needle between the dead man's fingers had become the most vivid image any of them possessed of the boundary between life and death: that you could be mending a seam and then you could be nothing, and the seam would remain unmended, and the needle would remain.

Second floor: Herr Löwenthal, the cougher, a bachelor of sixty-three who had once been a clerk in the Finance Ministry and who now sustained himself on a pension so small it could be expressed in a single column of figures. He was kind in the manner of men who have been thoroughly defeated — reflexively, without hope of reciprocity. Across from him, Frau Dennler, a widow of indeterminate age and tremendous, quiet fury, who cleaned offices at night and slept by day and existed in a temporal realm so removed from the building's daytime life that seeing her on the stairs at noon produced, in the other tenants, a mild feeling of dislocation, as though one of the building's walls had detached itself and gone for a walk.

Third floor: Herr Brandstätter, the retired civil servant, and his water glass. He had decorated his flat with a meticulousness that suggested not taste but terror — every object placed with the precision of a man who believes that if his external world is sufficiently ordered, his internal world might follow suit. It had not worked. Across from him, the flat stood empty, as it had for a year, its door a reminder that the building was slowly exhaling its population, one tenant at a time, into death or cheaper accommodation.

Fourth floor, the top of the building, where the ceilings sloped to meet the roof and the dormer windows admitted a gray light that seemed to arrive already exhausted by its journey through the Viennese sky: Elzbieta Rosenbaum.

She was eating a heel of bread. She ate it without butter, because there was no butter, and without hurry, because there was no reason to hurry. The bread was two days old and had achieved the consistency of something between food and furniture — dense, architectural, requiring the full commitment of the jaw. She ate it at the table by the window, which was also her palette table, which was also her letter-writing desk, though she had not written a letter in some months, there being no one left to whom she could write without the act feeling like a request, and she had made a private policy of requesting nothing.

The paintings — there were forty-seven of them, the accumulated sediment of nine years of uninterrupted work — were stacked against the walls of the flat's single room, all of them turned so that only their backs were visible: pale wooden stretchers, some warped, some bearing the faint ghost of pigment that had bled through the canvas over time. She had not destroyed them. Destruction would have required a conviction she did not possess — the conviction that the paintings were bad, or unworthy, or that the world was wrong to have ignored them. She did not believe any of these things with sufficient force to act. She believed, instead, something more corrosive: that the paintings existed, that they were what they were, and that the world's indifference to them was neither a judgment on the paintings nor on the world but simply a *fact*, the way gravity was a fact, requiring no opinion.

Her parents were dead. Her father had died in 1924, her mother in 1926, both in this same flat, in the bed that was now her bed, which she had inherited along with the lease and a porcelain clock that no longer kept time. She had been their only child. They had believed in her talent with the particular fervor of Jewish parents who understood that the world would offer their daughter few enough opportunities and that art, at least, could not be taken away — a belief that the twentieth century was in the process of correcting, though neither of them had survived long enough to witness the correction in its fullest expression.

Elzbieta was thirty-six. She had applied to seven galleries. Three had not responded. Two had responded with the kind of courteous rejection that is designed to be indistinguishable from encouragement, so that the rejected party may choose to interpret it either way and in so doing reveal themselves as either optimist or realist. One had asked to see slides, had received the slides, and had never written again — a silence that occupied a specific, well-worn groove in Elzbieta's mind, a groove she returned to at odd hours the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. The seventh gallery had accepted three paintings for a group exhibition that had then been canceled due to what the gallery owner described as "circumstances," a word that covered, in 1928, everything from bankruptcy to antisemitism to simple cowardice, and which Elzbieta had understood, correctly, as covering all three.

She finished the bread. She wiped the crumbs from the table into her palm and from her palm into her mouth, not out of starvation — she was poor but not yet starving — but out of a reflexive frugality that she had absorbed from her mother, who had absorbed it from the Galician necessity of an earlier century, and which persisted in Elzbieta's habits the way the smell of cabbage persisted in the stairwell: not as a choice but as a condition.

Through the wall to her left, whose thinness she had long ago ceased to notice the way one ceases to notice a chronic pain, she could hear the distinctive settling sounds of the unoccupied flat on the third floor — the creak of floorboards without feet, the tick of pipes without use and without purpose — and for a moment the building held its breath and was still, and the stillness had the quality of a system in equilibrium, every element balanced, every loss accounted for, every small ember of private misery burning at precisely the rate required to sustain itself without warmth or illumination.

Elzbieta took up a palette knife and began to scrape the dried pigment from a surface she had been meaning to reuse. The pigment came away in thin curls. Cadmium yellow. Viridian. A brownish red that had once been the color of a woman's cheek in a portrait no one had sat for, painted from memory, from the composite memory of faces seen on the Naschmarkt, and which had been one of her better pieces, she thought, though "better" was a word that had ceased to mean anything precise in the solitude of her practice, where there was no external calibration, no audience against which to measure the truth of her seeing. She scraped. The curls of pigment fell. Through the floorboards, very faintly, she heard the youngest Pavelka child begin to cry, and the sound rose through the building's body the way all sounds rose through it — vertically, indiscriminately, without hope of answer — and arrived in her room as a thin, disembodied keening that might have been anything: a child, a cat, a draft through a broken pane, the building itself grieving in the only register it knew.

## II.

She arrived on the first of November, which was All Saints' Day, a detail that the building would later absorb into its store of unexamined coincidences. The moving van — an optimistic term for the swayback cart and indifferent horse that bore her possessions — came at ten in the morning, and by ten-fifteen the Pavelka children had stationed themselves on the stairwell landing to watch, because a new arrival was an event of the first order in a building where the prevailing direction of movement was *out*.

The possessions came in carried by two men who had the look of men paid insufficiently for the weight they bore: a sofa of dark green velvet, worn at the arms to a paler shade that suggested decades of elbows; a table of solid oak, scarred by use; eight boxes of books, strapped with leather belts; a standing lamp with a copper shade that caught the stairwell's dim light and threw it back in a warm, reddish coin against the wall; linens, kitchen things, a porcelain hen that served some purpose no one could discern; and, last, borne with a care that distinguished it from everything else, a framed photograph in brown paper, which she carried herself, holding it against her chest with both arms, the way one holds something that is both fragile and necessary.

Frau Gergen.

She was somewhere between sixty and seventy — a range that, in the Vienna of 1931, covered a vast territory of possible experience, from the last complacencies of Empire to the first terrors of Republic. She was not tall. She was not thin. She had a body that suggested utility rather than vanity — sturdy calves, broad hands, a bosom that occupied space with the frank authority of a geographical feature. Her face was wide, pink-cheeked, creased at the eyes with the particular seams that come from decades of sustained smiling, or sustained squinting, or both. Her hair was gray, pulled back, pinned with the efficiency of a woman who had decided, at some point in the previous century, that her hair was a problem to be solved once and permanently, and had solved it. She wore a wool coat of dark blue, a hat that was neither fashionable nor unfashionable but merely *present*, and shoes that were polished — a detail that the Pavelka children noted without understanding its significance, and that Frau Dennler, ascending the stairs after a night of cleaning offices, noted with the full weight of her understanding: this was a woman for whom appearance was still a daily discipline, which meant either habitual pride or habitual anticipation of scrutiny, and these were not the same thing, though they produced the same shoes.

"Good morning," Frau Gergen said to the Pavelka children. She said it to each of them individually, bending slightly, finding their eyes. The oldest, Tomáš, performed his customary surveillance and found no threat. The youngest, who was four and named Alžběta — a name that rhymed imperfectly with the painter's name four floors above, though neither of them would ever discover this rhyme, which existed only in the building's passive, accumulating awareness of coincidence — the youngest offered her hand, and Frau Gergen took it with a gravity that honored the gesture.

"Is this your building?" Frau Gergen asked the four-year-old.

"Yes," said the four-year-old, who believed this to be true.

"Then I am your guest," said Frau Gergen, and the four-year-old accepted this with the magnanimity of a sovereign, and the interaction was complete, and it had been, in its way, perfect — the precise amount of attention, the precise tone, the precise elevation of the child's authority that transforms an encounter between a stranger and a small person from an awkwardness into a ceremony.

Word of her moved through the building in the manner of all information in Heumühlgasse 14: not announced but *seeped*, carried by the same channels that carried the smell of cabbage and the sound of Herr Löwenthal's cough. Herr Pavelka, returning from his workshop at midday, learned of her from his wife, who described her as "decent, seems clean" — the two cardinal virtues of the tenement. Herr Löwenthal, descending for his afternoon constitutional, passed her open door and saw her unpacking books — *books*, many books, an unmistakable marker of a certain kind of person — and reported this to Herr Brandstätter on the third floor, who received the information by marking the date on his calendar, not because the information required documentation but because Herr Brandstätter marked everything on his calendar, this being the nature of his condition. Frau Dennler, who was trying to sleep and had been disturbed by the sounds of furniture being placed and replaced below her, descended in her bathrobe with the intention of establishing, firmly and early, the hours during which noise was unacceptable, and returned twenty minutes later, still in her bathrobe, holding a cup of chamomile tea that Frau Gergen had pressed into her hands, her complaint not so much dismissed as *dissolved*, the way aspirin dissolves in water — completely, leaving no residue and no memory of the original solid.

Within three days, Frau Gergen had accomplished an act of such radical improbability that the building did not so much recognize it as *hallucinate* it: she had learned every tenant's name.

Not merely learned — *used*, deployed with a specificity that transformed the stairwell from a transit corridor into something approaching a village street.
