# Everything Accounted For

---

The thing about dried apricots is that nobody respects them, which is exactly the problem with most things that keep you alive. Two hundred and twelve calories per hundred grams, which doesn't sound like salvation until you're at 4,400 meters and your blood is running thick as library paste and somebody — Cal, it was Cal, it's always Cal — has forgotten to account for the additional day because he read the Soviet-era topographic survey as showing a passable saddle at elevation when in fact what he was looking at was a cartographic artifact from the 1961 triangulation series, which Yevgeny could have told him if Yevgeny were in the habit of telling people things, which he is not, because Yevgeny learned forty years ago on the Kazakh steppe that the fastest way to get people to reveal themselves is to let them believe you don't understand what they're saying, which, fine, is his prerogative, but the consequence is that I am standing on a col in the eastern Pamirs with nine people who need to eat and a pack that I loaded for seven days, not eight, because I trusted Cal's itinerary, which — and I want to be very precise here because precision is the only thing between us and the kind of disaster that gets written about by people who weren't there — I have not trusted since the Yucatán in 2014, but I trusted this time because the survey data looked clean and because Cal showed me the Soviet maps with such specific confidence that I thought, well, maybe this once he's actually done the cartographic reconciliation himself instead of waving at it with his beautiful hands and saying "Petra will sort the logistics."

Petra will sort the logistics.

Petra has sorted the logistics for twelve years across six continents and the thanks Petra has received could be inscribed on a single dried apricot with room left over for a haiku.

But that's not the — no. The apricots. Two hundred and twelve calories per hundred grams. Light. Dense enough to justify the pack weight. High in potassium, which matters above 4,000 meters more than anyone who hasn't carried a body down a mountain can appreciate, because potassium regulates cardiac rhythm and cardiac rhythm is what goes sideways first at altitude, not breathing, whatever the adventure memoirs tell you. Breathing is the symptom. The heart is the problem. The heart is always the problem, but I'm talking about electrolytes.

I should make a list. I always make a list. That's what I do while other people are making discoveries: I make lists of every object standing between the discovery and death, and then I carry most of those objects on my back, and then I watch from the supply tent while National Geographic shoots Cal standing on the lip of whatever he's accidentally stumbled into with his chin tilted eleven degrees toward the light because he once had a portrait photographer tell him that eleven degrees was his architecture, which is a thing a portrait photographer said to a man who subsequently let his chin determine his compass heading for a decade and a half.

Pack contents, Pamir expedition, day one, as loaded by me, verified by me, adjusted by me after Cal removed the backup water purification tablets to make room for a supplemental camera lens:

- Apricots, dried, Uzbek, 2.3 kg
- Rice, short-grain, 4 kg (not basmati, never basmati above 3,000 meters — the grain structure doesn't hold at a rolling boil that tops out at 87°C because the boiling point drops roughly 1°C per 300 meters of elevation gain, which means your basmati turns to paste while your short-grain still has tooth, and if you think this is a minor distinction then you have never tried to keep nine people from murdering each other on day six of a high-altitude survey when the only comfort left is textural integrity in a bowl of rice)
- Lentils, red, 3 kg
- Lentils, green, 1.5 kg (the green are for me; nobody else eats them; I have been bringing my own lentils to Cal Buchanan's expeditions since 2013, when our Peruvian resupply failed and I ate nothing but refined sugar and spite for two days)
- Salt, 800g, divided into four sealed portions because I have seen salt destroy a pack in the rain and I have no interest in seeing it again
- Black tea, loose, 500g
- Powdered milk, 1 kg
- Ghee, clarified, 750ml, in a screw-top steel canister that I machined myself after the incident in the Altay when the commercial container failed at sub-zero and I spent forty minutes scraping frozen butterfat off a sleeping bag with a trowel, which Cal later told an audience at the Explorers Club was "one of the funniest things that's ever happened to me," emphasis mine, to *him*, as though the butterfat and the trowel and the sleeping bag and the forty minutes in minus-twenty wind chill were experiences he had rather than experiences I had while he watched from inside the tent asking if I could also bring him some of that tea

I should tell you about the first time. That's what's underneath all of this, isn't it — the first time, the Yucatán, 2012, which is when Cal and I became Cal-and-Petra, or rather Cal-and-his-logistics-person, or rather Cal-and-the-woman-whose-name-appears-in-the-acknowledgments-section-in-a-paragraph-that-also-thanks-the-helicopter-company.

The Yucatán: Cal was twenty-nine. I was twenty-seven. He was finishing his doctorate at Chicago, I was finishing mine at Michigan — same field, different planets, because Cal's planet had a famous advisor and a jaw that made department secretaries forget their filing systems and mine had a 3.97 GPA and a filing system so good that famous advisors kept asking my advisor to "lend" me for their fieldwork. That's the word. *Lend.* Like I was a socket wrench with good Spanish.

Cal had a lead on a possible secondary site connected to the Calakmul complex. The lead was based on LIDAR data he'd obtained through his advisor, who'd obtained it through a friend at NASA, which is how Cal's world worked — everything arrived to him through other people's effort like fruit falling from a tree into the lap of a man who would later describe the fruit to an audience as something he'd "hunted down." His proposal was eight pages, which I know because I read it. His logistics plan was half a page, which I know because I lived it.

There was no potable water within four kilometers of the proposed base camp. I know this because I discovered it the first morning when I walked to the coordinates Cal had marked as the water source and found a silted limestone depression that had been dry since approximately the late Holocene. Cal had GPS'd his water source from Google Earth. From *Google Earth.* At a resolution that could not distinguish between standing water and the shadow of a ceiba tree. Which is how I ended up hiking eight kilometers round-trip every morning with thirty liters of water on my back while Cal ate the breakfast I'd made and reviewed the LIDAR printouts I'd organized and then set off into the jungle along the transect I'd flagged and subsequently fell — literally fell, boot caught on a root, full ground-impact, camera swinging — into a sinkhole that opened onto a previously unrecorded cave system containing what turned out to be Late Classic period ceramics with intact pigmentation, which was genuinely significant, I won't pretend it wasn't, the glazework alone was worth a monograph, but the discovery was made by gravity and a root and the fact that I had cleared that transect the day before so that Cal could walk through primary jungle as though it were a department hallway.

The photo that ran in *Archaeology* showed Cal at the sinkhole edge, headlamp on, face tilted — eleven degrees, even then — with the caption "Dr. Cal Buchanan at the entrance to the Calakmul South cave system, 2012." I am in the photo. If you look at the bottom-left corner, you can see my left hand holding the rope that is keeping Cal from falling into the sinkhole a second time. My left hand. Disembodied. Supporting the full weight of a man and his discovery like some kind of competent ghost.

I should not have gone back. Everyone knows this except me. Lena knows this — Lena who has a doctorate in historical cartography and an unrequited devotion to Cal Buchanan that has survived six expeditions, three public humiliations, and the time in Ethiopia when she redrew the survey grid from memory after the originals were lost in a flash flood and Cal thanked her by saying "good, now we're back on track" and then turned to a camera crew and explained the survey methodology as though he'd invented trigonometry.

Lena. If I'm making a list of people — and I am always making a list of people, filed under "reliable," "unreliable," "will panic," "will not panic but will become useless in a different way," and "Desmond" — then Lena goes under "reliable when not gazing at Cal across a campfire like a medieval astronomer who has just noticed a new star and intends to die of it."

Lena was on the Yucatán trip. The second one, 2014, which was the resupply failure I mentioned, the sugar-and-spite period. She was also on Ethiopia (2015), the Altay (2016), Oman (2018), and now the Pamirs (2024). She is the only person besides me who has been on more than four of Cal's expeditions, and the reason is the same as mine, which is that we are both addicted to something we can't name and the withdrawal symptoms feel worse than the disease, though I suspect Lena would call it love and I would call it — I would call it —

Red lentil soup, high-altitude preparation, serves six to nine depending on who has an appetite and who has been vomiting from altitude sickness, which on any given Buchanan expedition is at least two people by day three:

Soak one cup red lentils for thirty minutes if you have the water to spare, which you probably don't, in which case skip soaking and add ten minutes to cooking time and accept the slightly chalky texture as the cost of doing business on a mountain. Heat ghee — real ghee, not the hydrogenated abomination that Desmond brought to Ethiopia insisting it was "basically the same," Desmond who would not know the Maillard reaction if it caramelized his eyebrows, Desmond who is the money, always the money, sits on the board of three foundations and chairs a fourth and has never set foot on an expedition site without immediately asking where the lavatory is as though we are going to direct him to a porcelain fixture behind the second moraine — heat ghee in a pressure-adjusted pot (you must reduce your expected pressure by roughly 15% per 1,000 meters above sea level, which changes the seal dynamics, which is why I carry my own gaskets, which is why Desmond calls me "the gasket woman," which is why I imagine Desmond falling into crevasses more often than is probably healthy).

Heat ghee. Add cumin seeds — I carry whole, never ground, because ground cumin oxidizes in ten days and tastes like dust by day twelve and if I'm going to eat dust I'd prefer it to be the honest mineral dust of whatever godforsaken mountain Cal's chin has pointed us toward rather than the metabolic decay of a spice that deserved better. When the cumin crackles, add one diced onion if available, reconstituted dried onion if not. Turmeric, half-teaspoon. The turmeric is not for flavor. The turmeric is because curcumin is an anti-inflammatory and above 4,000 meters every joint in your body stages a small insurrection and you take your allies where you find them. Add the lentils. Add water — calculate 3:1 by volume and then add slightly more because the evaporation rate at altitude is aggressive and unforgiving, much like me. Boil. Reduce. Stir when you can. Salt at the end, never before, because salt toughens the lentil skins during cooking, which is a fact I have told Cal eleven times across six continents and which he has failed to retain even once, which I mention not out of bitterness but as an objective measure of the man's relationship to information that does not directly concern his own glory.

The soup, when it's right, is the color of last light on sandstone. I've made it at sea level in my apartment in Ann Arbor and it isn't the same. It needs the thin air. It needs the altitude and the exhaustion and the particular quality of silence you get above the tree line when the wind drops, which isn't really silence at all but the sound of the atmosphere admitting that it's running out of itself. In those moments, with the soup, with the team hunched and quiet and too tired to perform their personalities, I have felt something that I think other people call peace but which I experience as the temporary absence of the need to make a list.

Those moments last about nine seconds before someone needs something.

Where was I. The Pamirs. The personnel.

Desmond St. Clare: financier, board chair, expedition funder, lavatory inquirer. Desmond has the body of a man who played rugby at a mid-tier public school in 1987 and has been metabolically coasting on the memory ever since. He sweats in a way that seems theological, as though his pores are in ongoing negotiation with a deity about the fundamental fairness of physical exertion. He has funded five of Cal's expeditions and his reward is that Cal laughs at his jokes and mentions his foundation in interviews and occasionally lets him hold an artifact for a photograph, which Desmond then uses as his profile picture on LinkedIn, which is where Desmond conducts what he calls "the real expedition — the expedition for capital," which is a thing he has said out loud, to other humans, without apparent shame.

Desmond is on the Pamir expedition. Desmond is always on the expedition. You don't get the money without the man and you don't get the man without the lavatory question and a minimum two hours per day during which he is functionally inert and must be worked around like weather.

Lena Kowalczyk: cartographer, PhD (historical cartography, Jagiellonian, 2013), can redraw a 1:50,000 topographic survey in her sleep, has in fact done this, literally done this — in the Altay she woke at 2 a.m. and sleepwalked to the work table and corrected a contour interval error in the survey draft and went back to bed and did not remember it in the morning and I only know because I was awake, I am always awake, checking provisions, checking weather, checking the oxygen saturation of sleeping teammates with a pulse oximeter I carry in my vest pocket because I have seen what happens when you trust altitude to announce itself and what happens is someone stops breathing in a way that looks like sleep until it isn't.

Lena is in love with Cal. This is not speculation. This is gravitational observation. She orbits him. At base camp her chair migrates toward his throughout the evening at a rate of approximately four centimeters per hour, which I have measured, not because I am a lunatic but because I was testing the stability of the ground surface for equipment placement and Lena's chair kept appearing in my survey area. In Ethiopia she volunteered to do a predawn reconnaissance of a wadi crossing specifically because Cal mentioned he liked watching the sunrise from the eastern ridge, and she positioned herself on that ridge at 5:47 a.m. as though she had happened upon it by chance, except that I had seen her study the terrain map the night before with protractor and straightedge to calculate the optimal sightline, which is the most rigorous cartographic work she did that entire trip and it was in the service of being backlit.
