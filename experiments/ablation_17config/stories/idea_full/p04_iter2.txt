My name is Dolores Muñoz. I am the backup scorer for the Marywood Lane Wreckers, Lackawanna Recreational League, and we have finished last for three consecutive seasons, which is actually difficult to do when you consider that two of the other teams in the league have been functionally disbanded for over a year and only show up to avoid forfeiting their lane reservations.

I should explain what a backup scorer does, which is nothing, because electronic scoring has made the position obsolete since approximately 1991. I attend. I sit in the molded plastic seat behind the console and I keep my own score in a composition notebook because I do not trust the machine, and because I need something to do with my hands during the hours I cannot sleep, which is most of them.

Let me inventory the team.

Gary Potoczak. Captain. Retired HVAC technician, sixty-one years old, built like a man who once moved refrigerators for a living and now moves nothing but still carries the shape of it. His mustache is load-bearing. I mean that architecturally. Remove it and the whole structure of his face would shift. Gary bowls a 133 average, which is respectable in the way that showing up to a burning building with a glass of water is respectable — technically a response, but short of the event's requirements. He has been captain of this team for nine years and has never once raised his voice. He does not need to. He has the authority of a man who has seen the insides of every furnace in Lackawanna County and knows where the heat comes from.

Danny Brucato. Danny teaches eighth-grade earth science at West Scranton Intermediate and moonlights as a bar trivia host at two different establishments on alternating Thursdays. He knows the capital of every country that existed in 1987 and none of the ones that formed after. His bowling stance looks like a man trying to return something to a store that's already closed — you can see the resignation beginning in his shoulders before the ball ever leaves his hand, this full-body anticipation of refusal, and yet he approaches the line every time with a small preliminary hop that I think he believes is jaunty. He averages 114.

Teddy Swiak. The back brace. I have told you about Teddy.

Phil Duda. Phil is the one who whispers to the ball. He cradles it near his mouth the way you'd hold a phone when you don't want anyone to hear your conversation, and he says something to it — I have never been able to make out the words, and I have tried, including once with a directional microphone I ordered off the internet for this purpose which turned out to be a regular microphone in a misleading housing. Phil also whispers to his truck. He whispers to his coffeemaker. He whispers to his ex-wife's voicemail, which he still calls on purpose every few months just to hear the outgoing message. I know this because I was awake at 2 AM on a Wednesday in March and saw him sitting in his truck in the Idle Hour parking lot, phone pressed to his ear, whispering. I did not record this. Some things I let be.

Phil works at the Geisinger distribution warehouse and has the largest hands on the team, which should be an advantage but is not, because Phil's hands are large the way that a house can be large and still poorly designed — there is a lot of space but nothing is where it should be.

Ronnie Cuda. Youngest on the team, thirty-eight, which only counts as young in the Lackawanna Recreational League. Ronnie manages a Verizon store in the Viewmont Mall and has the overly cheerful demeanor of a man who has learned to perform enthusiasm as a professional obligation and now cannot turn it off. He is the only member of the team who high-fives after a spare, and the resulting contact — his hand meeting the reluctant hands of men who do not high-five — produces a sound I can only describe as upholstered.

And me. Dolores. I sit behind the console. I write numbers in a composition notebook. I have insomnia and have had it since 1996, though the seeds of it were planted much earlier, in what I will call my previous life, which I will not discuss here because this is not about me. This is about the team.

But I should explain the insomnia, briefly, because it is the reason this footage exists. When you do not sleep, you have to fill the hours. Some people read. Some people drink. I drive, and when I drive I record, and when I record I narrate, because the alternative is sitting in silence with my own thoughts between 1 and 5 AM, and I have been doing that for twenty-eight years and I have heard everything my thoughts have to say and I am no longer interested.

Here is Scranton at 3 AM on a Tuesday in November.

This is Lackawanna Avenue. The storefront that used to be a bridal shop is boarded. Next to it, the storefront that used to be the place that moved in after the bridal shop closed is also boarded. The plywood on the second one is newer. You can see where someone has spray-painted a phone number for a roofing company, which itself may no longer exist. The parking garage on Adams Avenue is half-demolished — they ran out of funding or will or both, so it stands with its upper deck open to the sky like a jaw mid-sentence, caught between saying something and deciding against it.

Here is the Lackawanna River at 4 AM. It is raining. The river does not look different in the rain because the river always looks like rain — it has the same gray, ongoing, uncommitted quality, neither flooding nor drying up, just continuing, the way certain kinds of disappointment continue.

Here is the Idle Hour Lanes from outside. The neon sign reads IDL H UR L NES because the second I, the O, the O again, the A, and the A again have all died at various points over the past decade and have not been replaced. The remaining letters glow the color of a gas flame on low. At 3 AM, the sign and the river and the parking garage are all saying the same thing, which is: *we are still here, and we don't know why either, but here we are.*

Now. Here is the league standings.

I am holding this up to the camera and I apologize for the grease stain — this was posted on the corkboard next to the snack bar and the proximity to the fryer is what it is. You can see the Wreckers at the bottom. You can see the Dunmore Kingpins at the top, where they have been for, at the time of this recording, going on five seasons. The gap between first and last is not competitive. It is geological. It is the kind of gap that suggests the two teams are not playing the same sport, or possibly not living on the same planet, and that the Kingpins' home lane exists in a slightly more favorable universe where the pins fall differently because the physics are kinder.

I want you to look at the numbers for a while before we continue. I want you to sit with them the way I have sat with them, across many nights, in this building, under these fluorescent lights that buzz at a frequency I have determined is a slightly flat B-flat, close enough to a musical note to be almost a sound and far enough to be, instead, a condition.

This is what losing looks like when it is not dramatic. When it is not a collapse or a streak or a tragedy. When it is just a way of life so ordinary that it becomes the texture of the weeks, the thing underneath the other things, the hum you stop hearing because it is always there.

I have been scoring these games for nine years. I have not missed a night. I have watched these men lose in every way that bowling permits and a few ways I believe they invented. And I want to be clear: I love them. I love them the way you love a city at 3 AM — not because it is beautiful but because you are both still here, awake, when everything reasonable has gone to sleep, and that shared insomnia is a kind of covenant.

Now. Let me tell you what Gary found.

## II.

This next footage is from Gary's garage. Date stamp: March 14th of last year. You can see the folding chairs set up between the tool cabinet and the workbench — Gary has cleared a space the way a man clears a space for something important, which is to say incompletely, so there are still socket wrenches on the bench and a Shop-Vac in the corner with its hose coiled like something sleeping. The overhead bulb is one of those caged utility lights, and it gives everyone the complexion of a police interrogation, which is appropriate given what is about to happen.

I recorded this from the corner, near the chest freezer. Gary had asked me to come. He had called each person individually, and I know this because I was awake when my phone rang at 11:30 PM and I heard in his voice the particular steadiness of a man who has rehearsed what he is going to say so many times that the words have become detached from their meaning and are now just a sequence of sounds he can produce reliably, like a prayer in a language you learned as a child and never understood.

Everyone is present. You can see Danny in the folding chair closest to the door, because Danny always sits closest to the door, which I attribute to a teaching career spent in rooms he needed to be able to exit quickly. Phil is in the back, hands on his knees, hands large and quiet. Teddy has brought his own cushion for the chair, for his back. Ronnie is smiling, as Ronnie does, but the smile has a new quality tonight — it is the smile of a man who has been told something serious is happening and has responded with the only tool he has.

Gary stands in front of the workbench. He has printed something. He is holding papers.

Let me play the audio.

*[Recording. Ambient garage noise — a faint ticking from the utility light, the distant sound of a neighbor's television through shared walls. Gary Potoczak's voice, slightly nasal, steady, Northeastern Pennsylvania cadence — the vowels compressed, the consonants carrying more weight than they were designed for.]*

GARY: Alright, so. I've been going through some things. Going through some numbers. I want to show you something and I want you to hear me out before anybody says anything, because I know how this is going to sound, and I need you to let me get through it.

*[Pause. Sound of papers being arranged on a surface.]*

GARY: The Kingpins have won the league five out of the last six seasons. That's not — that part you know. What you maybe don't know, because I didn't know until I started pulling it together, is the injury log. I filed a FOIA request. I know, I know. But the league keeps incident reports, and the incident reports are technically—

DANNY *(off-mic, muffled)*: Gary, did you FOIA the bowling league?

GARY: I made a request and they complied. That's all I'll say. Now. Look at this.

*[Sound of paper being held up. I did not get a clear shot from my angle, but I later obtained a copy. I am holding it up to the camera now. You can see the spreadsheet — hand-annotated in Gary's printing, which is mechanical and precise in the way of a man who spent decades writing serial numbers on work orders.]*

GARY: Wrist injuries. I highlighted the wrist injuries. Every team that played the Kingpins in the last three seasons — not some of them, every one — had at least one player report a wrist injury within two weeks of the match. Sprains. Strains. One guy, Roger Fitch from the Carbondale Statesmen, fractured his scaphoid bone reaching for a coffee mug the morning after their semifinal. Coffee mug. The Statesmen's anchor bowler developed carpal tunnel so fast his doctor thought the MRI was miscalibrated.

*[Silence. The utility light ticks.]*

GARY: And our guys? Teddy. What happened to your wrist before the crossover match in January?

TEDDY *(from his cushioned chair)*: It swelled up.

GARY: It swelled up. For no reason. No impact, no fall, no repetitive strain beyond the normal. It just swelled up. And Phil — you were wearing a brace.

PHIL *(barely audible)*: Yeah.

GARY: The wrist injuries are only wrist injuries. Not shoulders. Not knees. Not elbows. Wrists. The thing you use to *throw the ball*. And they only happen to teams playing Dunmore. I plotted it. I've got the chart.

*[He has the chart. I filmed it later, close-up. It is meticulous. The man made a chart.]*

GARY: Now. I'm going to say the next part, and I need — I just need the room to say it.

*[He breathes. In the footage, you can see him put one hand flat on the workbench, grounding himself, the way you'd touch a wall during an earthquake to confirm the building is still attached to the earth.]*

GARY: The Kingpins are sponsored by Marquardt Tire and Auto in Dunmore. Marquardt's been their sponsor going on ten years. Les Marquardt's wife's maiden name is Peale. Agatha Peale, from the Peales in Jessup. Now, I'm not — I know how it sounds when I say the next thing. But I have talked to people. Older people. People who remember things. And what I have been told, by more than one source, independently — I did not lead them, I asked open-ended questions — is that the Peale family, going back to at least the 1940s, has a tradition. A practice. A — the word people used was *work.* They do work. Agatha Peale does work. For the Kingpins. On the wrists of the teams that play against them.

*[He stops. He looks at the folding chairs. In the footage, I zoom in — not on Gary, but on the faces of the team. I want you to see what I saw. Not disbelief. Not laughter. Not the expression of men hearing something ridiculous. The expression of men hearing something they have always known but never been given permission to say out loud. It is the look of a diagnosis. The relief of a name.]*

GARY: Agatha Peale is a witch. A real one. A hereditary, generational, practicing witch, and she has been hexing the competition's wrists for the Dunmore Kingpins on behalf of a tire dealership for at least five years.

*[Nobody says anything. Danny does not laugh. Phil does not whisper. Ronnie's smile holds but changes — it becomes the smile of a man who now knows what he is smiling about, which is different from the smile of a man who doesn't. Teddy adjusts his cushion.]*

*[Twelve seconds of silence on the recording. I counted.]*

GARY: Alright. So here's what we do.

That's how you know something's true in Scranton. Nobody fights about it.

If Gary had said the Kingpins were using illegal lane oil or weighted balls or bribing the league secretary, there would have been argument. There would have been questions, cross-examination, at least one voice of reason pointing out the lack of evidence. Those are accusations that live in the world of the reasonable, and reasonable claims get reasonable resistance.

But a witch? A hex on the wrists? Something old and inherited and operating below the surface of the visible?
