# The Solis Inventory

---

## I.

The unit was ten feet by fifteen, climate-controlled, rented month-to-month from a company called EZ-Stor on Metropolitan Avenue in Ridgewood, Queens. It cost eighty-four dollars a month. The padlock was a Master No. 5, steel body, laminated case, the same model used on half the storage units in the five boroughs, which Solis had once found funny — that a woman who had spent seven years trying to track missing millions would secure her records with a lock you could defeat with a screwdriver and a firm opinion. She no longer found it funny. She had stopped finding things funny at a rate so gradual it had escaped her attention entirely, which was, in its own way, the most diagnostic fact about Marguerite Solis in the winter of 1985, though she would not have agreed, and no one was asking.

The $5.8 million was never recovered.

That figure requires a moment's stillness. It was $5.8 million in cash — not securities, not bearer bonds, not gold — American currency removed from a vault at the Lufthansa cargo terminal at John F. Kennedy International Airport on the morning of December 11, 1978. Adjusted for the intervening years, which had been characterized by inflation rates that would have seemed absurd to the generation that preceded them, the sum was by now closer to nine million in purchasing power, though purchasing power was an abstraction that mattered only to people who possessed the thing being abstracted, and nobody who was supposed to have it did, and the people who were not supposed to have it were, by the winter of 1985, largely dead. This is the story's first and most durable fact: the money went somewhere, and the somewhere was permanent.

Solis sat in a folding chair — aluminum frame, nylon seat, the kind sold at Kmart for $6.99 and rated for two hundred and twenty-five pounds, which gave her a margin of some seventy pounds, though she had been heavier once — and faced the boxes. There were forty-three of them. Banker's boxes, the white cardboard kind with lids that never quite seated flush, manufactured by the Fellowes company, each bearing a handwritten label in Solis's compressed, perpendicular script: dates, case numbers that had once corresponded to active NYPD files and now corresponded to nothing. The labels were clean. There was no dust on the boxes because she came every Thursday to check for moisture and every other Thursday to re-examine a file or follow a thought that had arrived on the subway or in the shower or, increasingly, at 3:00 a.m. in the efficiency apartment on Bleecker Street where she did not sleep so much as intermittently lose consciousness.

The fluorescent tube overhead was a Sylvania Cool White 40-watt, and it turned everything the same color — the boxes, the corkboard propped against the back wall, the timelines drawn in four colors of ink (black for confirmed movements, blue for probable, red for alleged, green for speculative — a system she had developed in 1979 and maintained with a consistency that would have been admirable in an active investigation and was, here, something else, something the English language does not have a precise word for, though the Germans might: the practice of maintaining perfect order in the service of an outcome that is no longer possible). Under that light her hands were the same institutional gray as the boxes, as the folding chair, as the eight-dollar wristwatch she wore because the department had not, in the end, given her anything worth wearing.

She had been terminated — the formal language was "separated from service" — four months prior, in September 1984, following the hearing. Her pension was under review, which was bureaucratic language for a process that would take long enough to constitute its own punishment. She was fifty-one. She had been a detective for twenty-two years. She was not, at this moment, doing anything that would be recognizable as detective work. She was sitting. The fluorescent tube hummed at a frequency of 120 hertz, which was the frequency of American electrical current doubled, a fact that Solis knew because she had once, in a different phase of the investigation, spent two weeks learning about electrical infrastructure at Kennedy Airport in the belief that a discrepancy in the maintenance logs might reveal the involvement of a third-party contractor. It had not. But she still knew the number.

Inside the third box from the left, second row, was a manila folder containing forty-six photocopied pages of the original Lufthansa cargo manifest for the week of December 4–11, 1978. Inside the seventh box from the left, bottom row, was a cassette tape — a Maxell UR-90, its transparent window showing the brown ribbon wound roughly three-quarters to the right — on which she had recorded, from memory, on June 14, 1983, her complete reconstruction of the eighteen minutes between the moment the vault was opened and the moment the van departed the cargo terminal. She had narrated it in real time, pausing where the robbers would have paused, hurrying where they would have hurried, so that the recording was itself eighteen minutes long. She had listened to it perhaps forty times. Her own voice on the tape was calm, methodical, specific about distances and sight lines, and listening to it now would be like hearing a command performance by an orchestra that no longer exists — technically impeccable, addressed to an empty hall.

The corkboard held eleven index cards arranged in a pattern she called the constellation, which mapped the relationships among the participants in the heist — robbery crew, Lucchese family associates, cargo handlers, the inside man — using colored pushpins and lengths of thread that had once been taut and were now, because corkboard dried and pins loosened, slightly slack, so that the threads drooped between the pins in shallow catenaries, as if the connections themselves had grown tired. Three of the index cards bore small black crosses in the upper-right corner. These indicated individuals who were dead. By 1985 there should have been more crosses, but Solis had stopped updating the corkboard two years ago, not out of negligence but because the rate of killing had made the exercise feel less like record-keeping and more like something she did not want it to feel like.

She sat. The unit smelled of cardboard and cold concrete and the faintly metallic scent of recycled air pushed through ductwork that had not been cleaned since the building's construction in 1971. Outside, on Metropolitan Avenue, a bus applied its brakes with the hydraulic exhalation that is one of New York City's native sounds, and the vibration traveled through the slab floor and into the aluminum legs of the folding chair and into the bones of Marguerite Solis, who registered it without interpretation, the way a seismograph registers a tremor too small to name.

She opened a box. She removed a file. She began to read.

There was no one to receive the findings. There was no case to attach them to. There was no badge in her pocket, no gun on her hip, no partner waiting in a vehicle outside, no supervisor to whom a report was owed. The fluorescent light treated her and the boxes with identical indifference. She read with the concentration of a woman who had been trained to find what others missed, and what she missed, what she had been missing for years, was not a clue or a lead or a discrepancy in the paperwork but the moment — impossible to identify from inside it — when the work had become the disease and the disease had become the work, so that they were now a single thing, circulating, and she could not528 stop because stopping would require her to distinguish between them, and she could not distinguish between them, and so she read.

The $5.8 million was never recovered.

It is necessary to say it twice. The first time establishes the fact. The second time establishes its weight.

## II.

In September of 1984, eleven months before the storage unit and seven years after the heist, in a room paneled in wood-grain laminate that had been manufactured to suggest oak and succeeded only in suggesting the idea of oak — a room on the fourth floor of One Police Plaza that was used for Internal Affairs proceedings and, when unoccupied, for birthday gatherings for civilian administrative staff, so that the table where witnesses sat sometimes bore a faint dusting of confectioner's sugar — a man named Vincent Arata retracted his statement.

He was forty-four years old. He was five feet seven inches tall. He weighed, at the time, approximately one hundred and sixty pounds, though no one recorded this and it matters only because the weight was fifteen pounds less than what he had carried when Solis first interviewed him in 1981, and the loss was visible in specific places — the looseness of his shirt collar, the new geography of his cheekbones — and if it could be attributed to any single cause, that cause was three years of living in the particular state of fear that a man occupies when he has told a story to a detective that the detective wanted to hear and that was not, in all its particulars, the story he would have told if he had been left to tell it on his own.

What Solis had done was this:

On the evening of March 3, 1981, in a diner on Cross Bay Boulevard in Howard Beach — not an interrogation room, not a precinct, not any space where the procedural protections that exist to prevent exactly what was about to happen would have been automatically in effect — she met with Vincent Arata alone. She did not record the conversation. She did not take contemporaneous notes. She would later write a summary of the interview from memory, and that summary would become Exhibit 14 in the case file, and it would contain no overt falsehoods, only the kind of distortions that are available to a person who is writing down what they believe happened rather than what did happen, because the two, by that point, had become indistinguishable inside her mind, and she was not lying, and this is worse than lying, because a lie can be corrected.

She told Arata that his cousin, Anthony Arata, had already been identified by two other sources as the driver of the secondary vehicle used to transfer the cash from the cargo van to a private garage in Ozone Park. This was true — two informants had named Anthony Arata — but what Solis did not tell Vincent was that both informants were demonstrably unreliable, that one had provided the name only after being told the name by Solis herself in a previous interview, and that the other had recanted within forty-eight hours and later claimed he had been "guessing." What Solis told Vincent Arata, in the yellow vinyl booth of that diner, with the remains of a club sandwich between them, was that the case against Anthony was already solid — that Vincent's testimony would be confirmatory, not foundational. She told him he would not be the one to bring his cousin down. The structure was already in place. He would merely be adding a brick to a wall that was already standing.

The wall did not exist.

She believed it did. This is the central fact. She was not manufacturing a case from nothing — she had spent three years gathering fragments that, viewed from the angle at which she viewed them, described a coherent shape. Anthony Arata *was* connected to people who *were* connected to the Lucchese operation that *had* planned the heist. The driver of the secondary vehicle *was* unaccounted for. Anthony *could* have been that driver. The gap between *could have been* and *was* is the width of the entire criminal justice system, and Solis stepped across it the way a person steps across a crack in the sidewalk — without looking down, without registering that there was a gap at all — because she had been looking at the case for so long that her eyes had adjusted to its darkness and she could no longer tell which shapes were real and which were the products of her own retinas.

Vincent Arata, in that diner, agreed to testify. He said yes. He said yes because he was afraid and because Solis was a detective and because she told him the thing was already done and because she looked at him with the absolute conviction of a woman who had memorized every flight manifest and every cargo log and every known associate of every known participant, and that conviction had a force that was, in the room, indistinguishable from the truth. He said yes and then went home and did not sleep well for three years.

Now, in the laminate-paneled room at One Police Plaza, he undid what he had done.

He did not look at Solis. This is important to note, because the specific quality of his not-looking was one that anyone who has been in a room with a person they have been made to fear would recognize: it was not avoidance born of shame — it was the involuntary flinch of the body away from a source of pain. He looked at the table. He looked at his hands. He looked at the IAB lieutenant who was conducting the hearing. He looked at the court reporter's fingers on the stenotype keys. He looked at the window, which offered a view of the Municipal Building's south face, a great deal of gray stone, nothing that would return his gaze.

"She told me it was already done," he said. "She said I would just be one more. She said it didn't depend on me. But it depended on me. She knew it depended on me."

Solis, seated at the opposite end of the table, did not react visibly. Her hands remained in her lap. Her face maintained the expression it had worn for the preceding forty minutes, which was the expression of a woman performing the act of listening with a precision that was indistinguishable from actually listening but was, in fact, something else — a watching, a rapid interior calculation of structural damage, the same assessment she would make at a crime scene. What collapsed when a floor gave way. What remained standing. What could be shored up.

Nothing could be shored up.

The IAB lieutenant — a man named Dwyer, whom Solis had known for eleven years and who, she knew, had once admired her, which made his current face worse — asked Arata to be specific about the March 3 meeting. Arata was specific. He described the diner. He described the booth. He described the club sandwich. He said Solis had told him that refusing to corroborate would not save his cousin — that Anthony was "going in regardless" — and that testifying would allow Vincent to "control the story" rather than be swept up in it. He said she had used the word *swept*. Three years later, in this room, his memory of that word was molecular. It had lodged in him the way a splinter lodges, too deep to extract, surrounded by inflamed tissue.

"I didn't see the thing she said I saw," Vincent Arata said. "I wasn't at the garage. I said I was because she made me understand that it was already known that I was, and that saying I wasn't would look like I was protecting Anthony, and that protecting Anthony would put me inside the case instead of outside it." He paused. "She didn't threaten me. She never said anything that sounded like a threat. She just — she made it clear. She made the situation clear. And the situation she described wasn't the real situation."

He was right.

Solis knew — had known since at least the middle of his statement, though it is possible she had known for longer than that, possible she had known for years in the way people know things they cannot afford to know, which is to say deeply and uselessly, the knowledge sealed in a compartment that the conscious mind declines to access — that the recantation was legitimate. That what she had done in that diner was not detective work. That the lucid certainty she had felt, the certainty that had made it possible for her to sit across from a frightened man and describe a reality that did not exist with the confidence of a woman reading from a record, was the same certainty she had felt in front of the corkboard and the timelines and the color-coded threads, and it was not knowledge, it was *pattern*, and pattern was not truth, and the distance between the two was the distance between investigation and fabrication, and there was no wall between them, there had never been a wall — only a line drawn in the confidence of the person crossing it, and she had crossed it without looking down.

Dwyer asked Arata if he wished to add anything for the record. Arata said no. Then he said: "I hope she understands what it felt like."

He left the room. He did not look at her on his way out. The air he had occupied was briefly cooler, the way any vacated space is cooler, and then the room's heating system filled the gap, and he was gone, and Solis remained, and the court reporter's hands paused on the keys, and the fluorescent lights — different fixtures but the same frequency, the same 120-hertz hum — continued their work of making everything in the room the same flat, undifferentiated color.
