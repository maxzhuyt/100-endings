# The Venues of Maren Ellsworth

---

## I.

The first thing you see are the files.

They fill the shelves behind her in a wall of uniform spines — lever-arch, colour-coded, each labelled in a hand so precise it might have been typeset were it not for the slight calligraphic flourish on every capital letter, a private vanity performed for no visible audience. *Amalfi Coast — June 2002. Brasserie Lipp, Paris — Claim Filed Nov. 2015. Grasmere Cottage — Claim Filed March 2016. Bruges, Chapel of the Holy Blood — Claim Filed Sept. 2016.* They continue. There are more than twenty.

Maren Ellsworth sits in front of them in a high-backed office chair, centred in the frame with the compositional rigidity of a passport photograph. She is mid-fifties, slender in a way that suggests maintenance rather than nature, and she is dressed in a charcoal blazer over a cream silk blouse whose top button is fastened. Everything about her presentation communicates the same message: *I have prepared for this. I have prepared for all of this.* Her posture is a closing argument.

"I want to be clear at the outset," she says, addressing the camera directly with the flat, unblinking affect of someone accustomed to depositions, "that what I am engaged in is not — as has been characterised — a campaign of harassment. It is civil litigation. There is a distinction, and the distinction is procedural, and the procedure is documented."

She gestures, without looking, to the wall behind her.

The camera holds.

It holds long enough for you to read the spines. Long enough for you to notice the map pinned above the desk — Western Europe, shot through with coloured pins, each connected by string to an index card bearing a date and a case reference number. Long enough for you to register the laminated timeline taped to the near wall: a horizontal bar spanning 2002 to the present, subdivided into colour-coded phases labelled *Discovery*, *Preliminary Filings*, *Active Litigation*, *Dormant/Pending*, and — in a final column whose thinness suggests not completion but exhaustion of options — *Resolved*.

The *Resolved* column is empty.

---

A note on how stories like this are told. The BBC commissions approximately fourteen documentary profiles per year in which an individual of no prior public significance is revealed, through patient accumulation of detail, to have constructed a life so singular in its fixations that the viewer cannot decide whether to be moved, amused, or unsettled, and so is all three simultaneously. The grammar of these films is codified: static medium shots, natural light where possible, the subject framed in their own environment so that the setting performs expository work the narration need not. Cutaways to documents, objects, surfaces. Interviews with bemused peripheral figures — neighbours, solicitors, council officials — who provide the comic relief of normality encountering obsession. And above it all, the narration: measured, unhurried, the diction of someone who studied English at a middling Oxford college in the 1980s and went into broadcasting rather than publishing because they wanted to be *heard* being reasonable.

The film about Maren Ellsworth follows this grammar to the letter. It opens on the files. It introduces its subject mid-process. It provides the viewer with exactly enough context to understand what she is doing — filing civil claims against a succession of European venues on grounds that are, to put it with the precision Maren herself would insist upon, *novel* — without yet explaining why. And the narration cooperates. It cooperates beautifully. It treats Maren's nine-year legal campaign with the sombre procedural gravity typically reserved for miscarriages of justice or the quiet heroism of archivists who rescued cultural artefacts during wartime. It says things like, "The documentation Maren has assembled over the past nine years represents, by any measure, a formidable body of work." It means this. It does not wink.

This is the first and most important lie the story tells.

Not because Maren is not formidable. She is formidable. She is — was — a solicitor, trained at the College of Law, twelve years in commercial property before her practising certificate was revoked under circumstances the film alludes to without specifying, in a single narrated line that will not receive its full weight until much later: "In 2013, the Solicitors Regulation Authority suspended Maren's right to practise, citing conduct that fell below the standards expected of the profession." The passive construction doing its usual work: containing a catastrophe in a subordinate clause.

No, the lie is not about Maren's competence. The lie is structural. It is the frame itself — the documentary form, with its implicit promise that evidence will be weighed, testimony will be tested, and a verdict will be reached. The audience is being told that this is a case to be solved. That the question before them is the question the film appears to be asking: *Why does this woman keep suing restaurants and cottages and chapels across Europe?*

But that is not the question. That is the door. The question is behind it, in a room that Maren herself has never entered, in a filing system she has never labelled, in a handwriting that is not hers.

---

She produces the laminated timeline. She produces it before anyone asks.

"The chronology is essential," she says. "Without the chronology, people tend to characterise what I do as scattershot. It isn't. There is a clear evidentiary through-line, and it begins" — she taps the leftmost point on the timeline, June 2002, a red pin — "here."

The timeline is impeccable. It is the work of months, possibly years. It tracks not only her own legal actions but Peter's movements — where he went, with whom and when, as far as she has been able to establish — with the meticulous cross-referencing of an intelligence analyst mapping a network. Arrows connect events. Footnotes, in a font size that requires the camera to zoom in to read, reference supporting documents by exhibit number.

She has given the timeline a title. The title is: *Chronological Index of Venue-Specific Emotional Misappropriations Perpetrated and/or Facilitated in Connection with the Dissolution of the Ellsworth Marriage (2002–Present)*.

The cameraman's slight, involuntary exhalation is audible on the recording.

Maren does not react. She has heard that breath before — the small, compressed sound people make when they do not know whether to laugh. She has, it is clear, categorised it and dismissed it. It is not relevant to the proceedings.

---

## II.

"I will begin," she says, "with the Amalfi Coast."

She says it the way a barrister says *I turn now to the events of the fourteenth*. It is not an invitation. It is a procedural notice.

"Peter and I were married on the first of June 2002 at Chelsea Register Office. The ceremony was attended by fourteen guests, which was the number I considered appropriate, having reviewed the room dimensions. We departed for Italy the following morning. The accommodation was a villa in Ravello — the Villa San Michele — booked for fourteen nights, the seventh through the twenty-first of June, at a total cost of four thousand two hundred pounds, which I paid from my own account because Peter's assets at that time were, as I would later discover, somewhat less liquid than represented."

She pauses. Not for effect — Maren does not do anything for effect — but to consult a photocopied receipt she has ready on the desk, confirming the figure.

"The first three days were satisfactory. I use that word deliberately. *Satisfactory.* The weather was good. The view was as described in the listing. The terrace faced south-southwest. Peter was attentive in the way that, at the time, I interpreted as genuine but which I have subsequently been forced to recategorise as performative, though I accept this recategorisation may itself be influenced by subsequent events, and I note that for the record."

The omniscient narration — the real narration, the one that lives above and beneath the documentary's frame — observes something here. It observes that Maren's account of the first three days of her honeymoon contains no sensory detail. She describes the weather as "good." The view as "as described in the listing." Peter's behaviour as "attentive." These are categories, not memories. They are the words of a woman who has described this event so many times, in so many legal filings and interview rooms and late-night rehearsals before the bathroom mirror, that the descriptions have been sanded smooth of everything particular — every texture, every scent, every specific quality of light on water — until what remains is a deposition. A perfectly admissible, perfectly empty deposition.

She does not describe the villa. She does not describe the bed, or the colour of the tiles, or what they ate, or whether the shower pressure was adequate, or whether Peter snored, or what his shoulders looked like when he leaned on the terrace railing in the morning. She does not describe anything that would require her to have been *there* — present in her body, in the room, in the marriage — rather than *observing there*, from the professionally detached vantage point she has occupied ever since.

She describes the *category*: honeymoon, villa, betrayal. A postcard of an experience. The documentary intercuts her testimony with B-roll footage of the Amalfi coastline — stock, or indistinguishable from stock — and the narration holds on those vistas a beat too long, two beats, three, until the lemon groves and terracotta and cerulean water begin to feel like what they are: generic. Interchangeable with fifty other travelogue montages for fifty other coasts. As though Maren's memory and the footage are made of the same material.

"On the fourth day," she says, and her voice does not change, "Peter received a phone call."

She describes the call with the specificity she denied the preceding days. She was on the terrace. He was in the bedroom. The door was ajar — seven to nine inches, she estimates. She heard him say the name "Kate" twice and "I know, I know" once. The call lasted between four and six minutes. She did not confront him immediately. She waited until dinner — they ate at a restaurant in Ravello whose name she provides along with the address and her assessment of the service — and she asked, in what she describes as "a measured tone," who Kate was.

"His response was inadequate."

This is all she says about the conversation that ended the marriage. Three words. *His response was inadequate.* As though the content of his answer — the specific configuration of lies, evasions, admissions, or cruelties — is immaterial to the proceedings and has been redacted accordingly.

"He left on day six. By taxi to Naples, then a flight to Gatwick. I have the flight records." She does. She has them in a clear plastic sleeve, exhibit-tabbed. "The remaining eight nights of the booking were non-refundable. The villa's cancellation policy, which I had reviewed at the time of reservation and which I had noted was unusually strict but which I had accepted because the property was desirable and I had no reason then to anticipate that its cancellation policy would become relevant — that policy was firm. The deposit would be forfeited, and the balance had already been paid."

She adjusts the receipt on the desk. Aligns it with the edge.

"So I stayed."

---

She stayed.

For eight mornings she ate breakfast on the terrace. She did not eat it weeping, or staring vacantly, or performing any of the recognizable postures of abandonment that a more conventionally sympathetic subject would offer a documentary. She ate it on schedule — she mentions the time, eight-fifteen, because she had established a routine during the first three days and saw no reason to disrupt it — and she ate whatever the villa's housekeeper had laid out, and she drank coffee, and she looked at the view.

And then she wrote in her journal.

Not, she is careful to clarify, a journal of her feelings. "I recorded what we would have done that day, had the marriage continued as contracted. Day seven: boat to Positano, lunch, afternoon on the beach. Day eight: drive to Pompeii, because Peter had expressed an interest. Day nine—"

She has the journal. She produces it — a Moleskine, the large ruled variety, its cover still faintly warped by what might be humidity or might be something else. The camera zooms in on the first entry. The handwriting is measured, neat, the same semi-calligraphic precision as the file labels, and the text reads: *Day 7 (June 13). If present, P. and I would take the 10:15 boat from Amalfi to Positano (timetable confirmed at harbour office). Lunch at a restaurant with a terrace — must have sea view. Afternoon: beach. Return on 17:30 boat. Dinner: villa. Early night.*

It is an itinerary for a ghost.

A schedule for the parallel life in which the phone call did not happen, laid out with the administrative rigour of a conference planner, and followed — this is the detail the narration surfaces quietly — *actually followed*. Maren took the boat to Positano on day seven. She ate lunch at a restaurant with a sea view. She sat on the beach. She returned on the 17:30 boat. She did all of it alone, and she recorded the completion of each item with small ticks in the margin.

She completed the honeymoon as a solo procedural exercise.

She administered the remaining terms of the arrangement.

The narration, at this point, does not comment. It does not need to. It simply holds on the image of the Moleskine — those tidy ticks, that haunted itinerary — and it lets the viewer sit in the silence between what is funny and what is annihilating, because the space between those two things, in this story, is the width of a tick mark in a ruled margin.

---

## III.

The documentary's rhythm, once established, is the rhythm of all good procedural narratives: *here is the next case*. And so Brasserie Lipp.

The narration introduces it with the measured delight of a Radio 4 presenter biting into a particularly ripe anecdote: "In November 2015, Maren filed her first formal civil claim against a commercial venue. The defendant was Brasserie Lipp, the historic Left Bank restaurant in the sixth arrondissement of Paris, a venue whose clientele has, over the decades, included Hemingway, Mitterrand, and — on the evening of the fourteenth of September 2015 — Peter Jon Ellsworth, dining with a woman identified in court documents as K. Rourke."

Maren straightens in her chair.

"I became aware of the booking through a process of ongoing informational review," she says. She does not elaborate on the nature of this review. The documentary, to its credit, presses gently — "Can you describe how you first learned Peter had visited Brasserie Lipp?" — and Maren responds with a silence so precisely calibrated, so freighted with legalistic non-engagement, that the interviewer moves on.

It is, a later interview with a private investigator named Gerald Kemp will establish, almost certainly Instagram. Or, more precisely, a systematic programme of monitoring Peter's social media presence, the social media presences of his known associates, the check-in features of various platforms, and the publicly available reservation records of certain high-profile European restaurants. "She had spreadsheets," Kemp says, in the slightly awed tone of a man who has worked surveillance for twenty years and has never seen a civilian maintain a more disciplined intelligence operation. "Cross-referenced. She could tell you where he'd eaten in the last eighteen months with better accuracy than his own diary."

But the documentary does not dwell on the surveillance. It dwells on the claim.
