# The Fortress

---

My father sat on the kitchen floor with the Book of Job open across his knees and he wept.

Not the way he wept at funerals, which was a controlled dampening of the eyes, a single pass of the handkerchief folded precisely into quarters, the grief of a professional mourner who understood that his role was to hold the shape of things while others broke apart. Not the way he wept when my mother died, which I had observed only once, through a two-inch crack in his bedroom door—a sound so private and so architectural, so load-bearing in its restraint, that I backed away from it the way you back away from a wall you suspect is the only thing keeping the ceiling up.

This was nothing like those. This was a man sitting barefoot on linoleum with his legs drawn up and his back against the cabinets beneath the sink and the ancient JPS translation—the one with the cracked spine, the one with forty years of marginal notes in his handwriting, the one he kept not on his study shelf but in the kitchen because he said Job deserved a room where people actually lived—open and facedown on his thighs, and his hands were covering his face, and the sounds coming from behind his hands were the sounds of something that had been sealed for a very long time breaking open in a way that could not be reversed.

He had read Job ten thousand times. I know this not as hyperbole but as a reasonable estimate. Weekly since ordination. More often during hospital visits, during shivah calls, during the long year after my mother. He had taught it in adult education classes so many times that his students could finish his sentences, and he let them, because he believed that was the point—that the words should live in other mouths. He knew the poetry in Hebrew and in English and, for two passages he considered especially crucial, in the Aramaic of the Targum. He had opinions about the Elihu speeches that he could defend for forty-five minutes without notes. He had once told me, when I was sixteen and furious about something I can no longer remember, that Job was the most honest book ever written because it had the courage to let God be incomprehensible and to call that incomprehensibility an answer.

He had read it ten thousand times, and sitting there on the floor on a Thursday evening in October, my father was hearing it.

I did not ask what had changed. I stood in the doorway between the hall and the kitchen—the same doorway where, as a child, I had stood waiting for him to notice me, which sometimes took a long time because a man deep in a text is a man who has left the room—and I watched him weep, and I did not go to him, and I did not leave.

I think he knew I was there. He did not cover himself. He did not apologize. He did not perform the recovery, the gathering-up, that I had seen him perform so many times after smaller fractures—the smoothing of the tie, the clearing of the throat, the resumption of authority like a coat lifted from the back of a chair and shrugged on. He just sat there, on the floor, barefoot, the linoleum cold I'm sure because he kept the heat low, he always kept the heat low, he believed in austerity the way other men believe in comfort, and the book was open, and his body was making sounds I had never heard it make in the thirty-eight years I had been alive to listen.

I am going to tell you what led to this. I am going to tell it the way it happened to me, which is to say backward, because I did not understand any of it in the living and have only come to understand it in the disassembly, the way you understand a clock not by watching it keep time but by removing the gears one by one and holding each up to the light and asking what this one did, what did this small thing do.

---

My father was a man of systems.

I should be more precise. My father was a man whose self was indistinguishable from his systems, and I mean this as a physical, observable claim—not a psychological interpretation. I am not a psychologist. I am an archivist at the Montclair Public Library, which means I am a person professionally trained to describe what exists without speculating about why, and I will try to honor that training here, though I will fail at several points because he was my father and a daughter's observation is never an uncontaminated instrument.

The systems:

He blessed bread with the right hand lifted to a height of precisely four inches above the challah—I measured once, as a teenager, with a ruler held surreptitiously behind a Shabbat candle, because I suspected but wanted to confirm that the gesture was identical each time, and it was. The blessing itself was spoken at a pace that, if you tapped your finger to it, resolved to exactly the tempo of a resting heartbeat. I don't know if this was deliberate. I suspect it was, because everything about my father's religious practice had the quality of engineering, of tolerances calculated and held.

He locked the front door each evening at nine. Not approximately nine. Not around nine. At nine. I know because the lock was a deadbolt that produced a specific heavy sound, a deep thunk that carried through the house, and as a child lying in bed I used that sound the way other children use a parent's voice saying goodnight—it meant the perimeter was established, the day was formally over, the house was sealed.

His bookshelves—and there were eleven of them, in three rooms—were organized by topic, then by author within topic, then by publication date within author. The topics themselves were arranged in an order that tracked, roughly, the order of the Jewish canon: Torah commentaries first, then Talmud, then medieval philosophy, then Hasidic texts, then modern theology, then responsa literature, then what he called "the necessary antagonists," by which he meant Spinoza and Kaplan and anyone else whose thinking he disagreed with profoundly but refused to disrespect by ignoring. In my entire life, from the age at which I was tall enough to read spines to the week I am about to describe, I never once saw those shelves rearranged. Books were added. None were removed. None migrated.

He corrected my Hebrew pronunciation well into my thirties. This is perhaps the detail that requires the most context. I speak Hebrew competently. I spent a year in Israel after college. I can read Torah at a level that has earned me quiet compliments from cantors. My father still, at dinner, at casual family gatherings, in the middle of unrelated conversations, would stop me if I softened a *tav* or swallowed a syllable. He did this without malice, without condescension. He did it the way a man tightens a hinge that has begun to loosen—automatically, because the loosening is intolerable not for reasons of anger but because the door must close properly or it is not a door.

He wore the same three suits in rotation. He used the same pen, a black Pilot G-2 07, and when a box of refills arrived from Amazon he transferred them into a desk drawer and discarded the packaging immediately, so that the drawer contained only ink cartridges in a neat row, like small dark soldiers awaiting deployment.

I am aware, reciting this, that I am drawing a portrait that could be read as coldness, as rigidity, as a man who substituted order for love. I want to resist that reading, not because it is entirely wrong but because it is incomplete in a way that falsifies. My father's order *was* a form of love. The locked door at nine was a promise: I have made the world safe for tonight. The corrected Hebrew was a promise: I will keep handing you this language even after you are old enough to refuse it. The unchanged shelves were a promise: the structure holds. I will not rearrange the universe on you. You can find everything where you last saw it. Everything is where it belongs.

I say all this so that you might understand what it meant when, in the space of a single week, I watched the bread go unblessed, the Hebrew go uncorrected, the door stand unlocked past midnight, and a book by Abraham Joshua Heschel appear on the kitchen counter with a cracked spine and a yellow sticky note on a page my father had—I could tell from the adhesive residue—opened and marked and reopened and marked and reopened so many times that the paper had gone soft as cloth.

The congregational board of Ner Tamid Synagogue met on a Wednesday evening in the wood-paneled room adjacent to the main sanctuary that also served, on alternating Sundays, as a space for the sisterhood's book club and, once a year, as the staging area for the Purim carnival. The room smelled, permanently, of coffee that had been heated too long and the particular vanilla dust of old prayer books. I have been in this room perhaps two hundred times in my life. I could draw its layout from memory: the rectangular table that seated fourteen, the framed photographs of past rabbis on the east wall, the thermostat that no one was allowed to touch except Murray Blumenthal, who served as building committee chair and who alone understood its mystery.

I was not present at this meeting. This account is assembled from three sources: my father, who mentioned it twice in the days that followed, both times with the same expression—a kind of baffled amusement, as if he were describing a play he had seen whose plot he could not entirely follow; Carol Weissman, the board secretary, who has been a friend of mine since we were girls and who told me afterward that the whole thing took eleven minutes; and my own knowledge of how these people behave in rooms together, which is extensive and, I think, reasonably reliable.

The agenda item was the hiring of an associate rabbi. The candidate was Nava Kaplan. She was thirty-one, ordained at the Jewish Theological Seminary, the author of a well-received essay in a journal I will not name because it would mean nothing to most people but which, in the small world of American rabbinics, constituted a genuine credential. She had spent two years as a hospital chaplain and one year teaching in a supplementary school in Brooklyn. Her references were strong. Her interview, which I am told lasted ninety minutes, had been—by all accounts—impressive in that way that is also slightly unsettling: she listened more than she spoke, and when she spoke, she was precise.

The board voted unanimously to hire her.

I want to stay with that sentence for a moment, because its simplicity conceals what I believe was actually happening in that room, and what was happening in that room was, in miniature, the same thing that was about to happen to my father, which is that a group of people performed a conviction they did not hold and mistook the performance for the thing itself.

Here is what I have pieced together: Sol Auerbach, the board president, supported the hire because he believed the younger members—specifically the cohort in their thirties who had joined the synagogue after the renovation and who tithed generously and who had twice raised the question of "the gender composition of the clergy" in congregational surveys—would leave if a female associate rabbi was not hired. Sol had no evidence for this belief. He had a feeling, and the feeling was sufficient.

The younger members, for their part, assumed that Sol and the older cohort had already been convinced—by the denomination's national leadership, by the example of other congregations in the area, by the slow tide of inevitability—and that any hesitation on their own part would mark them as the obstacle. Several of them had private reservations. Not about Nava specifically. About timing, about fit, about the congregation's readiness for a change in its spiritual leadership structure. They did not voice these reservations because the room felt settled.

Deborah Klein, who taught seventh-grade Hebrew school and who had served on the board for nine years, later told Carol that she voted yes because "everyone's face said yes." Carol told me she voted yes because Deborah's hand went up first.

And my father—my father voted yes because the board had reached a consensus, and he believed a rabbi's role was to serve the community's will, not to override it, and the community's will appeared to be unanimous, and who was he to stand alone against the unanimous?

No one stood alone. No one stood against. The vote was swift and cordial and there was no discussion beforehand because there was nothing to discuss—the outcome had been determined by each person's private calculation of what everyone else wanted, and those calculations, stacked and cross-referenced, produced a total that none of them had individually computed.

Eleven minutes.

Carol told me Murray adjusted the thermostat afterward. She told me there were rugelach from the Kosher Nosh on Broad Street and that Sol made a brief remark about "new chapters" and that my father said, "May her teaching be a blessing," which was exactly the right thing to say and which he said in exactly the tone he used for all such ceremonial utterances—warm, measured, complete—and that the meeting adjourned and everyone went home.

Nava Kaplan began the following Monday.

I remember thinking, when my father told me about the vote, that his face looked the way it looked when he had solved a problem that had not been difficult enough to be interesting. A mild satisfaction. A task discharged. I did not see anything beneath it. But I also did not look carefully, because I am his daughter, and a daughter's great blindness with her father is that she believes the surface is the substance, because the surface is what she was raised on, and it never occurred to her that the surface could be structural rather than expressive—a wall, rather than a window.
