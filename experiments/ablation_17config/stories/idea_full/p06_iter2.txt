# THE CONSUMING MOUTH

### *Being the Collected Dispatches of Celeste Ayo, filed for* The Gloss *between March 7 and March 19, 20—, from the motor yacht* Leucothea, *Papua New Guinea*

---

## DISPATCH 1: BOARDING THE FLOATING BORDELLO

**Filed: March 7 | Madang, Morobe Province | Word count: 1,840**

---

The *Leucothea* was waiting for us at the commercial dock in Madang like a kept woman at an airport Chili's — overdressed, undercontextualized, and absolutely certain of her effect.

She is a 47-meter Benetti, vintage 2016, with a displacement hull in cream-white gelcoat and teak decking the color of expensive honey. She has a helipad. She has a *plunge pool*. She has, according to the laminated welcome packet placed on my berth alongside a gardenia and a 50-milliliter bottle of New Zealand manuka-honey serum, "a curated onboard apothecary featuring high-vibration botanical essences selected by Dr. Henk Palliser especially for the Sepik passage." The apothecary is in fact a cabinet in the main salon containing twelve amber bottles of unlabeled tinctures, a brass mortar and pestle, and a dog-eared copy of Terence McKenna's *Food of the Gods* that has been handled so often its spine has given up and simply lies flat, like a woman in a Pre-Raphaelite painting, waiting to be read.

The trip is called the Sepik Encounter. (I know, Marcus. I *know*. But you sent me, so here we are.) Nine days aboard, motoring from Madang along the coast and then upriver into the Sepik system — one of the last major undammed rivers on the planet, a vast malarial capillary network that drains a watershed the size of Ireland and has, in recent decades, become a fixture on the ultra-premium expedition circuit. The pitch is *radical encounter*: with indigenous art, with pristine ecosystems, with your own inner whatever. The price is forty-two thousand U.S. dollars per passenger, not including the charter flight from Port Moresby. There is a waiting list. There has been one since 2019.

I should introduce the cast. I'll be cruel now and get it over with.

**Giancarlo Desmet**, 34. Belgian. Heir to the Desmet-Hoechst pharmaceutical fortune. Tan the color and apparent density of a glazed ceramic tile. Boarded carrying a single leather duffel and wearing an outfit I can only describe as *plantation-core* — unstructured linen, huarache sandals, a strand of hand-carved Sepik bone beads he says he acquired "on a previous journey." He has the bearing of a man who has never in his life been told that an experience was not for him.

**Margaux St. Germaine**, 61. Retired mezzo-soprano. San Francisco Opera, La Monnaie, a well-reviewed *Carmen* at the Aix Festival in 2004 that she brings up within eleven minutes of meeting you. Traveling with her "nephew," **Teodor** — 28, Moldovan, cheekbones doing more structural work than any load-bearing wall I've seen. No one aboard, including the crew, performs the fiction of the nephew relation with any conviction, which I find almost touching. Margaux wears caftans the way others wear armor. She has opinions about *everything* and delivers them in a mezzo's chest voice, so that even her observation about the airport coffee (fair — it was terrible) acquires the weight of Erda's warning from the depths.

**Sloane Koestler**, 44. Based in Woodside, California. Founder and CEO of something called *Synapt*, which she describes as "a somatic technology platform for relational deep-work." I ask her what this means. She tells me it means she "designs containers for intimacy." She has brought aboard three devices that look like EEG headsets mated with high-end vibrators. She refers to herself as an "intimacy architect" without detectable irony. (Marcus, I want you to know that I wrote down *intimacy architect* in my notebook and then physically closed my eyes for ten seconds, as a prayer.)

**Nathaniel and Cass Yeung**, mid-50s. He's Hong Kong money, second-generation, retired from structured finance. She's Tasmanian, an environmental lawyer who left practice "to focus on embodiment." They hold hands constantly. They have the careful tenderness of people who nearly divorced and are now performing their reunion for an audience they believe includes God.

**Dr. Ansgar Kleist**, 70. German. Some kind of emeritus professor — I have not determined of what. He has the body of a long-distance cyclist and the conversational manner of a man who has been asked to leave several dinner parties but considers this a mark of quality. He brought a nine-hundred-page biography of Alexander von Humboldt and reads it on deck with an expression of adversarial pleasure.

There are others — a retired tech couple from Austin, a performance artist from São Paulo, a Swiss woman of indeterminate purpose — but they haven't distinguished themselves yet, and I have a word count.

And then there is Dr. Henk Palliser, the expedition leader, about whom more in my next dispatch, because I am aware that the profile of the voyage is also a profile of him and I need more time before I commit a take to paper. What I can say now: he met us on the dock. He is tall — 6'3" at least — and lean in a way that reads as disciplinary rather than genetic. Late 50s. South African, originally, though the accent has been sanded into something mid-Atlantic and authoritative. He wore a white cotton shirt that had been ironed recently but not professionally, and the first thing he did was learn every name. Not in the gregarious way of a cruise director. In the quiet, absorptive way of a priest hearing whispered confession. He shook my hand and held it one beat too long and said, "Ms. Ayo. I've read your Varanasi piece three times," and I felt — I will be honest — the specific, targeted warmth of being *known* by a man who has made a practice of knowing. I have spent the last ninety minutes disassembling that warmth to ensure none of it remains operative.

(I trust you've got the legal team on standby, Marcus. These people are who they are, and I'm writing what I see.)

We pulled away from the dock at 4:15 PM. The harbor was flat and silver. Madang's waterfront — the rusted cranes, the betel-nut vendors, the evangelical church with its PA speaker pointing seaward as if God were out there treading water — receded slowly. The jungle was green in a way that made the word *green* feel like a technical failure. The hills behind town were bruised with cloud, and the air coming off the land was so thick and warm it didn't feel like air at all; it felt like breath, like the coast itself was exhaling toward us, and for a moment the light on the water was so heavy and gold that I thought — without thinking, the way you think things you don't mean — *I could be swallowed by this and be grateful*.

Which is a ridiculous sentence, and I blame the antimalarials. The doxycycline makes me maudlin. I've been warned.

Dinner was seared wahoo over taro purée with a coconut-lime emulsion. It was, annoyingly, excellent. Palliser uncorked the wine himself and spoke for ten minutes about the mythology of the Sepik in a voice that the entire table leaned into like plants toward light. I took notes. I did not lean.

I am here to report. The *Leucothea* is a floating reliquary for the desires of people who have run out of things to buy, and I am the most expensive thing aboard: the one they didn't purchase, the one who purchased herself, the one who writes it all down.

More soon. The river mouth is tomorrow. The signal will hold for another day or two.

—C.

## DISPATCH 2: DR. PALLISER'S OBSCENE COMPETENCE

**Filed: March 9 | Coastal transit, approaching Sepik mouth | Word count: 2,260**

---

I have been trying to write the Palliser paragraph for two days and it keeps going wrong.

Here is the assignment I have given myself: a clean, cold, 800-word portrait of a man who charges $42,000 to escort the ultra-wealthy up a river in Papua New Guinea while speaking in a voice that implies he is escorting them somewhere far more interior. The angle is obvious. The angle is *always* obvious with men like this. They are gurus. They are hosts. They are that specific breed of educated, cosmopolitan white man who has learned to frame his privilege as a practice, who converts his access to remote places and indigenous knowledge into a product that tastes like transformation. I have profiled six of them. I could write this in my sleep.

And yet.

The sentences keep curving.

I'll show you what I mean. Here is what I tried to write yesterday:

*Palliser has the practiced ease of a man who has learned that charisma, like good lighting, requires more preparation the more natural it appears.*

Good. Fine. Standard Celeste. Now here is what actually came out:

*Palliser moves through the ship with the unhurried precision of someone who has been here before — not this ship, not this river, but this exact situation, the one where a roomful of wealthy seekers has handed him the full weight of their expectation, and he has caught it without staggering, without even adjusting his stance, as if expectation were something he metabolized, something he could breathe.*

You see the problem. That's not a takedown. That's a *painting*. I don't paint. I X-ray.

Let me try again with the facts.

Henk Palliser, born 1967 in Stellenbosch, South Africa. Studied botanical ethnopharmacology at Leiden, then medical anthropology at Cambridge. Fieldwork in the Sepik basin intermittently since 1994. Published two books: *The Alkaloid Frontier* (academic, well-cited, University of Chicago Press, 2003) and *Hunger as a River* (crossover, more lyrical, Farrar, Straus, 2011, briefly on the *Times* list). The second book is the one his passengers have read. I read it on the flight from Sydney and I want to say it's the kind of pop-ethnobotanical vagueness that sells at airport kiosks to men in business class, except it isn't. It is strange and specific and uncomfortably good, and there is a passage in chapter nine about a Sepik initiation rite that I have reread four times because it makes me feel a way I don't have clean language for.

He started the Sepik Encounter voyages in 2015. They run three times a year, dry season only. He selects the passengers himself. How he selects them is not transparent. Sloane tells me she "applied" — she *applied*, Marcus, like it was a residency — and received a handwritten letter of acceptance six weeks later. Giancarlo says he was invited after a dinner in Antwerp where Palliser was the guest of his mother. Margaux won't say how she came aboard. The Yeungs say they were "called," and I am choosing not to unpack that.

What I observe: He knows the name of every crew member. He knows the name of the helmsman's daughter. He speaks Tok Pisin and what sounds like at least one local language with the fluency of a man who did not learn these languages at a university. When we passed a fishing canoe this morning, he leaned over the rail and called out something that made the fishermen laugh — genuine laughter, not the polite transactional laughter I've heard directed at white expedition leaders elsewhere. He identified a bird by its call while mid-conversation with Kleist about Humboldt's concept of *Naturgemälde*, and he did this without breaking syntax or cadence, the bird's name inserted like a grace note in an aria —

I'm doing it again.

(I know how this reads, Marcus. I'm handling it.)

Here's the clinical version: the man is competent. Obscenely, overwhelmingly competent. And I have built a career on the insight that competence, in men who run these kinds of operations, is always a performance covering an absence — that the guru is always less than the show. That there is always a seam. A gap between the projected self and the operational self, and if you press the right question into that gap, the whole edifice shivers and you can see the ordinary, acquisitive human inside.

I have been pressing. I cannot find the seam.

Two nights now I have found reasons to be near him — on deck after dinner, in the library nook where he sits reading with a single lamp. I ask questions. He answers them fully. He does not deflect. He does not perform mystery. He *engages*, and his engagement is — the only word I have is *adequate*. Not in the sense of "sufficient." In the older sense: proportionate, precisely fitted, as if whatever I bring to the conversation, he has the exact corresponding capacity. I have never felt this with a profile subject. I have always been the sharper one. That asymmetry is the motor of my entire practice.

If he is performing, it is the most perfect performance I have ever encountered, and I do not know what it would mean if he is not.

We entered the mouth of the Sepik this afternoon. The ocean stopped. I don't mean we crossed a boundary — I mean the ocean *stopped*: the water turned brown, the salt left the air, and it was as if we had crossed from one medium to another, from something open into something alimentary. The river is immense, Marcus. I know this intellectually — I've read the hydrology — but knowledge did not prepare me for the Sepik's mouth. It is wide enough that you cannot see both banks simultaneously in places. The water is the brown of strong tea, and it moves with a muscular, intestinal slowness that makes the yacht feel not like a vessel on a surface but like a body being drawn inward. The jungle on both banks is a solid wall — not individual trees, not a landscape, but a *surface*, green and matte and breathing. It breathes. I can hear it at night. Something exhales out there, all night long, and in the early morning mist the river is so still and the light so diffuse that the line between water and air dissolves and the yacht hangs in a middle space, not floating, not flying, just *held*.

I slept eleven hours last night. I never sleep more than six. I woke with the feeling of having been immersed in something warm, and there is a gap in my notebook — three blank pages between an entry at 10 PM and the next entry at 9 AM. I probably just dropped the pen. I was exhausted.

The passengers are settling in. Giancarlo has stopped wearing shoes. Cass Yeung told me at breakfast that she feels "more permeable," and when I asked her what she was becoming permeable *to*, she smiled in a way that was both radiant and slightly uninhabited, and said, "That's the whole question, isn't it?"
