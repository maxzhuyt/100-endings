# The Saint Who Remained

## I.

The sigma was the difficulty. It wanted to lean, as sigmas did when the ink was fresh and the nib too wet, and Alexios had learned across eleven years of this particular manuscript that the capital sigma of *Στέφανος* must stand upright at the head of the saint's name like the saint himself stood—planted, unbending, a man shaped like a refusal. He drew the stroke again. The ink gleamed in the late light that came through the scriptorium's east window, a window cut through three feet of tufa rock so that the light arrived thin and ancient-seeming, as if it had traveled not merely across the Cappadocian valley but across some vaster distance. The sentence he was writing read: *And Stephanos, seeing that the village of Korama would be taken by morning, did not retreat to the high caves where safety was certain, but planted his staff in the dust of the road and turned his face toward the advancing line, saying—*

The first arrows struck the outer wall with a sound like hail on wood, except that the monastery had almost no wood. It was stone and tufa and packed earth, and the sound the arrows made against it was a rapid, dry knocking, as of someone requesting entry with great urgency and no expectation of welcome. Plaster dust drifted from the ceiling. A fine silt of it settled on the wet page, on the word *advancing*, on the long downstroke of the sigma he had just steadied.

Alexios blew across the page. A small cloud rose. The dust cleared from most of the line, but the sigma had caught it in the ink's wetness, and the stroke was furred now, blurred, the letter leaning after all.

In the courtyard, he could hear Cyril shouting to bar the front gate—an unnecessary instruction, since the front gate had been barred for two weeks, but Cyril was a man who managed fear by repeating the procedures of safety, going over them like a rosary. Others were moving. He could hear the particular scraping shuffle of old Makarios being helped to the inner corridor, and the quick footsteps of someone—Eleni, probably—running toward the cistern. These were the sounds of a community of fourteen people who had practiced this response before and who moved through it with the grim efficiency of the rehearsed, each body knowing its corridor, its task, its place against the interior walls.

Alexios dipped his pen. He drew the sigma again on a fresh line, below the spoiled one. This time he controlled the wetness, pressing the nib lightly at the apex and letting the stroke fall with its own weight, and the letter stood upright and clean. He continued the sentence: *—saying, I am the door through which you must pass, and I am a door that does not open.*

A second volley hit. Closer. The sound was different this time—a deeper percussion suggesting the arrows had found the thinner wall near the refectory, or perhaps the wooden shutter of the kitchen window, the one piece of timber they had not yet burned. Someone shouted his name. It was Cyril's voice, carrying the particular exasperation reserved for those who failed to follow the known procedures.

Alexios rose. He lifted the manuscript from the writing desk by its edges, carefully, the way one lifts a sleeping child—supporting the spine, keeping the pages from folding against themselves. The quire he had been working on was uppermost, the ink still wet in places, and he held it angled slightly so that nothing would smear. He pressed the gathered pages against his ribs with his left arm. With his right hand he capped the ink. He placed the pen in its groove. He looked once at the desk to make sure nothing remained that could be damaged by plaster dust or by whatever worse thing might shake loose from the ceiling if the third volley came.

Then he moved into the corridor, holding the manuscript against his body the way a soldier holds a wound—firmly, with pressure, as though what mattered most was keeping the inside from getting out.

---

The monastery had been carved, not built. This was its nature and its advantage. Five hundred years earlier, or perhaps seven hundred—the histories were imprecise, and Alexios, who had once spent a full year trying to reconcile the sources, had settled on a range rather than a date—monks had come to this valley and seen in its soft volcanic rock an invitation. They had cut cells from the cliff face. They had hollowed chapels from cones of tufa that rose from the valley floor like deformed fingers pointing at God. They had painted the interiors with saints and apostles and geometric patterns in the Armenian style, red and ochre and a blue made from ground lapis carried overland from somewhere impossibly far, and over the centuries the painted faces had stared out from their rock walls while empires replaced one another in the valley below with the regularity of seasons. Romans, Persians, Arabs, Seljuks, Mongols, and now the Ottomans, whose raids into this stretch of Cappadocia had intensified in the last five years from occasional to persistent to—as of this autumn—systematic.

The rock could not be burned. This was the thing Alexios told himself when his thoughts became difficult. Whatever else they lacked—and the list of what they lacked had grown long enough to constitute its own manuscript—they had the rock. Arrows could not pierce it. Fire could not consume it. It was patient, porous, and deep, and the monastery's inner chambers extended far enough into the cliff face that a man could walk for five minutes and still be inside, the air growing cooler and more silent, the walls closing gently as if the earth were cupping its hands around him.

He thought of this as he joined the others in the inner corridor. He thought of the rock. He did not think of the sentence he had left unfinished at the writing desk, though his hand, pressed against the quire pages at his side, moved its thumb absently against the edge of the parchment, back and forth, the way a man strokes the ear of a dog he does not realize he is touching.

## II.

He found her on the sixth evening of what Cyril had begun calling the second siege, distinguishing it from the first, briefer incursion two weeks prior, though Alexios objected to the term *siege* in both cases, since a true siege implied a surrounding force sufficient to enforce total enclosure, and what they faced was something more sporadic—raiding parties, archers on the ridge, occasional forays toward the gate. The distinction mattered to him. A siege was historical. A siege was the sort of thing that happened to the saint in his manuscript. What was happening to them was smaller, meaner, less shaped.

He found her in the library room, which was not a true library but a chamber off the main corridor where the community kept those manuscripts and codices that had not been taken—a fact that required a particular grammatical construction in Alexios's mind: *had not been taken,* passive voice, agent unspecified, as though the missing books had simply ceased to exist rather than been carried away on the back of a mule by a particular man on a particular night. The room was small, low-ceilinged, and had been repurposed as sleeping quarters for Thekla when she arrived at the monastery eight months earlier, a widowed laywoman from Caesarea whose precise reasons for coming to a diminished monastic community in a valley under increasing threat had not been fully explained and had not been fully asked about, because the community had learned by then that people came for reasons they would share when ready and sometimes never.

She was sitting against the wall with a lamp beside her and an unbound quire across her lap. His quire. His handwriting. She was reading it aloud.

Her voice was low and even, a voice accustomed to reading—not the singsong of liturgical chant but the flatter, more investigative cadence of someone testing the weight of each clause. She was in the middle of a passage he recognized immediately, because he had written and rewritten it more times than any other section, a passage describing the saint's arrival at the monastery he would found, the moment when Stephanos first saw the valley and understood that the rock itself was God's provision.

"'*And the saint perceived that the stone was not dead matter but living shelter, given as the body is given: not to be fled from but to be inhabited fully, every fissure a capillary of grace, every hollow place a lung through which the breath of God might pass—*'"

She paused. She read the last phrase again, adjusting something Alexios could not identify—a shift of emphasis, perhaps, a slight pressing on *inhabited fully* that made the phrase sound less like description and more like command.

"That is not finished," Alexios said from the doorway. "And it is not meant to be read."

Thekla looked up. She was a woman of perhaps forty-five, angular, her face weathered in the particular way of people who had spent years in the high plateaus east of Caesarea, where the wind dried the skin and the sun carved lines around the eyes that looked like the work of a deliberate and not unkind sculptor. She did not appear embarrassed.

"Not meant to be read," she repeated. "A manuscript not meant to be read."

"Not meant to be read *yet*. It is unfinished. Eleven years unfinished."

"Yes. Eleven years is a long time to be unfinished."

He stepped into the room and held out his hand for the quire. She did not give it to him. Instead she turned a page, scanning, and found another passage. She read:

"'*Stephanos called his brothers to him and said: A man who leaves the place God has planted him is a man who has pulled his own roots from the ground, and such a man will find that he carries the soil's absence with him wherever he goes, a lightness in the chest where weight should be, for we were not made for freedom of movement but for the freedom of staying, which is the harder and truer freedom—*'"

She looked up. "Who is this meant to sound like?"

"The saint."

"The saint."

"Stephanos of Korama. You know who the manuscript concerns."

"I do." She read the passage again, silently this time, her lips barely moving, and then she asked: "Are you sure?"

"Am I sure of what?"

"That this is what the saint sounds like."

Alexios took the quire from her hands. She let it go, neither resisting nor assisting, and he was left holding it with a force that had been calibrated for a struggle that did not materialize, so that the pages bent slightly under his grip and he had to ease his fingers to avoid creasing the parchment.

"The saint sounds like the saint," he said. "That is tautology, Thekla, and tautology is the refuge of people who do not wish to say what they mean."

"Then we have something in common," she said, and turned back to the second book she had been reading, a psalter that belonged to the library and therefore to everyone, and which she opened now with the clear and faintly maddening patience of a woman who had said exactly what she intended to say and was content to let it work.

He took the quire back to the scriptorium. He placed it with the other completed sections, in the chest he had fashioned from a crate that once held dried figs. He did not write that evening. He told himself this was because the light was failing, which was true. He told himself the ink was too cold, which was possibly true. He did not tell himself that the passage Thekla had read aloud—*we were not made for freedom of movement but for the freedom of staying*—was reverberating in his skull in a voice that was not the saint's voice and not his own voice but a third voice, one he knew the way the tongue knows the location of a broken tooth, by the flinch of contact and the discipline of avoidance.

## III.

Brother Ioannis fell ill on the ninth day, and Photini on the tenth. The sickness was intestinal, violent, probably caused by the water in the deeper cistern, which they had been forced to use when the upper cistern ran low. Ioannis was young, barely twenty, and fought it with the animal stamina of youth, sweating through his robes and clutching his abdomen and saying, between bouts, that he was better, he was better, it was passing, though it was visibly not passing. Photini was older and quieter about her suffering, which made it worse to witness. She lay on her side in the infirmary cave, which was simply the widest of the interior chambers with a few straw pallets arranged along the wall, and she made no sound except a thin, rhythmic exhalation that Alexios eventually realized was prayer stripped to its most elemental form—not words, not even syllables, just the shape of breath that the mouth makes when it has been saying *Kyrie eleison* for so long that the consonants have worn away and only the vowels remain.

Everyone was awake that night. The pretense of a schedule—Matins, Lauds, the daylit hours of work and reading, Vespers, Compline, sleep—had thinned like the plaster on the walls. People slept when they could in whatever corner they occupied. Time had become a function of the siege rather than the sun. There was the time when arrows came and the time when arrows did not come. There was the time when the sick needed water and the time, shorter, when they did not.

Alexios was sitting in the corridor between the infirmary and the library room, his back against the tufa wall, the manuscript across his knees. He was not writing. He was reading what he had written, which was not the same thing and which he did only rarely, because reading his own prose called up a self-consciousness that disturbed the fluid, almost involuntary motion of composition. When he wrote, the saint's voice came through him like water through limestone, finding its own channels. When he read, he could see the channels, and seeing them made him wonder who had cut them.

Thekla came from the infirmary with an empty cup. She had been giving Photini water mixed with a little honey—the last of the honey, a jar Makarios had hidden so well that even he had forgotten it until the crisis shook it loose from his memory like the plaster dust from the ceiling.

She sat across from him. The lamp between them was clay and ancient and burned olive oil they could not afford to burn, and it threw a light that made both their faces look carved from the same stone as the walls.

"How is she?" Alexios asked.

"She is praying. She will pray until it resolves one way or the other."

He nodded. In the infirmary, Ioannis coughed—a deep, rib-cracking sound followed by a silence in which you could hear the young man deciding not to groan.

"You should sleep," Alexios said.

"So should you. But you are reading your manuscript instead." She set the cup down. "May I ask you about Gregorios?"

The name arrived in the corridor like a change in temperature. Alexios felt his fingers tighten on the edges of the parchment.

"What about him?"

"You never speak of him."

"Others speak of him. Cyril speaks of him. Makarios speaks of him too often."

"Cyril calls him *the former abbot.* Makarios calls him *poor Gregorios,* which is a way of not speaking about him while appearing to. I am asking about the man. The man who was here and then was not."

"He left," Alexios said. "Three years and four months ago. Between Vespers and Matins. He took a mule, provisions, and three manuscripts from this library. He left no letter, no instruction, no designation of a successor. He left."

"He abandoned you."

"He left."
