# The Roots and the Remember

## I.

Before the first word of any scripture was pressed into clay, there was rain, and there was light, and there was the slow argument between mineral and root over who owned the dark below.

It does not think in words. Let that be understood now, before the temptation arises to make it speak as you speak. It thinks in pressures. In the lateral conversation of calcium throughite. In the moment, repeated ten million times, when a root-hair finds water and the finding radiates upward through cambium and sapwood in a signal that is not joy—there is no joy—but is the cessation of a seeking, which is close enough.

It was old when the walled city went up. It remembers wall—not as history, not as architecture, but as a change in the water table. Foundations sank. Stone pressed aquifers into new geometries. The roots adjusted. The roots always adjust. Adjustment is the only intelligence that matters across sufficient time.

It registered the Mughals as a shift in soil composition. New animals arrived—horses in quantities that changed the nitrogen cycle of the streets. New rhythms of foot-traffic. New vibrations in the earth: the striking of chisels on marble, a frequency it had not felt before, sharper than sandstone-work, almost bell-like through dense ground. It grew toward the vibration, because that is what roots do when they encounter the unfamiliar—they do not flee; they investigate. It sent a root sixty-two meters toward what would later be called the Badshahi Mosque, and the root tasted the mortar, and the mortar was not food, so the root stopped.

It does not think in words, but it collects vibrations the way a riverbed collects sediment: involuntarily, completely, without discrimination. Every footfall within the radius of its root system deposits a vibration-signature that persists in the cellulose the way tree rings persist—not as a memory recalled but as a memory *recorded*, structural, part of the wood itself. Slice it open. Count the rings. But between the rings, in the density variations no saw is fine enough to reveal, there are four hundred years of footsteps. There are armies. There are weddings. There are single men stumbling home in the dark.

The British arrived and the water changed. This is not metaphor. They built new drains. The water table shifted northeast by an imperceptible degree that was not imperceptible to roots. It spent eleven years adjusting. It lost a tertiary root-cluster to the new dryness on its southern side, and the loss registered as a slow absence, a limb-forgetting, the way a river forgets a channel it no longer uses.

It does not understand religion. Let that also be understood. When the azaan began sounding from new minarets, it registered a frequency: human voice, amplified by architecture, arriving five times daily with a regularity that the tree—which is, in its own way, an organism built entirely on regularity, on the daily negotiation with sunlight, on the seasonal dropping and the seasonal return—recognized as kin. Not the content. The *rhythm.* When bells arrived in church towers, it registered a different frequency, metallic, less nuanced in overtone but more penetrating through soil. When the gurdwara's kirtan started, it was a third vibration-signature, entering through the aerial roots that hung like listening instruments from the lower canopy. Each frequency deposited itself in the wood. Layer over layer. The tree does not know it is a record of every prayer ever uttered within two hundred meters of its trunk. It simply is.

But the children. The children were different.

It does not think in words. But it thinks in *voices.*

The distinction must be made carefully. The adult human voice is a vibration like any other—wind, wheel on stone, the crack of a monsoon-split branch. It enters the bark and disperses. But a child's voice—specifically, the voice of a child at play, a child unperforming, a child making sounds for the sheer caloric excess of being young and alive—operates at a frequency that does something to the phloem that no other vibration does. It is not heard. It is *metabolized.* The sugars move faster. The auxin production shifts. The tree, when children play beneath it, *grows toward them* in the same way it grows toward light, not because it decides to but because their voices are, to its chemistry, indistinguishable from sun.

It has collected children's voices for four centuries. They are laid down in the wood like songs in amber. It cannot play them back. It cannot *recall.* But when a new child arrives, the tree responds to the new voice in the context of all the voices that preceded it, the way a river responds to new rain in the context of every rain that has ever fed it. The new voice joins the old ones. It becomes part of the tree's body. The tree gets, in some sense that no language is adequate to describe, *larger*—not in diameter but in the density of what it holds.

In the 1930s, five new voices began.

They came gradually, as children come to a tree—not by appointment but by the slow discovery that a banyan, with its cathedral architecture of aerial roots and its shade that holds the cool of morning well into the blaze of afternoon, is the finest thing a street has to offer. The first voice arrived in spring. The last arrived that winter. And by the time all five were present, the tree had begun doing something it had never done in four hundred years of collecting.

It had begun to listen.

## II.

They came in a knot, always. Five of them tangled like the banyan's own roots, impossible to say where one ended and another began. The street's adults, watching from windows and storefronts, could not always tell whose child was whose, which was precisely the point, though the children did not know they were making a point. They were simply moving.

They ran. They ran through the gali where the coppersmith hammered vessels into being and the sound made a tunnel of ringing that they passed through like thread through a needle. They ran past the halwai's shop where jalebis coiled in oil and the smell was a wall you hit with your whole face. They ran past the church on the corner and the gurdwara behind the cloth market and the fire temple whose gate was always closed but through whose walls a faint sweet smoke leaked like a secret. They ran to the tree.

The tree was theirs. This was not a claim anyone disputed because no one else wanted it—the adults found the hanging roots unsettling, too much like fingers, and the shade was so dense it felt, past a certain hour, less like shade and more like a room with a ceiling. But the children wanted it. The children wanted precisely that room. They wanted the way you could push through the curtain of aerial roots and be inside—inside a living thing, surrounded by pillars that were not stone but growing, a cathedral that added a new column every monsoon season.

Inside, they convened.

Disputes from the morning—whose turn, whose marble, whose fault—were adjudicated. A tribunal formed naturally, the way water finds its own level: Harpreet presided because he had a voice that carried an unlikely authority for a boy of nine, a voice that would later fill a courtroom in Chandigarh and then, much later, fail him entirely when he needed it most. Harpreet would listen to both sides and then say something so obviously fair that even the aggrieved party recognized it as fair, which was the most infuriating thing about him.

Farid climbed. He was always above them, balanced on a branch or swinging from a root, looking down at the proceedings with the benevolent detachment of a boy who found the ground insufficient. His hands were permanently scraped, the bark-marks on his palms like a map of every route he'd taken into the canopy. Later, people would say he climbed so high to be closer to God, and Farid would laugh and say he climbed so high because the figs were sweeter at the top, and both things were true.

Amrita translated. This is the only word for what she did. When the tree made sounds—and it did, every child knew it did, the adults could say what they liked about wind and settling wood—Amrita was the one who said what it meant. She would press her ear flat against the trunk, both palms spread on the bark, and her face would go still the way water goes still before it shows you what's underneath, and then she would report. _The tree says it rained two days ago in the mountains and the water just now reached its deepest root._ Or: _The tree says a parrot died in the top branches last night and it is taking the parrot back into itself._ The others did not fully believe her. The others did not fully disbelieve her. She occupied the essential position of the oracle: she was the one through whom the inhuman spoke in human terms, and the accuracy of her translations could never be verified, which was exactly what made them sacred.

Denzil remembered. He was the group's archive, its external hard drive before such a concept existed—the boy who could tell you not just what had happened but exactly when, in what order, and what everyone had been wearing. "That was the Tuesday Farid fell," he would say, "and Amrita was in the blue dupatta, not the green one, the green one was the Thursday before, when we found the cat." This was not a skill anyone taught him. It was a compulsion, an involuntary recording, as if his mind refused to let any moment pass unregistered. It made him invaluable. It would later make him unbearable. To remember everything after Partition is a kind of sentence.

And Roshni asked. She asked questions the way other children threw stones into wells—to find out how deep things went. "Why is the bark this color?" "Why do the roots go down and not sideways?" "Why does my grandmother pray to a fire and your grandmother prays to a book and Farid's grandmother prays to a wall and the wall and the fire and the book all seem to work the same?" No one could answer her. Harpreet tried, once, with his adjudicator's logic: because different peoplelike different things. Roshni looked at him with the particular pity of a child who has recognized an adult-type answer coming from a child-type mouth. "That's not a reason," she said. "That's just a description."

Together they were a single organism. This is not a metaphor. Watch them move: when Farid climbed, the others spotted him—Harpreet below and to the left, Denzil below and to the right, Amrita calling out handholds she said the tree was revealing, Roshni asking what would happen if he fell (not morbidly; scientifically). When Amrita listened to the tree, the others arranged themselves in a perimeter of silence—Farid in the canopy watching for adults who might interrupt, Harpreet enforcing the quiet, Denzil recording the translation the moment it came, Roshni preparing her follow-up questions. When Roshni asked something unanswerable, all five of them stared at the question together, turning it like a stone, and the thinking they did was collective, a kind of murmuration, a flock-mind that no single bird could replicate alone.

Food moved between them without thought. Farid's mother made keema samosas on Fridays and Farid brought six, always six, because his mother understood without being told that her son was one-fifth of something that required feeding as a whole. Amrita brought laddoos after pujas. Harpreet brought karah prasad, still warm from the gurdwara's kitchen, and Roshni brought the Parsi biscuits her aunt baked in industrial quantities—hard, butter-dense, flavored with cardamom, structurally capable of surviving a monsoon. Denzil brought whatever his mother had made for the church bake sale, which was usually a cake so dense with dried fruit it could anchor a boat. They ate everything. They had eaten everything together since before they understood that the everything came from different gods.

On Eid, they went to Farid's house and his grandmother put money in all ten of their hands—her own grandchild's and the eight hands that were, to her accounting, equally her grandchild's. On Diwali, they arranged diyas along the banyan's roots, and the tree became a constellation, and Amrita said the tree told her it could feel the heat of each small flame individually, like ten warm fingertips pressing its skin. On Baisakhi, they danced in Harpreet's courtyard. At Christmas, Denzil's mother sang "O Holy Night" in a voice that made even the street dogs stop and point their ears toward the sound, and the five children sat in a row on the church steps and the song went through them like a river goes through stones—shaping them without their knowing. On the days when Roshni went with her grandmother to the fire temple, she went alone, because non-Parsis could not enter, and the other four sat outside the gate and waited, and she came out smelling of sandalwood and said, "The fire was high today," and they nodded as if this information was vital, because it was.

Their festivals bled into each other. This is the point. This is the thing that must be understood before everything else can be understood. By the time they were ten, not one of them could say with certainty which sweet belonged to which occasion. Denzil's Christmas cake appeared at Eid. Farid's samosas were consumed on Gurpurab. The laddoos were, by universal agreement, suitable for all events at all times. The calendar of the street was not Hindu or Muslim or Sikh or Christian or Parsi. It was a calendar of sugar and oil and the specific alchemy of a grandmother's hands, and the festivals were not five religions but one unbroken excuse for abundance.

The adults knew. The adults smiled or frowned depending on their temperament, but the adults also ate the samosas and the laddoos and the cake, and the adults, for the most part, let the children be what children are before the world tells them what they must become instead.

Underneath them, the tree took it in. The chemical signature of five bodies: their sweat, carrying different diets, different spices, different traces of whatever soap their mothers used. Five distinct biochemical profiles that the tree absorbed through bark and aerial root and registered as—not individuals, the tree did not think in individuals—as a *pattern.* A five-part pattern. A chord.

And then, one evening, the chord resolved into something else.

---

It was almost dark. The hour when the gali empties and the heat pulls back like a tide retreating. They were inside the root-curtain, all five, sprawled on the ground that the tree's canopy had kept cool all day. Amrita had her palms on the trunk. Farid was low for once, sitting with his back against a root. Harpreet was between Denzil and Roshni, arbitrating nothing for once, just breathing.

The sound came from below.

It was not wind. Wind moves laterally and the ear knows it. This came up. Through the ground, through the roots, through the trunk, through the bark under Amrita's palms—a vibration that started so low it was felt in the stomach before the ears registered anything. It rose. It became, for a moment, almost a tone—a hum, a resonance, the way a struck bell continues to speak long after the striking.

Amrita's eyes went wide.

"It's talking," she said.

"What's it saying?" Farid asked, from reflex, because he always asked.

Amrita pressed harder. The bark was warm and that was wrong—the bark should have been cooler now, the sun was gone—but it was warm the way a living body is warm, warm from inside.

"It's saying—" She stopped. She frowned.

_"Suno,"_ she said. Listen.
