# The Light Over Roswell

## I.

The van smelled like bail agreements and sun-heated vinyl and a pine air freshener that had stopped working in March. My father had hung a new one over the rearview mirror in April, and when that one died too he hung another over the first, so now there were three little green trees hanging in a cluster, giving off nothing, and beneath them the van still smelled like what it was: a mobile office for a man whose actual office had been repossessed along with the lease and the ficus and, I assumed, whatever dignity the ficus had been standing in for.

I was reading *The Brothers Karamazov* for the second time. The first time I'd read it in ninth grade on a dare from Mrs. Gutierrez, who taught AP English to twelve students in a portable classroom and who must have seen something in me that she wanted to either cultivate or punish. The second time I read it because we'd been parked outside a Motel 6 in Artesia for four hours and my phone was dead and the charger only worked when the engine was running and my father had said to keep the engine off because the idle sounded wrong and the last thing he needed was a dead van on top of everything else.

Everything else. I could catalog it. I had been cataloging it for two years, the way you catalog the contents of a room when you're trapped in it: not for any purpose, but because the inventory is what your mind does instead of screaming.

Item: one bail bondsman's license, suspended eighteen months ago by the state of New Mexico following a complaint filed by a client who alleged my father had neglected to file a forfeiture motion, which was true, because he'd been in the hospital for what he called a "cardiac event" and what his doctor called a mild heart attack brought on by stress, poor diet, and five years of pretending he was still the man he'd been before the disciplinary hearing.

Item: one disciplinary hearing, three years ago, convened by the Second Judicial District Court, resulting in a formal sanction for commingling client funds with operating expenses. My father would want me to note that no money was stolen. This is true. What happened was sloppier and sadder than theft: he'd been keeping both accounts in the same ledger because he couldn't afford a bookkeeper and his system was a yellow legal pad and a mechanical pencil, and for a few months the columns had blurred, and a judge had looked at the blur and seen what judges see, which is a man who cannot be trusted with other people's money.

Item: one daughter. Seventeen. Unlicensed to drive in the state of New Mexico, licensed by her father to drive this specific van on roads he deemed low-risk, which turned out to be most roads in southeastern New Mexico, where the traffic was sparse and the cops had larger concerns and my father needed someone behind the wheel while he worked the phone.

He worked the phone now. I could hear him through the cracked window—he was standing in the motel parking lot with his back to me, one hand shading his eyes against the four o'clock sun, the other pressing his cell phone to his ear. His voice carried the way it always did: too loud, too certain, a voice that had been trained in courtrooms and now operated in parking lots with the same procedural confidence, as if volume were a form of jurisdiction.

"Eddie. Eddie, listen. I'm not asking you to violate a confidence. I'm asking you to tell me if he's still driving the Dodge. That's a yes or no. That's not a confidence, that's a vehicle."

The bail schedule for Lincoln County was on the passenger seat, annotated in my father's handwriting. I'd learned to read his annotations the way scholars learn to read marginalia in medieval manuscripts: as a record of obsession encoded in shorthand. Next to the name JAMES WILLIAM DEVERAUX he'd written $80K and circled it, then underlined it, then drawn an arrow pointing to the words *make things right*.

"Make things right" was my father's phrase for the Deveraux job. He used it the way people use phrases they've polished smooth through repetition, phrases that have stopped meaning what they say and started meaning something more compressed: *I have failed at nearly everything and this is what's left.*

James William Deveraux. Forty-six. Former CFO of a mid-size agricultural supply company based in Roswell. Charged with embezzling $2.3 million over a period of five years through a series of fabricated vendor accounts that, according to the charging documents, demonstrated "a sustained and sophisticated pattern of financial deception." Bonded out at $200,000. My father's exposure: $80,000, which was not his money—it was a collateral arrangement with a surety company in Albuquerque that had extended him credit on the strength of his reputation, which was the reputation he'd had before the sanction, which meant the credit was a kind of loan against a version of himself that no longer existed.

Deveraux had skipped. Missed his court date, missed the grace period, gone. My father had six weeks to bring him back or lose everything, and "everything" at this point was not a figure of speech. It was $80,000 he would owe and could not pay, plus the van, plus whatever arrangement he and my mother's ghost had made with the mortgage company—I say ghost because she wasn't dead, only in Tucson, which my father treated as a similar condition.

So we drove. Las Cruces to Alamogordo to Artesia, following the skip trace, which was a mix of database searches, old contacts, and my father's dogged, almost animal instinct for where frightened men go. He'd been a bail bondsman for twenty-two years. Before the sanction, before the suspension, before the yellow legal pad and the blurred columns, he had been good at it. Not good the way people in movies are good—no cleverness, no intuition that looked like magic. Good the way a plumber is good: he understood the system's pipes, its pressure points, the places where things backed up and overflowed, and he could get his hands in and clear the line.

He talked about his defendants—his "people," he called them—without contempt. This was the thing about my father that I could not resolve into a single judgment, the thing that made me keep reading Dostoevsky instead of something that would give me cleaner answers.

"Most of them," he'd told me once, parked outside a courthouse in Albuquerque, "most of them just need a door. They're in a room and the room is closing on them and they need someone to open a door. That's what bail is. You're not saying they're innocent. You're saying they deserve a door."

He meant it. That was what I couldn't resolve. He meant it, and he also commingled funds and lost his license and drove his daughter through the desert chasing a man who'd stolen $2.3 million, and the meaning and the mess were not different things that happened to coexist in the same man—they were the same thing, expressed in two registers, the way a chord contains its own dissonance.

Dostoevsky understood this. Or at least his characters did, which was close enough.

My father got back in the van. His face was red from the sun and tight around the mouth.

"Roswell," he said.

"Why Roswell?"

"Because Eddie says the Dodge was spotted near the fairgrounds, and because the festival starts tomorrow, which means ten thousand people in a town that usually has fifty thousand, which means cash businesses everywhere, which means a man who knows how to hide money has a lot of places to wash what he's carrying."

He pulled the door shut. The three dead air fresheners swayed.

"Also," he said, "because where else would he go. Where else does a man go when he's made a mess so big he can't see the edges of it."

I didn't ask what he meant. I started the van. The idle sounded wrong.

## II.

The motel in Roswell was called the Desert Star Inn, and it had the distinction of being one of the few lodgings in town that had not redecorated for the festival. No alien-shaped neon, no flying saucer murals, no WE COME IN PEACE on the marquee. Just a plain stucco rectangle with a red door on every room and a parking lot that needed resurfacing. My father approved. He was suspicious of establishments that tried too hard, which I thought was a reasonable position for a man who had tried too hard and been sanctioned for it.

We checked in at six. By six-thirty my father was at the window with binoculars, scanning the parking lot of the Denny's across the road, because Eddie had amended his tip: the Dodge might be a Silverado now, silver or gray, New Mexico plates, and the driver might have a beard.

"Might," I said.

"Might's good enough."

"Is it? In Dostoevsky, 'might' gets people sent to Siberia."

"Put the book down and watch the east end of the lot."

I put the book down. I watched the east end of the lot. A man came out of the Denny's carrying a to-go bag, and he was not James William Deveraux, and he got into a Honda and drove away, and the lot was empty again, and the light over Roswell was the pale amber of a place that wants to be remembered for something other than what it's remembered for.

The accountant arrived at seven-fifteen.

I say "the accountant" because that's what he was, although his title was Senior Forensic Analyst with the Internal Revenue Service, Criminal Investigation Division. His name was Peter Linh. He was forty-one, though I didn't know that then—I guessed late thirties based on the way he moved, which was deliberate and unhurried, the gait of a man who has calculated the optimal walking speed for arriving at places without appearing either eager or reluctant. He was wearing a white dress shirt with the sleeves rolled precisely to the elbow, khakis that held a crease despite the heat, and shoes that I recognized, with the pattern-matching brain of a girl who has spent too much time in parking lots, as the kind of shoes a person buys when they want to walk a lot but refuse to wear sneakers.

He parked a silver government sedan three spaces from our van. He got out. He looked at the van. He looked at the motel. He opened his trunk and removed a briefcase and a single rolling suitcase, both black, both modest, and he walked to the office and checked in, and four minutes later emerged and walked to a room on the second floor, directly above ours.

My father lowered the binoculars.

"IRS," he said.

"How do you know?"

"Government plates. Gov sedan. The shoes."

I was gratified to have the shoes confirmed.

"Also," my father said, "I know his name. Peter Linh. He's been on Deveraux since before the indictment. He's the one who built the case. Forensic accounting—he's the one who found the fake vendor accounts."

"So he's here for Deveraux too."

My father set the binoculars on the nightstand. He sat on the bed. The bed made a sound that I associated with every motel bed in New Mexico: the sound of springs that had been asked to support too many bodies carrying too much weight, both literal and otherwise.

"He's here for Deveraux's money. I'm here for Deveraux's body. And right now Deveraux's body and Deveraux's money are in the same place, which means Peter Linh and I are about to have a problem."

The problem materialized at the vending machine at eight o'clock. I was buying a Sprite because the tap water tasted like copper, and Peter Linh was buying a water because he was the kind of man who bought water from vending machines rather than drink what was free, and my father was buying nothing because he'd followed me out to the vending machine for the sole purpose of intercepting Peter Linh, which I understood only in retrospect, when my father said, "Mr. Linh," in his courtroom voice.

Peter Linh turned. He looked at my father the way you look at a fact you've already computed but hoped wouldn't appear in your data set.

"Mr. Gunderson."

"Ray."

"Mr. Gunderson, I'm not in a position to discuss—"

"I'm not asking you to discuss anything. I'm telling you that I know you're here for the same man, and I'm suggesting that we not get in each other's way, because if we do, the only person who benefits is the one we're both looking for."

Peter Linh held his water bottle with both hands, one on the cap and one on the base, as if it were a document he was reviewing for accuracy. He looked at my father. He looked at me. He looked back at my father.

"Your daughter?" he said.

"My daughter."

"She should be in school."

"She's homeschooled."

This was technically true. I was schooled at home in the sense that the van was home and I was schooled in the sense that I read whatever I wanted and took the tests online and passed them, and what I wanted to read was Dostoevsky and case law and the search function on the New Mexico Courts website, and if this did not constitute an education then education was a narrower thing than I thought it was.

Peter Linh appeared to perform a rapid internal audit of whether my educational status fell within his professional jurisdiction. It did not. He moved on.

"I have no interest in your bond," he said to my father. "I have no interest in physical custody. I need his financial records. Specifically, I need access to whatever digital storage he's maintaining, because we believe he has documentation of accounts in—" He stopped himself. "I'm not authorized to share investigative details."

"I'm not asking for details. I'm asking you to let me know when you find him so I can bring him in, and in return I'll let you know when I find him so you can take his laptop."

"That's not how federal investigations—"

"That's how *this* works, Mr. Linh, because you're one man in a town that's about to fill with sixty thousand people dressed like aliens, and I'm one man with a suspended license and a seventeen-year-old driver, and Deveraux is smarter than both of us or he wouldn't have stolen two million dollars from people who count fertilizer for a living."

Peter Linh looked at the water bottle. He was quiet for a moment that I could feel him measuring, parceling into units of acceptable silence before response.

"I'll consider it," he said.

He went upstairs. I opened my Sprite. My father leaned against the vending machine and watched him go.

"He'll consider it," my father said, in the voice he used for judges' rulings and weather forecasts—the voice that acknowledged the statement while dismissing its relevance to what was going to happen next.

What happened next was that Peter Linh came downstairs at nine-thirty with a manila folder and knocked on our door and sat in the single chair by the window while my father sat on the bed and I sat on the floor with my Dostoevsky and listened to two men negotiate the terms of a collaboration that neither would call a collaboration.

The terms were simple: share sightings, don't interfere with apprehension, don't share information with local law enforcement (my father because his license was suspended and any police contact could result in charges; Peter Linh because the IRS investigation was federal and he did not trust local departments to maintain operational security near a tourist event). The terms were also, I understood, impossible: each man needed Deveraux for a purpose that precluded the other's purpose, because the body could flee while you counted the money, and the money could be destroyed while you tackled the body, and both men knew this, and they agreed anyway, because the alternative was to work alone in a town that was about to become a carnival.

After Peter Linh left, my father turned off the light and lay on the bed in his clothes.

"What do you think?" he said.

"I think he's a true believer."

"In what?"

"In the numbers. He thinks if the books balance, the world is fair."

My father was quiet for a while.

"There are worse things to believe in," he said.

This was so precisely the thing a man who had failed to keep his own books straight would say that I almost laughed. I didn't. I opened Dostoevsky to the Grand Inquisitor chapter and read by the light of my phone until my phone, too, died, and I lay in the dark listening to my father breathe and the traffic on Main Street, which was already thickening with early arrivals, cars and RVs and vans like ours, all of them pulling toward Roswell, toward the festival, toward whatever it was that people came here looking for and never found and came back for again.
