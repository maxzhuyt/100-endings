# The Fruit Vendor's Wife

## I.

The word came on a boy's legs. He was nine or ten, Filippo Giarratano's youngest, and he came tearing down Decatur Street with his shirt untucked and his voice already breaking from the running. *Assolti. Assolti.* Acquitted. All of them.

Concetta was stacking blood oranges into a pyramid, the way her mother had taught her in Palermo — largest at the base, smallest at the crown, each one turned so the navel faced outward — and when the boy screamed the word a third time, her husband dropped the crate he was carrying and crossed himself so violently he struck his own lip with his thumbnail. A thread of blood appeared on his mouth. He didn't notice. He was already embracing the man at the next stall, and then the woman beyond that, and then someone she didn't recognize, and the whole street contracted into a knot of bodies and noise. Somewhere a concertina started up. A woman was weeping into her hands.

Concetta set the last orange on the pyramid.

She should have felt it. She searched herself for the feeling she was supposed to have — relief, or joy, or at least the sudden absence of the dread that had lived in her chest for five months — and found instead a quietness that frightened her. It was the wrong kind of quiet. Not peace. Attention.

She looked past the celebration, past the fruit stalls and the fish stalls and the luggage carts, to the corner of Chartres Street where a group of American men stood outside the Pickwick Club. They were not celebrating. They were not dispersing. They were clustering.

She knew that posture.

She had grown up in the Kalsa, the old Arab quarter of Palermo, three houses from the courtyard where Salvatore Ferrara held his councils. She had seen that posture her entire girlhood — shoulders drawn close, heads inclined, weight on the balls of the feet, the whole body organized around a center of gravity that was not the speaker but the decision. The decision was always violence. It was the posture men assumed when they had already agreed on what would happen and were now only settling the when and the how. It was the same in Palermo as it was in New Orleans. It was the same at the corner of Chartres Street as it was in the Kalsa. She had crossed an ocean and learned a new language and married a gentle man who sold fruit, and the posture was the same.

One of the Pickwick Club men — she would later learn his name was Wickliffe — turned his head and caught her looking. Their eyes met. He held her gaze for exactly as long as it took to dismiss her, and then he turned back to the circle.

Concetta picked up the half-empty crate, told her husband she felt a headache, and walked home. She locked the front door and then the back door and then the gate to the courtyard. She did not light a lamp. She sat in the kitchen in the last of the afternoon light and listened to the celebration moving through the streets like a river with no idea it was approaching a falls.

The acquittal was the end of the story. Everyone believed this. The judge had said it. The lawyers had said it. Her husband was saying it now to anyone who would listen: *È finita. It is finished.* But Concetta had read the posture at the Pickwick Club, and she knew, the way she had always known such things — in her body, in the architecture of her attention — that the acquittal was not the end. The law had spoken, and the men on Chartres Street had heard it, and what they had heard was not justice. What they had heard was permission.

---

She did not sleep well. Her husband came home sour with wine and joyful and tried to hold her, and she let him, but she lay awake after, watching the patterns the streetlamp made through the shutters. Bars. The light came through the slats and laid bars across the ceiling, and she watched them and thought: *They are still in the parish prison. Acquitted, but still in the cells.* The formal charges had been dropped on some and mistrialed on others, but the men had not been released. A bureaucratic delay, the lawyers said. Paperwork. It would be sorted in the morning.

She thought of the man at the Pickwick Club, the one named Wickliffe, the way his eyes had moved over her face with the efficient incuriosity of a man inventorying crates on a dock. She was a crate. She was cargo. She was one of the things on the manifest that could be sorted in the morning.

Outside, a dog barked and would not stop barking, and Concetta listened to it the way she had listened to dogs bark in Palermo, as instruments of atmospheric pressure, as things that felt the change before the weather arrived.

## II.

She went to the French Market at half past four, as she always did, while the air still smelled of river and the gas lamps along the colonnade were still lit. The market was its own country at that hour — Black oystermen and Sicilian greengrocers and Choctaw women selling filé powder and Creole butchers in aprons stiff with blood, all of them moving in the dark with the practiced silence of people who understood that the city's first transaction of the day was not commerce but coexistence.

Emmanuel was at his station, the stone counter where he had shucked oysters every morning for eleven years. He was already working when she arrived. The pile of shells to his left was a foot high. His knife — the short, blunt-nosed blade he called *Marie*, the only possession she had ever heard him name — flicked and twisted and pried, and the oysters opened with a sound like small bones surrendering.

"Emmanuel."

"Mornin'."

"The verdict."

"Heard it."

He did not look up. His hands moved. Left hand held the oyster against the stone, thumb pressed into the hinge. Right hand inserted the blade. A twist. A scrape. The flesh released. He placed the half-shell on the wooden tray, already accumulating a galaxy of grey and silver and mother-of-pearl. His hands were enormous. The scars on them were layered in a way that made her think of tree rings — the old cuts smooth and white, the newer ones still pink, the freshest crusted over and disappearing under a film of oyster liquor that gave his skin the color of wet slate. They were the hands of a man who had reached into sharp things every day for over a decade, and they were never still. Even when he paused to stack the tray, his fingers drummed the stone.

"Emmanuel. Look at me."

He didn't. The knife went into the next oyster.

"Something's wrong," she said.

"Lot of things wrong."

"With you. Something's wrong with you today."

The knife stopped. He held it very still against the shell, the way a man holds a pen against a page when he is deciding whether to write the sentence. Then the blade moved, the oyster opened, the flesh was scraped, the shell was placed. He reached for the next.

"Three nights ago," Concetta said. "The celebration. Everyone in the street. You weren't there."

"Wasn't my celebration."

"You've worked beside us for years. These are your —"

"Mrs. Concetta." He said her name carefully, the way he always did, with the accent on the second syllable that turned it into something almost French. "These are my nothing. These are people I sell oysters next to. Ain't nobody on that street that's my anything, and ain't nobody on that street that I'm their anything. That's how this works and you know it."

She did know it. She stood there knowing it and hating that she knew it and hating more that she had tried, for one sentence, to pretend she didn't.

"I need to ask you something."

"Then ask."

"The night Hennessy was shot. October. Were you working?"

The knife stopped again. This time it stayed stopped. He set the oyster down on the stone and placed the blade beside it and looked at her for the first time. His eyes were the color of the coffee they served at Café Du Monde — not brown, not black, but the darkness between those two words, the darkness that absorbs light and returns nothing. In those eyes she saw the calculation that preceded every sentence a Black man spoke to a white woman in the city of New Orleans in the year of our Lord 1891.

"I was emptying slop," he said. "Buckets. Down the alley back of Girod Street."

He stopped. She waited. The market moved around them — a cart of cabbages rolled past, wheels grinding on the flagstones — and in the small eddy of silence between his stall and the world, Emmanuel picked up the knife again and opened another oyster as if the rhythm of the work was the only thing that could carry the weight of what he was about to say.

"Heard the shots. Four, five. Then the shouting. Then a man come running down the alley. Not away from the precinct. Toward it. Coming from the direction where the shots was. Running toward Basin Street. Toward the station house."

"What did he look like?"

"Didn't see his face. Saw his coat. Police coat. The brass buttons. They caught the gas lamp when he turned the corner. I know a police coat, Mrs. Concetta. I have had considerable occasion to study police coats."

She felt the blood leave her face. She reached out and put her hand on the stone counter, and it was wet and cold and real.

"This man — running toward the precinct —"

"After the shooting. Yes, ma'am."

"Not an Italian."

"Not an Italian."

"And you've told —"

"Who am I gonna tell that to?"

He said it without inflection. Without bitterness, without self-pity, without the dramatic weight that a lesser man might have placed on the words to make them resonate. He said it the way he might have said *the tide comes in at six* or *oysters are three cents apiece* — as a fact so thoroughly verified by the accumulated evidence of his entire life that it required no emphasis. It was not a question. It was a conclusion. He had identified the problem, surveyed the available institutions, assessed the probability that any of them would accept testimony from a Black oysterman about a white policeman's coat, and arrived at the answer, and the answer was the silence in which he had been living for five months.

"I'm nobody's witness," he said. "I'm nobody's anything. I'm a man who opens oysters."

His hands moved. The knife went in. The shell opened. The flesh surrendered.

Concetta bought a dozen and paid for two.

She walked home through streets that were just beginning to fill with morning light, and the oranges on her husband's cart were bright as lanterns, and the whole city seemed to her like a house with a rotting foundation — painted, decorated, inhabited, and sinking.

She had always believed that the truth, once found, could be used. That it was like a key, or a warrant, or a weapon — a thing that, if you held it and showed it to the right person, would unlock the right door. Emmanuel had handed her the truth on a stone counter wet with oyster liquor, and she had taken it, and now she understood what he had understood for five months: the truth was not a key. The truth was another oyster. You could open it. You could look at the flesh inside. But there was no one to serve it to, no table that would accept the plate.

## III.

She began with Maria Lanza, who did laundry for a boarding house on Baronne Street where several policemen kept rooms.

"Maria. The deputy. The young one. Burrows."

"What about him?"

"What do you hear?"

Maria Lanza was elbow-deep in a washtub, working a shirt against the board with a violence that suggested the shirt had personally wronged her. Her kitchen smelled of lye and garlic and the milky warmth of the infant sleeping in a basket beside the stove. She did not look up.

"I hear he's trouble."

"What kind of trouble?"

"The kind that comes home at three in the morning and leaves the sheets smelling like perfume that isn't his wife's. The kind that argues. Someone is always hearing him argue."

"With who?"

"Everyone. His landlady. The other boarders. I heard — and this isn't from me, this is from Tessie Giacona, who does the linens at the Hotel Royal — that he was arguing with Hennessy himself. Two nights before Hennessy was killed. In the street, like animals. Someone had to separate them."

Concetta let the name settle. Burrows. It was not yet a shape. It was a thread.

She pulled it.

Over the next six days she moved through the network that no man in New Orleans knew existed, because no man in New Orleans had ever considered the possibility that the women who washed their shirts and cooked their meals and emptied their chamber pots were paying attention. It was not a spy ring. It was not an organization. It had no name and no leader and no meetings. It was simply the ordinary fact that women who worked in houses heard what was said in those houses, and women who knew other women told them what they had heard, and the aggregate of this telling and re-telling and cross-referencing was an intelligence apparatus more comprehensive than anything the Metropolitan Police possessed.

Concetta operated within this apparatus not as a detective — no one would have permitted that, least of all the women themselves — but as a gossip. She traded. She listened. She offered a piece about this husband in exchange for a scrap about that deputy. She brought oranges.

The pieces came in the language of women's work. Over flour and rolling pins. Over mending baskets. Over the small bodies of sleeping children, spoken in whispers because the children must not hear and the husbands must not know and the employers must never suspect that the woman scrubbing their floors had ears and a memory and a mind that could cross-reference.

From Tessie Giacona at the Hotel Royal: the argument between Burrows and Hennessy had been about a woman. Tessie had been collecting glasses from a private dining room and had heard the word "Margaret" spoken three times, each time louder.

From Rosa Ferrara, who cleaned parlors on St. Charles Avenue: "Margaret" was Margaret Caldwell, wife of Alton Caldwell, the cotton broker. Beautiful. Auburn hair. Seen once in a carriage with a man who was not her husband, near Congo Square, at an hour when no married woman of St. Charles Avenue should have been near Congo Square.

From Concetta's own cousin, Filomena, who turned down beds at the St. Charles Hotel: Margaret Caldwell had not been seen in society since November. Her husband told people she was visiting family in Virginia. But Filomena had overheard the Caldwell housekeeper, a Creole woman named Odette, say to the hotel laundress in a voice thin as glass: "That woman is not in Virginia. That woman is not anywhere."

From Lucia Matranga, wife of a stevedore and a woman who missed nothing that happened on the waterfront: Chief Hennessy, in the weeks before his death, had been asking questions about the Caldwell household. Not about the cotton business. About the household itself. About comings and goings. About a carriage house that had recently been repaved with flagstone, in November, when no one repaves anything.

*A carriage house repaved in November.*

Concetta sat in her kitchen with these pieces arranged in her mind the way she arranged oranges on the cart — largest at the base, smallest at the crown, each one placed so that the whole structure held — and she saw it. Not all of it. Not yet. But enough to understand that the murder of David Hennessy had nothing to do with the Matrangas or the Provenzanos or the waterfront wars or any of the things the newspapers had screamed about for five months. It had to do with a deputy and a cotton broker's wife, a body and a carriage house, a secret that Hennessy had gotten close enough to smell.

Her husband came home. He kissed her forehead. He said the lawyers were confident the prison release would happen any day now. He said he had sold eleven crates of oranges and the Americans were buying again, the ones who had stopped coming to Sicilian stalls, and wasn't that a sign that things were getting better?

"Yes," Concetta said. "Things are getting better."
