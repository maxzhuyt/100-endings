# The Noticing

---

## I.

The chairs on the fourth floor of Logos Unlimited are ergonomic the way confession is therapeutic — someone designed them with the theoretical body in mind, the body as concept, the body as it might appear in a brief to stakeholders, and if your actual back, your empirical back, the back you haul up the fire stairs each morning because the lifts smell of Kevin's vaporiser and something older, something geological, if that back disagrees with the concept, well, the chair was never really for you, was it. The chair was for the *idea* of you, which is a distinction I am going to ask you to hold in your mind because it will matter later in ways I did not anticipate when I started noticing and could not stop.

But I am already off the point, which is Declan, which is what happened on the fourth floor, which is what I am trying to tell you about from my helpdesk on a Tuesday afternoon in what I believe to be November though the windows on this floor look out onto a courtyard that has looked like March since I started here. The helpdesk, I should say, is not a desk that helps. It is a desk at which I sit and am asked to help, which makes it, if anything, a desk that receives helplessness, a basin, a catchment area for the baffled and the locked-out and the people who have somehow set their default printer to a device in a branch office in Sligo that may not exist. I am the man who resets passwords. I am the man who asks if you've tried turning it off and on again, not because I think that will fix it but because the act of turning a thing off and on again is a small rehearsal for the possibility that the world might re-present itself differently the second time, which it sometimes does, and isn't that, at bottom, the whole pitch? The whole logos of Logos, as I used to pun to myself before the pun turned inside out and bit me?

Logos Unlimited. *Logos*. I have had time — too much time, the particular quantity of time that accrues at a helpdesk between calls, which is not free time but a kind of pressurised emptiness, the time equivalent of the air in a sealed room — I have had time to think about that name. The company does data integration. It builds middleware solutions for financial services clients. It is, on paper, the least luminous enterprise imaginable. And yet someone, some founder or branding consultant or whatever serpent whispered into whatever ear, named it *Logos*. The word. The reason. The thing that was in the beginning and was with God and was God, if you're the John sort of person, which I am not, or was not, though lately I have been rethinking the gospel's opening gambit because there is something in it, isn't there, about the word being the substrate, the word being what everything is *made of*, and if you work in data integration you might think that's a metaphor for information architecture and if you work on the fourth floor of Logos Unlimited you might, in the last six months, have begun to think it is not a metaphor at all.

Six months ago Declan Walsh, floor manager, our Declan, discovered he could hear what every employee on the fourth floor was thinking.

I'm saying it plainly now because I've found that if I don't anchor the thing in a flat sentence it floats away into the kind of territory where you stop listening, and I need you to listen, though I am aware that *need* is a complicated verb for a man in my situation, a man whose situation I am not yet ready to describe. Declan could hear thoughts. Not metaphorically. Not in the sense that a perceptive manager "reads the room." He could hear the interior monologue, the unvoiced sentence, the private weather. He heard Brenda in accounts receivable thinking about whether her mother's consultant was being straight with her about the biopsy. He heard Tomás in DevOps composing, silently, a resignation letter that began *Dear Declan, you are the specific reason I no longer believe in the social contract*. He heard me — or so I assume, so I have to assume, though what he heard when he turned that particular receiver toward my desk is a question that has since become, let us say, *structurally significant*.

But here is the thing, and it is the thing I need you to understand before I go further: nobody made a fuss. Declan acquired the ability to hear the private thoughts of approximately forty-three employees and the organisational response was identical to the organisational response when they changed the milk supplier in the canteen from Avonmore to some own-brand atrocity that turned tea the colour of a Band-Aid. Which is to say: people noticed, people were unhappy, people adapted. Someone made a joke in the kitchen. Life went on. There was no confrontation, no all-hands, no emergency session with the employee assistance programme. The telepathy was absorbed into the texture of the fourth floor the way all aberrations are absorbed into the texture of any workplace, which is to say totally and without acknowledgement, the way a body absorbs a splinter it cannot expel — just builds tissue around it, just says *fine, you live here now*.

I think about that. I think about that a lot. The not-fussing. The perfect, frictionless incorporation of the impossible into the mundane. I used to think it said something about us, about the modern office worker's capacity for dissociation, about late capitalism's ability to metabolise any disruption including the disruption of the boundary between private and public selfhood. I used to think that and feel clever.

I don't feel clever anymore.

## II.

Let me tell you about the standups. Every morning at nine fifteen, fourth floor, by the whiteboard that someone in Facilities has labelled IDEATION ZONE in letters that peel at the corners, Declan assembled his team — his *resources*, he called us, in that way managers have of using a word that means "things to be extracted from" and expecting gratitude — and conducted a fifteen-minute standup meeting. The format predated the telepathy. Agile methodology, or Logos's peculiar bastardisation of it, which involved the vocabulary of agile and the command structure of a nineteenth-century mill. You said what you'd done yesterday. You said what you'd do today. You named your blockers. Declan nodded or didn't nod and the nodding or not-nodding was the entire performance-review apparatus compressed into a single vertebral gesture.

After the telepathy, the standups became something else. I want to describe this carefully because the texture of it matters, the specific quality of the discomfort, which was not the discomfort of surveillance — it was the discomfort of surveillance that *pretends to be intuition*.

Here is a scene. Brenda — Brenda in accounts receivable, Brenda of the mother and the biopsy and the worry that sat behind her eyes like a second face — Brenda begins her standup update. She says: "Yesterday I reconciled the Pemberton invoices, today I'll start on the Q3 accruals, my blocker is that I'm waiting on figures from—" And Declan says: "From Galway, yes, and you're wondering whether to escalate because you feel that Miriam in Galway doesn't take your emails seriously and you've been composing a more forceful version in your head since the bus this morning but you're worried it'll come across as aggressive and you'll be *that woman*, the one who can't manage lateral relationships, which is exactly the phrase — *lateral relationships* — that appeared in your last performance review, which I wrote, which you thought was unfair, which it was."

And then he paused. And then he said: "I'll send Miriam a note myself. Unblock you. Next?"

And Brenda said nothing. And we all said nothing. And the standup continued.

That was the structure of it. Every morning. Each person's turn became a small theatre of preemption, Declan intercepting not just the content of the update but the *subtext*, the unspoken anxiety beneath the professional surface, and resolving it — always resolving it, that was the key, he always *fixed the thing* — in a way that left the employee scraped clean, emptied, like a window someone has washed so thoroughly you keep walking into it.

He did it to Tomás. Tomás would begin talking about a sprint backlog and Declan would gently, almost tenderly, narrate Tomás's doubts about the architectural decisions being made two levels above him, would articulate Tomás's conviction that the whole platform migration was an exercise in stakeholder theatre, and would then produce, from his laptop, a revised technical roadmap that addressed every silent objection. Tomás would stare at the roadmap. The roadmap would be good. That was the worst part. The interventions were *competent*. Declan heard your fears and he *fixed them*, the way a mechanic fixes your car while informing you that he knows about the affair.

The efficiency was extraordinary. In six months, the fourth floor's output metrics tripled. The client-satisfaction scores became the subject of a case study that someone in Marketing was writing up for a conference in Düsseldorf. Declan was invited to present. He began using phrases like *deep-listening culture* and *anticipatory alignment* in all-hands emails, phrases that were, if you knew what was actually happening, so grotesque in their euphemism that they amounted to a kind of private comedy — a joke only Declan and his forty-three captive punchlines could appreciate.

No one said anything.

I need to sit with that sentence. No one said anything. In a building full of people whose thoughts were being read aloud, no one opened their mouth to name the thing that was happening. I filed no ticket. Brenda raised no grievance. Tomás did not, in the end, send his resignation letter. The employee assistance programme went unconsulted. We had, without discussion, developed a protocol for the telepathy the way one develops a protocol for anything in an office: through silent collective practice, through the accumulation of small capitulations, through the organic emergence of norms that no one authored and everyone obeys. It was — I wrote this in a note-to-self on my phone one evening, standing at the bus stop, the words arriving with the conviction of discovered truth — *an unminuted standing agenda item*. We all knew it was on the agenda. It was never discussed. It was never voted on. It simply *was*, the way the weather is, the way the chairs are.

Declan held one-on-ones. Monthly. Thirty minutes in the glass-walled meeting room that everyone called the Fishbowl, which, given the circumstances, had become a name almost too precise to bear. I sat in mine and listened to Declan describe my performance in language that was a patchwork of my own private thoughts quoted back to me without attribution. "You feel," he said — *you feel*, as though he were a therapist, as though this were care — "you feel that your skills are underutilised at the helpdesk. You feel that you have a diagnostic mind, a pattern-recognition aptitude, that the password-reset workflow doesn't serve. You've been thinking about this in terms of — what was it — *a mind-shaped problem in search of a problem-shaped mind*?" He smiled. He had taken that phrase directly from my interior monologue at 3:47 on a Wednesday. I had been proud of that phrase. I had been composing it privately, savouring its symmetry, the way you savour a line of poetry you believe you've invented. And Declan served it back to me as managerial insight, as evidence of his own perceptiveness, and I sat across from him in the Fishbowl and felt — what? Violated? No. Violation implies a boundary that existed and was crossed. What I felt was more like the discovery that the boundary had never been there. That the thought I believed I was having privately, in the sovereign territory of my own skull, had always been, in some sense, *on the network*.

"I think," Declan said, leaning back, touching his fingertips together in the gesture of a man who has learned management body language from a YouTube video he did not finish, "I think we can find you something more diagnostic. Something that uses — " and here he waved a hand, and the wave was meant to encompass my mind, my *logos*, my inner word, "— *all of that*."

He never did find me anything. The diagnostic role never materialised. The promise was the intervention. The promise was Declan demonstrating that he had access — access to what I wanted, access to how I described what I wanted to myself, access to the specific syntax of my longing — and that the access itself, the *having been seen*, should be sufficient. Should be, in some way, the same as being helped.

I think he believed that. I think Declan genuinely believed that hearing a person's thoughts and reciting them back constituted a form of care. He was a man who had acquired omniscience within a forty-metre radius and had used it to become, essentially, a better CC line. He took the contents of your soul and he put them in an email with your line manager copied. That is not a metaphor. He literally did this once to Brenda. She had been thinking about applying for a transfer to the Cork office to be closer to her mother during treatment, and Declan, in a move he clearly considered magnanimous, sent her an email — CC'd to HR, CC'd to the Cork branch head, CC'd to Brenda's skip-level — that said: *Brenda has been reflecting on her personal circumstances and I believe a conversation about flexible location options would support both her wellbeing and her continued high performance*. He had translated her private grief into a stakeholder communication. He had taken the most frightened part of her and made it *legible to the organisation*.

She transferred to Cork. She was, by all metrics, grateful.

I have never been more frightened of gratitude.

## III.

Sable started on a Monday. I know it was a Monday because the fourth floor's Monday energy is specific and Pavlovian — the smell of instant coffee from the kitchen, the particular hum of machines that have been sleeping for two days waking into their processes, the sound of forty-three people settling into their roles like actors finding their marks. I was at my desk. I watched her come through the security door with her lanyard still in its plastic sleeve, the way new hires carry them, like a passport in an unfamiliar country.

She was not tall. She was not short. She had dark hair cut to a length that seemed chosen for no reason other than that it stopped where it stopped. She carried a backpack, not a laptop bag, which marked her immediately as someone from the technical track — the systems people carry backpacks, the business people carry laptop bags, the managers carry nothing because their hands must be free for the gesturing. She walked to the workstation that Facilities had prepared for her — I knew it was hers because I had provisioned her login the previous Friday, username *s.okafor*, temporary password *Welcome1!*, which is the default and which I have typed so many times that it has become a kind of liturgical response, my own call-and-answer with each new arrival: *Welcome. One. Exclamation mark.* I gave everyone the same greeting. I never asked whether the exclamation was sincere.

She set up her workstation in silence. Not uncomfortable silence — not the silence of someone who is nervous and hoping to be approached, the silence that performs its own aloneness as an invitation. This was a different kind. Operational silence. The silence of someone who is doing a thing and does not require the thing to be narrated. She plugged in her monitors.
