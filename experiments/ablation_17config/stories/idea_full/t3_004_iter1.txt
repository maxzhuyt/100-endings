# Nell's Wednesday

## I.

Let us look in on Nell Carden, who is already awake, because Nell Carden is always awake.

Not in the manner of the industrious or the anxious — though she is both, in her way — but in the manner of the occupied, the inhabited, the ceaselessly tenanted, for Nell Carden has not known a quiet mind since before she had language enough to call it quiet or mind enough to know it was hers. She lies on a workhouse cot in the women's dormitory of the Southwark Provisional Labour House, third floor, east wing, bed forty-one of sixty, and the hour — should the reader care, and the narrator doubts the reader does, though the narrator will provide it anyway, being constitutionally unable to resist the texture of a fact — is four minutes past four on a Wednesday morning in November, and the bell will not ring for another hour and fifty-six minutes, and Nell knows this, and Nell waits.

Around her, in sixty cots arranged with that peculiar institutional geometry which suggests not so much efficiency as a deep civic embarrassment about the existence of human bodies, forty-three women sleep. Seventeen cots are empty — their occupants removed by the Organic Compliance Bureau at intervals the narrator will not, for the moment, detail, though the narrator savours the details, holds them at the back of its throat like a sourball, promises itself it will get to them presently, because cruelty documented is cruelty indicted and cruelty merely gestured at is sentiment, and the narrator has no patience for sentiment, though it has, as will become evident, an inexhaustible appetite for cruelty.

Forty-three women sleep, and their sleeping minds are not silent.

They have never been silent, not to Nell, who lies in bed forty-one with her hands flat against her stomach and her eyes open, receiving.

Mrs. Doole in bed twelve dreams of a basin of hot water and the dream has the specificity of desire long deferred: the water is not merely hot but hot in a particular way, heated on a particular stove that Mrs. Doole once owned, in a kitchen whose wallpaper was a pattern of yellow sprigs against cream, and in the dream Mrs. Doole lowers her hands into the basin and the relief moves through her in a wave that has the colour, to Nell, of an old bruise leaching toward yellow, and Nell receives it, the heat and the relief and the yellow-sprig wallpaper, without wanting any of it, the way a stone at the bottom of a stream receives sediment.

In bed twenty-six, a woman whose name Nell has never learned — they do not all know each other here, despite the reformers' quaint insistence that the workhouse is a "community of purpose" — rehearses a conversation with her dead father, and the conversation is not a dream but the half-waking rumination of a mind that has one foot in sleep and the other in a grief so thoroughly worn that it is no longer grief but furniture, something the mind navigates around on its way to other rooms, and the woman is saying, *Da, they've changed the bread again, it's that grey sort now, you'd not touch it, you'd say it was paving*, and her father, who is dead and whose responses the woman fabricates with the practiced fluency of long mourning, says nothing, because the woman has never been able to convincingly imagine her father's voice, only its absence, and so the conversation is a monologue that believes itself a dialogue, and Nell receives this too, the particular loneliness of talking to the dead and being unable even to hallucinate a reply.

In bed seven, a girl of perhaps sixteen — the records are unreliable; the Provisional Labour Houses are a bureaucratic twilight in which ages shift to suit budgets — thinks about her own teeth. She is awake. She is running her tongue over the back of her lower incisors, cataloguing their topography, wondering if the ridge near her left canine is natural or a sign, and by sign she means evidence, and by evidence she means something that might flag on a compliance scan, and the fear is so routine in its texture that it barely registers as fear at all; it is merely the bass note on which this girl's consciousness plays its meagre melody every night, the same question, *is my body my own, and if it is, will they believe me?*

Nell receives it. Nell receives all of it. Mrs. Doole's basin and the dead father and the girl's teeth and thirty-nine other streams she has learned not to individuate, the way a person who lives beside a motorway learns not to hear individual engines but cannot, despite this adaptation, claim to live in silence. The implant does not have a switch. The implant does not have a range she can modulate, a volume she can lower, a channel she can change. The implant was not designed for her, was not chosen by her, was not — but the narrator is getting ahead of itself, and the narrator, unlike Nell, has the luxury of sequence, of choosing what to reveal and when, and the narrator intends to exercise this luxury with the serene deliberation of a man choosing which wine to open at a dinner where the guest of honour is already dead.

She waits for the bell.

Let the reader understand what is being established: the narrator can read minds, and so can Nell. The narrator, however, reads them from a position of architectural authority — this high, dry, well-upholstered perch from which suffering can be observed and sentences can be built and ironies can be mortised into their frames and the whole edifice can be admired as a piece of craftsmanship. The narrator knows what everyone thinks and feels about what everyone thinks and will tell you about it at length, in the voice of a man who has been to very good schools and wants you to know it, because style is the narrator's weapon, and clarity is the narrator's alibi, and omniscience is the narrator's *pleasure*.

Nell has the same information and none of the power. She hears what everyone thinks and it is not a privilege but a condition, not a gift but a noise floor, not omniscience but omnipresence — she is present in every mind within range and absent from the only one that might give her rest, which is her own, which she has not experienced as quiet since she was nine days old, when the implant — but there it is again, that story, that foundational bureaucratic catastrophe, pressing forward, and the narrator will tell it now because the bell has not yet rung and Nell is still lying in bed forty-one with her hands flat on her stomach and her eyes open and her jaw set in that way she has, that way that makes her face, in repose, look not peaceful but *decided*, as though she has made a resolution and the resolution is simply to continue, and continuing is, for Nell Carden, the most strenuous act available.

## II.

The facts of the matter, as the Organic Compliance Bureau might render them — and the Bureau does love its renderings, its dockets, its sub-clauses, its procedural language scrubbed clean of the human stain it was manufactured to contain — are these:

On the seventh of March, fourteen years before the Wednesday in question, a woman named Catherine Carden, twenty-six years of age, employed intermittently, domiciled in a sub-let bedsit above a chip shop on the Old Kent Road, entered the rear door of a premises operating without licence in Bermondsey — a graft clinic, which is to say a room with a table and a man with instruments and an arrangement of mutual illegality that bound patient and practitioner in the democracy of shared crime — and submitted herself to a procedure she had, in fact, requested, which was a minor and in those days common gestational modification: a prenatal auditory enhancement, intended to correct a hereditary deafness that ran in her family, a deafness her own mother had carried, and her mother's mother before her, passed down like a surname or a debt.

The file was wrong.

Not dramatically wrong, not wrong in the way of gothic catastrophe or cinematic revelation — wrong in the way of a Wednesday, which is to say wrong in the way things usually go wrong: a mislabelled folder, a digit transposed, a sequence of characters that should have read AE-4402 and instead read NE-4402, and the difference between those two prefixes was the difference between a cochlear frequency adjustment and a Kerrigan-Marsh Neural Empathy Graft, Model 9, which was, at the time of its implantation, an experimental military-grade perceptual modification designed to allow reconnaissance operatives to read the cognitive-emotive states of subjects at distances of up to forty metres, a device that had failed its third-stage trials on account of producing, in its adult test subjects, acute psychotic breaks within seventy-two hours of activation, and which had therefore been diverted — as failed military technology is always diverted, with the same gravitational inevitability that carries sewage downhill — into the black-market supply chain, where it found its way to the graft clinic in Bermondsey, where it found its way into the wrong file, where it found its way into the gestational sac of Catherine Carden's unremarkable pregnancy, where it grafted itself, with the blind efficiency of the engineered organism it was, to the developing neural architecture of the child who would, nine days after birth, begin to hear the thoughts of every person within range, beginning with her mother's.

The clinic was closed by the time Catherine understood what had happened. The practitioner had moved on, as practitioners do, to another rented room in another part of the city, following the migratory pattern of unlicensed medicine, which flows wherever enforcement ebbs. Catherine had no recourse. She had entered the arrangement illegally. She could not report the error without reporting herself. She was given, in the cosmic lottery of bureaucratic negligence, a daughter who could read minds but could not legally exist, a child whose very neurology was a prosecutable offence under the Organic Purity Act of 2037, which defined cognitive modification in persons under eighteen as a Category 2 violation carrying a mandatory minimum of seven years' institutional correction for the minor and twelve years' imprisonment for the consenting guardian, and Catherine had consented, yes, she had consented to *something*, though not to this, never to this, and the law did not, as the law so rarely does, distinguish between the something she had wanted and the everything she had received.

Let the narrator note, with the dispassion of a coroner filing a routine report, what Catherine Carden's inner life became in the years that followed, as reported — involuntarily, incessantly, without appeal — by the daughter who could not stop hearing it.

Each evening — and it was always evening, always the hours after the chip shop below had closed and the smell of old oil rose through the floorboards and Catherine sat at the table that served as desk, dining surface, and impromptu altar to the small domestic gods of paperwork — Catherine performed an arithmetic. Nell heard it the way one hears a clock in a quiet room: not as information but as rhythm, the tick-tick-tick of a calculation so frequently rehearsed that it had ceased to be thought and become reflex.

*Fourteen pounds sixty per week, the supplemental ration. Forty-one pounds for the bedsit. Six pounds for the card, the forged compliance card, renewed quarterly. Sixteen pounds for the quarterly renewal. Nine hundred and twelve pounds per annum, the total additional cost of raising a modified child. The bounty for reporting a Category 2 violation: two thousand, four hundred pounds, payable upon conviction, plus a six-month amnesty on the reporter's own compliance record.*

The numbers shifted as prices changed, as the Bureau adjusted its bounty schedules, as the amnesty windows widened or narrowed according to political weather, but the structure of the calculation never varied: on one side of the ledger, the cost of keeping Nell; on the other, the reward for giving her up. And Catherine Carden, who was not a monster, who loved her daughter in the way that people love their chronic pain — with resentment, with intimacy, with the grim acknowledgment that it has become part of one's body and cannot be separated without surgery — Catherine Carden ran these numbers every night the way other women said their prayers, and each night she arrived at the same conclusion, which was that keeping Nell was, on balance, the more expensive option, and each night she chose it, and each night Nell heard her choose it, and heard the reluctance in the choosing, and heard behind the reluctance a fatigue that was not quite love and not quite its absence but something worse than either: a continuous, low-grade *resentment* at being forced, by conscience or cowardice or habit, to make the generous choice.

Nell heard this from the age of nine days. She heard it when she could not yet understand words, felt it as a colour — a muddy, coppery sorrow that tasted of old oil — and later, when language came, heard it as language, heard the sums, heard the hesitation between sums, heard the moment each night when her mother's mind touched the bounty figure and lingered there, two seconds, three, the way a tongue probes a broken tooth, and then moved on, and chose, again, to keep her daughter, and was, again, not glad about it.

By the time Nell was twelve, she could have performed her mother's arithmetic faster than Catherine could. She knew the price of herself to the penny. She knew the exact width of the margin by which she was, each night, not turned in. She knew it was narrowing.

At thirteen she was placed in the workhouse system — not reported, not formally, but *released*, which is the word the Bureau uses when a guardian delivers a minor to Provisional Labour without filing a violation report, an act of bureaucratic mercy that allows the guardian to avoid prosecution and the child to avoid a Category 2 designation, provided the child submits to institutional supervision and makes no attempt to seek private employment, education, or autonomous housing until the age of majority, which the Organic Purity Act sets, for modified persons, at thirty.

Thirty. The narrator pauses on this figure and invites the reader to hold it. Nell is seventeen. She has thirteen years of institutional supervision remaining. Thirteen years of cots and workhouse floors and compliance scans and the particular flavour of existence that obtains when one is not imprisoned and not free but something the bureaucracy has not yet named because naming it would require admitting it exists.

Her mother's mind, after the placement, became inaudible — not because Catherine stopped thinking but because she was beyond the implant's range, which is forty metres in open air and somewhat less through walls, and the distance between the Old Kent Road and the Southwark Provisional Labour House is one mile and four hundred metres, which is to say, approximately thirty-five times the maximum distance at which Nell Carden will ever hear another person's unguarded thought.

The file was wrong. The narrator repeats this because the repetition is structurally necessary: no one chose this. Not Nell, not Catherine, not the Bermondsey practitioner who mixed up the folders, not the military programme that discarded the device, not the black-market supply chain that absorbed it. The implant was an orphan of negligence, a parentless error, and it attached itself to Nell the way errors do — permanently, and without anyone to blame who could be made to fix it.

There was, the narrator notes — with the careful, almost parenthetical emphasis of a solicitor introducing an exhibit that will not matter until closing arguments — a procedure. There had always been a procedure. Severance, it was called, because the medical profession, like the literary one, cannot resist a metaphor. The Kerrigan-Marsh graft could be disconnected from the host neural architecture by a qualified neurosurgeon in a sterile theatre with appropriate anaesthesia and monitoring, at a success rate of approximately eighty-seven percent, with the remaining thirteen percent of cases resulting in outcomes that the clinical literature described as "significant and irreversible cognitive alteration," which is to say: the procedure could free you, or it could empty you, and the odds were roughly those of a revolver with one bullet in eight chambers, and the cost was four thousand pounds if done legally, which could not be done legally, because the procedure presupposed the existence of an implant, and the existence of the implant was a crime, and so one arrived, as one always arrives in these systems, at the most Dickensian of cul-de-sacs: the cure was available only through the same black-market channels that had produced the disease, at prices set by the same unregulated market, in rooms not meaningfully different from the one in Bermondsey where this story began.

Nell knew about severance. She had heard it in the thoughts of others — the whisper of a clinic address, the stray fantasy of a woman three cots over picturing herself walking into a building and walking out *alone in her own head*, the way someone might picture walking into water and not feeling the cold. Nell knew the address the way she knew most things: involuntarily, at second-hand, without having sought the knowledge or the burden of it.

The bell rang
