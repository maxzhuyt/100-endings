# The Actual Glory

## I.

The freshman had the ankles of a newborn deer — all tendon and optimism, no history yet — and Mags knelt on the training-room floor with a roll of Mueller athletic tape and thought, as she always thought when she touched a new ankle for the first time, *I will remember this one by how she heals.*

She'd been doing this for thirty-one years. The kneeling was harder now. Her own knees sounded like someone stepping on bubble wrap, and there was a thing happening in her left hip that she refused to name because naming it would make it an appointment and an appointment would make it a diagnosis and a diagnosis, at fifty-eight, was just a fancy way of being told you were aging, which — she tore the tape with her teeth — she already knew.

"Too tight?"

"No, ma'am."

*Ma'am.* This one was from Georgia. Mags had had a girl from Georgia in oh-three, oh-four — Tamika Banks, six-one, left-handed, a hitter who could bend a cross-court shot like she was negotiating with physics. Tamika had blown out her ACL on a wet gym floor in Harrisonburg, Virginia, on a Tuesday night in February, and the sound it made — everyone always talked about the pop, but it wasn't a pop, it was more like someone snapping a thick rubber band inside a jar of peanut butter, a muffled, wet, final sound — and Tamika had looked up at Mags from the floor with an expression Mags had seen maybe forty times in her career, the expression that said *tell me it's not what I think it is*, and Mags had said nothing because she never lied about injuries, she just held Tamika's hand and waited for the team doctor who was also the campus allergist and who arrived seven minutes later smelling like the sandwich he'd been eating in his car.

Tamika taught kindergarten in Decatur now. She'd sent Mags a Christmas card last year with a photo of twenty-three five-year-olds and a handwritten note that said *I still can't do a full squat but I can do the Macarena which is more important for my career*. Mags had pinned it to the corkboard above her desk next to the photo of the '09 team at nationals, the one where everyone was crying and Mags was in the background holding a Ziploc bag of ice against someone's shoulder, eyes on the body, not the trophy.

She finished the freshman's ankle. Neat work. Consistent tension. You could tell an athletic trainer by her tape jobs the way you could tell a carpenter by her joints — the ones who rushed left wrinkles that bunched and blistered; the ones who cared left skin that could breathe.

"You're good. Ice after, twenty minutes, not nineteen, not twenty-one."

"Yes, ma'am."

The gym was not theirs. The gym belonged to the Baptist church on Devereaux Street, which rented it to the college for a price that the athletic director called "reasonable" and Mags called "the best we're going to do since our actual gym has a condemned weight room and a ceiling tile problem that's one heavy rain away from being a lawsuit." The floor was multipurpose laminate, scuffed from church-league basketball and children's ministry roller-skating nights. The lights buzzed at a frequency that Mags had measured with a free phone app: 120 hertz, which she'd been told was fine but which she didn't believe because the girls got headaches on Tuesdays after long practices and nobody got headaches on Thursdays when they practiced in the auxiliary room at the Y.

The net was theirs, at least. That and the tape and the ibuprofen and the one foam roller with the dent in it and the portable training table Mags had bought with her own money in 2014 because the requisition form had been "lost" three times. The athletic budget for women's volleyball at Chesdin College was, if you calculated it per athlete per season, roughly what a middling public school spent on copy paper. Mags did not calculate this aloud. She'd learned, by her twentieth year in small-college athletics, that administrators responded to budget complaints the way cats responded to oboe music — with visible discomfort followed by departure.

She heard it then: the particular metronomic violence of a volleyball hitting a cinder-block wall. Evenly spaced. Hard. *Thwack. Thwack. Thwack.* Every interval the same, which meant it wasn't frustration — frustration was arrhythmic — it was practice. Deliberate, solitary, angry practice.

Kieran Voss had arrived forty minutes before anyone else, as she had arrived forty minutes before anyone else every day since the semester started. Mags had offered her the foam roller exactly once.

"Wastes minutes," Kieran had said, not rudely — Kieran was never rude exactly — but with the clipped efficiency of someone who had decided that the world contained a finite number of useful minutes and was committed to spending none of them on recovery accessories. She stretched alone, in the corner, a routine that Mags recognized as something borrowed from a Division I strength program: hip-flexor work, thoracic spine mobility, a series of shoulder stabilizations that nobody at Chesdin would've known to teach her. Kieran had brought those exercises with her the way a refugee brings the one cookbook from home — not out of nostalgia but because the recipes still worked and the new kitchen had nothing in the pantry.

She was five-ten, which wasn't tall for a setter but was tall for this program, where the roster topped out at six feet and the libero was five-three and had been recruited primarily because she was willing to attend Chesdin College, which was—Mags had no illusions—nobody's first choice. It was a school for the girls who wanted to keep playing. The ones whose high-school careers had been bright enough to leave a taste but not bright enough to open doors. The ones who came because Chesdin said yes.

Kieran was different. Kieran had been somewhere that had said yes — UNC Wilmington, a decent mid-major, a real program with real money and a real strength coach and a gym that didn't double as a roller-skating venue — and had sat on the bench for two years and then transferred. Mags had read her file. She'd talked to the trainer at Wilmington, a guy named Pete Reese whom she'd met at a conference in 2011, and Pete had said, "Good kid. Works harder than anyone. Couldn't crack the lineup. It was eating her alive."

Mags had understood this perfectly. She'd been around long enough to know that athletic talent came in two varieties: the kind that settled into a body like weather, natural and unselfconscious, and the kind that was bolted on through sheer force, every skill an argument against some original insufficiency. Kieran was the second kind. You could see it in the way she hit the wall — not for the pleasure of contact but for the proof of it, each *thwack* a small rebuttal to whoever had told her, or whatever she had told herself, that she wasn't enough.

Mags had seen this before. In 1994, in 2002, in 2011. She had a taxonomy of athletic heartbreak that she kept in her head the way an ornithologist kept a life list — not for display but because the cataloging mattered, because each bird was real and had flown through her sky and deserved to be noted.

She stood, her hip doing the thing she refused to name, and began laying out ankle braces for the rest of the team. The season's first real game was Saturday. Ferrum College, away, a three-hour bus ride in a vehicle that also served as the campus shuttle on weekdays and smelled permanently of the vanilla air freshener the driver, Carl, hung from the rearview mirror and which Mags had asked him to remove three seasons running because two of her girls got migraines and Carl had agreed each time and then replaced it.

Her phone buzzed. An email from the college president, subject line: *Campus Health Update — COVID-19 Preparedness*. She opened it, scanned three paragraphs of bureaucratic reassurance, noted the phrase "abundance of caution" twice and "monitoring the situation" three times, and closed it. She'd seen similar emails before — for H1N1, for campus mold, for the one year the cafeteria gave eleven students simultaneous food poisoning and the president had written an email that used the word "regrettable" as if it were a form of currency.

Someone's mother had called the athletic department that morning, worried about the virus. Mags didn't know whose mother. The secretary had mentioned it the way you mentioned weather — *oh, one of the volleyball moms called, something about the pandemic* — and then asked Mags if she had change for the vending machine.

Outside, the Virginia winter was doing its slow retreat, and through the high church windows Mags could see the pale, cautious March light that meant the season would be played in borrowed gyms under borrowed time, though she didn't know that yet. Nobody did. That was the thing about March 2020 that she would think about later, in the years when she had time to think about it: how normal things felt until they didn't, how the world ended not with a sound but with a memo.

Kieran hit the wall. *Thwack.* The freshman from Georgia flexed her newly taped ankle. The lights buzzed at 120 hertz. Mags set out the ibuprofen, counted the ice bags, and began the season the way she'd begun every season: by paying attention to what was in front of her, which was all she'd ever known how to do.

## II.

The lights at the Baptist church gym buzzed the same way the lights had buzzed at the old Chesdin auxiliary gym, the one they'd used from '95 to '08 before the ceiling tiles finally made good on their threat, and it was the buzzing that pulled Mags sideways into the memory of Delia Royce.

Not sideways. Downward. The memory of Delia was not a lateral movement; it was a trapdoor.

It was a Tuesday practice, third week of the season. Kieran was running a hitting drill with the starters, and one of the outside hitters — Bree Carleton, a junior who'd improved fifty percent over the summer and who would, in a different program, be the best story on the team — shanked a pass so badly it hit the church's donation box mounted on the far wall. Kieran caught the next ball out of the air and said, without raising her voice but with a precision that was worse than yelling, "That ball has to be to zone four. Every time. Not sometimes. Not when you feel like it."

Bree's face did the thing faces do when the hit lands clean: nothing. She went still, and her stillness was loud. The other girls looked at the floor or the ceiling or their shoes, the way teammates always did when someone drew a line in public and everyone had to decide which side they were on.

Mags watched from the training table where she was re-taping a metatarsal pad. She did not intervene. She'd intervened in her twenties, in her thirties, sometimes in her forties — stepped between a captain and a freshman, defused, mediated, smoothed. She didn't anymore. Not because she didn't care but because she'd learned that some frictions were the friction of a team becoming itself, and you couldn't sand that away without also sanding away the grain.

But she clocked it. She clocked Kieran's voice and Bree's stillness and the angle of Kieran's jaw, which was the angle of someone who had been on the receiving end of that same correction — delivered with less restraint and more cruelty — at a Division I program where the margin between playing and not playing was a razor's width, and who had internalized the razor as a tool and not a wound.

And she thought about Delia.

Delia Royce played for Mags from 1996 to 1999. She was a middle blocker, five-eleven, from a tobacco town in southside Virginia where the tallest structure was either the Baptist church steeple or the water tower depending on who you asked and how they felt about God. She had hands that were almost comically large for her body and a blocking instinct that Mags had seen maybe three times in thirty years — the ability to read a setter's shoulders and be in the air before the ball left the setter's hands, as if she lived one second ahead of everyone else and was waiting for them to catch up.

She also stopped eating during the spring of her sophomore year. Mags noticed because Mags always noticed. It started with the things that were easy to miss if you weren't looking: the granola bar tucked into a jacket pocket instead of eaten, the lunch tray with the food rearranged but not diminished, the water bottle refilled four, five, six times during a practice that wasn't hot enough to justify it. Then the things that were harder to miss: the collarbone sharpening above the neckline of her jersey, the extra layer she wore to practice in a gym that was seventy-four degrees, the afternoon Mags found her sitting on the training table with her eyes closed and her pulse at fifty-one, which was low even for an athlete.

Mags had gone to the head coach — this was before Mags understood that the head coach, a man named Dale Worthy who had the emotional range of a filing cabinet, would respond to the information the way a filing cabinet would respond, which was to file it and close the drawer. She'd gone to the campus counselor, who had a six-week waitlist. She'd sat with Delia one afternoon in the training room after everyone else had left and said, carefully, "I'm noticing some things, and I want to make sure you're okay," and Delia had looked at her with the expression Mags had come to think of as *the wall* — the flat, pleasant, impenetrable surface that people in crisis built around themselves, brick by brick, smile by smile — and said, "I'm fine, Mags. I've just been stressed about organic chemistry."

She left the program at the end of that year. Didn't transfer. Just stopped. Mags found her one night in the parking lot behind the gym, sitting in a 1993 Honda Civic with the engine off and the radio on, and the song playing was something by Alanis Morissette, which Mags remembered because the specificity of the memory was the thing that made it real and not a parable. She'd knocked on the window. Delia had rolled it down. They'd sat there for a while. Mags didn't ask what she was doing in a parking lot at midnight. Delia didn't explain. At some point Delia said, "I think I just wanted to be somewhere that wasn't my room and wasn't the gym," and Mags had said, "Okay," and they'd listened to the rest of the song, which was "Hand in My Pocket," the one about being free but broken and being brave but chicken, and then Mags had followed Delia's taillights back to the dorm to make sure she got there.

Delia was alive. Mags knew this because she kept a list — not written down, but maintained in the muscle memory of her conscience — of every player who had left her care under circumstances that created the obligation to keep knowing. Delia worked for a nonprofit in Roanoke that did something with housing. She'd sent Mags a LinkedIn request in 2017, which Mags had accepted because she accepted all of them, because the notification was always a small relief: *still here, still here, still here.*

Mags stored the memory back in its drawer — not closed, never fully closed, but pushed to where it wouldn't interfere with the metatarsal pad — and looked up.

Kieran was demonstrating the correct passing angle to Bree. Her body was a textbook. Every line efficient, every motion purposeful. She did not touch Bree to correct her platform; she stood beside her and showed her, and expected her to learn by proximity and will. There was no warmth in the instruction, but there was no contempt either. It was purely transactional. *Here is the correct way. Now do it.*

Mags had seen transfer athletes before. She could read their bodies the way a geologist reads strata — each layer a record of the pressure that formed it. Kieran's layers were legible: the DI strength program that had built her shoulders and her discipline; the two years of riding the bench that had built her jaw and her impatience; the decision to come to Chesdin, which had built nothing yet because she hadn't let it.
