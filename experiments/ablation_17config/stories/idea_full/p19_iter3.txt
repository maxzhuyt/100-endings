# The Pensjonat at Hamar

---

**From the ISU Incident Log, Case File 94-LH-0041, filed 17 February 1994:**

*Charter vehicle 3 (Volvo B10M, reg. ØT 4471, Oppland Trafikkselskap) departed the Olympic Village auxiliary lot at 13:40 on 14 February en route to the Hamar Olympic Hall (Vikingskipet). Vehicle experienced mechanical failure at km 138.4 on the E6, approximately 11 km south-southeast of Hamar. Twelve passengers. Replacement transport was delayed due to weather conditions. Passengers were housed overnight at Sørli Pensjonat, a roadside inn. All passengers were accounted for and transported to the venue the following afternoon. No formal complaints were filed.*

*No formal complaints were filed.* I want you to hold that sentence in your mind. We will come back to it.

---

**From Aftenposten, 14 February 1994, Oslo edition, weather sidebar, page 6:**

*A low-pressure system moving southeast across Hedmark is expected to bring sustained winds of 20–25 m/s and heavy snowfall through Tuesday morning. The E6 between Minnesund and Hamar is subject to intermittent closures. Travelers are advised to delay all nonessential journeys.*

---

My name is Maren Eilertsen. In February 1994 I was thirty-seven years old and worked as a physiotherapist attached to the Norwegian Olympic delegation, specializing in the rehabilitation of figure skaters and short-track speed skaters. I had previously served in the same capacity at Albertville in 1992. I was, at the time of the events I am going to describe, recently divorced, living in a rented flat in Lillehammer that smelled of the previous tenant's cat, and in possession of a lower back spasm on my right side that I had been ignoring for three weeks. I mention these facts because I want you to understand at the start that I am a person who reads bodies for a living and fails to read her own.

I was on the bus.

Charter 3 was not meant for people like me. It was overflow transport — a catch-basin for the delegation staff, technical officials, minor federation personnel, and assorted functionaries who did not warrant seats on the primary coaches that left the Village on the hour. The twelve of us who boarded at 13:40 on the afternoon of 14 February were, in organizational terms, the remainder. The ones nobody was tracking.

I have, over the years, reconstructed the passenger list from memory, from the charter manifest fragment I was able to obtain from Oppland Trafikkselskap before they went defunct in 2003, and from the ISU incident log cited above. I will not list all twelve. Most of them pass through this story the way fellow passengers on any transit pass through your life — as coats, coughs, the smell of someone else's sandwich. I will tell you about two of them.

---

**The American:**

Donald "Don" Beech, age fifty-four. Pairs coach, United States Figure Skating Association. Based in Hackensack, New Jersey, though he had been born in Duluth, Minnesota, and you could hear it sometimes when he was tired — the vowels opening up, the consonants getting blunt, the whole apparatus of the voice sinking an inch closer to the ice he'd spent his life on. He had coached three Olympic pairs teams across two Games, placing seventh, ninth, and sixth, which in the economy of American pairs skating constituted a distinguished career. His right knee was surgically reconstructed in 1987 — ACL and medial meniscus, I could tell from the gait — and he walked with a heaviness on that side that he had long ago incorporated into a kind of rolling authority, the way a ship lists but does not capsize. He was six foot one and carried his weight in his chest and shoulders, which made him look, when he sat down, considerably smaller than when he stood. He had been married to a woman from Brattleboro, Vermont, for nineteen years; they had divorced in 1991. He had a daughter, Caitlin, age twenty, who was at that time a sophomore at the University of Michigan and who I would later learn had not spoken to him in seven months. He sat in the third row of the charter bus, left side, aisle seat. He was reading a paperback — I could not see the title — and he held it in his right hand only, his left arm folded across his body in the unconscious protective posture of a man whose rotator cuff was announcing the first forecasts of its eventual failure.

He did not look across the aisle. Not once.

**The Russian:**

Irina Viktorovna Zelenko, age forty-eight. Technical judge, Russian Figure Skating Federation, ISU certified since 1983. Federation rank: Senior International, Category A. She had judged at Worlds in 1988, 1990, and 1993, at Europeans in four consecutive years, and at the Albertville Games in 1992, where she sat on the panel for the pairs event. She was small — one hundred sixty centimeters, perhaps fifty-three kilograms — with the particular uprightness of posture I associate with former dancers, the thoracic spine held as if it were still being watched. Her hair was dark, cut short in a way that might have been stylish or might have been practical, and she had a habit of tucking it behind her left ear with two fingers in a motion so automatic it had become invisible to her but was not invisible to me. She was wearing a dark blue coat that was not warm enough for the weather. She smoked Sobranie Classics — the colored ones, in the black box — though not on the bus. I knew the brand because I saw the box when she opened her bag later, the gold Cyrillic catching the pensjonat light, and because I asked her, later still, and she told me, and then offered me one, and I accepted, and it tasted like someone else's century.

Her hotel room at the Lillehammer Turisthotell was 307. I know this because it was printed on the small paper key sleeve she tucked into her coat pocket when she boarded the bus, and I was standing close enough to read it. I read everything. It is an occupational hazard or an occupational gift, depending on what happens next.

She sat in the third row, right side, window seat.

They were separated by the aisle — perhaps eighty centimeters of institutional carpet — and they did not look at each other with the specific, practiced, muscular quality of two people expending effort to not look at each other. I want to be precise about this. There is a version of not-looking that is the absence of interest, and there is a version that is the presence of will. The physiotherapist sees the difference the way a cardiologist sees it on a monitor: one is flatline, and the other is a rhythm, and the rhythm has a rate, and the rate was elevated on both sides of the aisle.

I knew, in the way I know when an athlete is compensating — not from any single indicator but from the gestalt, the whole field of micro-data assembling itself into a clinical picture I can read before I can articulate — that these two people had a history they were carrying in their bodies like an old fracture that had healed off-axis.

I did not yet know what it was.

---

Albertville. I need to give you Albertville, because without it none of the rest makes sense, and because the story of what happened at the Pensjonat at Hamar is, in a structural sense, an aftershock of something that happened two years earlier in a French scoring room where I was not present. I am reconstructing. I am inferring. I am doing what I do with an athlete's injury history when they won't tell me where it started — I am reading the scar tissue and working backward.

At the 1992 Albertville Winter Olympics, Irina Viktorovna Zelenko sat on the judging panel for the pairs free skate. One of the American pairs teams — Don Beech's team, his best team, the one he had been building for six years, Elena Moretti and Jason Park, who were twenty-two and twenty-three and who had, privately, quietly, in the way of pairs skaters, fallen in love during the long daily communion of catching each other in the air — received marks that effectively eliminated them from medal contention. The Russian judge's scores were the outliers. Zelenko's technical marks for Moretti-Park were, by the accounts I have been able to locate, between 0.2 and 0.4 points below the panel mean, and her artistic impression marks were lower still. The margins were small in absolute terms and enormous in their consequences. Moretti and Park placed eighth. They needed sixth to secure continued USFSA funding. The funding was not renewed. The pair split the following autumn. Moretti retired from competitive skating and moved back to Parma, Ohio. Park joined an ice show and tore his Achilles tendon in rehearsal in Boise, Idaho, in July 1993.

Don Beech filed a formal protest with the ISU. The protest was reviewed. No scoring irregularity was found. Whether or not one existed — whether Zelenko's marks reflected genuine technical assessment or the kind of strategic block judging that was endemic to the Cold War–era scoring system and that the ISU would not begin to seriously address for another decade — is a question I am not qualified to answer and that this story is not equipped to resolve. I will tell you only that Don Beech believed, with the unshakable certainty of a man who had watched his skaters land every element and then watched the numbers destroy them, that Irina Zelenko had buried his team. And I will tell you that Irina Zelenko, whatever she believed about her own marks, would have known — because everyone in the judging community knew — that she was thereafter regarded by a not-insignificant portion of the American delegation as the specific instrument of a specific injustice.

This is the history that was on the bus. This is the old fracture I was reading through two bodies that would not look at each other.

---

At approximately 14:15, the bus shuddered. I felt it in my feet first — a vibration that was wrong in frequency, a mechanical stammer — and then the engine note changed, dropping from the pressurized hum of a diesel under load to something effortful and arrhythmic, and then the bus listed right, and the driver — his name was Geir, he was perhaps fifty-five, he had the manner of a man who had been driving buses in Hedmark winters for decades and who was not easily alarmed — steered us onto the shoulder with the calm, deliberate motions of a man performing a task he has rehearsed. The engine died. The heater died with it. Geir tried the ignition three times. On the third attempt, nothing happened at all — no catch, no turnover, no sound but the wind outside and the settling of twelve bodies adjusting to the new fact of their stillness.

Geir stood and faced us. He spoke in Norwegian first, then in English that was careful and lightly accented.

"We are stopped. I have radioed Hamar. There is a replacement bus, but the road is closed north of Minnesund because of the storm. They cannot tell me when it will open. It may be one hour or six." He paused. He looked at the windows, which had become opaque with driven snow in the ninety seconds since we'd stopped. "There is a pensjonat — an inn — three hundred meters ahead. I know the owner. There are rooms. I recommend we walk."

Someone — I think it was the Finnish ice dance attaché, a nervous man whose name I have genuinely forgotten — asked how long the heater would last without the engine.

"It won't," Geir said.

We filed off the bus. The cold was not the cold of a thermometer reading; it was architectural. It had structure. The wind came from the northwest in flattened sheets of snow that hit the left side of the body and, within ten steps, numbed everything from the ear to the ankle on that side. I pulled my collar up and leaned into it. The road surface was invisible beneath the drift. We walked in the tire track of what must have been the last vehicle to pass, a shallow trench already filling in.

I was near the back of the line. Don Beech was ahead of me and to my left, walking with his slightly rolling gait, his collar up, his kit bag slung across his body. Irina Zelenko was ahead of him and to the right, walking with her erect carriage and her insufficient coat, her overnight bag held close. I watched her step off the bus. I watched her right foot find the ground and then her left foot find the ground and something happen in the transition — a micro-stumble, the left ankle rolling inward on the uneven frozen surface, her weight shifting to compensate — and I watched her keep walking without any change in her pace or posture, which told me two things: one, that she had turned the ankle, and two, that she was not going to acknowledge it.

The pensjonat materialized out of the whiteness like a memory of a building — first the yellow light of a window, then the dark mass of the roof, then the wooden walls, then the door.

---

## II.

The Sørli Pensjonat was two stories of dark timber on a stone foundation, the kind of roadside inn that had been in continuous operation since before the war and that survived not through renovation but through the sheer inertia of Norwegian wood and Norwegian stubbornness. The lobby was low-ceilinged and paneled in knotted pine that had darkened with decades of diesel fume and wood smoke to the color of old tea. There was a front desk that was also, apparently, a breakfast counter. There was a rack of cross-country skis by the door that may have been for guests or may have been decorative or may have simply been stored there since 1974. The entire ground floor smelled of wool — wet wool, dry wool, the permanent ghost of a thousand wool coats that had hung steaming by the radiator — and beneath the wool, the diesel-and-dust smell of the heating system, which was running and which produced a warmth that was aggressive and localized, scalding near the vents and absent three paces away.

The owner was a woman named Astrid who was perhaps sixty and who looked at twelve foreign strangers appearing out of a blizzard with the expression of someone conducting a rapid mental inventory of available bed linens. Geir spoke to her in the Hedmark dialect, which is softer than Oslo Norwegian and which I, a Bergen woman, followed well enough. He explained the bus. She nodded. She asked how many. He said twelve. She closed her eyes briefly, performing an interior calculation, and then named her rooms.

I stepped forward because I was the one among us who spoke the language, and because logistics is, I have found, the role that physiotherapists are unconsciously assigned in all group situations — we are the profession of functional arrangement, of making broken systems work well enough. Astrid had five rooms available. One double, two twins, one single, and one room in the attic with a fold-out sofa that could sleep two if they were friendly.

Five rooms. Twelve people.

The math was simple and the execution was not. I will spare you the negotiation — the Finnish attaché wanted a room alone, the two Japanese media liaisons were willing to share but wanted to be on the ground floor, the Italian ice dance coach had a CPAP machine and could not share with a stranger. I handled it the way I handle a crowded treatment room during warm-ups: triage, priority, efficiency, a certain quiet authority that comes from being the person holding the clipboard. In fifteen minutes I had assigned everyone. The double went to the two Japanese liaisons. One twin went to the Finnish attaché and the Italian coach, who accepted this pairing with the resignation of men who had not gotten what they wanted but could see there was nothing left to argue for. The single went to a German media coordinator who was running a fever and needed isolation. The attic sofa went to Geir and a Swiss timing official who had bonded on the walk over their shared military service backgrounds.

That left one twin room. Two beds. A nightstand between them. Second floor, end of the hall.

And two people.

Don Beech looked at me. He didn't say anything dramatic. He said: "Is there — could the sofa in the common room work?"

I told him I had asked Astrid. The common room sofa was broken — the frame had cracked under a previous guest — and was awaiting repair. There was no other furniture in the building suitable for sleeping that was not already assigned.

"It's one night," I said, in English. "Maybe less, if the E6 opens."

He looked at the stairs. He looked at me again. Something moved across his jaw — the masseter muscle tightening, a clench he released before it became visible to someone who was not watching for it. I was watching for it.

"Fine," he said. "Fine, sure."
