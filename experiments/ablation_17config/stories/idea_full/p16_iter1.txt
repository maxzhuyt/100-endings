## I. PRELIMINARY STATEMENT

This document is an official incident report filed in compliance with Interagency Directive 9-40.7, which requires all personnel actions involving field operatives to be documented within seventy-two hours of the relevant event. I am aware that Interagency Directive 9-40.7 was rescinded on March 14, 2011, along with the agency it governed, the division I work for, the office I sit in, and, in a meaningful sense, me. I am filing it anyway.

I am filing it because in fifteen years of service to an office that has not officially existed for thirteen of those years, I have never once failed to file the paperwork. I did not fail to file the paperwork when our budget was zeroed out. I did not fail to file the paperwork when our SCIF was converted into a WeWork. I did not fail to file the paperwork when the General Services Administration physically removed my filing cabinets and I began storing classified documents in a mini-fridge I brought from home. I will not fail to file the paperwork now, even though what I am documenting is, by any reasonable legal standard, a sequence of federal crimes I committed in a forty-seven-minute window on a Tuesday in October while drinking a Diet Dr Pepper that I did not expense because I am not a monster.

Let the record show.

The situation is as follows.

At 2:14 PM Central Time on Tuesday, October 8, 2024, I received a phone call from Duke R. Brannigan on an encrypted line that is not supposed to exist anymore, regarding a DARPA-era bioweapons research facility designated Site 371-Echo, located approximately forty feet beneath the Conestoga Mall in Grand Island, Nebraska, which is also not supposed to exist anymore. The facility, which was sealed in 1987 and subsequently lost by every agency that had ever acknowledged it, had suffered a containment breach. A self-replicating fungal biological agent — I am told it has a technical designation, but Duke called it "the white stuff" and then, later, with more urgency, "the *fucking* white stuff" — had begun an uncontrolled bloom within the sealed laboratory space and was consuming the concrete containment walls at an observed rate of approximately four inches per hour.

Duke had accessed the site through a maintenance tunnel he remembered from 1991. He sent me photographs. I will not attach the photographs to this report because they are on my personal phone and also because I would like to sleep again at some point in my life.

According to Duke's assessment, which I have learned over the years to trust with approximately the same reluctance one trusts a tornado warning issued by a man at a gas station who claims he can "feel it in his knees," the bloom would reach the surface-level ventilation system in approximately forty-seven minutes. Once it hit open air and found the jet stream, the spore column — I am quoting Duke here — "would make the Dust Bowl look like a Swiffer commercial."

I asked Duke if he had contacted the Department of Defense.

He said, "They'd take three hours just to figure out who's supposed to answer the phone."

I asked if he had contacted FEMA.

He said a word that I will not include in this report.

I asked him why he was calling me.

He said: "Janet. I need five people. I need them here in forty-seven minutes. And you are the only person on this planet who can make five people exist on paper fast enough to get them through a checkpoint."

This is, I should note, technically a compliment. It is also accurate. I have the filing codes. I have the laminator. I have the seal of an office that no longer exists, which means no one can verify it isn't real, which means in a certain bureaucratic sense it is *more* real than offices that do exist and can be fact-checked. I operate in the gap between institutional memory and institutional amnesia, and that gap, in the United States federal government in the year 2024, is vast enough to drive a truck through.

I told Duke I needed him to understand that what he was asking me to do was — and I used this precise language, because I wanted it on the record, because I am filing this report, because I always file the report — "indefensible, illegal, and maybe necessary."

He said: "Forty-six minutes, Janet."

I said: "Tell me who you need."

He gave me five names.

I will now describe each recruitment in the order they ruined my life.

## II. RECRUITMENT ONE: MARCUS YAU

**Requisition Reference:** IPD-EMER-2024-0001 (fabricated)
**Personnel Action:** Emergency reactivation of inactive field asset
**Subject:** Yau, Marcus T. — Former Demolitions Specialist, DOL Field Operations
**Skillset Required:** Controlled demolition of subsurface access tunnels; containment zone isolation
**Current Status at Time of Contact:** Standing in the parking lot of the Sarpy County Courthouse, Papillion, Nebraska, forty minutes into a custody hearing he had walked out of, removing Precious Moments figurines from a rented U-Haul and throwing them, one by one, onto the asphalt while crying

**Time Remaining to Critical Mass:** 46 minutes

I know Marcus Yau's personnel file the way a cardiologist knows an EKG that keeps showing the same arrhythmia. I have processed his intake paperwork, his field deployment orders, his three commendations, his two formal reprimands, his unofficial fourth commendation that was downgraded to a reprimand when he blew up the wrong bridge in Kandahar (it was, he maintained, "the right bridge strategically but the wrong bridge jurisdictionally"), his medical leave, his return from medical leave, his second medical leave, his administrative separation, and his post-separation request including eleven pages of handwritten notes asking me to make sure his pension was calculated using the 2003 pay scale, not the 2007 revision. I know Marcus Yau. I have always known Marcus Yau. He is the kind of man who makes personnel coordinators age in dog years.

So when Duke told me he needed Marcus, and then told me where Marcus was and what Marcus was doing, I experienced an emotion I can only describe as the opposite of surprise. It was something closer to recognition. Like hearing a song you've been humming all day finally come on the radio.

"He's throwing the figurines?" I said.

"He is launching them," Duke said, and I could hear, faintly, the sound of porcelain shattering. "He is — Janet, he's narrating. He's holding each one up and announcing which one it is before he throws it."

"That sounds right."

"He just said, 'This one is called *Love Is the Sweetest Gift*, Rachel, and I want you to know it retails for forty-seven dollars.'"

"What do you need from me?"

"I need him reactivated. I need a security clearance. His expired in 2014."

"You can't reactivate an expired TS/SCI in forty-six minutes."

"I need you to do something that looks enough like reactivating a TS/SCI that a twenty-three-year-old National Guard sergeant at a checkpoint will salute it."

"Duke."

"Forty-five minutes, Janet."

I put the phone on speaker. I opened a drawer. The drawer contained, among other things: blank Federal Personnel Action forms (SF-50) that I had printed on government paper in 2011 before they took our printer; a laminator I am not technically authorized to use but which has never been inventoried and therefore, ontologically speaking, does not exist; and a rubber stamp bearing the seal of the Interagency Personnel Division, Central Region, which I carved myself from a potato in 2016 when the original stamp was requisitioned by the GSA and which, I maintain, produces an impression that is indistinguishable from the original at any distance greater than six inches.

I fabricated an Emergency Reactivation Order. I fabricated a temporary security clearance. I fabricated a memorandum from the "Office of the Deputy Undersecretary for Interagency Field Coordination," an office that has never existed but whose name is so plausible, so perfectly calibrated to the federal government's infinite appetite for meaningless hierarchy, that no one has ever questioned it. I have used this fake office on nine prior occasions. It has a letterhead. I made the letterhead.

While the laminator warmed up, I called Sergeant First Class Brenda Coolidge at the Nebraska National Guard's 402nd Logistics Support Battalion, because Brenda owes me for the time I expedited a DD-214 correction for her brother-in-law, and because Brenda, like me, is a person who operates the machinery of institutions that have largely forgotten we exist, and there is a freemasonry among such people.

"Brenda, I need a FEMA-loaned specialist cleared through your checkpoint at the Conestoga Mall in Grand Island within the next thirty minutes. He'll have papers. Don't look at the papers too closely."

"Janet, the last time you said that, a man showed up at my gate with a flamethrower."

"That was a different situation."

"Is this going to be like the flamethrower situation?"

"He needs detonation cord."

"Janet."

"Military-grade."

"*Janet.*"

"Brenda, I would not call you if I had anyone else to call. I don't have anyone else to call. I have never had anyone else to call. We are the people who don't have anyone else to call, and that is why we call each other."

A pause. Then: "What kind of det cord, and how much?"

I loved Brenda Coolidge in that moment with the kind of love that is only possible between two women who have spent their careers being the last person in the phone tree.

Meanwhile, Duke was working the other end of the problem, which was that Marcus Yau was in the parking lot of a county courthouse performing a one-man demolition of his marriage using hand-painted porcelain figurines as ordnance.

Duke's voice, low and steady — the voice I have heard him use on hostage-takers, IED wires, and a Rottweiler in Bogotá that had eaten a classified hard drive and needed to be persuaded to enter a crate. He does not use inspirational language. He does not appeal to duty or courage or the greater good. He simply tells you the next thing you need to do in a voice that makes the next thing sound survivable.
