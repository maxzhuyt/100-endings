## I.

**[PRODUCER VOICEOVER, laid over black screen, timecode running in the upper left corner:]**

*What you're looking at — what you're about to look at, if legal doesn't kill me first — was never supposed to be assembled in this order. Some of it is pre-production footage. Some of it is stolen. Some of it was shot by Deshawn on their phone because our second camera op quit outside Amarillo and the first one wouldn't go into the truck stop bathroom at night, which, fair. I've seen the footage. I wouldn't have gone in either.*

*I need you to understand that when we started this project, I thought we were making a story about a whistleblower. Some kind of Erin Brockovich thing but weirder. A person with an unusual ability uncovers a pharmaceutical conspiracy, gathers a team, exposes the truth. That's the pitch I took to the network. That's the story I thought I was producing.*

*I was wrong about what this is.*

*But we'll get there.*

*Let's start with what I was right about, which is that Deshawn Kepler is remarkable, and that remarkable people are almost always having a worse time than you think.*

---

**[CUT TO: Interior. Dallas/Fort Worth International Airport. Terminal C. Men's restroom. 2:47 PM local time. The camera is small, mounted on what appears to be a bag strap — the footage has the low, tilted, half-obscured quality of something recorded semi-covertly. Deshawn Kepler is visible in the mirror over the sinks: late twenties, Black, close-cropped hair, wearing a gray hoodie and the particular facial expression of someone waiting out a migraine. They are standing at the sink. They are not washing their hands. They are gripping the porcelain with both hands and breathing through their mouth.]**

**[PRODUCER VOICEOVER:]**

*This is the footage that started everything. Deshawn recorded this on their own, before we'd even met in person. They told me later they'd started recording themselves in public restrooms about a year before this — not for any documentary purpose, just to prove to themselves that what was happening was real. That they weren't imagining it. They had about four hundred hours of this on a hard drive. Themselves, standing at sinks, or sitting in stalls, or leaning against tile walls, and if you watch the footage with the right headphones, you can hear them whispering. Writing down what they're hearing. What other people are thinking.*

*Not what other people are saying. What they are thinking.*

*I need to be precise about this because the mechanism matters. Deshawn Kepler can hear the unspoken thoughts of any person within approximately thirty feet of a functioning toilet. Not a broken toilet. Not a decorative toilet in a showroom. A toilet that is connected to plumbing, that flushes, that is actively part of a sewage system. The connection appears to be — and I cannot believe I am saying this in a professional context — the connection appears to be fecal. Or at least excretory. Something about the proximity of human bodies to the infrastructure of human waste opens a channel in Deshawn's brain that they cannot close.*

*They did not ask for this. I want to be clear about that too.*

---

Deshawn had mapped every public restroom in DFW International the way other people mapped emergency exits. Not the locations — any traveler knew those — but the *densities*. Terminal C's main restroom near gate C22 had eighteen stalls across the men's and women's rooms. At capacity, during a wave of delayed flights, that was thirty-six minds unspooling simultaneously within Deshawn's range. Terminal E's family restroom near baggage claim was a single unit, lockable, one mind at a time, usually a parent and a screaming toddler, and the toddler's thoughts were a wash of color and need and the mercifully pre-verbal, and Deshawn could handle that. They could handle one stream. They could handle two or three if the thinkers were calm or distracted or bored, which was most people most of the time, because most people's bathroom thoughts were the cognitive equivalent of elevator music — low, repetitive, weirdly banal.

What Deshawn could not handle was the Terminal C men's room at 2:47 PM on a Tuesday in March, which was when a bank of six flights had just landed from various humid places and every man in a thirty-foot radius was either thinking about the thing he'd eaten in Houston that was now disemboweling him or rehearsing, with the frantic specificity of a man who believed himself to be totally alone with his own mind, the lie he was going to tell his wife about where he'd been since Thursday.

Deshawn gripped the sink and breathed.

*—she's going to check the AmEx again, she always checks the AmEx, I should've used the Visa, no, the Visa has the wrong billing address since the move, Christ, okay, what if I just say it was a conference, she knows I have conferences, but she'll ask who else was there and if she calls Derek, Derek will—*

That was stall two.

*—shouldn't have done the brisket, shouldn't have done the brisket, I told myself no brisket after the last time but that sauce, that goddamn sauce, if I die in this airport bathroom my mother will say she expected this, she literally said Randall you're going to die on a toilet, she said that to me when I was eleven—*

That was stall four.

*—inventory report's due by five, inventory report's due by five, nobody gives a shit about the inventory report, Linda gives a shit, Linda lives for the inventory report, if I get back to the office by four I can fake the western warehouse numbers and Linda won't know until next quarter when I am either promoted or dead by my own hand—*

That was stall five. Deshawn had learned to let these streams pass through like radio stations scanned in a moving car. The trick was not to engage. Not to care. Every thought was equally weightless if you let it be. They were all the same, underneath — the same fears, the same hungers, the same petty negotiations with the self. Deshawn had been hearing them since puberty. Fourteen years. Almost half a lifetime of other people's interior weather, arriving unbidden every time they walked within thirty feet of a working toilet, which was — as any urban planner could tell you — essentially everywhere. Buildings were riddled with plumbing. Homes, offices, restaurants, schools. Deshawn had once mapped a route from their apartment in Oak Cliff to their job at the print shop that involved a fourteen-minute detour through a stretch of vacant lots just to avoid the row of restaurants on Jefferson Boulevard, each of which had a bathroom, each of which had a radius, and the radii overlapped, which meant the stretch was a psychic gauntlet of cooks thinking about their debts, waitresses thinking about the thing the manager said, diners thinking about their bodies, their lovers, their bowels, their God, their exes, the news, the things they would never say aloud, the things that lived in the sub-basement of their minds and only surfaced when they were alone with porcelain and privacy and the illusion that nobody could hear them.

Nobody could hear them.

Except Deshawn, who heard all of them, all the time, and wanted very much to hear none of it.

*—look, the point is, the hearing's in nine days and Bridwell's already prepping the denial, but if the contamination data from the Las Cruces wells gets into discovery, we're not talking about a fine, we're talking about—*

Deshawn's hands tightened on the sink.

That stream was different. Not in volume — it was the same interior murmur as all the others, a man alone with his thoughts and his intestines, believing himself utterly private. But the *texture* was different. The other streams were personal. This one was structural. This one had the quality of a man rehearsing before a mirror, choosing his words, pressure-testing his lies, but the rehearsal was aimed not at a wife or a boss but at a committee. A hearing. There were proper nouns: Bridwell, Las Cruces, something that sounded like Halcyenne, which Deshawn's brain parsed first as a woman's name and then, with a small cold lurch, as a pharmaceutical company.

Halcyenne Biologics.

Deshawn knew that name the way they knew their own blood type. It was on the paperwork their mother kept in a shoebox in the closet. It was on the class-action settlement that had paid for two years of their mother's rent. It was on the FDA filing that described the fertility drug that had been administered to their mother in 1996 as part of a Phase II clinical trial that was terminated early for reasons that were listed, in the paperwork, as "strategic portfolio realignment."

Deshawn was the realignment. Deshawn was the strategic outcome. Deshawn and whatever the drug had done to their mother's ovaries, their mother's endocrine system, the fetus that became Deshawn and arrived in the world with a body that didn't match any of the developmental models and a brain that, starting at age thirteen, began picking up transmissions from the minds of nearby humans every time a toilet flushed.

*—the wells are the problem, the wells have been the problem since '19, because the compound doesn't break down in groundwater the way the original environmental assessment said it would, and we knew that, I mean Christ, I've seen the internal models, the half-life in alkaline soil is two hundred years minimum, and it's not inert, it's bioactive, it's doing the same thing in the water table that it did in the trial patients—*

Deshawn didn't want to hear this.

*—the same endocrine disruption. The exact same hormonal cascade. And the exposure is ambient, it's in the drinking water, it's been in the drinking water in Las Cruces for at least four years and in El Paso for maybe longer and the preliminary numbers from Phoenix are—*

Deshawn didn't want to hear this. They pressed their palms against the cool porcelain and tried to do what they always did: let it pass, let it be weightless, let it be someone else's problem.

*—and children have been born. That's the part that. I mean I've read the file. Bridwell showed me the file. Children have been born with the same. I don't know what to call it. The same developmental profile as the original trial cohort. The same endocrine signature. And the company has — there is a protocol. I've seen the term used. "Pharmacovigilance containment." It sounds like safety monitoring but it's not. It's—*

*Don't tell me*, Deshawn thought. *I don't want to know what it is.*

*—it's a suppression architecture. They have people. They track the births. They intervene early. The families are offered settlements contingent on surveillance and nondisclosure and there is a pediatric monitoring program that is actually a—*

*Stop.*

*—and I know this because I approved the budget. I personally approved the budget for the containment protocol in 2021 and I am going to have to sit in front of Mathers' committee in nine days and say I have no knowledge of any ongoing—*

*Stop stop stop.*

*—and I'm frightened. I am genuinely frightened. Because the people who run the containment program are not scientists. They are — I don't know what they are. I asked Bridwell once what happens if one of the families doesn't comply and he said the word "resolution" in a way that—*

The man in stall six flushed. The stream cut out for two seconds — the flush disrupted something, it always did, the burst of water through the pipes scrambled Deshawn's reception like static on a radio — and then it came back, weaker now, the man standing, buckling his belt, his thoughts shifting from the systematic to the personal, the way thoughts do when the body reasserts itself:

*—Jesus Christ, I need to eat more fiber. Margaret's been saying. Margaret's been right. If I have a stroke on the toilet like Dad it'll be—*

The stall door opened. Deshawn, without turning from the sink, watched in the mirror as United States Senator Dale Everett Hutchins (R-TX) emerged from stall six, checked his phone, did not wash his hands, and walked out of the restroom on legs that were slightly unsteady.

**[PRODUCER VOICEOVER:]**

*Deshawn recorded all of that. You can see the moment they recognize Hutchins in the mirror — there's a very brief expression, blink-and-you-miss-it, that I've watched maybe a hundred times. It's not shock. It's not excitement. It looks most like someone who has just been handed a package they did not order and cannot return.*

*What Deshawn does next is — to me, this is the thing that made me want to tell this story. They don't call anyone. They don't follow the senator. They don't start furiously Googling Halcyenne Biologics or Las Cruces groundwater contamination. They turn off the camera, sit down on the restroom floor, and — I know this because they told me, later — they sit there for forty minutes, until the restroom is empty, and they wait for the psychic noise to drain away, and then they get up and go to their gate, and they fly home to Dallas, and the next morning they drive to their mother's house.*

*I asked them why. Why go to your mother? Why not a journalist, a lawyer, the FBI?*

*They said: Because I needed to know if she already knew.*
