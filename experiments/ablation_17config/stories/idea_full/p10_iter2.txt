# The Building

## I.

Esteban Olivera, retired, sixty-seven, formerly of the Compañía Nacional de Seguros where for forty-one years he had modeled catastrophic risk on behalf of clients who did not wish to think about catastrophe, dropped the mate gourd at 7:14 on a Tuesday morning. It struck the kitchen tile and broke into three pieces of unequal size—the largest retaining the silver rim, the two smaller fragments spinning away under the refrigerator where they would be difficult to retrieve. He registered the break. He registered the particular sound, ceramic on ceramic, a sound with a dry finality he associated with events that could not be walked back. He thought of his mother, who had given him the gourd. Then the gourd was in his hand, whole, and the clock read 7:13, and the mate was still hot, and the tile was unmarked, and the sound had not yet happened.

Esteban set the gourd on the counter with the care one might give a detonator. He examined his hands. He examined the tile. He picked the gourd up again and held it over the floor, as if preparing to reproduce the experiment, then did not. He stood in his kitchen for a long time. The clock turned to 7:14 and nothing happened. He drank the mate. He charted nothing—he would not begin charting for eleven more days—but he did open a small notebook and write: *Gourd. 7:14 → 7:13. Apx. 43 seconds.* Then he underlined *43* and put a question mark beside it and sat looking at the question mark until it became abstract, just a shape, a fishhook dangling in white space.

---

On the third floor, six days earlier—though Esteban would not learn this for weeks—Delmira Etcheverry had quoted a man forty pesos too few for a screen replacement. She knew the figure was wrong the instant it left her mouth, could feel the error like a hair on her tongue, and the man across the counter was already nodding, already reaching for his wallet, and she opened her mouth to correct herself. Then she was opening her mouth for the first time. The man was still reaching, but reaching for his phone, sliding it across to her, and the words she had not yet said were available again, like a blank space in a form she could fill in differently.

She said the correct price. The man paid it.

That evening, alone in her apartment, Delmira stood before the bathroom mirror and said, "I told him the wrong price and then it hadn't happened." She said it in a flat tone, a recitation. She said it again. The mirror confirmed nothing. She turned away and sat on the edge of the bathtub and tried to recall the last time she had told anyone something important while looking them in the face, and could not, and did not try the experiment again for four days—though when she did, deliberately misquoting a charging cable to a woman who was already impatient, the same reversal occurred: 43 seconds, mechanical, total, irrefutable, private.

---

Sofía Briceño, seventeen, fourteen weeks along, had slipped on the stairwell between the second floor and the third on a Sunday when the elevator was out. She fell three steps—not many, but enough. Her hand missed the railing by the width of two fingers. Her left knee struck the edge of a step and her center of gravity, newly shifted in ways she was still learning, failed her, and for a half-second she was in the air, and in the air she thought with a clarity she associated only with examinations she had not studied for: *This is the kind of fall that—*

Then she was standing at the top of the three steps, her knee intact, her hand on the railing, her weight balanced, the stairwell quiet. She sat down on the top step and placed both hands on her abdomen and stayed there for a long time, listening to the building settle around her.

She crossed herself. She crossed herself again. She descended the stairs slowly, one hand on the railing and the other where it had been, and when she reached the bottom she looked back up the stairwell and said, to no one, "Thank you," though she could not have said to whom.

---

Artigas Maldonado, who kept his notarial office on the ground floor in what had been the building's portería before the management company converted it, stamped CERTIFICADO on a power-of-attorney document that should have received COPIA FIEL. The ink was indelible. The document was not his. The client was a lawyer from Pocitos who had made it clear, through the elaborate courtesy of the professional class, that mistakes were something other people made. Artigas looked at the stamp. The stamp looked back at him, as stamps do—blunt, indifferent, already dry.

Then the document was clean. The stamp was in his hand, un-pressed. The ink pad was still open. The lawyer from Pocitos was still looking at her phone.

Artigas pressed COPIA FIEL—correctly, this time—with a hand that did not tremble, because he was a man who had learned not to tremble in front of lawyers. After the client left, he locked the office door and sat at his desk and pressed the wrong stamp on a blank sheet of paper and waited. Nothing happened. He pressed it again. Nothing. He pressed the correct stamp, then the wrong one, then the correct one again, and still nothing happened, and he was beginning to persuade himself it had been an error of perception, the kind of brief neurological misfiring that men of fifty-three should report to their doctors but do not, when his elbow knocked the inkwell off the desk and time ran backward forty-three seconds and the inkwell was upright and he was holding the wrong stamp over the blank paper again and every hair on his forearms was standing.

He locked the inkwell in his desk. He went upstairs. He poured a glass of grappa and drank it and poured another and held it without drinking and thought: *Only when it was an accident. Only when I did something I regretted. Only when no one was looking.*

---

On the fourth floor—the same evening Artigas locked his inkwell away, though neither of them would know this for some time—Claudette Paternó nicked the left ear of a wire-haired fox terrier named Duque, whose owner had gone out to buy cigarettes and who was therefore, for the purposes of the cut, an audience of zero.

The blood came quickly, the way ear blood does. Claudette said: "Did I just—?" She reached for the styptic powder. Then Duque's ear was whole and the scissors were where they had been three snips ago and the tuft of fur she'd cut was back on the ear, unsevered, and the blood had retreated into the capillary it came from.

Claudette lowered the scissors. She looked at Duque, who looked back at her with the philosophical indifference of a terrier who has been groomed before.

"Did that happen?" she asked the dog.

Duque panted.

"Did I cut you and then not cut you?"

Duque shifted on the table.

"Is this something I should—? Would you say this is—? Am I having a—?"

She did not finish any of the questions, because Claudette Paternó had not finished a declarative sentence in thirteen years—not since the afternoon her daughter had said, from the doorway, suitcase in hand, *"I'm going and I won't be back,"* and Claudette had discovered that certainty, spoken aloud, was a kind of violence she could no longer perform. She asked questions now. Only questions. It was not a decision she remembered making. It was a weather system that had moved in and never moved out.

She finished grooming Duque. The cut was excellent. She could not say so.

---

Five discoveries. Five solitudes. The same forty-three seconds, precise as a metronome, operating on five floors of a seven-story building in Montevideo's Ciudad Vieja whose street address—1037 Reconquista—appeared on no lists of architectural distinction and in no databases of paranormal significance and whose only notable feature, if you asked anyone, was that the elevator was slow and the portero, a man named Cabrera, never seemed to leave.

Each person was alone when it happened. Each would be alone every time it happened. The power had the architecture of a secret: it required a room with no one else in it. And so each resident absorbed the discovery into the existing structure of their privacy—Esteban into his notebooks, Delmira into her bathroom mirror, Sofía into her faith, Artigas into his locked desk, Claudette into her questions—the way a building absorbs water damage: invisibly, structurally, one floor at a time.

---

## II.

On the Friday, at 3:47 in the afternoon, Claudette began the Schnauzer. The Schnauzer—Reina, belonging to the woman in 4B who traveled for work and who had left a key and a list of instructions that Claudette thought might be longer than some constitutions—was skittish, inclined to snap, and equipped with a wiry coat that forgave nothing. A crooked line on a Schnauzer stayed crooked. A pulled hair produced a yelp. Claudette had groomed Reina eleven times and had never once been satisfied.

Today she would be. She would be because today she had a margin of forty-three seconds on every error, and she had learned in the weeks since Duque that the rewind was reliable, reproducible, and morally—was it morally anything? She did not know. Was it cheating to undo a nick? Was it different from simply being careful? Did the dog experience the cut, in any world that mattered, if the cut unhappened?

She did not answer these questions. She began.

*

One floor below, at 3:51, Sofía Briceño stood in the hallway outside the utility room and listened. She could hear Claudette's clippers above her, faintly, through the ceiling. She could hear Cabrera's radio from the lobby: a cumbia station, the same one always. She could hear no one on her floor.

The utility room served as a shared pantry for residents who paid into the building's kitchen cooperative—a system that had existed since the 1970s and that Sofía, who had moved in only seven months ago, had never been invited to join. The room contained, among other things, dry pasta, canned tomatoes, cooking oil, packages of yerba, and a wheel of cheese that someone refreshed monthly and whose provenance was one of the building's small, jealously guarded mysteries.

Sofía had stolen from this room three times. Each time, she opened the padlock with a key she had found in the fire-hose cabinet, took what she needed, and relocked. Each time, the click of the padlock going home—a small, sharp sound in the quiet hallway—was the moment she rewound. She would hear the click, feel the guilt arrive like a hand on her shoulder, and then be standing in front of the closed door again, the food in her apartment, the lock apparently untouched, the click unclicked.

She did not fully understand the mechanics. She knew only that the food stayed taken, the lock stayed locked, and the sound of her trespass—which was, she believed, the only evidence—was swallowed back into the silence it came from.

Today she needed rice. She put the key in the lock.

*

At 3:53, Delmira pressed CALL.

She was sitting on her bed with the phone on speaker and her back against the headboard and a pillow clutched in both hands because she needed something to hold during this or she would hold her own face and she had promised herself she would stop doing that.

The phone rang. Rang. Rang. Then Alejandro's voice—not a recording, the man himself, live, saying "Hola?" the way he always said it, with the faint upward lilt that suggested he was hoping it might be someone interesting and was prepared to be disappointed.

"It's me," Delmira said.

"Delmira."

"Don't say my name like that."

"Like what?"

"Like it's a chore."

And they were off, down the same corridor they always went down, and Delmira could feel the conversation heading toward the place it always headed—the place where he would say something reasonable and calm and she would say something honest and jagged and the call would end with him sighing and her throat full of glass—except that today she had forty-three seconds. Today, when the conversation reached its worst point, she could rewind and arrive there again, better armed.

She had been practicing. In the weeks since the first accidental rewind, she had called Alejandro four times and rewound six—no, seven—times across those calls. She had honed her responses. She had found the sentence that actually hurt him, the one that punctured the composure, and she had delivered it with increasing precision, like a surgeon locating a nerve.

The sentence was: "You left because you were afraid the baby wasn't yours and you were right to be afraid."

There was no baby. There had never been a baby. That was what made the sentence perfect—it was a lie calibrated to the exact shape of his worst fear, and when she said it, in exactly the right tone, with exactly the right pause before *afraid*, his silence on the other end was a silence she could feel in her chest, a silence that reorganized the room.

She was going to say it again today. She was going to say it and then rewind and then not say it, because the power, she had discovered, preserved her memory of both timelines—the one where the words landed and the one where they didn't—and what she wanted was not cruelty but the *memory* of cruelty, the knowledge that she could have destroyed him and chose not to, which was a different and more sustaining kind of power than destruction itself.

The conversation was reaching the corridor. She adjusted the pillow. She opened her mouth.

*

At 3:55, Esteban was seated at his kitchen table with a portable radio tuned to a station that would, at 4:00, broadcast the day's lottery results for the first of three draws. He had a pen. He had a square of graph paper—one of several hundred squares he had cut, over the years, from larger sheets, because a full sheet of graph paper implied a problem of full-sheet magnitude, and the lottery was a problem that fit inside a 10×10 grid.

His system was simple. He would listen to the numbers. He would memorize them. Time would rewind. He would write them down. He would go to the kiosk on Sarandí before the 4:30 cutoff and buy a ticket with the numbers he had already heard.

He had tested this twice. The first time, he had won thirteen hundred pesos—a sum so modest it confirmed the system's viability without attracting attention. The second time, two days later, he had deliberately chosen only four of the six numbers, reducing his prize to an even smaller amount, because Esteban understood something about catastrophic risk that his neighbors did not: *the danger is never the first success. The danger is the pattern of success. The danger is the moment the dataset contains enough points to describe a curve that someone else can see.*

Today he was going to win again. A small amount. Just enough.

He checked the clock. 3:56. Four minutes.

*

At 3:58, Artigas Maldonado unlocked his office, closed the door, and laid three documents on his desk.

The first was a bill of sale for a property in Colonia del Sacramento, correctly dated, correctly notarized, waiting only for a signature that would arrive Monday. The second was the same bill of sale, but with a date three months earlier—before a zoning change that had made the property significantly more valuable. The third was a blank sheet of notarial paper with a government watermark.

The arrangement was not complicated. A man named Fossati had approached him two weeks ago with a proposal: if Artigas could produce a notarized bill of sale dated before May 15th, the property's value under the old zoning would apply, and Fossati would share the difference—a difference of approximately two hundred and forty thousand pesos, of which Artigas's portion would be sixty thousand. Sixty thousand pesos for a stamp and a date. Artigas's rent was fourteen thousand a month.
