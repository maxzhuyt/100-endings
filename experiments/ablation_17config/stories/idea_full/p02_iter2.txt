# The Analyst's Record

## I.

Six feeds. I will start there because that is where it started for me — not on the lakebed, not in the dark below, but in a room I am not permitted to describe, in a building whose location I am not permitted to name, seated in a chair with a broken lumbar support that someone had shimmed with a folded piece of cardboard from a case of copy paper. I remember the cardboard. I remember the brand name printed on it. I could tell you, but it would not help you see what I saw. I am trying to be precise about what matters.

0347 local. Six biometric feeds activated in sequence: One, Two, Three, Four, Five, Six. The designations are mine. They had call signs — proper ones, vetted, randomized — but I will not use them here, and not because of any regulation. I will not use them because by the end I had given them other names, privately, and those names are between me and whatever this document becomes.

One's feed resolved first. Heartrate 64. Blood oxygen 98. Core temperature 36.8 Celsius. Cortisol within normal parameters. A body at rest in a body that knew how to be at rest in places that should not permit it. I noted this. I note it again now.

The helmet cameras came online with static — that particular digital visual noise that is not snow, not grain, but the camera's own nervous system trying to make sense of insufficient light. Then the auto-exposure caught. And what it caught was this:

A lakebed. White. Not sand-white. Salt-white. The white of a place where water died and left its mineral skeleton behind. The cameras' auto-adjustment could not decide what to do with the albedo; the footage pulsed between overexposed wash and sudden, almost granular clarity. In the clear frames I could see the surface was not smooth. It was tessellated — cracked into polygonal plates like the skin of something desiccated beyond recognition. Each plate was slightly cupped at its edges, curling upward, so the ground looked as though it were trying to peel itself away from the earth beneath.

And the boats. You must understand what I mean when I say boats. I do not mean boats. I mean the bodies of boats — keels and ribs and rusted hull plates, half-buried, listing at angles that suggested the water had not retreated gradually but in lurches, stranding each vessel in whatever attitude it held when the ground seized it. The thermal overlay painted them as dark voids against the faintly warmer lakebed. Cold metal holds no heat. They registered as absences. Holes in the image where objects should have been. I found this appropriate, though I did not think so at the time. At the time I was checking feeds.

The six operatives moved in formation: two rows of three, staggered, covering their sectors. On thermal they were amber. Not the amber of caution lights or autumn — the amber of living bodies rendered in false color, the particular warm glow that a forward-looking infrared system assigns to anything that maintains its own temperature against the environment. They were the only warm things in the frame. For kilometers in every direction, the camera showed nothing but the blue-black cold of salt and rust and dead earth. Six amber shapes moving across a dead world. I have seen this configuration hundreds of times. I have seen it in deserts, in cities, in mountain passes where the wind chill turned the background nearly black and the bodies burned like processional candles. It never looks like anything other than what it is: the loneliest kind of purpose.

They were converging on a structure. The briefing materials called it Facility 211-B, which tells you nothing except that someone once assigned it a number. On the body cameras it looked like this: a concrete rectangle, partially buried, as though the lakebed had spent four decades trying to swallow it and had gotten roughly halfway. The exposed portion rose perhaps two meters above grade. The concrete was stained with mineral deposits — white and yellow streaks that had run down the walls like the facility was weeping salt. There was a steel door set into the face of it, or rather there was a steel door set into a concrete collar that protruded from the structure's western face at a slight angle, as though the whole building had shifted on its foundation. The door was recessed. The collar framed it like a mouth.

I noted the time: 0351. I noted heart rates: 64, 58, 70, 79, 61, 83. I noted a cortisol estimate on Six that flagged amber. Not red. Amber. Elevated but within acceptable range for a pre-breach posture. I logged it. I have logged thousands of cortisol readings. They are numbers. They are only numbers until they stop being numbers, and you cannot know when that transition will occur, which means you must treat every number as though it is already more than a number, which means you must not treat any number as more than a number, because if you do you will not survive this work.

I did not know their faces. The helmet cameras pointed outward. I saw what they saw, not what they looked like. This is by design. You become attached to faces. You do not become attached to a camera feed. This is the theory.

No one had spoken. The formation advanced across the salt in silence, and the silence had a texture on the audio monitors — a flat, faintly hissing nothing, the sound of a landscape with no vegetation, no water, no wildlife, nothing to interrupt the passage of air across a microphone's foam windscreen. I listened to six channels of that nothing. Beneath it, if I isolated the feeds and pushed the gain, I could hear footsteps. The crunch of salt crust giving way. And beneath that, respiration. Six rhythms of breathing, each slightly different, each as specific as a fingerprint.

I was recording. I am always recording. It is my function. It is, if I am honest — and I am trying to be honest here, I am trying to be more honest than the format permits — the only function I have ever been adequate to. I watch. I record. I maintain the record.

I did not yet understand that maintaining the record would become an act I cannot find the correct word for. Witness is too grand. Surveillance is too sterile. I was keeping vigil. I did not know for whom.

## II.

I should say something about what I am. What my role is. I have been told not to, and I have been told that this document is to be a technical account, and I have decided that a technical account is impossible. The technical data exist elsewhere, in servers I cannot access, in formats I am not cleared to audit. What I can provide — what I am compelled to provide, because the technical data without this are a skeleton without the thing that moved inside it — is the record of what it was to sit in that room with those feeds and to watch.

I am an analyst. This means I analyze. The word shares a root with *analysis*, which shares a root with the Greek *analusis*, a loosening, an undoing. I have thought about this. I loosen events into their components. I undo what happened into what can be measured. Heartrate. Respiratory rate. Ambient temperature. Audio amplitude. Positional data. Camera angle and focal depth. I receive a human experience and I render it into streams of quantified information, and from those streams I build an assessment: Is the operative compromised? Is the mission proceeding within acceptable parameters? Is intervention required?

I have never intervened. My role is advisory. I advise a chain of command that exists above me and to which I transmit but from which I rarely receive. I am, in the topology of these operations, an endpoint. Data flows to me. Assessments flow from me. Nothing else returns.

I had never met any of the six. This is by design. I knew their call signs, which I will not reproduce. I knew their medical histories, which were comprehensive: surgical records, allergy profiles, dental charts, genetic markers for susceptibility to specific chemical agents. I knew that One had elevated liver enzymes that were being monitored. I knew that Two had a titanium intramedullary rod in her left femur — a repair from a training injury four years prior — and that on thermal imaging this rod appeared as a thin bright line, warmer than the surrounding bone, a filament of false heat that pulsed gently with arterial flow. I came to think of it as a wick. I knew that Four's resting heartrate ran high — not pathologically, just constitutionally, the way some people run warm or dream in color. His baseline was 76. I had to recalibrate my expectations for him. What looked like mild stress for anyone else was, for Four, simple existence.

I knew their blood types. I knew their maximum recorded VO2 thresholds. I did not know where they were from, whether they had children, whether they preferred silence or noise in their off-hours, whether they had been afraid before this or if this was the first time.

This is easier. I was told this during training — that anonymity protects the analyst from identification, and identification leads to compromised judgment. The analyst who sees a name will imagine a life. The analyst who imagines a life will hesitate when the data require action. Hesitation is latency. Latency is failure. Failure, in this context, means someone dies who might not have.

I understood the logic. I agreed with it. I practiced it for seven years across I will not say how many operations. I was adequate. I was, in my evaluations, described as *precise*, *consistent*, and *appropriately detached*. I was proud of this. Detachment was a skill I had cultivated with the same rigor that the operatives cultivated their ability to move in silence, to breathe in a controlled cadence, to528suppress the flinch responses that every human body produces when it is frightened. We were, all of us, professionals of suppression. We had merely suppressed different things.

I am not fully convinced. I should tell you that. The system works. Anonymity does protect the analyst. I do not doubt this. I doubt only whether what it protects the analyst *from* is something the analyst should be protected from. I doubt whether the container it builds around the watching — the prophylactic distance, the numeric abstraction — is a tool or a wound. I did not doubt this before. I am telling you when I began to doubt.

I am telling you about this operation.

I am writing this because the technical record will show that Facility 211-B was breached at 0353 local time and that the operation concluded at 0541 with a total loss of the six-member team, and that the acoustic-triggered dispersal system functioned as designed, and that the agent was later identified as a binary organophosphate consistent with late-Soviet antipersonnel research. The technical record will show heartrate curves and time-of-death estimates and ambient toxicology readings. The technical record is accurate. The technical record is not enough.

## III.

The breach.

0353. One placed a shaped charge on the steel door. The detonation was a white flash on all six cameras simultaneously, then a compression artifact as the audio feeds clipped — the sound too loud for the microphones, rendered as a flat digital ceiling, a plateau of maximum amplitude that held for 1.3 seconds and then dropped to a ringing decay. The door failed along its hinge line. Thermal showed the metal edges glowing — not red, not in false color, but in the particular hot white that means recent energy transfer, means a thing has been forced.

They entered.

What the cameras showed me first: a vertical shaft. A steel-rung ladder descending into dark so complete that the night-vision systems switched to active infrared, projecting their own invisible light and reading its return. The walls of the shaft were poured concrete, stained with mineral efflorescence — white crystalline blooms that caught the IR illumination and flared like frost. The ladder rungs were intact but corroded. I could see the oxidation as a textural roughness in the footage. Operative Two tested the first rung with her weight. It held. She began the descent.

I watched her thermal signature drop below the lip of the shaft and into the earth.

One by one, they followed. Six amber shapes sinking into a column of cold. The thermal gradient was visible in real time: surface temperature at the shaft opening was 11 degrees Celsius; at ten meters depth it was 6; at twenty, it registered 2.8. The earth was cold. The facility had not been heated in decades. It held the temperature of a cellar, of the deep ground, of a place that had been sealed and forgotten and left to equilibrate with geology.

The ladder ended at a landing. A corridor extended in both directions — north and south, according to the positional overlay. The walls were poured concrete with a skim coat of plaster that had cracked and separated in sheets, hanging from the walls like dead skin. Cyrillic signage. I could not read it. The text-recognition system provided partial translations: LEVEL 2. LABORATORY ACCESS. AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY. And below one sign, hand-lettered in what appeared to be marker, something the system could not parse — a note, or a joke, or a warning left by someone who had worked here and felt the need to add a human annotation to an institutional sign. I will never know what it said.

They split. One, Two, and Three moved north. Four, Five, and Six moved south. My screen divided accordingly. I maintained all twelve feeds — six biometric, six visual — on a single array. The audio I monitored through headphones, cycling between channels on a six-second rotation. When I was not actively listening to a channel, it ran through a threshold-alert algorithm that would flag any sound above ambient baseline.

This is how I see rooms. Not as rooms. As composites.

One's camera showed a corridor that narrowed as it advanced. The ceiling lowered incrementally — not by design, I think, but because the structure had settled, the weight of the lakebed above pressing the concrete down by millimeters over decades. Pipes ran along the ceiling. They were lagged with material I could not identify on camera — fibrous, gray-white, likely asbestos insulation. Some of the lagging had fallen away, exposing pipe joints that wept mineral stains.

Three's camera, from two meters behind and slightly to the left, showed the same corridor but differently — the angle catching the floor, which was poured concrete covered in a skin of fine sediment. Their footsteps left prints. I could see this on Three's feed as One moved forward: each boot pressed a clear tread pattern into dust that had been undisturbed for perhaps forty years. First footprints in four decades. I noted this. I should not have noted this. It was not operationally relevant.

Two's camera caught the first doorway. She paused at the threshold and swept the room with her light. What the camera showed: a laboratory. Long benches — steel-topped, bolted to the floor. Rows of glass vessels — fermenters, I learned later, used for culturing biological material at scale. They were sealed, or had been; some of the seals had degraded, and the glass was clouded from the inside with a residue that looked, on camera, like fog frozen mid-drift. The color was uncertain. The camera's white balance shifted as Two moved her head, and the residue went from yellowish to gray to a faint, unsettling rose. I do not know which was accurate. I do not know if color accuracy matters when describing the inside of a vessel that once held something designed to be lethal at the microgram level.

There were personal effects. This is the part I need to tell carefully.

In the second laboratory Two entered, there was a desk pushed against the wall. On the desk: a ceramic mug, white, with a chip on the rim. A ballpoint pen. A notebook, closed, its cover warped with moisture damage. Beside the notebook, a framed photograph — small, the size of a playing card — but the camera's resolution could not resolve the image. I saw a shape that might have been a person or two people or a person holding something. A child, perhaps. I am aware that I may be furnishing this from my own expectation. The camera showed a small framed object with a light-colored shape in its center. Everything else is my inference.

Down the corridor, through a reinforced door that stood open — its hydraulic closer having long since failed, the arm hanging limp — Four's team found a recreation room. I saw this on Four's feed. A table. Four chairs. A chessboard, set up mid-game. The pieces were wooden — I could see the grain on the close-up when Four paused, as he should not have paused, to look. Someone was playing white. The position was developed beyond the opening into something that looked, to my untrained eye, like the middle of an actual game — not a diagram or a puzzle but a real game between two real people who had gotten up from the table and left and never returned.
