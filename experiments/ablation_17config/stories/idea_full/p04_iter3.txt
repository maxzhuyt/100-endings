# The Air Purifier

The money is still in there. I want you to know that now.

Mariana Vargas is, at this very moment — or at the moment that matters, the one I'm going to unspool for you like thread off a bobbin while I sit here with this glass of Sancerre that I've earned — pulling her Civic into her sister Lucia's driveway at an angle that suggests she learned to park from a YouTube video she didn't finish. She has a canvas tote in the passenger seat. Inside the tote: two bottles of that rosé with the illustrated woman on the label, the one every woman I know has been bringing to every gathering since 2021 like it's a sacrament, and beneath the bottles, folded neatly, a reusable shopping bag large enough to hold a discontinued Molekule air purifier. The air purifier is on a shelf in what used to be the guest bedroom of Lucia's house. Inside the air purifier, in the space where the PECO filter once lived before the company's class-action recall, there is sixty thousand dollars in cash. Twenties and hundreds, vacuum-sealed in freezer bags. Mariana put it there herself two summers ago. She is going to spend the next six hours in that house — drinking the rosé, eating Lucia's grain bowl with the turmeric dressing, laughing at the right moments, and trying, with increasing desperation, to get thirty seconds alone with an object she could lift with one hand — and she is going to leave at 4:47 p.m. without the money and without having told anyone why she needed it.

She never did learn to park straight.

I know how it ends because I was there. I'm always there. Celeste who brings the good cheese. Celeste who doesn't overstay. Celeste who has been Mariana's best friend since sophomore orientation at Rutgers, when Mar knocked on my dorm room door holding a handle of Svedka and said, "I'm Mariana, I think I'm on the wrong floor, do you have cups," and I said, "I have mugs," and she said, "Even better," and that was it, that was the whole audition, we were cast. Twenty-two years. I've watched her fall in love four times, get her real estate license twice, and lie to her sister with a fluency that would make you weep if you understood what it cost her. I understand what it costs her. That's why I'm telling you.

Here. Sit. Have some wine. Watch this.

---

The morning started at 10:15 with Lucia's front door already open, because Lucia leaves the front door open during gatherings as a statement of philosophy. Lucia believes in *flow*. She has said this to me using that word no fewer than six times. The flow of people, the flow of air, the flow of conversation. What flows most reliably through Lucia's open front door is her cat, who then sits in the driveway and stares at traffic with the bearing of a deposed monarch, but this is not a concern Lucia has integrated into her philosophy.

Mariana walked in and I watched her from the kitchen island, where I'd been stationed since 9:40 helping Lucia arrange charcuterie on a board made from reclaimed barn wood. I should say: I watched her *catalog*. You could see it happen behind her eyes, this quiet inventory of everything that had changed. The kitchen was different. Quartz countertops — bright white, veined with gray, the kind that look like frozen rivers if you're the sort of person who thinks about frozen rivers while making toast, and Lucia is that sort of person. The old tile backsplash was gone. In its place, a sage-green zellige that caught the light in a way that was, I have to admit, beautiful. A mounted herb garden hung above the sink: rosemary, thyme, something purple I couldn't identify. Basil, maybe. Everything was labeled in Lucia's handwriting on little slate tags. The family photos on the hallway wall had been rearranged — same frames, new configuration, as if someone had played a shell game with the Vargas family history and hoped no one would notice that the photo from the Outer Banks trip, the one where Greg had his arm around Lucia and Mariana was standing three inches too close to Greg's other side, had been quietly retired.

Mariana noticed. I saw her notice. She hugged Lucia. She said the kitchen looked incredible. She put the rosé in the fridge. She said hi to me and squeezed my hand and I squeezed back and there was a whole conversation in that squeeze — *I'm here, I see you, please don't do anything insane* — and then she did the thing I knew she would do, because I know Mariana the way you know a song, not the words necessarily but the structure, where it's going to swell, where the bridge is, where it'll break.

She drifted toward the hallway.

You have to understand the geography. Lucia's house is a three-bedroom colonial in Maplewood, New Jersey, built in 1987 and renovated in rolling waves that correspond to the emotional weather of Lucia's marriage. First renovation: 2018, the kitchen, right after Greg made VP at the consulting firm and Lucia felt, I think, that she needed a room in the house that reflected his success back at him so he'd keep associating home with winning. Second renovation: 2020, the back patio, during lockdown, when the house became everything and they needed an outside that felt like an inside. Third renovation: 2023, the guest bedroom, after Greg's "awakening," which is what Lucia calls it, and which I will describe to you now with as much generosity as I can summon, which is some, because I am a generous person who also has eyes.

Mariana rounded the hallway corner. I know what she expected: the room as she'd last seen it during those three weeks in June 2022 when Lucia took Amara to her parents' in Bayonne and Mariana — who was between apartments, between jobs, between the person she'd been and the person she was becoming, and I'm going to be careful here because there are things I know that I choose when to say — stayed at the house. To help with the cat. That was the stated reason. She stayed, and Greg stayed, and the guest bedroom had a queen bed with a gray linen duvet and a nightstand with a brass lamp and, on the shelf by the window, a Molekule air purifier that Greg had bought during his indoor-air-quality phase, which preceded his fermentation phase, which preceded his breathwork phase, in the way that Greg's phases always precede each other like waves, each one erasing the footprints of the last.

The door was open.

The bed was gone.

In its place: a low wooden platform, maybe eight inches off the floor, with a thin cushion covered in undyed linen. Bolsters in various sizes lined the perimeter — some cylindrical, some crescent-shaped, all in muted earth tones, like the room had been decorated by a very tasteful desert. Two Himalayan salt lamps glowed amber on either side of the platform, though it was 10:30 in the morning and there was plenty of natural light. The blinds had been replaced with bamboo shades. A Bluetooth speaker on the windowsill emitted a sound I can only describe as marine — not whale song exactly, but the ambient noise of a large creature existing peacefully in deep water. Or a man exhaling into a didgeridoo. One or the other. There was a small altar — I'm going to call it an altar because that's what it was, even though Greg calls it an "intention shelf" — with a candle, a chunk of amethyst, a framed photo of Amara at the beach, and a copy of a book whose title I will not dignify by repeating but which involved the words *masculine*, *wound*, and *reclaiming*.

And there, on the shelf by the window — the same shelf, the only surviving landmark from the room's previous life — was the air purifier. White. Cylindrical. Unplugged. The little blue LED ring on its top panel was dark. It sat between a singing bowl and a bundle of dried sage, and if you didn't know what was inside it, you'd think it was just another prop in the theater of Greg's renovation, another object that used to do something practical and now existed to *hold space*.

Mariana stared.

I could see her from the kitchen island. I could see her shoulders. I have known those shoulders for twenty-two years and I can read them the way a sailor reads weather, and what they were saying was: *oh no, oh no, oh no.*

Greg appeared behind her. I don't know where he came from. Greg has a way of materializing in doorways like a man who has been practicing his entrances, which, knowing Greg's recent curriculum, he probably has. He was wearing a linen shirt, untucked, sleeves rolled to the forearm, and joggers that cost more than joggers should, and his feet were bare, and he had the particular glow of a forty-four-year-old man who has recently discovered cold plunging and wants you to know it without saying so.

"Beautiful, right?" he said. He was looking at the room but also at Mariana, or at Mariana looking at the room, which is a different thing. "The energy in there is completely different since the renovation."

Mariana said, "Wow."

"Lucia thought I was crazy when I pulled the bed out," Greg said, and he leaned against the doorframe with a casualness that was the opposite of casual. "But I told her — this room was *holding* something. You could feel it. Something from before. I wanted to clear it."

He said *clear it*. He said *something from before*. And Mariana said, "It looks great, Greg," in a voice as thin as paper, and turned around, and went back to the kitchen, and picked up her wine, and looked at me.

"The countertops are nice," she said.

I said, "Aren't they."

---

Brunch was on the patio. Lucia had made grain bowls — quinoa, roasted sweet potato, avocado, a turmeric-tahini dressing that was, genuinely, delicious, I'll give her that. Greg was grilling halloumi, which he did with the silent intensity of a man performing a sacred rite. Amara, Lucia's six-year-old, was running between the patio and the backyard in a costume that was half princess, half astronaut, fully committed to neither. There were seven of us total: Lucia, Greg, Mariana, me, Lucia's friend Dawn from her ceramics class, Dawn's husband Rob who looked like he'd rather be anywhere else, and Amara, who counted as a full person because she had the conversational energy of at least two adults.

Mariana was sitting across from me. She was eating. She was laughing at Dawn's story about a kiln mishap. She was, to anyone who didn't know her, completely fine.

At 11:40 she said, "I think I'm getting a headache."

She pressed two fingers to her temple in a gesture she'd been doing since college to indicate distress, and which I had seen her deploy in contexts ranging from genuinely debilitating migraines to *I don't want to talk to this person at this party anymore*. Today it lived somewhere in the middle. The headache was not real, but the pain was.

"Oh no," Lucia said. "Do you want Advil? I have Advil."

"I might just go lie down for a bit," Mariana said. "In the guest — in that room. The dark might help."

"There's no bed in there anymore, remember?" Lucia said, with the cheerful obliviousness that is her most defining and most maddening quality. "It's Greg's space now. But you could lie on the couch in the living room? I can close the curtains."

"The bolsters are actually really supportive," Mariana said, and I watched her build this lie in real time, brick by brick, and it was like watching someone construct a house on a foundation of pudding. "I just need to lie down somewhere dark and quiet for ten minutes."

Greg stood up from the grill.

"I have a protocol for headaches," he said. And the way he said *protocol* — with the earnest, almost tender precision of a man who has recently learned a word and wants to use it in every available sentence — you'd think he was offering to perform surgery. "It's a breathwork sequence. Very gentle. Buteyko-based, with some craniosacral integration. It's been — honestly, Mar, it's been life-changing for me."

He called her *Mar*. He had always called her Mar. So did I. So did Lucia. It was a common nickname and it meant nothing and it meant everything and Mariana looked at me and I looked at my wine.

"You don't have to—" Mariana started.

"No, seriously, it's like ten minutes. Twelve tops. You just lie on the platform and I guide you through the breathing pattern. It recalibrates the vagus nerve. I've been studying the craniosacral rhythms — you know the craniosacral system? It's like — okay, so your cerebrospinal fluid has its own tide—"

"Greg," Lucia said, fond and familiar, a wife's shorthand for *you're doing it again.*

"I'm just saying it works," Greg said. "Let me walk you through it."

And before Mariana could refuse — before she could construct the next lie, or find the exit, or do the thing she actually needed to do — Greg was on his feet, his hand not quite on her back but near it, guiding her down the hallway toward the Breathing Room, and she was going, because what else could she do? She had announced a headache. A headache cure was being offered. To refuse would require a reason, and every honest reason was a door she could not open.

I stayed at the kitchen island. I refilled my glass. Through the hallway I could hear Greg's voice, low and rhythmic, counting.

*In for four. Hold for four. Out for six.*

She was in the room. She was lying on the platform. The air purifier was on the shelf behind Greg's left shoulder — I knew this because I had seen the room, I had noted the shelf, I had done the spatial math that Mariana was too panicked to articulate. Three feet. Maybe less. She could have reached out and touched it. She could have said, *Greg, can you hand me that air purifier, I'd like to hold an appliance while I breathe,* and he would have done it, probably, because Greg was in a phase of life where no request was too strange if it was framed as healing.

She did not say this.

*In for four. Hold for four. Out for six.*

The room was dim. The salt lamps were glowing. The speaker was doing its deep-ocean thing. And two people who had, on a Tuesday night in June 2022, in a room that used to have a bed where this platform now was, done things to each other that I know about because Mariana told me at 2 a.m. in my kitchen with mascara on her cheeks and a guilt so thick you could have carved it — those two people were now lying three feet apart, eyes closed, breathing on command, in a room that Greg had built specifically to *clear* the energy of what they had done.

*In for four. Hold for four.*

She breathed when told. She did not get the money.

After twelve minutes Greg said, "How do you feel?"

And Mariana said, "Better," and it was the most defeated pronunciation of that word I have ever heard.

---

Here is what could have happened.

In August of 2022, six weeks after Lucia came home from Bayonne, Mariana could have called her sister and said, *I need to talk to you.* She could have driven to Maplewood on a Sunday afternoon when Greg was at his men's group or his cycling class or wherever Greg went to be around other men who were also, at that time, beginning to sense that their lives were insufficiently examined.
