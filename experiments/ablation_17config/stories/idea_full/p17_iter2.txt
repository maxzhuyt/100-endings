# The Girl Who Never Falls

## I.

There are two folding chairs in the parking lot of Grace Redeemer when I arrive, both the color of nothing, and one of them has a pamphlet on it about Sunday's bake sale and the other has a clipboard with the carpool sign-up sheet that Denise Okafor-Walsh printed at her office, the font too small, the columns not aligned, because Denise does everything with the energy of a woman being chased and the precision of a woman who has already been caught. I move the pamphlet. I sit in the chair that faces the entrance to the lot, which also faces the side door of the fellowship hall, which also gives me a sightline to the dumpster alcove and the narrow corridor between the building and the fence where someone has left a paint bucket and a bag of road salt from last winter. I notice these things. I have been told I do not need to notice these things. I notice them anyway, the way a man who has quit smoking still registers, upon entering any room, which window is cracked.

The orange slices are in a Tupperware container on the second chair now because I've moved to the first. Denise brings orange slices every Tuesday and Thursday. She cuts them into eighths, not quarters, because she read somewhere that smaller segments prevent choking in children, and Denise is the kind of mother who has read somewhere about everything and implemented roughly sixty percent of it, which is, if I'm being honest, a better ratio than most of the systems I've served under.

I am the youth safety liaison for the Lucky County Junior Roller Derby League, which is a title the way a tourniquet is a bracelet. You become a youth safety liaison when the state needs a word for a man it no longer trusts with his own hands. There is a certificate. It is laminated. I keep it in the glove compartment of the van they make me drive, which is white and has the county seal on the door and smells like something between a hospital and a vacation, some antiseptic tropical air freshener that a previous liaison must have hung from the rearview and that has long since desiccated into a small blue fossil I cannot bring myself to throw away.

The girls arrive in twos and threes, hauling gear bags that scrape the asphalt with the particular sound of girls who have not yet learned to carry heavy things off the ground, who let the world's friction do the work for them. I watch them come. I watch the way Socks—her derby name, her legal name is on a form I've filed and forgotten—lists to her left when she walks, favoring a knee she torqued three weeks ago and hasn't told anyone about. I watch the way Twist—I don't have her legal name, which is its own kind of information—enters the lot with her shoulders already up around her ears, a posture I have seen in men walking into arraignments, though I would not say that to her, I would not say that to anyone, I am saying it to you now because you are not here and cannot do anything about it.

The team is called the Holy Rollers, a name chosen by the church that donates the practice space, and they are last in their division, and last-funded in the league, and they practice on a rink that is, on Sundays, the fellowship hall of Grace Redeemer Tabernacle, and the track is laid out in masking tape over linoleum, and on one turn you can feel the seam where the floor was patched after a pipe burst in 2019, a raised lip of subfloor that catches wheels if you don't know it's there. They all know it's there. They hit it anyway, some of them, the ones whose bodies have not yet learned the difference between knowing and doing, which is most of them, which is most of everyone I have ever trained or fought beside or tried to keep from being hurt, which is—

I'm getting off the subject. The subject is the parking lot. The subject is the orange slices and the Tuesday light and the twelve girls between the ages of eleven and fourteen who are about to skate in a church fellowship hall while I sit on a folding chair and watch for things that might go wrong, which is all of it, always, everything, the whole gorgeous catastrophe of bodies in motion, which I am no longer permitted to participate in but which I can see—I have always been able to see—with a clarity that has been, at various times in my life, parsing it generously, both my single gift and the thing that has ruined me.

But you don't need that yet.

You need the parking lot. You need the folding chairs. You need to know that I am here, and that I am watching, and that watching is the verb that has replaced all the other verbs I used to conjugate.

The girls go inside. I pick up the Tupperware. I follow them in.

## II.

She came in late, the way weather comes in. I mean that precisely.

There was practice already happening—Socks working on crossovers, Twist orbiting the far turn with the cautious, rigid lean of someone who has memorized the physics but not yet trusted them—and then the side door opened and a girl walked in carrying her gear bag on her shoulder, not dragging it, the only one who carried it off the ground, and the room reorganized around her the way a current reorganizes around a stone, except she was not the stone. She was the current. Everyone else was the stone.

Marguerite Okafor-Walsh was thirteen. Narrow-shouldered. Braces. A face that was still deciding what it would look like in five years, the geometry of it provisional, unfinished, the way a sketch by someone very good is more alive than a painting by someone average. She laced her skates on the bench without speaking to anyone, and no one spoke to her, and this silence had the quality not of exclusion but of deference, the way you do not chat with the officiant before the service begins.

She entered the track and I understood two things immediately, in the body, before language caught up:

First, that she was the best skater I had ever seen at any age on any surface in any context, and I have seen people move for a living, I have built a life around the assessment of what bodies can and cannot do, and her body could do things that did not make structural sense. She did not skate through the pack. She *happened* to the pack. The blockers were there and then Marguerite was somewhere else and the blockers had not moved wrong, they had moved the way blockers move, correctly, in formation, and it did not matter. She slipped between them the way a word slips between the ribs of a sentence and changes its meaning, without friction, without visible effort, and when she emerged on the other side she was already turning, already coming back, already threading the same pack she had just undone, and I—

I sat down. I had been standing. I don't remember standing up.

Second, and this took longer to understand, the way a bone bruise takes longer to bloom than a cut: she had never been knocked down. I don't mean this the way coaches mean it, as shorthand for resilience or grit or whatever word they're selling that season. I mean it as fact. Marguerite Okafor-Walsh had not, in two and a half seasons of junior roller derby, made contact with the floor. I checked. I pulled the bout records. I watched old footage on a parent's phone, grainy, water-spotted with a cracked screen. She did not fall. Other girls fell around her—Loss of Balance, Loss of Consciousness I once saw, though she was wearing a helmet and was fine and quit the next week—but Marguerite moved through space as if she had negotiated a private covenant with the ground: I will not touch you, you will not touch me, and we will both pretend this arrangement is normal.

It was not normal. I knew what normal looked like. Normal was bruises and floor burns and the particular grunting sound of a body absorbing an impact it could not redirect. Normal was gravity. Marguerite was not operating within normal parameters, and I say this as a man who once, in a different life, in a cage in Tulsa, Oklahoma, hit another man so hard that the referee—not the doctor, the referee—called the fight, and that man's body, going down, made a sound I will not describe to you except to say that it was the sound of normal, of the world's contracts being enforced, and Marguerite's body made no such sound, ever, and the silence where that sound should have been was the loudest thing in the fellowship hall.

After practice she stood by the water fountain with her pads still on and two girls in front of her—I didn't know their names yet, they quit within a month, which is its own evidence—and she delivered the following speech, which I am reconstructing from memory and from the fact that I could not stop listening:

"You don't actually want it. You think you want it, which is different. Wanting it is when you go home and your body hurts and you're glad, you're actually *glad*, because the hurt means you went somewhere you weren't supposed to go and you're still here. What you do is you go home and you feel bad about the hurt and you call your mom and your mom says it's okay, baby, it's just a game, and you believe her. Don't believe her. It's not a game. Nothing is a game. A game is what people call something when they want you to stop caring about it so they can beat you."

The girls stared at her. One of them, the shorter one, whose lip was trembling in a way that was familiar to me because I have seen lips tremble in this precise way in gyms and locker rooms and hallways outside courtrooms, said, almost whispering, "Marguerite, I just messed up the formation, I'm sor—"

"Don't be sorry. Be *mad*. Being sorry is what you do when you break someone's lamp. This isn't a lamp. This is the only thing that's yours. Are you going to be sorry about the only thing that's yours?"

The girl cried. Not dramatically. Just a leaking, private kind of crying, the kind that has no audience in mind, and Marguerite watched her cry with an expression that I recognized because I had seen it in the mirror in the years when I still looked in mirrors after training, which was the expression of a person who has said the truest thing they know how to say and is now watching it land wrong and cannot understand why the truth, which is supposed to fix things, keeps breaking them instead.

I did not intervene. It is in my job description to intervene. I did not intervene because what I saw was not bullying, exactly, though it contained bullying the way a river contains the things it drowns. What I saw was a thirteen-year-old girl who had learned the language of competitive cruelty the way some children learn a second language—by immersion, by ear, by repeating phrases she'd heard from adults who did not know she was listening—and who was now fluent in a tongue she did not yet understand she was speaking. I knew the dialect. I'd grown up in it. It was the language of my coaches, my trainers, the men who had built me into a weapon and then expressed surprise when the weapon went off in rooms where it wasn't supposed to. Marguerite spoke it with the syntax of a seventh-grader who had read too many motivational captions and absorbed them at the level of music rather than meaning, and the music was right, the music was exactly right, and that was the problem.

In the van afterward—I drove, she sat in the back seat behind the passenger side because she always sat in the back seat behind the passenger side, a habit or a superstition or a tactical choice, I never asked—she was quiet for eleven blocks. I counted. The dashboard light was on because the dashboard light was always on; the van had a persistent electrical advisory that nobody at the county had authorized the funds to diagnose. In the amber glow of the dashboard light, in the silence of the eleven blocks, Marguerite looked her age, which was young, which was so young that it made my chest ache in a place that was not my chest exactly but the idea of my chest, the memory of a time when my chest was a thing I used for breathing instead of for storing the accumulated weight of things I had done with my hands.

"Dov," she said. She was the only one of the girls who called me by my name. The others called me Coach, which I had corrected, or Mr. Dov, which I had tolerated, or Hey, which at least had the virtue of accuracy.

"Yeah."

"Being the best is like being a ghost."

I adjusted the mirror. Her eyes were there, reflective, the braces catching the passing streetlight.

"Because nobody wants to stand next to you," she said. "Not because they're scared. Because you make them feel like they're already gone."

I drove. The dashboard light pulsed. The blue fossil of the air freshener turned slowly on its string, a dead thing orbiting nothing, filling the van with the faintest memory of someone else's idea of paradise.

"I hear you," I said, which is what I have learned to say when what I mean is: *Yes. I know. I know, and I am so sorry that you know it too, and you are thirteen, and knowing it at thirteen is a different thing than knowing it at thirty-four, it is worse, it is better, I cannot tell which and I will not pretend that I can.*

"I hear you." I said it twice, because some things are liturgical, and because she was right, and because I could not say what I meant, so I said the only thing I could say, and I said it with the reverence it was owed, and we drove the rest of the way in the light of the dashboard, which was amber, which was a warning, which neither of us heeded.

## III.

Now I need to tell you about Twist, and I need to tell you correctly, because what happens to her later is the kind of thing that only breaks you if you have been built, carefully, across the duration of a story, into a person who can be broken, and I am the one building, and the materials are specific, and if I get them wrong the breaking is just noise.

It was early October, the third bout of the season. We were playing a team from Kenner that had real uniforms—matching, screen-printed, with a sponsor logo on the back, some orthodontist—and we had our usual taped-together aesthetic, mismatched helmets, Socks wearing one green knepad and one black because she lost the black one's twin at a rest stop and Denise hadn't replaced it yet. The bout was not competitive. The Kenner girls were bigger, faster, coached by a woman in a visor who kept a whiteboard on the bench and drew diagrams during timeouts, actual Xs and Os, and I thought: *Good for you, visor woman. Good for you with your diagrams. I once watched a man draw a fight plan on a napkin in a Denny's at 2 a.m. and then execute it exactly nine hours later and the napkin meant nothing, the plan meant nothing, what mattered was that he had hands that knew things his brain hadn't authorized, and your whiteboard is the same napkin, and the knowing is in the body or it isn't.* But I did not say this. I said, "Great effort, keep your hips low," from the bench, because that is what a youth safety liaison says, and my hands stayed in my lap where they belonged.

Twist was skating blocker. She was good at it, or she was becoming good at it—there is a difference, and the difference is the gap between the body's intention and the body's execution, and in that gap lives everything interesting about athletics and also everything dangerous. She had good instincts for position. She read the track with an intelligence that was almost geometric, angles and vectors, and she set her blocks early and clean and legal. But there was a thing she did, a thing I saw in the first week and tracked with the kind of attention I used to pay to a sparring partner's tell—the microflicker in the shoulder, the shift in weight that precedes the punch by a half-second and is, if you know to look for it, a letter from the future: she hesitated. Not always. Not predictably. But in moments of convergence—when the pack compressed and bodies crowded her periphery from multiple angles—Twist's legs would stop, just stop, as if someone had reached inside her and turned off the motor, and she would stand there, rigid, a statue in a river of rolling girls, and take the hit she should have given.

In the Kenner bout, it happened in the third jam. The pack closed, someone's elbow caught her ribs, she stopped, she took a check to the shoulder that spun her sideways and she stumbled but didn't go down—she never went down dramatically, Twist, she just *diminished*, became less of herself on the track—and the jam ended and the session ended and we lost by a number I won't dignify with memory.
