# The Last Sacrifice

The rosin dust catches the afternoon light in a way that makes me think of incense, which is probably the church's fault. It hasn't been a church for eleven years — the diocese sold it to a chamber music society that could barely afford the heating — but the building remembers. The ceiling still vaults toward a God who has, presumably, found other accommodations. The acoustics are absurd, designed to carry a single human voice to the back pew with the clarity of revelation, which means that every note we play blooms too large and then hangs in the air a half-second longer than it should, as though the room is reluctant to let go of sound. You learn to play drier here. Less pedal. You learn to trust the space to do the work.

Elena is tuning. She does this the way she has always done it: A first, then the slow descent through the strings, her left ear tilted toward the body of the instrument as though it is telling her something private. The cello is a Gagliano, early nineteenth century — not the most valuable instrument she's been offered, but the one she has never given up, even when she gave up nearly everything else. The A comes clean and steady. The D takes longer. Her fingers on the pegs are precise but thinner than they were five years ago, and the skin over her knuckles has gone translucent in a way that I track without meaning to, the way you track a weather change at the edge of the horizon.

I adjust the bench. It's the same bench that's been here since the conversion — a proper piano bench, at least, not a folding chair, though the leather is split along the front edge and exhales a small puff of dust when I sit down. The piano itself is a Bösendorfer that someone donated in lieu of a tax write-off, good bones but slightly out in the upper register, a problem I've reported twice and compensated for silently ever since. I play a quiet chord to settle my hands. She doesn't look up.

There are thirty-two chairs set out in rows for the recital on Saturday, which is optimistic by about fifteen. The last recital drew twenty-three, counting the board members who are contractually obligated to attend, and the one before that was nineteen. Elena does not count the chairs. I don't know if she doesn't notice or doesn't care, and after this many years the distinction has stopped mattering to me in any way I can act on.

The coffee in my thermos is from the place on Barrow Street that over-roasts everything, and it's gone tepid. I drink it anyway, during the tuning, because this is when I drink it — always during the tuning, never once she's ready, never once we've started. These things calcify. You do them three times and they become ritual; you do them for twenty years and they become liturgy. I screw the cap back on. She finishes the C string. We look at each other, a glance so brief and practiced it barely qualifies as eye contact — more like two instruments checking pitch against each other — and we begin.

We're playing the Brahms E minor, the first sonata, and we begin with the second movement because Elena always begins with the second movement. She has never explained this and I have never asked. I suspect it is because the first movement requires a kind of reckless commitment she needs to warm into, while the second is architecture — clean lines, a minuet's restraint — and she can enter it like entering a room she knows.

Eight bars in, she stops.

Not abruptly. She lifts the bow and lets the note decay naturally, the room holding it for that extra half-second, and then she turns her head toward the high window on the east wall. Outside, a truck is grinding through its lower gears on the avenue. She listens to it — not the way you listen to an interruption, impatient for it to pass, but the way you listen to something you've been waiting for. Her chin lifts slightly. Her eyes are focused on a middle distance that has nothing to do with the window or the truck or the room.

I wait. My hands hover above the keys, fingers lightly curled, not touching but close enough that I could resume within a breath. I know how long this takes. I know the duration of these pauses the way I know the duration of a rest in a score — not by counting but by feeling the weight of the silence, the precise moment when it has said what it needs to say and the music can re-enter.

The truck passes. She turns back. We begin again from where we left off, not from the top, because she has never once gone back to the top after one of these pauses, and I have never once suggested it. The phrase picks up as though the silence were written into the score, and we continue.

---

I have been asked, by the few people who know enough to ask, why I have stayed. The question always assumes its own answer — love, usually, or loyalty, or some kind of contractual obligation of the soul. I've let each assumption stand because correcting it would require me to supply something more accurate, and I have never, in twenty years, been able to finish that sentence. I stay because. I have stayed because. Once, at a dinner party I shouldn't have attended, someone used the word "devotion," and I excused myself to the bathroom and gripped the edge of the sink until the porcelain bit into my palms because the word was both completely wrong and so close to right that I couldn't breathe around it.

What I have is this: I was there. I was there at the beginning, and because I was there, I cannot be anywhere else.

---

It was the Schoenfeld competition, twenty years ago this October. I don't say that to Elena — I have never said the word "Schoenfeld" in her presence — but the date sits in me like a stone in a riverbed, and everything flows around it.

We were twenty-four. Students of Viktor Laskin at the conservatory, both of us, though in different orbits — Elena was the prodigy, the one Laskin called "my inheritance," and I was the accompanist he kept on hand because I could sight-read anything and never drew attention from the soloist. I understood this hierarchy. I had understood it from the age of fifteen, when I first grasped that my talent was genuine but non-singular — that I would never be the reason anyone bought a ticket. There is a particular kind of clarity that comes with that understanding. It doesn't feel like loss; it feels like being handed a very accurate map. I could read a room, read a soloist, read the space between a performer's intention and their execution, and fill it without being detected. Laskin recognized this. "You have the rarest gift," he told me once, and I am still not certain whether he meant it as praise or diagnosis. "You hear what is needed."

Elena, in those years, was something I had never encountered. Her technique was — I want to say "flawless," but that word suggests sterility, and she was not sterile. She was *complete*. Every note she played was the note she intended; every phrase was shaped with a control so total it seemed effortless, the way a suspension bridge seems effortless when you don't understand the engineering. She could play the Dvořák concerto from memory at seventeen with a tonal purity that made her teachers uneasy, because purity in a teenager suggests either genius or a fundamental disconnection from the emotional content of the music, and no one could determine which it was.

Laskin believed it was the latter. He said so, privately, to colleagues, and less privately to me, in the way that powerful people confide in those they consider furniture. "She plays like a woman in a glass room," he said to me once, late, after a departmental recital where Elena had performed the Shostakovich First Concerto with a precision that left the audience stunned and, somehow, unsatisfied. "Every note perfect. Every note a lie." He was drinking Calvados from a coffee mug. I remember the smell. "She feels nothing, or she feels everything and has built a wall around it so thick that no sound escapes. Either way, the music suffers. She will have a career — she is too good not to — but she will never be an artist until something breaks that wall."

I said nothing to this. I never said anything to Laskin's pronouncements. I am not sure, even now, whether I agreed. I had sat at the piano beside Elena for two years by then, accompanying her through recitals and juries and competition preliminaries, and what I heard in her playing was not absence but *containment* — something held so carefully it appeared still. But Laskin was Laskin. He had studied with Rostropovich. He had performed the Elgar at the Proms in 1979 before a hand injury ended his solo career and made him what he was: a man who could no longer play and therefore knew, with the fervor of the dispossessed, exactly how everyone else should. His authority was absolute, and I — I had the rarest gift. I heard what was needed. What was needed, apparently, was my silence.

The Schoenfeld was the audition that would have launched her. A prestigious international competition — not the largest, but the most discriminating, with a jury that included three principal cellists from major orchestras and a reputation for identifying careers rather than merely rewarding them. Elena had been preparing for six months. Laskin had selected her repertoire: the Kodály Solo Sonata, a Beethoven variation set, and the Shostakovich First. I was to accompany her for the Beethoven.

What I am about to describe, I have reconstructed many times. Certain details I am sure of. Others have the feel of things I decided were true after the fact, because the alternative — that I noticed in the moment and still said nothing — is a room I have never been able to stay in long enough to verify its furniture.

The morning of the audition, Laskin spoke with Elena privately. I know this because I saw her coming out of his studio forty minutes before her scheduled time. I was in the hallway, warming up my hands on a practice keyboard in one of the small rooms. Through the open door, I saw her pass. Her face was — I want to say "changed," but that implies a before and after that I could clearly distinguish, and the truth is that I knew something was wrong only in the way you know a pitch is off: not analytically, but in the body, a faint nausea of recognition that you can't yet name.

What he told her — what I believe he told her, based on fragments she let slip years later, and things Laskin himself said to me afterward with the casualness of a man who does not believe he has done anything wrong — was this: he told her the jury had already decided. He told her they had heard her in the preliminary round and found her technically irreproachable and emotionally insufficient. He told her — and this is the part I have turned over so many times that the words have become smooth and featureless, like sea glass — that she had one chance to change their minds, and that chance was to stop playing *for* them and start playing *from* something true, something she had never been willing to risk. He told her to abandon the interpretation they had rehearsed and to play, instead, from whatever she was most afraid of. "Play the thing you have been hiding from," he said, or something to that effect. "Otherwise, you will play a perfect audition and lose, and you will never understand why."

This was, in the most charitable reading I can construct, a radical pedagogical intervention delivered at the worst possible moment. In a less charitable reading, which I have also constructed, at three in the morning, alone, many times — it was a bomb placed under the foundation of a building he had spent three years erecting and then detonated because he wanted to see what the rubble looked like.

She walked onstage and played the Kodály from memory. I was not in the hall for this — I was backstage, preparing for the Beethoven — but I heard it through the walls, the way you hear a disaster through the walls: not the details but the scale of it. The Kodály Solo Sonata is one of the most technically and emotionally demanding works in the cello repertoire. It requires a controlled ferocity, and what I heard through the walls was the ferocity without the control. Not chaos — Elena could never be chaotic — but something unmoored. The phrasing lurched between restraint and a rawness that sounded, even muffled, like exposure. The kind of playing that might have been extraordinary if it had been prepared, lived with, understood. Instead it was an open wound performed before strangers.

By the time I joined her onstage for the Beethoven, I could see her hands. They were steady — Elena's hands were always steady — but the skin around her jaw was tight, and there was a quality to her breathing that I had never heard before, a shallowness, as though she was rationing her air. We played the Beethoven. I adjusted. I slowed the opening by a fraction, gave her more space in the transitions, softened my touch so that she had room to find whatever ground she was standing on. She played competently. Not brilliantly. The damage had already been done in the Kodály, and what remained was something worse than a bad performance: it was an inconsistent one, an audition that demonstrated both the capacity for greatness and the inability to sustain it, which is the one thing a jury cannot forgive.

She did not advance.

Laskin, afterward, expressed genuine surprise. Not at the result — I think he anticipated the result — but at the magnitude of the rupture. He had expected a crack through which deeper music would flow. What he got was a detonation. "She was not ready," he said to me, a week later, with something I interpreted then as regret and now interpret as bewilderment — the confusion of an engineer whose controlled demolition has leveled the wrong building. "I thought she was ready."

I said nothing.

I said nothing then, in the hallway, standing in the smell of Calvados and institutional floor wax, and I said nothing to Elena, who did not speak about the audition to me or to anyone for three months, and I said nothing in the years that followed, as it became clear that what Laskin had broken was not a wall but a load-bearing structure, and that Elena, in the rubble, had found not vulnerability but a theology.

Because here is what Laskin did not anticipate, what I did not anticipate, what nobody could have anticipated: Elena did not reject what he did to her. She *accepted* it. She accepted the premise — that her playing had been emotionally false, that the wall had needed breaking — and she accepted the method. She accepted that authentic art required authentic destruction, that the music had been better in those unmoored minutes of the Kodály than in anything she had played before, and that the only error was in the timing, not the principle. She took Laskin's sabotage and rebuilt it as a faith. What he had meant as a single, corrective shock, she understood as a revelation of permanent truth: that the music would only be as real as the ruin she brought to it.

And the terrible thing — the thing I have never been able to argue my way past, not once in twenty years — is that she was not entirely wrong.

---

The marriage ended in the fourth year. Peter — a sound engineer, quiet, competent, a man who built stable things for a living — Peter left on a Tuesday in March, and I know it was a Tuesday because Elena came to rehearsal that Wednesday with a blister on her left index finger from practicing through the night, and when I asked about Peter she said, "He took the good coffee maker," and we began the Schubert Arpeggione.

I saw him once more after that, six months later. He was loading equipment into a van outside a recording studio in midtown. He saw me, and his face did something I can only describe as *organizing itself around a decision not to feel anything*, and he nodded and said, "She sounds amazing, I hear. The recital in Boston — people said it was extraordinary." He said it without bitterness, which was worse than bitterness. He said it the way you report the weather in a country you no longer live in. Then he closed the van doors and drove away, and I stood on the sidewalk with a paper bag of sandwiches I was bringing to rehearsal and understood, not for the first time but with a new precision, that the music was eating her life and that her life, eaten, was feeding the music, and that I was bringing sandwiches.
