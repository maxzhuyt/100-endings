# Pell Osworth and the Jubilee

---

## I.

You should know about the festivals before you know about the boy. Everyone makes that mistake — arriving at the story of Pell Osworth and wanting to begin with Pell Osworth — but the festivals came first. They came first by centuries. And if you begin with the boy, you will love him too quickly. I would like you to love him at the proper speed.

So: the kingdom of Caeth Moraine, and its festivals.

There were four in the old calendar. The Greening in spring, when the municipal witches hung the streets of every town with garlands of sweet clover and nightbloom and the flowers that have no name in your language but which smell the way aeli's first laugh sounds — that is, impossible to describe and impossible to forget. The Hallowing at midsummer, when the bonfires on the hills burned in colors that ordinary fire does not possess, and children ran between the flames and emerged with their cheeks flushed and their eyes bright and their small bodies trailing smoke that smelled of cinnamon and cut hay. The Gathering in autumn, with its long tables set in public squares, bread baked in communal ovens, cider pressed from apples that grew in the coven's own orchards and tasted very slightly of starlight, which is to say: of something you could almost name but never quite, a sweetness with a question inside it. And the Midwinter Fete, the grandest of all, when the snow itself seemed to participate — falling in arrangements, in patterns, as though choreographed — and the entire kingdom came together beneath lanterns that floated unsupported in the winter dark, bobbing gently like the luminous fauna of a warm and depthless sea.

They were, all four, genuinely beautiful. I want to be clear about that. It would be easier for both of us if they weren't, if I could describe them with the gray, perfunctory language of institutional function — *quarterly siphoning events, operated under municipal charter* — but that would be a lie. They were beautiful the way a cathedral is beautiful: the beauty was the point, and the beauty was also the mechanism, and most people never needed to ask what the mechanism was *for*.

After each festival, people went home feeling pleasantly spent. Wonderfully lightened. *Hollowed*, if you forced them to choose a word, but they would say it smiling, the way you might say *emptied* after a long cry that resolved something, or *wrung out* after lovemaking. It felt like the natural aftermath of joy — that slight, sweet depletion, as though happiness were a liquid in a cup and you had drunk it all and now the cup was clean and you were satisfied.

It was not the natural aftermath of joy.

The coven of Caeth Moraine had operated for four hundred years under a charter so old that its vellum had gone the color of weak tea and its language required three translation spells to parse. What the charter authorized, in its ancient and unembarrassed syntax, was this: the municipal witches were empowered to *collect, refine, and redirect the affective surplus generated during public celebrations* for the purpose of maintaining the kingdom's protective wards. The border enchantments. The shielding spells. The vast, invisible, constantly eroding architecture of magic that stood between Caeth Moraine and what lived in the mountains to the north.

I will not describe what lived in the mountains to the north. You do not need the details to understand that it was real, that it was patient, and that without the wards it would come down into the valleys and the kingdom would end. Not metaphorically. Not in the way kingdoms usually end, with a slow decay of institutions and a gradual changing of names on maps. It would end the way a candle ends when you close your fist around the flame.

The wards required power. Power required joy. Joy — human joy, the real and undiluted thing, not its alchemical approximations — was the only fuel that burned clean enough to maintain the enchantments without corruption or decay. And so the festivals existed to generate joy at scale, and the coven's hidden mechanisms existed to harvest it, and the wards held, and the kingdom endured, and the people went home after each celebration feeling pleasantly, sweetly, merely *tired*.

The coven's moral position was not complicated. It was arithmetic. A little joy from many people — so little that no individual noticed the loss — pooled together into enough raw power to keep everyone alive for another season. It was a tax. A tithe. It was the same bargain every civilization makes with its citizens, expressed in magical rather than monetary terms: you will give a small portion of what you have, and in return you will be protected from what you cannot face alone.

It was, from a certain angle, not even objectionable.

I am telling you this because I want you to hold it — the reasonableness, the genuine necessity — alongside everything that follows. The coven were not villains. The festivals were not traps. The kingdom was not a dystopia. It was a place where people lived and loved and were, on the whole, happy and safe, and the cost of their happiness and safety was so diffuse and so mild that it registered as nothing more than the pleasant ache after a good day. Most of them never knew. Those who did mostly shrugged.

This is what systems look like when they are working. You live inside half a dozen of them right now, and the ones that are working are the ones you never think about.

The festivals came first.

Remember that.

---

## II.

Now: the boy.

Pell Osworth was fifteen, or nearly fifteen, or had been fifteen for so long that it had begun to feel like a permanent condition — the age at which you are old enough to understand that you are excluded and too young to have developed the calluses that make exclusion bearable. He was apprenticed to Maren Gald, who was a coven elder, who was patient with him, who had taken him on when no one else would, and who was — by every visible measure — kind.

Every morning Pell tried. That is the simplest way to describe his life, and the truest. Every morning he sat at the worktable in Maren's study, surrounded by the residue of yesterday's failures — scorch marks on the wood, a faint smell of sulfur, the lingering accusation of a potion that had separated into something that looked like curdled milk filmed over gray water — and he tried again. He tried the Kindling Cantrip, which was the first spell taught to first-year apprentices, which most children mastered before their twelfth birthday, and which, in Pell's hands, produced a brief, apologetic wisp of smoke and then nothing. He tried the Mending Thread, which was meant to knit torn fabric and which instead, once, set his own sleeve quietly on fire. He tried the Familiar's Call, which was designed to summon a bonded animal companion and which, on three separate occasions, summoned a pigeon that bit him and left.

The pigeon was not even the same pigeon each time. They were three different pigeons, none of them magically bonded to him, all of them apparently united in their conviction that Pell Osworth was a person who deserved to be bitten.

He could not do magic. This is not the same as saying he had no magic — every soul in Caeth Moraine had some, the way every person has a singing voice whether or not that voice should be raised in public — but whatever magic lived in Pell was sunk so deep, or so tangled, or so stubbornly dormant that no technique could coax it to the surface. He was a well with the water table impossibly far down. You could drop a stone into him and listen and never hear it land.

Maren was patient. Maren sat beside him and guided his fingers through the gestures and said, "Again. There is no failure in repetition, Pell, only rhythm that has not yet found its beat." Maren laid a hand on his shoulder when the day's last cantrip fizzled — always the same gesture, the broad palm settling with a gentle, almost proprietary weight, the thumb resting in the hollow beside his collarbone, lingering there for a moment that was perhaps half a beat longer than comfort required, though Pell never noticed, because Pell was busy being grateful that anyone at all was willing to touch him.

You are already hoping this ends well for him. That is exactly the problem.

Every market day he would walk to the village square, where the other apprentices gathered to practice and show off and buy roasted chestnuts from the cart that smelled of char and sugar. He did not buy the chestnuts. He stood near the cart, close enough to smell them, watching the others flick sparks between their fingers or levitate copper coins into each other's palms. Once, a girl his age — Senna, her name was, red-haired, with the easy confidence of someone for whom spells had always worked — caught him watching and smiled. Not cruelly. Worse: pityingly. She held out a chestnut and said, "Here, you look cold," and the kindness of it was so precise and so careless that it cut him more cleanly than mockery would have.

Each festival, he stood at the crowd's outermost fringe. The Greening, the Hallowing, the Gathering: he attended them all, always at the back, always slightly outside the ring of warmth that the celebrations seemed to cast. He felt their beauty. He felt it keenly — he was not numb, not sullen; he loved the lanterns and the music and the way the bonfire smoke rose in sweet spirals and the way people leaned into one another with the uncomplicated affection that festivals permit. He loved all of it the way you love something you are watching through a window. He could see it. He could almost feel it.

He always went home early, before the drowsy, pleasant exhaustion set in, because you have to be *inside* the joy to be hollowed by its leaving, and Pell had never been far enough inside to be touched by the coven's harvest. He walked back to Maren's house on streets that were empty because everyone else was still at the festival, and the sound of his footsteps on the cobblestones was the only sound in the world, and the lanterns that he passed beneath made his shadow long and then short and then long again, and each time his shadow stretched out ahead of him it looked like it was reaching for something it could not quite touch.

Maren would be home before him. Maren always left the festivals early, too, though for different reasons, and when Pell came through the door Maren would look up from the worktable — where the coven-work happened, the work Pell was never quite taught, the ledgers and calibration charts and the silver instruments whose functions were not explained — and Maren would smile at him with an expression that was fond and something else, something that the narrator of this story might describe as *appraising*, the way a jeweler looks at an uncut stone, not with affection for what it is but with interest in what it might become if struck at the correct angle.

"How was the festival, Pell?"

"Beautiful," Pell always said.

"Did you enjoy yourself?"

And Pell always paused. Not for long enough to be dramatic. Just long enough for the honest answer to flash across his face before the polite one replaced it. "Everyone seemed happy," he said, which was not the same thing. Maren heard the difference. Maren always heard the difference, and noted it, and said nothing, and smiled.

Maren was kind to him. I want you to believe that. I need you to believe it, because Maren believed it, and the distance between what Maren believed and what Maren did is the darkest location in this story, and you cannot see it from here, and I am not yet willing to light it.

## III.

The Grand Jubilee came once every seven years, and it was not like the other festivals.

The seasonal celebrations were, in the language of the charter, *routine collections* — modest harvests designed to maintain the wards at their baseline strength. The Jubilee was a *renewal*, a massive infusion of power meant to reinforce the deep foundations of the border enchantments for the cycle ahead. It required more joy than the quarterly festivals could generate. It required a concentration of joy so dense and so pure that the wards would ring with it for years.

The coven had spent months in preparation. Pell had watched the work without understanding it: Maren's late nights in the study, the correspondence with other elders arriving by spelled courier, the silver instruments coming out of their velvet-lined cases more and more frequently, the cellar door locked now where it had always been open before. He interpreted all of it as important adult business that did not concern him, which it was and did not, until it did.

The Jubilee itself was, you must understand, magnificent.

The entire kingdom came. Villages emptied. Roads filled with families carrying baskets and blankets and children on their shoulders. The festival grounds outside the capital were transformed into a landscape from a fairy tale — which, in a sense, is exactly what it was, since most fairy tales are descriptions of enchantments written by people who experienced them and survived but could not quite convey the mechanism, only the wonder. Tents of spangled silk. Food stalls serving dishes that tasted like your fondest edible memory, whatever that happened to be. (For Pell, the stew he was given tasted like the bread his mother used to make before she died, and he stood in the food line holding his bowl with both hands, unable to eat for a moment, unable to understand how a simple stew could taste like grief and love in equal proportions and somehow be delicious.) Musicians played on floating stages that turned slowly in the air like the pages of a book being read by the wind. The lanterns — oh, the lanterns. The legendary Jubilee lanterns, hundreds of them, rising into the dusk like a second set of stars being born below the first, each one casting a glow that was warm and gold and made every face beneath it look like a face you had loved for years.

Pell had been assigned to carry things. Of course he had. He was the apprentice who could not do magic, and so he was given the tasks that did not require it: hauling crates of spell components from the supply wagons to the coven's staging area behind the main pavilion, sweeping the aisles between vendor stalls, refilling lantern oil. He did these tasks without complaint and — this is important — without self-pity. Pell was not bitter. He was grateful to be included at all. He carried the crates carefully. He swept with attention. He was glad to be near the beauty even in a custodial capacity, and that gladness, small and dignified and unseen by anyone, was the most genuinely moving thing at the festival, and no one was watching.

No. That is not quite true. Maren was watching.

Here is what happened. The specifics matter, because the specifics are what make it look like an accident, and it is very important to the coven that it looked like an accident, and it is very important to *me* that you know it was not.

The Grand Jubilee's harvesting enchantment was built into the architecture of the festival grounds — woven into the tent poles, chalked beneath the cobblestones, threaded through the lantern wicks. It was a web, essentially, designed to catch the ambient joy the way a spider's web catches dew: passively, invisibly, through the exploitation of a natural tendency. Joy, like dew, likes to cling to things, and the coven's web gave it things to cling to — the beautiful music, the enchanted food, the warmly glowing lanterns — and then it drew the clinging joy inward, along invisible channels, toward the central collection point in the main pavilion, where a great spelled vessel sat beneath the stage like a heart beneath a breastbone.

The vessel was Pell's size. That detail meant nothing to him.

He was carrying a crate of silver binding-pins — the long, thin, sharp-ended kind that the coven used to anchor enchantments to physical structures — across the backstage area of the main pavilion. The crate was heavy. The ground was uneven, because the main pavilion had been erected over an old cobblestone square whose stones had shifted over the centuries into a topography of subtle, ankle-turning traps. Pell was walking carefully. He was watching his feet. He did not see the guy-rope.

He tripped.

The crate went forward. Pell went sideways. The binding-pins scattered across the cobblestones in a bright, musical spray, and three of them — three precisely, not two, not four — skidded into the gap beneath the stage and struck the collection vessel at three points that, if you looked at them on a diagram, formed a triangle whose geometry was significant in ways that only someone who had studied advanced ward theory would recognize.
