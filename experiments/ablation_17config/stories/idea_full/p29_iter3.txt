# The Most American Monster

## I.

The water never left. That is the first thing you need to understand about Harlan Falls — not that the flood came, because floods come everywhere, because water is the oldest thing there is with a grudge, but that the water, having come, decided to stay. It settled into our basements like a relative who'd lost a lease. It blackened the joists. It warped the gym floor of Harlan Falls High School into something you could read with your fingers, a topographic map of surrender, the maple planks heaving up in ridges where moisture had worked for years beneath the polyurethane like a mole tunneling the dirt of a grave. You could not dribble a basketball on that floor without it taking a hard theological bounce — skipping off at some angle you had not asked for, had not planned, that seemed to have been planned for you by whatever malice lived in the buckling grain.

I coached wrestling. I have coached wrestling at Harlan Falls for nineteen years, and in that time we have won thirty-four dual meets and lost two hundred and eleven. I do not say this for sympathy. I say it the way you'd say the river is north of the school and the cemetery is east and the Dollar General is the building with the lights on latest — because you can't orient yourself here without the losing. It is a cardinal direction. We had no state qualifiers. We had no regional champions. We had, in the winter of the year I am telling you about, a co-ed roster of eleven kids in a sport that requires fourteen weight classes, which meant we forfeited three matches every meet before anyone had laced a shoe, which meant we began every evening already buried and spent the next two hours digging in the wrong direction.

The practice room was a repurposed storage space behind the gymnasium, fourteen feet by twenty-two, with a ceiling low enough that Jake Mullins, our heavyweight, could not jump rope without scuffing the acoustic tile. It smelled like mildew and adhesive tape and the sweet chemical ghost of rubber cement, because before it was a practice room it had been used for theater set construction, and before that for storing textbooks on West Virginia history that the district replaced in 2006 and never hauled away. If you peeled up the edge of the mat you could find them still — water-swollen, the pages fused into bricks, the ink bled to a blue-gray wash so that the photographs of coal tipples and river baptisms and the Battle of Blair Mountain had merged into a single undifferentiated murk, which I suppose is as good a metaphor as any, though I am not a man who reaches for metaphors. I reach for what is in front of me, which is generally a mop, or a roll of athletic tape, or the shoulder of a kid who has just been pinned in the first period and is trying to cry in a direction I can't see.

My name is Dale Slocum. I have been married twice and unmarried twice, both times to women who were right to leave, and I say that without the self-pity you might expect because the truth is I did not fight either departure with anything close to the energy I spend on a Saturday morning, alone, re-lining the practice mat with duct tape. Cheryl said I loved losing the way some men loved whiskey — not the taste of it but the way it organized the evening, gave the hours a shape, a direction, a thing to be. I told her that was a hell of a thing to say. She said she knew. She said that was the point.

What I know is this: you can lose so long that losing becomes a discipline, a way of holding the body, a theology of the near-miss and the almost and the not-quite. My kids did not quit. That was always the sermon. I said it at every booster dinner, every school board budget meeting, every conversation at the Quick Mart where someone in a UK Wildcats hoodie asked me why I didn't just fold the program and give the funding to girls' basketball, who at least had a shot. My kids did not quit. They showed up to a room that smelled like ruin and they drilled shots and sprawls and half-Nelsons until the mildew was in their lungs and they were coughing it up at dinner, and they went out on Friday nights and did not quit even when the score was such that not-quitting was the only victory available, and if that is not enough for you then I want you to ask yourself what is, and I want you to sit with the answer.

The season I am telling you about began in November, after the second round of subsidence cracked the foundation of the intermediate school and they had to bus the fifth-graders to the Methodist church for three weeks. Subsidence is what happens when you pull the coal out and leave the nothing behind and the nothing, after years of patient work, wins. The ground drops. Houses tilt. Walls crack along lines so clean they look drawn with a ruler, as if the earth had planned this, had drafted it. The water table shifts and suddenly your well is dry or your basement is full and either way the nothing under your feet has made itself known. We are a town built on voids. I am aware of how that sounds. You do not need to tell me.

## II.

She came in from the hollows in January, when the season was already half over and we had won twice and lost eight times and forfeited three weight classes at every meet, and the first thing I registered was not her strangeness but her stillness.

She stood in the doorway of the practice room during live-go rounds. The door was propped open with a cinder block because what ventilation we had depended on the hallway draft, and she was behind the cinder block, just standing, wearing a canvas jacket two sizes too large and jeans stiff with mud from the knee down, and what I noticed — what I remember — was that she was not shifting her weight. Everyone shifts. You stand in a doorway, you lean on one hip, you cross your arms, you fidget. She stood the way a fence post stands. Driven in.

"Help you?" I said.

She didn't answer. She watched. On the mat, Terrance Bowes was working a single-leg on Devon Kimper, and Devon was sprawling badly, hips too high, and I was about to bark a correction when I saw the girl's right hand move — just the fingers, flexing once in a sequence that tracked Devon's sprawl, almost like she was running the counter-move in miniature, from the doorway, without knowing she was doing it.

"You wrestle?" I said.

She looked at me. She had dark eyes set deep under a brow that seemed built for shadow, and her face had the scrubbed, windburned rawness of someone who spent time outside not for recreation but for the reasons people in the hollows spend time outside, which are older reasons and have to do with the gaps between houses and the hours that need to be survived between the last thing that happened and the next thing that will. She was maybe sixteen. Maybe younger. It was hard to tell with her. Time seemed to have worked on her the way water works on limestone — not aging the surface but hollowing it beneath.

She said, "I have."

"Where?"

She didn't answer that either. I let it sit. You coach long enough, you learn there are kids who come in the front door with a permission slip signed by an enthusiastic parent, and there are kids who come in the back door at odd hours because the room is warm and the mats are softer than where they sleep and the particular formality of a coach saying *good drill* or *reset your hips* or *get your head up* is the closest thing to being addressed as a human being they will find that day. I have coached both kinds. The second kind is why I did not fold the program and give the funding to girls' basketball.

"You want to drill?" I said. "You can drill."

She stepped over the cinder block and onto the mat and she shook my hand, and the shock of it went through me like I'd grabbed a pipe in February.

Her hand was cold. Not cool. Not the ordinary cool of a person who'd been standing in a hallway draft. Cold the way metal is cold, the way water from a deep well is cold, the kind of cold that doesn't feel like an absence of heat but like a positive presence, as if cold were a thing she generated rather than a thing she'd failed to ward off. My fingers ached. I felt the bones in my knuckles register the temperature before my skin did, a deep interior chill that lingered for seconds after I pulled away.

"Lord," I said. "You need gloves."

She looked at her hand the way you look at a hand, which is to say she didn't, because you don't look at your own hand unless someone tells you there's something wrong with it.

I gave her a practice shirt and she went behind the bleachers to change and when she came back I saw the rest of it. Her elbows, when she let her arms hang, did not stop at straight. They kept going — just slightly, maybe five or six degrees past flat, so that her forearms angled forward at the bottom of each swing as though the joint had been installed with too much give. Her knees, when she stood waiting for me to pair her up, locked into a faint backward curve at full extension, a recurve, like a bow strung in the wrong direction. She stood in this posture and it did not appear to cause her pain. It appeared to be how she was built. Looking at it hurt me in the place where you carry your assumptions about how a body is supposed to hold itself upright — some deep architectural conviction that a joint should stop where a joint stops, and if it doesn't, something has gone wrong that should be named and fixed.

I did not name it. I did not try to fix it. I paired her with Devon Kimper at 138 and I said, "Neutral position, work your ties, light hands," and she stepped to the center of the mat and the moment Devon took a collar tie on her neck he yanked his hand back like he'd touched a stove, except the opposite.

"What the hell," he said.

"Language," I said.

"Coach, feel her. She's — she's like ice."

"She's cold, Devon. Some people run cold. Tie up."

He tied up. She threw him in nine seconds. It was a move I'd never seen in twenty years — she dropped to a level that shouldn't have been accessible, because her knees went to a depth that knees should not go, and she caught his far ankle with fingers that wrapped the joint like she had an extra knuckle's worth of reach, and she elevated the ankle while turning her shoulder into his hip at an angle available only to a body that bends the way hers bends, and Devon was airborne for a half-second and then on his back, and she was on top of him in something close to a cradle, and his face — I will not forget the look on Devon's face. It was not the humiliation of being thrown. It was the animal shock of being held by something colder than any living thing he had ever touched.

She let him up. She returned to neutral. She waited.

I stood at the edge of the mat and I felt something I had not felt in nineteen years of coaching at Harlan Falls High School, something I had preached against and organized my entire theology to accommodate the absence of, and the feeling was hope, and it was the most dangerous thing I had ever let into that room.

Her name — the name she gave me, the name that appeared on no school record I could find, the name that the district office eventually processed through channels I did not fully understand and do not have the standing to question — was Leda Brack. She said she was from Lick Fork, which is a hollow so deep in the drainage above town that the school bus does not run there and the census, I am told, does not always reach. She had no parent with her. She had no phone number. She had a social security card in a plastic sleeve in the pocket of her canvas jacket and it was cold to the touch, the way everything about her was cold, the way even the practice shirt grew cold an hour after she put it on, and when she turned it in at the end of practice I hung it on the line in the furnace room and it took until the next morning to come up to temperature.

I registered her for the team. I told myself I was giving her a place to be. That is what I told myself. I am telling you what I told myself so you can understand the shape of it later, when the shape matters.

## III.

We won. That is the simplest way to say it, so let me say what is not simple.

The first match was at home against Fayette County, a team that had beaten us eleven consecutive years. Leda weighed in at 138 and the official's hand, when he checked her singlet straps, flinched from her shoulder. I saw it. A micro-recoil, there and gone, the way you'd pull back from a countertop you didn't know was wet. He said nothing. He checked the strap and moved on and his fingers, I noticed, curled into his palm afterward, as if they were holding onto the sensation, trying to decide what it was.

Her match lasted forty-one seconds. She shot a double-leg from a distance that should have been too far — her arms reaching a range that gave her entry where no entry should have existed — and she locked her hands behind her opponent's knees, and the boy (Fayette County was a combined-roster school, like ours, boys and girls in the same weight class) tried to sprawl and couldn't, because her grip was a closed thing, a finality, and his legs were pinned together in the vise of her hyperextended elbows, and she ran the pipe, drove him backward, and put him on his back so hard the mat bounced, and when the ref slapped the pin the gym — our gym, our forty-person crowd of parents and siblings and the man from the Dairy Queen who kept score because no one else would — went silent for a full beat before it went loud, because what they had seen was not what they expected to see, was something the eye needed a moment to process, the way you need a moment when a card trick works and you know you saw the queen and you know it can't be there and yet.

We won the dual meet 42–36. It was our first victory over Fayette County since 2011.

The next week, we beat Greenbrier West. Leda pinned her opponent in the second period with a move I can only describe by what it looked like, which was an anaconda choke executed from a position that required her left arm to wrap a neck at an angle my own arm cannot reach. The opponent, a girl named Skyler Potts who was ranked fourth in the region, tapped before the ref could call it. She came off the mat breathing hard and holding her own arms as if checking that they still worked the way arms are supposed to work, and I heard her say to her coach, in a voice that was not angry but bewildered: "She's so cold. She's *so cold*."

Then we beat Pocahontas County, and then we beat James Monroe, and then we beat the team from the consolidated Mercer district that had three state qualifiers and a head coach who'd wrestled at Pitt, and at that point, four weeks into Leda's tenure, our record was seven and eight and improving every meet, and the path to the regional tournament — the path that had been a fantasy, a sentence I completed for comic effect at booster dinners ("And next year, regionals—" and the dads would laugh, because the laughter was part of the liturgy, the call-and-response of knowing your place) — the path was, for the first time in thirty years, real.

The team felt it. You could see it in the way they moved. Terrance Bowes, who had been wrestling at 152 with the philosophical detachment of a man waiting for a bus, began drilling after practice on his own, hitting reps against the wall with a fury I had never seen in him. Devon Kimper stopped talking about quitting. The Mullins twins, heavy and heavier, learned how to chain-wrestle because Leda showed them — not with words, because she did not use many words, but with her body, demonstrating the flow from failed single to inside trip to far-ankle pick with the patience of something very old teaching something very young. The team was not good. Let me be clear. We were not transformed into a juggernaut. We were transformed into a team that *believed something was possible*, and that belief — I have seen it twice before in nineteen years, both times briefly, both times ending — is the most volatile substance you can carry into a gymnasium.
