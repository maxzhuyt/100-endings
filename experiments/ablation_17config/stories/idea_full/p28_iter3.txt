# Ordinance No. 2024-17: Regarding the Shared Use, Maintenance, and Disposition of Commercial Walk-In Cold Storage Units in Multi-Tenant Retail Properties Zoned C-2

---

The Kensington Crossing Shopping Center occupies 22,400 square feet of single-story commercial retail space at 1414 Elm Street, at the intersection of Elm and Oak, in an unincorporated census-designated place that the United States Postal Service recognizes as part of Crestwood but that Crestwood's municipal government does not. The building was constructed in 1986. Its current vacancy rate is 72.7 percent. The Supercuts in Unit 4 closed on March 3, 2022. The H&R Block in Unit 6 closed on April 15, 2023, a date its sole remaining employee described to her husband as "almost funny." The pet-supply store in Units 7 and 8, which had previously been a tanning salon, which had previously been a Blockbuster Video, closed without filing the required notice of business cessation with the county clerk's office, a violation of Section 4.12(b) of the Crestwood Commercial Activity Code that no one has pursued and no one will pursue, because the code-enforcement division for the unincorporated area consists of one man, and he is currently occupied with a signage complaint sixteen miles away that he has been driving to, and failing to resolve, since October.

The parking lot contains forty-four marked spaces, of which forty-three are empty. In the forty-fourth—second row, northwest quadrant, beneath a parking-lot light that has not functioned since a thunderstorm on August 9 of this year—sits a 2011 Nissan Sentra, Brilliant Silver Metallic, Virginia plate number VLT-4491, odometer reading 187,422 miles. The driver's seat is reclined to its maximum 42-degree angle. A man is asleep in it.

His name is Dale Andrew Purcell. He is sixty-one years old. He was born in this county and has never lived outside it. He was evicted from a studio apartment at the Briarwood Gardens complex, Unit 2-F, on the fourteenth of last month for nonpayment of $1,175 in accumulated rent, a sum he described to the process server as "a misunderstanding," which it was not. His retirement from the county Department of Environmental Health, effective February 1, 2023, was listed in departmental records as voluntary, the result of a mutual agreement following an internal investigation into Dale's handling of a reinspection at a Waffle House on Route 9—specifically, the allegation that he had accepted a $200 gift card to the Waffle House in exchange for reclassifying a critical violation as non-critical. Dale denied this. The Waffle House gift card was found in his car. The internal investigation concluded that while the evidence was "suggestive," it did not meet the threshold for termination, and Dale was offered the opportunity to retire with sixty percent of his pension, which works out to $1,407 per month, which is deposited on the first of each month into a checking account at a regional bank, an account whose current balance is $314.12, $297.00 of which constitutes a pending hold from an EZ-Pass transponder Dale believed he had deactivated in 2019 but which has, in fact, been accruing charges every time he passes through the westbound toll plaza on Route 33, a route he takes because it is the only way to reach the Walmart where he showers in the family restroom, using a gallon jug he fills at the strip mall's outdoor spigot.

Dale's blood pressure, as measured at a free screening at a Walgreens nine days ago, is 158 over 94.

He keeps his remaining possessions in a duffel bag in the back seat. It is a navy blue Jansport, bought at a Kohl's in 2013, and it contains: four shirts, three pairs of pants, seven pairs of underwear, a toiletry kit, a phone charger, a folder of documents including his birth certificate and a lapsed auto-insurance card and a photograph of his mother in 1974, and a paperback copy of a Tom Clancy novel he has not opened. The duffel bag is relevant only in distinction. It is not the duffel bag. It is Dale's duffel bag. There is another one. We will arrive at it.

The two surviving businesses at 1414 Elm Street are Saigon Bánh Mì House, which occupies Unit 1 on the Elm Street side, and Taquería Los Primos, which occupies Unit 3 on the Oak Street side. They are separated by Unit 2, which is vacant, and by a shared wall, and by a walk-in cold-storage unit whose structural and legal peculiarities will become important shortly.

Both businesses serve food. Saigon Bánh Mì House is open from 10:00 a.m. to 7:00 p.m., Tuesday through Saturday. Its menu consists of fourteen varieties of bánh mì, three varieties of phở, spring rolls, a rice plate, and Vietnamese iced coffee. The food is good. This is not sarcasm or narrative hedging; the food is genuinely, rigorously, almost unnecessarily good—Mrs. Lê Thị Hương, the owner and sole cook, trained in Saigon and spent eleven years cooking at a restaurant in Falls Church before opening this shop with the proceeds from the sale of her late husband's taxi medallion, and she brings to a $7.50 sandwich the technical precision and the silent, annihilating contempt for mediocrity that another person might bring to a surgical residency. Taquería Los Primos is open from 11:00 a.m. to 9:00 p.m., Monday through Saturday. Its menu is shorter: tacos, burritos, quesadillas, a torta, and horchata. The al pastor is exceptional. The carne asada is above average. The horchata is made from a recipe belonging to Tomás Ochoa's grandmother in Jalisco, and it is transcendent, and it is irrelevant to the plot, but the narrator is unwilling to omit it.

Both businesses are also conducting secondary commerce that has nothing to do with food.

Saigon Bánh Mì House is a fencing operation for stolen pharmaceutical shipments—primarily insulin, Humira, and Ozempic—managed by a supply chain that begins with cargo theft from a distribution warehouse in Manassas and terminates in resale to a network of unlicensed pharmacies and online gray-market vendors operating across four states. Mrs. Lê is aware of this. She is not the architect. The architect is her nephew, Bảo, who is thirty-four, who has a computer science degree from George Mason, and who handles the logistics from a rented office in Annandale with a discipline and a spreadsheet fluency that the narrator notes would be, in a legal enterprise, highly employable. Mrs. Lê's kitchen functions as a transshipment point. Pharmaceutical cartons arrive in produce boxes and depart in catering trays, and the food continues to be made with a ferocity that suggests Mrs. Lê regards the criminal activity not as the primary business but as an indignity she tolerates in order to maintain the restaurant, which she considers the primary business, and for which the fencing revenue is, she has calculated, a necessary subsidy, because fourteen varieties of bánh mì in a strip mall with a seventy-three-percent vacancy rate does not, on its own, generate adequate cash flow.

Taquería Los Primos is a fencing operation for stolen consumer electronics—laptops, tablets, high-end headphones, gaming consoles—managed by a looser and more temperamental organization that the narrator will decline to describe in detail because its organizational chart changes with a frequency that resists summary. Tomás Ochoa is the nominal owner. He is forty-seven. He is also aware. He is also not the architect. The operation is run by his brother-in-law, who lives in Maryland and who communicates primarily through encrypted messages that Tomás reads in the walk-in with his back to the door, a habit that is not as secretive as he believes it to be.

The two operations are aware of each other in the way that neighbors in a duplex are aware of each other: they can hear movement through the walls, they have formed theories, they have not spoken directly, and they prefer it that way. The strip mall's parking lot is a shared territory governed by an unwritten protocol that resembles, in its territorial logic, the demilitarized zone of a conflict no one has formally declared. Delivery trucks for Saigon Bánh Mì House arrive between 6:00 and 7:00 a.m. Delivery trucks for Los Primos arrive between 10:00 and 10:30 p.m. This schedule has not overlapped once in fourteen months.

The landlord's name is Gerald Frisch. He lives in Sarasota, Florida. He purchased 1414 Elm Street in 2004 as part of a six-property portfolio assembled using a 1031 exchange from the sale of a self-storage facility in Fairfax. He has not visited the property since 2016. His property-management duties are handled by a company in Tysons Corner that deposits two rent checks per month, deducts an eleven-percent management fee, and has not conducted a physical inspection of the premises in the entire duration of its contract. Gerald Frisch is sixty-nine years old. His blood pressure is better than Dale's. His checking account balance is larger. This has no bearing on his moral status and is noted only as fact.

Dale Purcell sleeps in the parking lot of this place. He has been sleeping here for twenty-two nights. The Sentra's engine ticks and cools. The parking-lot light above him does not function. The two restaurants on either side of the vacant unit are closed, and inside the shared walk-in freezer between them, behind a rack of pork shoulders on one side and a stack of frozen flour tortillas on the other, a black duffel bag containing $340,000 sits on the floor at a temperature of 28°F, which, as Dale could tell you if he knew it was there, is ten degrees below the acceptable range for food storage and is suggestive.

Dale does not yet know it is there.

He will.

---

The shared restroom at 1414 Elm Street is located in a short hallway between Units 1 and 2, accessible from the parking lot through a metal door whose lock accepts a key that Dale obtained twenty-one days ago by purchasing a single bánh mì—the No. 7, grilled pork, which Mrs. Lê made for him with the same exactitude she would have applied if he had ordered twelve—and asking to use the bathroom. He pocketed the key. Mrs. Lê noticed. She decided, on the basis of a set of calculations the narrator will not pretend to access in their entirety, to allow it. Dale looked, to her, like a man who needed a bathroom more than she needed a key. She had a spare.

Dale uses the restroom every morning between 6:15 and 6:40 a.m. He brushes his teeth. He washes his face with hand soap from a dispenser he refills himself, using a bulk bottle of Softsoap he purchased at the Dollar Tree for $1.25 because the dispenser was empty when he arrived three weeks ago and he could not bring himself to brush his teeth in a room with an empty soap dispenser. He changes his shirt. He looks at himself in the mirror. The narrator will not describe what he sees there because Dale does not linger on it, and this narrative has contracted to stay level with the facts.

What Dale sees instead—not in the mirror but through the hallway's propped-open fire door, and through the service corridor that connects the back-of-house areas of Units 1 through 4—is the following:

At 6:22 a.m. on a Tuesday, a delivery truck bearing the logo of Chesapeake Restaurant Supply backs into the loading area behind Unit 1. Two men unload fourteen cases. Dale, tying his shoes in the hallway, counts them. Eleven of the cases are the correct dimensions for standard food-service packaging—50-pound boxes of pork shoulder, cases of rice, produce flats. Three of the cases are not. They are smaller, denser, and the men carry them differently: with two hands, from the bottom, with the posture of people transporting something they've been told not to drop. Dale notes this. He does not act on it. He ties his other shoe.

At 10:47 p.m. on the following Thursday—Dale is in the Sentra, not sleeping, reading nothing, watching the parking lot because there is nothing else to watch—a white Ford Transit van with no commercial markings pulls behind Unit 3. Two men he has not seen before carry in six black hard-shell cases of a size and type consistent with Pelican storage cases, the kind used for camera equipment or, as Dale does not yet know, laptops packed in custom foam inserts. The men enter through the kitchen's rear door. They exit eleven minutes later carrying nothing. The van departs.

Dale has witnessed, in twenty-three years of restaurant inspection, approximately four thousand deliveries. He has developed an intuitive throughput model that he could not, if asked, articulate in formal terms, but that operates with considerable accuracy: given a restaurant's menu, its hours of operation, its seating capacity, and its observed customer traffic, Dale can estimate within fifteen percent the volume of food product that should be entering the kitchen in a given week. It is a skill comparable to a mechanic's ability to diagnose an engine by its sound, and like that skill, it resists automation and is undervalued by every institutional structure that benefits from it.

Dale's throughput estimate for Saigon Bánh Mì House, based on his own observations of customer traffic—he has counted an average of eleven paying customers per day during the hours he is awake to observe—is approximately $1,800 per week in raw food product. The actual volume of food-coded deliveries to Unit 1, which Dale has now tracked casually but consistently over two weeks of having nothing else to do, is approximately $6,200. The discrepancy is $4,400, or 244 percent.

Dale does not know what this means. He has several theories, none correct. He thinks it might be insurance fraud. He thinks it might be a money-laundering pass-through using inflated food invoices. He thinks, briefly and incoherently, that Mrs. Lê might be running an unlicensed catering operation out of a second location, which is the kind of violation he spent two decades citing and which triggers in him a reflexive interest so deeply ingrained that he is, for a moment, annoyed on behalf of whichever licensed caterers might be losing business to this hypothetical operation.

The narrator will note what Dale is actually seeing: approximately $285,000 per month in stolen pharmaceutical product entering through the kitchen of Saigon Bánh Mì House, packed in food-service boxes that are inventoried, stored temporarily in the walk-in cold-storage unit at the rear of the kitchen, and redistributed within forty-eight to seventy-two hours to five downstream buyers operating out of Annandale, College Park, Philadelphia, and two locations in the Bronx. Bảo's spreadsheet tracks this with an accuracy that the narrator respects while declining to endorse.

At Los Primos, the pattern is different but the arithmetic is equally wrong. Taquería Los Primos averages nineteen paying customers per day—more than Saigon Bánh Mì House, owing to a lunch rush from a nearby construction site—but receives after-hours deliveries of non-food merchandise at a rate and volume that Dale finds, in the professional sense, inconsistent. He watches the loading area from the Sentra. He counts cases. He cannot reconcile the numbers. The numbers do not reconcile because the after-hours deliveries are stolen consumer electronics, primarily sourced from hijacked freight shipments and retail-warehouse employee theft, redistributed through a regional network that ships to buyers via a legitimate-looking e-commerce storefront that the narrator will not name because it is still, as of the time of this telling, operational, and the narrator's omniscience does not extend to a desire to be subpoenaed.

Dale notices one more thing, and this is the thing that matters.

On a Wednesday morning, entering the hallway to brush his teeth, he passes the door to the walk-in cold-storage unit. The door is a shared-access design, an original feature of the building's construction—a single large walk-in unit with entry points from both Unit 1 and Unit 3, dating from the 1986 floor plan, when all three units were occupied by a single restaurant called The Greenery, which served Continental-American cuisine to a clientele that no longer lives in the area and which closed in 1997.
