# The Last Credulous Person

## I.

The check was at the bail bondsman's on Cascade, which used to be called Lovers' Lane before the city council renamed it during the first round of closures, when everyone still believed renaming things could save them. I drove the seven blocks from the efficiency unit because the Civic's air conditioning still worked and my legs had that feeling again, the one where the bones remember they're supposed to be inside a person who sleeps in a bed. The dashboard read 107. The town sat under it like a dog that had stopped panting.

I passed the Cupid's Arrow Motor Lodge, whose neon sign had been reduced by electrical attrition to just the word UP, blinking pink against the white sky. The heart-shaped pool in its courtyard was visible from the road. Someone had drained it years ago, and the basin had cracked along the cleft at the top of the heart so that the two lobes had separated slightly, a fissure running down between them into the deep end. Desert scrub had taken root in the crack. A greasewood bush grew from the exact center of where the heart divided, about four feet tall now, dense with small waxy leaves. It was the healthiest thing in town.

The Cliffside, where you used to be able to get a $200 tasting menu with a view of the canyon while a pianist played Gershwin, was a Moneytree Payday Loans. They'd kept the floor-to-ceiling windows. The view of the canyon was still there, enormous and indifferent, visible behind a woman at a particleboard desk processing someone's title loan against a Kia Sorento. The pianist's alcove held a plastic ficus and a standing sign: ASK ABOUT OUR LOYALTY PROGRAM.

Skip-trace check was $340. Marcy at the bondsman's counter slid it across without eye contact, already back to her crossword. The check was drawn on an account at a bank in Phoenix. I held it under the fluorescent light like a jeweler, not because I suspected fraud but because I wanted to look at it.

The gas station on the corner of Cascade and Bridal Veil Road still sold coffee, twenty ounces for a dollar sixty. The building had been, in its previous life, a champagne bar called Fizz, and whoever converted it had not fully committed. The espresso machine was gone but the Italian tile remained, white and gold hexagons running from the floor up to the wainscoting behind the Doritos rack. A cursive F was still etched into the glass of the front door, visible when the light hit it. I poured French roast into a styrofoam cup and stood in the doorway and drank it looking out at the town.

Bridal Veil Road ran east toward the canyon, lined with buildings that had been built to look like something — Swiss chalets, Tuscan villas, Parisian cafés — and now looked like themselves: wooden façades bleaching in the sun, plaster cracking to reveal chicken wire, awnings faded past their original colors into the single universal color of canvas that has given up. Every fourth or fifth storefront still operated. A nail salon. A vape shop. A store that sold crystals and also ammunition and also notarized documents, because in a town this size you diversify or you die. The rest were dark behind their windows, displaying the final artifacts of whatever they had been: a mannequin in a wedding dress turned ivory by sun damage, a menu board still advertising COUPLES MASSAGE $89, a wooden cutout of two people holding hands with the faces removed, where you were supposed to stand behind it and have your photo taken.

The town had been built, in the 1950s, as a destination for newlyweds. The brochures had called it "The Sweetheart of the Desert." It was situated at the edge of a canyon that was not quite grand enough to be the Grand Canyon but was, by any reasonable standard, a spectacular thing — a red gash in the earth that went down eight hundred feet and turned colors at sunset that could make a person believe temporarily in God or love or the essential benevolence of geological time. The town had been built to capitalize on this belief. Every business, every architectural detail, every street name was a technology designed to produce the sensation of romance in people who had just legally committed to experiencing it. For decades, it had worked. Couples had driven in from Phoenix and Tucson and Los Angeles and stood at the canyon's edge and felt what the town was selling: the conviction that the landscape agreed with their decision, that the earth itself was celebrating their union, that this was the kind of place where love was not a choice but a property of the physical environment, as reliable as gravity or the sunset.

Then the interstate rerouted. Then the airlines got cheap. Then the internet made it possible to go anywhere, which meant no one had to come here. The couples stopped arriving with the slow, diagnostic regularity of a condition no one had tested for. The champagne bar became a gas station. The tasting menu became a title loan. The heart-shaped pools were drained one by one, monuments to a hydraulic commitment the water table could no longer support.

I finished the coffee. The sun was vertical and merciless. A jackrabbit crossed Bridal Veil Road without urgency, as if it had been briefed on the traffic situation. Across the street, the wooden cutout couple held hands in the window of the former photo studio, faceless and patient, waiting for someone to stand behind them and complete the joke.

I got back in the Civic and drove to the efficiency unit, which was Room 6 of the Cupid's Arrow, rate negotiated monthly, and which I did not call home because I was the kind of person who noticed that distinction and the kind of person who pretended the noticing was a choice.

## II.

The councilwoman's office was in the municipal building on Sweetheart Avenue, second floor, between the water district and a room marked TOURISM AND ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT that had been dark every time I passed it. Her name was on the door in adhesive letters: PATRICIA KERN, TOWN COUNCIL, DISTRICT 2. The door was open. The office was clean in a way that suggested someone had cleaned it recently and for a purpose.

She was younger than I'd expected from the voice on the phone. Mid-forties, maybe. Dark hair pulled back. A face that was working hard to be composed and succeeding well enough that you could see the effort only if you were the kind of person who looked at faces professionally, which I was, or at least which I told people I was when they asked what I did.

"Thank you for coming," she said, and gestured to a chair.

The chair was new. The desk was not. On the wall behind her was a framed photograph of the town taken from the canyon rim, back when the town was lit up at night, every motel and restaurant and champagne bar burning electricity like a declaration of faith. The photo was at least thirty years old. The town in it looked like a different organism — a cluster of light at the edge of a vast darkness, small and absurd and defiant. She'd hung it where clients would see it. I noted this.

"My husband," she said. "Robert. He's been gone eleven days."

She slid a photograph across the desk. Robert Kern was a square-faced man with the kind of tan that accrues to people who live in the desert without particularly wanting to. He looked like a person who fixed things. I would learn later that he had, in fact, managed the maintenance contracts for three of the town's remaining hotels.

"Police?"

"Filed the report on day three. Deputy Cortez has been handling it. He's thorough, he's doing his best, but—" She paused and recalibrated. The recalibration was smooth, professional, a politician's muscle memory. "It's a small department. Two full-time officers. I don't think they have the resources."

"What do you think happened?"

"I don't know." She said this with the precision of someone who had rehearsed not knowing. "He didn't take a bag. His truck was found at the Miners' Point overlook, keys on the seat. His phone was at home, charging. I've called everyone. His sister in Flagstaff. His friends. The men he worked with." She stopped. "I'm not a woman who panics, Mr. Garvey. I'm a woman who makes phone calls and follows procedures and gets results. I've done all three, and I have nothing."

"And you want me to find him."

"I want you to find what happened." Another recalibration, finer this time — a shift in her eyes that might have been grief or might have been the careful management of someone who understood that grief, properly exhibited, was a kind of evidence. "I want to know. Whatever it is. Even if it's bad."

I looked at the photograph of Robert Kern. I looked at the photograph of the town behind her. Both images felt like arguments for something — for permanence, for the idea that things stay where you put them.

"My fee is four hundred a day plus expenses," I said. This was high for the town and low for anywhere else and I had pulled the number from the part of my brain that calculates not what I'm worth but what someone would have to pay me for the transaction to feel real.

"I can do three hundred," she said. "And expenses."

"Okay."

She took an envelope from the desk drawer. Inside was a cashier's check for $2,100 — a week's retainer at the negotiated rate, drawn on the town's one remaining credit union. The check was on blue paper with a watermark of the canyon. I looked at it under the overhead light.

"Is that sufficient to start?"

"It's sufficient."

We shook hands. Her hand was dry and firm and held mine for exactly the duration that professionalism required and not one second more, and I felt, in the grip, something I hadn't felt in more than a year: the sensation of being needed by someone who was looking at me while they needed me, whose eyes were on my face and who was, for the duration of the handshake, treating me as a person whose presence in a room could change the room's meaning.

"I'll call you with updates," I said.

"Please," she said. "Whatever you find."

I took the check and the photograph of Robert Kern and walked out of the municipal building into the sun. I drove to the credit union and deposited the check. In the car, I sat with the engine running and the air conditioning on and calculated: $2,100 minus the efficiency unit rate at the Cupid's Arrow, minus gas, minus food. Enough to operate. Enough, if the case extended, to put a deposit on the storefront on Bridal Veil Road that had been a notary and before that a wedding calligraphy studio and was now available month-to-month at a rate that reflected the town's opinion of its own commercial prospects. Enough to buy a desk and a nameplate and a second phone line. Enough to begin.

I put the car in gear. The air tasted like creosote and hot asphalt, and underneath it, barely, like a frequency my body could detect, a trace of something—the ghost of the perfume some previous version of this car, some previous guest of this town, had left in the upholstery decades ago. Something floral and deliberate. Something chosen with care for someone else.

I rolled the windows down and let the desert burn it out.

## III.

The Miners' Point overlook was a gravel pullout on Route 14, five miles east of town, where a low stone wall separated the road from an eight-hundred-foot drop into the canyon. The wall had been built in the 1960s with native sandstone and a plaque that read LOVERS' LOOKOUT in raised bronze letters, though someone had scratched out the O in LOVERS' with a key or a knife, so it read L*VERS' LOOKOUT, which was either vandalism or commentary. Tourists used to park here at sunset and watch the canyon walls turn from ochre to vermillion to a deep arterial red, and somewhere in the gift shop that was now a plywood-boarded husk at the edge of the pullout, they would have bought postcards of this view and written on the back: *Wish you were here.* Or: *You wouldn't believe this place.* Or: *I have never been so happy.*

Robert Kern's truck had been found here. The police report, which Deputy Cortez had shared with me after a phone call from the councilwoman — shared willingly, even eagerly, in the way of a man who is relieved to hand something to someone else — placed the truck in the southwest corner of the pullout, driver's door unlocked, keys on the passenger seat. No blood. No signs of struggle. The truck had been towed to the impound lot behind the municipal building. I'd already looked at it. The cab smelled like sunscreen and work gloves and the faintly alkaline odor of industrial cleaning solution. The glove compartment held a registration, a tire pressure gauge, and a photograph of Patricia Kern standing at this same overlook, younger, her hair loose, smiling at whoever held the camera with the particular openness of a person who does not yet know they will need to compose their face for professional purposes.

I stood at the wall and looked at the canyon. It was 2 p.m. The light was flat and enormous. The canyon did not perform. It was the only thing in town that didn't.

From the overlook, I drove to the Desert Blossom Inn, which had been the town's premier honeymoon hotel until 2014, when the last owners surrendered it to the bank, who surrendered it to weather and time, who were still in possession. The building was three stories of coral-pink stucco with wrought-iron balconies and, on the roof, a sign in cursive neon that had once read *Desert Blossom Inn: Where Love Blooms* but now read, in the partial alphabet of electrical failure, *D s rt Bl s m nn: W re Lo Bl ms.* The front door was chained. The chain had been cut and re-looped to look intact, and a person who wasn't looking wouldn't notice, but I was a person who was looking.

Inside, the lobby was a time capsule in active decomposition. The carpet was rose-colored under the dust. The front desk still held a bell and a guest register, which I opened: the last entry was dated March 9, 2014, a couple from San Diego, the Murakamis, who had reserved the Eternity Suite. I photographed the page. The chandelier above was missing half its crystals and the other half threw small, dirty rainbows on the walls when I moved my flashlight across them.

I went up. The Eternity Suite was on the third floor. The hallway was papered in a pattern of roses and interlocking rings, peeling now in long, slow curls from the walls, like the building was undressing. The door to the suite was ajar.

Inside: a king bed with a bare mattress, stained in patterns that could have been water damage or wine or time. A bathroom with a tub shaped like a clamshell — the real, full-commitment kind, with the fluted edges, the kind of tub that had been ordered from a specialty catalog and installed by a plumber who understood that his job was not plumbing but *theater.* A window overlooking the canyon. Curtains that had once been red and were now the color of dried blood, which is to say the color of something that has spent a long time being slowly abandoned by whatever made it vivid.

I sprayed luminol on the bathroom floor. The flashlight went off. The room went black. And then the floor spoke: a constellation of pale blue luminescence, brightest near the base of the clamshell tub, trailing in a pattern toward the door — not a pool but a transit, a movement, the record of something liquid traveling across a surface in a direction.

I photographed it. The shutter sound in the dark room was very loud. The luminol glow was already fading — that's how it works, you have seconds — and I took seven frames in those seconds, moving the camera in a practiced arc, and on the fifth frame my hand drifted and I took the photograph at a wrong angle, too high, and caught instead of the floor a section of the wall above the tub where the tile had been laid in a pattern of small hearts, pink and white, alternating, and in the photograph the hearts were lit from below by the luminol's glow and looked, in the image, like hearts on fire, a wall of burning hearts, the dumbest and most relentless decorative motif imaginable, designed by someone who believed you could tile love into a surface, grout it in, make it structural — and I stared at the image on the camera's screen for longer than I should have, longer than the investigation required, standing in the dark bathroom of a dead hotel where the floor was printing evidence that someone had bled and been moved.
