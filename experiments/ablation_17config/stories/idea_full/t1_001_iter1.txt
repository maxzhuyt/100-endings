# The Harvest Birthday

## I.

Here is a thing that is true about love: it does not require success. It does not even require hope. It requires only the willingness to show up again on Monday.

Marlo Vance and Seph Ouédraogo had been showing up on Mondays for fifteen years, and on every other day besides, hauling speaker cabinets with blown-out corners and milk crates of vinyl that Seph still refused to leave behind despite the world's decisive pivot to digital, despite the warping, despite the fact that their last turntable had been held together since June by electrical tape and a prayer Seph had copied from the inside cover of a hymnal she'd stolen from a Holiday Inn in Scranton. They had been DJs — had been, were, would continue to be, because neither of them knew how to be anything else, and because the alternative was to admit that the universe had been correct about them all along, and they were both, in their separate but adjoining ways, too stubborn for that.

On this particular Friday in October, they were loading the van — a 2007 Ford Econoline the color of a thing that had once been white and had given up — and they were arguing about the playlist, which was their way of saying I love you without the inconvenience of saying it.

"You cannot open with 'September,'" Marlo said. She was sitting on the bumper, a clipboard on her knee, her handwriting so small and dense it looked like an autopsy of the English language. She was thin in the way of women who forgot to eat not out of vanity but out of a genuine, recurring surprise that the body required maintenance. Her hair was cut short and unevenly, because she cut it herself, in the dark, when she couldn't sleep. She was a poet who had stopped writing poems seven years ago but had not stopped thinking in them, which was worse, like a surgeon who had retired but still involuntarily mapped the veins in every hand she shook.

"I can and I will open with 'September,'" Seph said from inside the van, where she was rearranging equipment with the tender authority of a woman settling children into car seats. Seph was broad-shouldered and round-hipped and had a laugh that arrived in a room before she did and stayed after she left. She had been raised in the A.M.E. church — three services every Sunday until she was seventeen — and although she had left the pews, the pews had not left her. She heard the world in four-part harmony. She could not listen to a cash register beep without assigning it a key. When she cared about something, she cared in the way of gospel: too much, too loud, with the expectation that the ceiling would open at any moment.

"September," Marlo said, "is the musical equivalent of a golden retriever."

"People love golden retrievers."

"People love golden retrievers because golden retrievers love them back unconditionally, and people are so starved for unconditional love that they will accept it from an animal that also eats its own vomit."

"So what you're saying is 'September' is exactly right for a festival full of strangers who hired us off Craigslist."

Marlo opened her mouth. Closed it. Wrote something on the clipboard.

"Did you just add 'September' to the list?"

"I added 'September' to the list."

"Marlo Vance."

"Seph Ouédraogo."

"You are a profoundly difficult woman."

"This is not news."

It was not news. It had not been news since the night they met, which was also, as it happened, a disaster — a fraternity party at Temple University in 2008, where Seph had been hired to DJ and Marlo had been hired to tend bar, and an electrical fire had cleared the building at 11:40 p.m. and they had found themselves standing on the sidewalk sharing a cigarette that neither of them had lit, watching the fire trucks arrive with the calm of two people who had already intuited that this was how things would go for them: early endings, faulty wiring, the persistent sense that any room they entered would find a way to empty itself.

They had not become friends immediately. Friendship, for people like Marlo and Seph — people who had been disappointed often enough to develop a science of it — was not a spark but a slow chemical process, like rust, or like the way a river, given enough centuries, will carve a canyon simply by refusing to stop. Seph had called Marlo three weeks later to ask if she knew anyone who could tend bar at a wedding she was spinning. Marlo had said she could do it herself. She could not do it herself. The bride's mother threw a champagne flute at the wall after Marlo mixed a martini with the confidence and accuracy of someone who had never seen a martini made. But Seph had covered for her — told the bride's mother it was a regional style, a "Philadelphia dry," improvising not just the lie but an entire fictional cocktail history with the fluency of a woman whose first language was survival — and afterward they had sat in Seph's car in the parking lot of a Wawa and eaten hoagies and laughed until Seph's mascara ran and Marlo's stomach hurt and the windows fogged up like a confession booth.

After that, they were simply together. Not romantically — or not in any way either of them had vocabulary for, which may have been the same thing — but operationally, constitutionally, the way a left hand is with a right. They formed what they called a "mobile DJ and event services company," which was a grand name for two women, a van, and an evolving catalog of small catastrophes. There was the retirement party where Seph's speakers had emitted, for thirteen uninterrupted minutes, a frequency that made every dog within a quarter mile howl in eerie, mournful unison. There was the sweet sixteen where Marlo, handed the microphone for the birthday toast, had inexplicably recited the opening stanza of Sylvia Plath's "Lady Lazarus" before Seph tackled her at the word *peanut-crunching.* There was the anniversary dinner — the bad one, the one they still referred to only as "the incident" — where Marlo, scanning for a love song, had queued up Tammy Wynette's "D-I-V-O-R-C-E" during the first dance, and had not even noticed until the groom began to weep, and Seph, in trying to fix it, had accidentally cross-faded into "Hit the Road Jack," which made things geometrically worse in ways that still echoed through their Yelp reviews like munitions.

They had not gotten better at the work. That was the thing. Fifteen years, and they had not appreciably improved. What they had done — what they had gotten so good at that it no longer registered as a skill — was survive the work together. Survive each other. Survive the moment, which came after every gig, when the equipment was loaded and the van smelled like sweat and cheap fog-machine fluid and one of them would say, without irony, "That actually went okay," and the other would begin listing the ways it had not gone okay, and they would argue — lovingly, exhaustively, in the way of an old marriage, which is what this was, if a marriage could exist without anyone having the courage to name it — all the way home.

The gig they were loading for now had materialized three days ago: an email to their website (which Seph maintained with a devotion that the website did not deserve, the website being essentially a GeoCities page with a stock photo of a turntable and the motto "We Bring the Vibe" in Comic Sans, which Marlo had begged her to change and which Seph kept out of what she called loyalty and what Marlo called spite). The email was from a woman named Patrice Gooding, who identified herself as the events coordinator for the annual Harvest Birthday Festival in Harmon, Pennsylvania. She needed DJs for Saturday night. The pay was four thousand dollars.

"That's a typo," Marlo had said.

"Typos don't come in even thousands."

"Maybe she meant four hundred."

"Four hundred is also more than we've ever been paid for anything."

This was true. Their typical fee was somewhere between one hundred and fifty dollars and whatever miscellaneous items the host pressed into their hands at the end of the night in lieu of the second half of payment. Marlo owned a set of steak knives, a taxidermied quail, and a framed portrait of someone else's grandmother, all acquired in this fashion.

They had Googled Harmon, Pennsylvania. The results were sparse: a census listing (population 412), a single TripAdvisor review for a bed-and-breakfast ("Clean towels. Weird town. Would not return."), and what appeared to be a hand-scanned page from a 19th-century county history that mentioned an annual fall festival of unspecified nature. The Harvest Birthday had no website, no Facebook event, no Instagram hashtag. It existed in the manner of a rumor: spoken about, not documented.

"We're not going," Marlo had said, which was what Marlo always said, which was how Seph knew they were going.

Seph put the last speaker in the van. Fastened the strap. Pressed her palm against the cabinet the way a jockey presses a palm against a horse's flank — communicating something old and animal, something beneath language. She came around to the bumper where Marlo sat and took the clipboard gently from her hands.

"Happy almost birthday," Seph said.

Marlo blinked. "That's not until Sunday."

"I know when your birthday is."

"It's a meaningless calendrical event."

"It is not, and you know it's not, and you can pretend it's not all the way to Harmon, and I will let you, because I love you, but when we get there I am going to find you a cake."

"Seph."

"A cake, Marlo. With frosting. And I will make you blow out candles. And you will make a wish. And probably the wish won't come true, because that's how things go for us, but you will make it anyway, because that is the deal."

"What deal?"

"The deal where we keep going."

Marlo looked at her. Seph looked back. Between them, in the space that neither of them had ever learned to cross or been brave enough to close, the October air held its breath, and the afternoon light did something that the narrator would like to describe as golden but was, in truth, simply the light of an ordinary day in which two extraordinary women were about to drive toward something that was not ordinary at all.

Marlo's birthday was October 19th. This weekend. Remember this.

They got in the van. The windshield was cracked in a pattern that looked, if you squinted — and Seph had squinted, many times, from many angles — like a river delta, or a family tree, or the circulatory system of something vast and not quite alive. Seph turned the key. The engine caught on the third try.

"September," Seph said, putting the van in gear.

"Don't you dare."

Seph pressed play. Earth, Wind & Fire filled the cracked-open afternoon. They pulled out of the lot and onto I-76 heading west, toward a town that was waiting for them with a patience that was older than the highway, older than the van, older than either of their lives, though not — and this mattered, this mattered more than anything — older than what they were to each other.

## II.

They arrived in Harmon at the hour when the sky can't make up its mind, when the light is the color of a bruise that doesn't hurt yet, and found that the town had been expecting them the way a table set for dinner expects its guests: with a completeness that implied the meal had been planned for a very long time.

Bunting first. Red and gold and a burnt orange that Marlo would later describe, in the privacy of her own thinking, as the color of a word you can't remember — bunting strung from every lamppost, every porch rail, every surface that would accept a nail or a staple or, in some cases, what appeared to be hand-forged iron hooks sunk directly into the bark of living trees. Then the banners. HAPPY HARVEST BIRTHDAY, they read, in variations that ranged from professional-grade vinyl to what was unmistakably a bedsheet spray-painted by someone in the grip of genuine enthusiasm. One, stretched across Main Street between a hardware store and a building with no sign at all, read HAPPY HARVEST BIRTHDAY: THE BODY REMEMBERS in letters so ornate they might have been calligraphy.

"The body remembers what?" Marlo said.

"Probably like a wellness thing," Seph said. "Like yoga."

"Yoga."

"Small towns love yoga now. I read that."

"Where?"

"I don't remember where. I read things."

They parked in front of the bed-and-breakfast, which was a Victorian painted in improbable pastels — lilac siding, cream trim, a door the color of a robin's egg that opened before they could knock.

The woman in the doorway was somewhere between fifty and seventy, which is to say she had the face of a person who had decided, at some point, that aging was a collaborative process between the body and the landscape, and had let both have their way. She was tall. She wore a cardigan the same burnt orange as the bunting, and her smile was so immediate and so total that it crossed the threshold before she did.

"You're here," she said, and the way she said it — the emphasis on *here*, as if the word contained a history — made Seph think, involuntarily, of her grandmother, who used to greet everyone at the church door as though their arrival was the specific miracle she'd been praying for.

"Patrice?" Seph said.

"Patrice Gooding, yes, and you must be — well, I know who you are, I've been — please, come in, come in, there are rooms and there are flowers and there is a great deal to tell you."

The rooms were upstairs: adjoining, with a shared bathroom tiled in hexagonal white, and a window that looked out over a town that, in the plum-colored dusk, appeared to be *performing* itself — people moving through the streets with the purposeful cheerfulness of a cast before curtain. Fresh flowers in every room. Not the bodega bouquets of carnations and baby's breath that Marlo and Seph were accustomed to receiving at the ends of events they had partially ruined, but *arranged* flowers, the kind that suggested a vocabulary — dahlias, marigolds, chrysanthemums, all in the autumn spectrum, all at the absolute peak of their blooming, which was also, if one thought about it, the moment just before they would begin to die. Seph did not think about it. Marlo did.

"The festival," Patrice said, sitting on the edge of the bed in Marlo's room with the comfort of someone who had sat on many beds in many rooms and spoken these same words many times, "is our oldest tradition. Every October, we celebrate the birthdays of the month. Everyone born in October — and there are many of us, Harmon has always been an October town — is honored. But the real heart of the Harvest Birthday is the Twinning."

"The twinning," Marlo said. She was standing by the window. She had not sat down. She was a woman who used verticality as a defense system.

"Each birthday honoree is given a twin. Someone from outside Harmon — a visitor, a traveler, someone born in October who the festival brings to us. The twins spend the weekend together. They eat together. They are celebrated together. It's — well, it's hard to describe until you've seen it, but it is the most beautiful thing we do." Patrice smiled again, and the smile was so genuine that it hurt in the way that genuine things sometimes do, the way a real sunset can be almost too much after a long day of artificial light. "Now. I have to ask what I suspect I already know. Do either of you have an October birthday?"

What happened next was this: Seph, who had spent fifteen years being proud of Marlo in ways that Marlo would never allow if she knew the full extent of them — proud of her stubbornness, proud of her dark humor, proud of the way she could make a metaphor feel like a door opening onto a room you didn't know your house contained — said, with the warmth of someone offering a gift she believed was harmless:

"Marlo does. October nineteenth. This Sunday."
