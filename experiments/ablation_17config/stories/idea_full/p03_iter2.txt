# The Preacher's Daughter

---

## I.

What you have to understand is that I was already married and divorced before I turned sixteen. So when people say to me, *Blanche, however did you end up with a man like Buck Barrow,* I have to laugh. I have to. Because the question assumes a girl who had somewhere better to be.

My father was a minister. Church of Christ, not the showy kind — the kind where the hymns don't have instruments and the suppers don't have dancing and the Lord is someone you meet in the silence between verses. We lived in a parsonage in southwest Missouri, and the screen door never latched right, and on summer nights you could hear the cicadas through the walls like the whole county was humming a chord nobody had written down. That was my childhood. Cicadas and Wednesday suppers and my father's voice carrying through the floor while he practiced sermons in the kitchen. He practiced on my mother, who ironed while he preached. I think she listened. I know she ironed.

I married the first time at fourteen, to a boy whose name I will not give you because he does not deserve the oxygen of being remembered in connection with what came after. He was not cruel. He was simply not anything. We lasted — oh, I don't know. Long enough for me to learn that a marriage can be a room with no furniture in it. You sit on the floor and wait for someone to bring a chair and after a while you realize no one is coming and the door was open the whole time.

So I walked through the door. I was fifteen, divorced, back in my father's house, and in 1929 that was a particular kind of ruined. Not a scarlet woman — nothing so exciting. Just a girl who had used up her story too early. People in town looked at me the way you look at a library book that's been returned with the last chapter torn out. What's the point of her now.

I am telling you this because people want to hear about the dying. I need to start with the marrying.

Buck was at a filling station outside Willow Springs. I don't remember the month. I remember he was leaning against the pump like he'd been placed there by a set designer, one boot crossed over the other, hat pushed back, grinning at absolutely nothing. Some men smile because they're happy. Buck smiled because he thought the world was hilarious and nobody else had noticed yet.

He'd been paroled from Huntsville the week before. I did not know this. He told me he was in the "automotive trade." This was technically true the way saying a tornado is "in the roofing business" is technically true.

He asked me my name. I told him. He said, "Blanche. Like a blank page." And he tapped his chest and said, "I've got a few stories that need writing down."

My father would have recognized the devil's small talk. But my father was not at that filling station. I was. And Marvin Ivan "Buck" Barrow had a laugh that sounded like someone had startled joy out of him against his will, and he bought me a Coca-Cola without asking if I wanted one, which I did.

Two worlds. I knew that even then. The world of hymnals and screen doors and Wednesday suppers, where every story ended with a moral and the moral was always *be still.* And then whatever world Buck Barrow had walked out of, which smelled like engine grease and distance and something else I couldn't name. Something with teeth that I mistook, at the time, for excitement.

I married him. Of course I married him.

You marry the first one because you don't know better. You marry the second one because you do.

---

Now. I've been told I should explain the recording. I've been told that a voice needs a machine to justify it, that the listener — you, I suppose — requires some accounting for why an old woman in Texas is talking into a device about people who've been dead thirty years.

I'll tell you this much. A man from the university came to my house. He had a tape recorder the size of a suitcase and a list of questions about Clyde. They always have questions about Clyde. Sometimes about Bonnie. Occasionally, if they've done their homework, about my eyes — I lost most of the vision in my left eye at Platte City, and the scar tissue has a certain drama that photographs well, and I have learned that people enjoy a woman's suffering most when it has left a visible mark.

This man set up his machine and clicked his pen and said, "Mrs. Frasure" — that's my married name now, the third one, the one that stuck — "can you tell me what it was like to ride with Bonnie and Clyde?"

And I said, "Son, I didn't ride *with* Bonnie and Clyde. I rode with my husband. Bonnie and Clyde just happened to be in the car."

He did not write that down correctly. They never do.

---

## II.

The first time I met them properly was in a rented tourist cabin outside Fort Smith, the kind of place that charged by the week and didn't ask for real names. Buck drove us there in a stolen Chevrolet that he swore was borrowed, a distinction I chose to believe because the alternative was to open a door I was not yet ready to walk through.

Bonnie had cleaned. That was the first thing I noticed. The cabin was the kind of place where the wallpaper depicted a pastoral scene that had yellowed into something closer to a pastoral nightmare — little shepherdesses with jaundiced skin, lambs the color of old teeth — but Bonnie had hung curtains. Real curtains, not sheets. She'd bought them somewhere, or stolen them somewhere, and hemmed them herself, and they were a blue gingham that fought the shepherdesses to a draw.

She'd set a table. Four plates, four forks, four glasses. She'd made fried chicken, or what she called fried chicken — Bonnie cooked the way some people sing in church, with tremendous conviction and limited technique. The chicken had been baptized in flour and submerged in oil with the intensity of someone performing a sacrament, and it emerged golden and righteous on the outside and still bearing a grudge on the inside. I ate it. I smiled. There are things you survive that prepare you for nothing except surviving other things, and my first marriage had at least taught me to eat bad chicken and look grateful.

Clyde was — and I must be careful here, because the word I want to use is *charming,* and that word has been so abused by the newspapers and the picture shows that it arrives pre-ruined, like a dress that's been worn to too many dances. But Clyde, that first night, was charming the way a piano is charming when someone who knows how to play it sits down. The music was not in the instrument. It was in the intention. He gave Buck the good chair. He called me "Sister Blanche," half teasing, half tender, and opened a bottle of soda for me before I'd set my purse down. He asked about my family. He listened to the answers — or performed the listening so convincingly that the difference didn't seem to matter.

Bonnie touched his arm every time she passed behind his chair. Not a grab. A graze. Fingertips across his shoulder blade, a current running from her hand to his skin and back again, a circuit that completed itself every few minutes as she moved between the stove and the table. He'd go still when she touched him. Just for a half-second. Like a compass needle finding north.

After supper, Bonnie sat on the arm of Clyde's chair and recited a poem. I believe it was Edna St. Vincent Millay, though it might have been one of her own — Bonnie's poems and other people's poems existed in the same register in her mind, which was the register of *things Bonnie Parker thought were beautiful enough to say aloud.* She had a voice for recitation, I'll give her that. A voice that belonged in a better room than any room she'd ever been in.

Clyde watched her recite the way my father watched the congregation during the invocation. Like he was witnessing something sacred and he wanted to be sure everyone else was witnessing it too.

They played cards after. Hearts, I think. Buck cheated. Clyde caught him. Bonnie laughed so hard she knocked over her glass, and Clyde mopped it up with his sleeve without looking away from her face, and Buck said, "Brother, you are the worst card player in Texas and Oklahoma combined," and Clyde said, "I'm worse than that, I'm the worst card player who ever got the best-looking woman in both states to sit on his chair arm," and Bonnie said, "Three states, honey, don't sell me short," and Buck threw his cards at both of them.

I laughed. I laughed honestly. It was the most normal evening I'd had in months, and the fact that it was taking place in a fugitive's cabin with stolen curtains and wanted felons did not, in the softness of that lamplight, seem to be the most important fact in the room.

We drove back to our own motel. Buck was humming. He hummed when he was happy — old songs, half-remembered, melody without words, like a man who had feelings he didn't have the vocabulary for and had to borrow the music from somewhere.

I put my hand on his knee. He put his hand on mine.

I thought: well, at least Clyde found his match. They were like two kids playing house, and the house was wherever they decided to put the curtains.

I did not yet understand that playing house was all it ever was. That the curtains were the load-bearing wall. That if you pulled them down, there was nothing behind them but highway and a windshield full of dark.

---

## III.

We moved in with them. That's what we did. Lord help me, that is exactly what we did.

I should explain the logistics of fugitive domesticity, because the picture shows get it wrong every time. In the pictures it's all fast driving and tommy guns and motel rooms where people have meaningful arguments in beautiful lighting. In life it was laundry.

It was *so much laundry.*

Five people in a two-room apartment — Clyde's boy W.D. Jones had joined by then, sixteen years old, still growing, eating everything that wasn't nailed to a plate and most things that were. Five people, two rooms, one bathroom, and a hot plate that worked only if you held the cord at a specific angle, like tuning a radio to a station that didn't want to be found.

I ironed. Every morning, I ironed. I set up the board by the window — there was always a window, in every apartment and tourist cabin and rented farmhouse — and I pressed Buck's shirts and sometimes Clyde's shirts and occasionally, during a particularly grim stretch in Missouri, W.D.'s shirts, which were a boy's shirts straining at the seams with a boy's sweat and a man's fear worked into the fabric. I ironed them all with the same even strokes my mother had used on my father's preaching shirts, and I did not allow myself to think about what the shirts might be worn to.

Clyde cleaned guns at the kitchen table. He had a kit — oils, rags, a brush that was softer than anything in that apartment deserved to be — and he laid the pieces out like a surgeon laying out instruments. The Browning automatic rifle, disassembled, took up most of the table. I made my grocery lists on the corner that was left.

*Soap (bar, not flake)*
*Red beans, 2 lb*
*Cornmeal*
*Sardines for CDB* — that was Clyde; he liked sardines the way a man lost at sea likes a raft — *tinned milk, newspapers*

I wrote *newspapers* because Bonnie wanted them. Bonnie read every newspaper she could find, turning pages with the intensity of a stockbroker reading the ticker, except what Bonnie was tracking was not a market but a mythology. She was looking for herself. She was looking for what they said about her. And she read the good parts aloud — the parts that called her beautiful, that called Clyde daring, that called them a *love story —* the same way she read poetry, with her chin tilted up and her voice carrying, performing for an audience that, in that cramped apartment, consisted of four people who already knew the unglamorous truth: that Bonnie Parker had not washed her hair in four days and Clyde Barrow had a limp that made stairs an engineering problem and the most-wanted couple in America could not agree on whose turn it was to go to the filling station for gas.

The arguments. People don't think about the arguments.

"You put too much pepper in. You *always* put too much pepper in."

"Blanche, this is the amount of pepper that a grown human woman puts in red beans. What you want is the amount of pepper that a sparrow puts in, which is none, because a sparrow does not eat red beans. He eats seeds and the Lord's dropped crumbs."

"My mother's red beans won Best Savory at the Callaway County Fair three years running."

"Your mother's red beans are a beautiful tribute to the color red and the shape of a bean."

That was Bonnie. She said that. And I laughed — Lord, I didn't want to, but I laughed — and she laughed, and for a moment we were just two women fighting over a pot of beans while the men did whatever the men were doing in the other room, which in a normal household would have been listening to the radio and in ours was plotting the armed robbery of a filling station in Alma, Arkansas.

We switched license plates the way other families switched seasonal decorations. It was a Saturday chore. Buck and Clyde would take the cars somewhere and come back with new plates, and W.D. would bolt them on while eating a sandwich, and I would watch from the window with the same expression my mother wore when she watched my father climb a ladder to clean the church gutters — the expression of a woman evaluating the specific way a man she loves is likely to die.

We could never stay. That was the part the movies didn't tell you. The way a bed would just begin to feel like *yours* — the specific sag in the mattress that your body had finally memorized, the particular angle of morning light that you'd learned to sleep with — and then Clyde would say *time to move* and everything you'd settled into was packed into the Ford in twenty minutes and the sheets went back to being a stranger's sheets.

You don't know what that does to a person. The not-staying. You stop learning the names of streets. You stop noting which drawer you put your things in. You start living the way a bird lives, which looks like freedom from the outside but is actually just the inability to stop.

I will tell you the moment I think of most often.

We were outside Amarillo, or maybe it was outside Shamrock — the Texas Panhandle all looks like God started a painting and got called away — and the Ford had died in a ditch. Not a dramatic ditch. A polite ditch, the kind that apologizes while it swallows your front axle. We'd been driving all night, and the radiator had opinions, and somewhere around dawn the car simply decided it had been stolen enough and lay down in the mud like a dog that will not walk further.

Bonnie got out.

Now, understand: Bonnie that morning was wearing a silk blouse. Peach colored. She'd bought it — or Clyde had bought it, or somebody had acquired it through channels I had trained myself not to inquire about — and she wore it the way a woman wears something she believes she deserves, which is to say with her shoulders back and her neck long and an air of absolute entitlement that would have been infuriating if it hadn't been, in that specific moment, standing in red Texas mud at six in the morning next to a dead Ford on a road that went nowhere good — so deeply, catastrophically funny.

"Push," said Clyde.

And Bonnie — Bonnie Parker, who filed her nails in getaway cars and read Edna St. Vincent Millay to armed men and believed herself to be the heroine of a story that would outlive them all — planted her heels in the mud, put her French-manicured hands on the trunk of a stolen automobile, and pushed.

Her heels sank. The silk blouse went translucent with sweat. And she swore.
