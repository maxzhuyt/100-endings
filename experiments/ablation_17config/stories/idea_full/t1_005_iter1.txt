# The Parking Citation

## I.

His name is Theo Achland and I need you to understand a few things about him before we go any further.

He is twenty-eight years old. He graduated first in his class from Columbia Law, which would be impressive enough except that he also graduated first in his class from Columbia undergrad, which would be impressive enough except that when he was seventeen he won the Virginia state chess championship in a bracket that did not have an age division, meaning he beat men who had been playing since before his parents met. He has an eidetic memory for case law, a conversational fluency in four languages, and a crossword time that I once watched reduce a partner at Covington to silence at a cocktail party. He plays bar trivia on Thursday nights at a place called The Gavel, where the bartender has stopped keeping a running board because the other teams complained it was demoralizing. When he argues before a judge, stenographers have told me they forget to type. He is, by essentially any metric the Western legal tradition has devised to measure such things, brilliant.

I am his law clerk. My name is Danielle. I graduated middle of my pack from Georgetown, which is fine, which is respectable, which means that in any room not containing Theo Achland I am the smartest person in it and in any room containing Theo Achland I am the person who remembers to bring the stapler. I have worked for him for fourteen months. I pick up his dry cleaning on Wednesdays because the shop closes at six and he does not acknowledge the existence of six p.m. as a meaningful boundary. I refill his coffee without being asked because I have learned the way his left hand drifts toward the mug at forty-minute intervals, a gesture so reliable I could set laboratory equipment by it. I admire him. I want to be clear about that. Not in the breathless, subordinate way that word sometimes implies—I am not dazzled, or not only dazzled—but in the way you admire a cathedral, or aeli suspension bridge, or anything built with such total commitment to its own internal logic that the presence of a single misaligned element would bring the entire thing screaming down.

This is the story of the worst Tuesday of his life.

I know how it ends. I could have stopped it. I need you to understand that too. I stood at the threshold of several perfectly ordinary moments—a doorframe, a car door, the counter at the municipal building—where a single sentence from me would have rerouted the entire day into something forgettable. I said nothing. By the time you finish, you'll understand why, and you will not forgive me, and that's all right, because I have not entirely forgiven myself, and the difference between us is that you will only have to carry the story and I have to carry the look on his face.

But I'm getting ahead of myself.

This is where you'll want to feel sorry for him. Don't. Or—do, but save most of it. You'll need it later, and there won't be enough.

---

## II.

The previous Friday, Theo received a parking citation. Ninety dollars.

I'll tell you the facts because the facts matter—they are, in a sense, the only thing Theo has ever believed in, and it seems right that they should be the instrument. He parked his car, a gray Civic with a cracked rear bumper that he's never bothered to fix because it does not affect the car's *function* and Theo does not acknowledge the existence of aesthetic maintenance, on the east side of Beaumont Street between Third and Fourth. There is a sign at the north end of the block that reads NO PARKING 8AM–6PM MON–FRI. There is a sign at the south end of the block that reads 2-HOUR PARKING 8AM–6PM MON–FRI. He parked in the approximate middle of the block, equidistant from both signs, at 3:47 p.m. on a Friday. He was inside the county records office for two hours and twenty-three minutes. When he returned, the citation was under his wiper blade.

He was, by any reasonable reading of the situation, in a gray area. The signage was contradictory. Two signs, one block, opposite instructions. A person could look at the north sign and conclude that parking was prohibited. A person could look at the south sign and conclude that parking was permitted for two hours. Theo had parked for two hours and twenty-three minutes, which violated the south sign by twenty-three minutes if you applied it, and violated the north sign absolutely if you applied that one instead. The citation was issued under the north sign's authority. Theo maintained the south sign governed his space. The municipal code was, on this particular half-block, at war with itself, and Theo's Civic was parked in no-man's-land.

He appealed. Of course he appealed. He filed the paperwork that afternoon, a three-page document that I proofread out of professional habit and that was, I will tell you plainly, airtight. He cited the relevant sections of the municipal traffic code. He cited the principle of lenity—that ambiguity in regulatory language should be construed in favor of the person regulated. He cited the GPS coordinates of both signs and the measured distance to his vehicle. He included a diagram. It was a beautiful appeal. If appeals could be framed and hung in galleries, this one would have occupied a modest but well-lit room (at the Smithsonian).

Municipal Judge Alma Gersh, semi-retired, heard the appeal Monday afternoon. Theo did not attend in person because he did not consider this a matter that required his physical presence. The brief, he told me, was sufficient. The brief spoke for itself.

Judge Gersh ruled against him. The fine stood.

I watched him read the ruling Tuesday morning. I was at my desk, which faces his through a doorless opening between our offices, an arrangement that allows me to monitor the coffee hand and also, when necessary, to witness the small private moments that people in adjacent rooms are not supposed to see. He opened the envelope at 8:41 a.m. I know because I looked at the clock afterward, the way people do when something has happened that they sense will require a timestamp.

He read it. His eyes moved to the bottom of the page, where the ruling was. Then they moved back to the top. He read the whole thing again. I watched his hand flatten against the paper as though smoothing a crease, but there was no crease. The paper was flat. His hand was the thing that needed steadying.

He set it down with both hands. Placed it on the desk the way you place something that might, if moved too quickly, detonate. His palms stayed on it for a moment. Then he lifted them, and looked at the document with an expression I had never seen on his face before and have not seen since—an expression I can only describe as the look of a man who has opened a door in his own house and found a room he did not build.

"This is wrong," he said.

He said it calmly. He said it the way you'd report a stopped clock. Not outrage. Diagnosis.

I had coffee with Judge Gersh two days before. But I'll get to that.

## III.

Between nine and noon, Theo drafted and filed a formal judicial conduct complaint against the Honorable Alma M. Gersh with the Municipal Oversight and Judicial Standards Board of Henner County.

I want to make sure you appreciate the temporality of this. He did it *in addition to* his actual caseload, which that morning included a landlord-tenant brief involving a disputed lease assignment on behalf of a woman named Gloria Watts, whose landlord had changed the locks while she was visiting her sister in Roanoke. The Watts brief was due by three. The judicial conduct complaint was due by nothing, because no one had asked for it, because it was a parking ticket and the appropriate response to an unfavorable ruling on a parking ticket is to pay the ninety dollars and go to lunch.

But if you're looking for the appropriate response, you have the wrong attorney.

I sat at my desk and watched him work both documents simultaneously. He had two windows open on his screen, and he moved between them with the fluency of a pianist alternating hands—five minutes on the Watts brief, seven minutes on the complaint, three minutes on the Watts brief, twelve minutes on the complaint. The complaint was growing faster. I could tell by the scroll bar. By ten-fifteen, the Watts brief was a sturdy, competent piece of tenant-rights advocacy, and the complaint was a living thing, spreading across pages with the feverish precision of something that had been waiting inside him for a long time and had finally found a crack to grow through.

It was eighteen pages long when he printed it. I know because I carried it to him from the printer and felt the weight.

Eighteen pages. Single-spaced. The Fourteenth Amendment appeared on page three and did not leave. Due process. Equal protection. The fundamental right of every citizen to receive a reasoned application of unambiguous law, and the corresponding duty of every judicial officer to provide one. He cited *Mathews v. Eldridge*. He cited *Goldberg v. Kelly*. He cited a 1987 Seventh Circuit opinion on the procedural requirements for administrative adjudication that I had to look up to verify was real, and it was, and it was exactly on point.

I need you to hear this clearly: everything in the complaint was technically valid.

His procedural knowledge was flawless. His citations were correct. His argument that the ruling lacked sufficient analysis of the signage ambiguity was, in a vacuum, defensible. If you read the complaint without knowing it was about a parking ticket—if you stripped away the dollar amount and the subject matter and read only the legal reasoning—you would think you were reading a serious constitutional challenge drafted by a serious litigator on behalf of a seriously aggrieved client. And you would be right about everything except the seriousness.

"Take this to the oversight board," he said, holding the document out to me without looking up. He was already back in the Watts brief. He was typing one-handed on the Watts brief and extending the complaint to me with his other hand, and neither hand knew the other hand's business, and both were performing flawlessly.

I took it.

The Municipal Oversight and Judicial Standards Board of Henner County is located on the second floor of the county administrative building, which is a structure made of beige concrete and civic regret. I pushed through glass doors that had not been cleaned recently enough and walked past a directory board with removable plastic letters, several of which had fallen and been replaced in the wrong holes so that the Office of Budget Management was listed as the Office of Bu get Manage ent. The elevator smelled like someone's lunch from the day before. The hallway on the second floor was lit by fluorescent tubes, one of which was buzzing at a frequency that suggested it had been threatening to die for several fiscal quarters and had not yet been granted the budget to do so.

The office of the oversight board had a glass door with gold stenciled lettering, a waiting area with four chairs upholstered in a fabric that could only be described as municipal mauve, and a motivational poster on the far wall that read TEAMWORK: TOGETHER EVERYONE ACHIEVES MORE, with a photograph of a rowing team at sunset. One of the rowers appeared to be Photoshopped in. Below the poster, on a metal filing cabinet, a fern was dying with the slow, public dignity of a plant that had been watered exactly enough to prolong its suffering without any realistic hope of recovery.

The receptionist's name was Phil. I know this because he had a nameplate on his desk that read PHIL MANCINI, INTAKE SPECIALIST, and also because Phil is the kind of person whose name you learn and retain, not because he is memorable but because he is so comprehensively unmemorable that your brain, in its confusion, grips the name like a handrail. Phil was perhaps fifty. He wore a short-sleeved dress shirt. He had a lanyard around his neck with an ID badge and a keycard and what appeared to be a small rubber duck, the purpose of which I did not inquire about and do not wish to know. He looked at me over the top of reading glasses that had slid to the end of his nose in what appeared to be their permanent and preferred position.

I placed the eighteen pages on the laminate counter between us.

"Judicial conduct complaint," I said.

Phil looked at the document. He picked it up. He looked at the first page. His eyebrows performed a small, private negotiation with each other.

"This about the parking thing?" he said.

I don't know how he knew. Small town, I suppose. Or maybe it was the diagram on page seven, which was reproduced from the appeal and clearly depicted a car parked between two contradictory signs. Or maybe Phil, intake specialist, had developed over years of service the same diagnostic instinct that ER nurses have: the ability to look at a presenting complaint and identify, before any tests are run, the underlying condition.

"It's a formal complaint regarding a judicial ruling that my supervising attorney believes failed to meet the constitutional standard for procedural due process," I said, because I am a professional.

Phil looked at me. He looked at the document. He stapled it. The stapler made a sound like a small bone breaking. He placed it in a tray labeled INCOMING.

"It'll be processed in the order received," he said.

"When might he expect a response?"

Phil looked at the INCOMING tray. It contained, beneath Theo's complaint, a stack of other documents approximately four inches high. He looked back at me.

"Six to eight weeks," he said.

I thanked Phil. I walked back down the hallway with the dying fluorescent tube. I rode the elevator that smelled like yesterday's lunch. I pushed through the smudged glass doors into the parking lot, where the sun was shining with the bland indifference of a star that does not care about municipal signage, and I sat in my car for a moment before starting the engine.

This is the thing I need you to see: Theo had built something. Something technically flawless, procedurally perfect. Every complaint he filed would be received. Logged. Entered into the system. He was creating a record. He was constructing, with the immaculate craftsmanship of a man who has never built anything that didn't work, the architecture of his own public reckoning. Every document was a brick. Every filing was a row of seats. He was building the amphitheater, and he was building it to the exact specifications required for its purpose, and its purpose was the one thing he could not see because it was the one thing his framework had no category for: the possibility that the correct answer and the victorious answer were not the same answer.

I delivered the papers. I said nothing. I could have said something. The doorframe between our offices is four feet wide. I pass through it thirty times a day. Any one of those passages could have included a sentence—*Theo, it's ninety dollars*—that would have ended this story in the first act.

I didn't.

I want to tell you I don't know why, but I do, and I'll get there, and you're going to need the gas station sandwich first.

## IV.

At 12:15, Theo announced that we were getting lunch. This was unusual. Theo does not typically announce lunch; lunch happens to Theo the way weather happens to a mountain, something that arrives and departs without altering the essential structure. But today he stood up from his desk with the decisive energy of a man who has been productive and has earned a brief intermission in his productivity, and he said, "Come on, I'll drive," and picked up his keys, and I understood that what he actually needed was an audience.

We drove to a gas station. Not a restaurant, not a deli, not even the passable Thai place on Carver Street that would have taken six additional minutes. A Sunoco on Route 9 with two pumps, a air machine missing its hose, and a hand-lettered sign in the window reading FRESH SANDWICHES in quotation marks that I can only describe as ominous. Theo went in.
