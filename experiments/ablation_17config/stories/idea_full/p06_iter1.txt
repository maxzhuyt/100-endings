# IT (Remembrance of Things Passed)

---

## I.

The call came on a Tuesday in October, which was already wrong, because the terrible things had always happened in summer—had always been July things, buzzing and overripe, carrying with them the insistent green smell of a season that would not stop growing. She was standing in the kitchen of her farmhouse in Yarmouth when Susan's name floated onto the phone screen, and the sight of those five letters arranged in that particular configuration produced in her thorax a sensation she could only describe as *architectural*—as though a wall she had long relied upon for structural support had revealed itself, in the space between one breath and the next, to be hollow.

"It's happening again," Susan said. Her voice was flattened in a way that suggested she had been rehearsing this call, turning the words over in her mouth until all the inflection had worn off them, the way a child will repeat a word—*truck, truck, truck*—until it becomes mere sound, depopulated of meaning. "The sewers. The sounds. It's the same."

"I know," said the narrator, whose name was Catherine but who had not been called Catherine in Bellham since she was thirteen, since the summer the thing in the culvert had opened its face and shown them what it kept behind its face, which was another face, which was not a face at all.

"You'll come," Susan said. It was not a question.

"I'll come."

She packed a bag. She packed it with the methodical detachment of a woman who had been, for the past eleven years, the owner and sole proprietor of a small-batch preserves company called *Stone Orchard*, which specialized in jams made from heirloom fruit varieties and which had been profiled, twice, in publications that used the word *terroir* without embarrassment. She folded two shirts. She rolled a pair of jeans. She placed her toiletry bag—canvas, waxed, purchased from a vendor at the Portland flea market who sourced his brass hardware from a decommissioned naval yard in Bath—into the suitcase's mesh compartment, and she zipped the suitcase shut, and she carried it to the car, and she pulled out of the gravel driveway onto Route 1, and she merged onto I-95 heading north, and it was somewhere between the Freeport exit and the first service plaza that the air freshener dangling from her rearview mirror—a cardboard pine tree, vestigial, left by the mechanic who had rotated her tires in September—began, in the sealed and solar-heated cabin of the car, to emit its smell.

It was not the smell of pine. It was the smell of the *idea* of pine as conceived by a chemical engineer in 1974—a turpentine-adjacent sweetness, vaguely medicinal, carrying within its synthetic structure the ghost of an actual forest in the same way that a photograph of a person carries within its emulsion the ghost of an actual face. And the smell was so precisely, so *catastrophically* identical to the smell of the air freshener that had hung from the rearview mirror of her mother's Plymouth Voyager—powder blue, a 1985 model, the third-row seat perpetually folded down to accommodate the detritus of a family that had not yet learned the word *declutter* because the word had not yet been invented, or had been invented but had not yet migrated from the professional-organizational literature into the colloquial mainstream, which would not happen until approximately 2002, by which point the Plymouth Voyager had been dead for a decade and her mother had moved to a condominium in Sarasota whose ruthless cleanliness suggested that she had, in fact, been among the word's early adopters—

The smell. The air freshener. She was speaking of the air freshener.

It hung from the mirror of the Voyager on a length of white string that her mother periodically replaced, because the string yellowed—everything in that car yellowed, the headliner yellowed, the visors yellowed, the seatbelt straps acquired, over the course of each summer, a patina that was not quite yellow and not quite brown but occupied the precise chromatic territory between the two, a territory for which no crayon in the Crayola 64-box had been named, though if it had been named it would have been called something like *Forgotten Khaki* or *August Domestic* or simply *1986*. The seatbelt straps. Yes. She had not thought of this in—how long? Decades. Decades in which the specific tactile memory of a nylon seatbelt strap lying against a sunburned thigh in the back seat of a Plymouth Voyager on a July afternoon had been stored somewhere in the deep architecture of her nervous system, folded and filed and forgotten, only to be detonated now, on I-95, by a two-dollar air freshener, and the memory was so vivid, so dimensionally complete, that for a moment she could feel it—the flat, faintly abraded texture of the nylon against skin that was itself a landscape, tight and hot and coated in a residue that was partly dried sweat and partly the petrochemical ghost of Coppertone SPF 4, which came in an orange bottle and smelled like the entire decade, like the contract the 1980s had signed with the sun, a contract written in cocoa butter and honored in melanoma.

They drove—she and her mother and her brother, who sat beside her in the back seat performing unspeakable experiments on a wad of Bubble Yum—to Lake Mesalonskee every third Saturday in July and August, and the drive took forty-five minutes but occupied, in the temporal economy of childhood, an entire geological epoch, and during this epoch she would press her face against the window and watch the towns pass—their white churches, their gas stations, their lawns on which American flags hung with the particular limpness of flags in windless heat, a limpness that she now recognized as patriotic exhaustion but which at the time had seemed simply like the way things were, the way fabric behaved in proximity to a nation—and the window glass was warm and smelled like dust and the inside of a car and something else, something she had never been able to name, a smell that was perhaps the smell of the window's own molecular composition releasing itself in the heat, the vitreous body exhaling.

The road had had a name. Route 137. She remembered this now with a certainty that felt almost physical, as though the number had been embossed somewhere on her skeleton. Route 137, which passed through Rome (the town, not the city, a distinction that had caused her no confusion as a child because she did not yet know there was a city, a fact that retrospectively embarrassed her, though the embarrassment was itself a kind of snobbery, was it not, the snobbery of the subsequently educated who cannot forgive their younger selves for not yet knowing what they had not yet been taught)—Route 137 through Rome and into the Belgrade Lakes region, where the lake itself waited at the end of a gravel access road, its surface carrying the particular glitter of fresh water in high sun, a glitter that was distinct from the ocean's glitter in ways she could enumerate but would not, because to enumerate them would be to translate sensation into taxonomy, and she was not interested in taxonomy, she was interested in the way the light on the lake had entered her eyes at age nine and laid down in her visual cortex a pattern that was still, thirty-three years later, her involuntary referent for the word *beautiful*.

The lake. The towel. The sand that was not really sand but a coarse brown aggregate that stuck to wet skin and could not be fully brushed off, so that her mother would make her sit on a beach towel in the back seat on the drive home, the towel printed with a design she could suddenly, with hallucinatory clarity, see: tropical fish in neon colors against a turquoise ground, the fish rendered in the confident, slightly deranged style of 1980s towel illustration, their eyes too large, their fins too exuberant, as though the designer had been working not from ichthyological reference but from the memory of a dream about fish, a fever-fish, a fish as imagined by someone who had taken a modest amount of psilocybin and then been asked to produce, under deadline, a beach towel for the Kmart summer collection—

She was on I-95. She was approaching the Waterville exit. She was going to Bellham. People were dead, or might be dead, or were in the process of becoming dead, because the thing in the culvert—the thing that had no name, or whose name was all names, or whose name was the absence concealed behind the act of naming—was awake again, and Susan had called, and the pact they had made at thirteen was, it seemed, still in force, still binding, a contract signed in blood (Richie's blood; he had been the one with the pocketknife, and his cut had been too deep, and Susan had wrapped his hand in the tail of her shirt, which was a Bellham Middle School fun-run shirt, white, the blood blooming through the cotton with the slow deliberateness of a time-lapse flower)—

She took the exit. The ramp curved. The trees had changed. She could not say when or how, exactly, they had changed—they were the same species, the same white pines and paper birches that lined every road in central Maine—but they had changed. They were too still. They were performing stillness the way a person in a hospital waiting room performs calm. The sky beyond them was a shade she did not care to investigate.

A sign: BELLHAM 14 MI.

Below it, nailed to the same post, a smaller sign, newer, the kind of corrugated plastic sign used for yard sales and local elections: MISSING — HANNAH ALBRECHT — AGE 8. A photograph. A face she did not look at because she was remembering—could not stop remembering—the specific resilience of a Fruit Roll-Up as it peeled away from its wax-paper backing, the way it resisted and then yielded, its surface tacky and bright and terrifyingly red, red like a thing that had decided to be red with its whole body, and the taste which was not the taste of fruit but the taste of *the letter F*, of the concept of fruit, of a committee's consensus regarding what fruit might taste like if fruit were a feeling rather than a thing—

She drove into Bellham as though washing ashore.

## II.

The diner was called Mitzi's, though there had never been a Mitzi, or perhaps there had been a Mitzi once and she had been metabolized by the town's general entropy, absorbed into the infrastructure the way all proper nouns in Bellham eventually were—street names that no longer corresponded to streets, business names that memorialized businesses two or three commercial deaths prior to the current occupant. The sign outside still worked, mostly, its neon script spelling M_TZI'S against the dusk, the missing letter lending it the appearance of a word caught in the act of forgetting itself.

Doug was sitting in the third booth from the door. She knew it was Doug because he looked exactly like Doug—which is to say, he looked like a man who had been engineered by a pragmatic God to fill out a polo shirt and operate a dental practice in suburban Connecticut, which was in fact what he did. He had not changed. He had merely *aged*, which was different, which was the application of time's finish to an underlying form that had always been, and would always be, Doug. He had a manila folder in front of him on the table. He had a manila folder. She nearly wept at the manila folder. Of course Doug had a manila folder. He had come to fight an interdimensional entity of unknowable malice and he had brought *documentation*.

"You're late," he said.

"I'm here," she said, and slid into the booth across from him, and the vinyl of the seat made a sound as she sat—a specific, slightly adhesive exhalation, the booth's upholstery greeting her weight with a wheeze of trapped air—and the sound was so precisely the sound of every diner booth she had ever sat in that it opened, in her chest, a window she had not known was there, and through that window came the booths of every diner in her personal history: the IHOP on Route 2 where her father took them after church, its syrup dispensers shaped like small cabins; the Friendly's where she had eaten her first Fribble, the word itself—*Fribble*—carrying within its two ridiculous syllables the entire phenomenology of 1988, of a period when ice cream could be a verb, when consumption was still innocent, or at least had not yet been diagnosed—

"Catherine."

"Yes."

"Have you been listening?"

She had not been listening. She had been—where? Friendly's. The Fribble. The plastic cup in which the Fribble arrived, its surface beaded with condensation that she would trace with one finger, drawing patterns in the moisture, writing words that evaporated even as she wrote them, a practice that now struck her as prophetic, as precisely the kind of preparatory exercise the universe might assign to a person who would spend her adult life in the business of preservation, of capturing in glass jars the season's last sweetness before it rotted—

"I'm listening," she said.

"The Hendersons' dog," Doug said. He opened the manila folder. Inside were printed photographs, printed on printer paper, the images slightly pixelated, slightly too dark, in the way of photographs printed by a man who had not updated his printer drivers since 2016. "Found in the storm drain on Kossuth Street. Or—parts of it. The parts that were left." He slid a photograph across the table. She glanced at it and immediately away, not because of what it showed—she registered only a blur of dark shapes against concrete—but because the Formica tabletop on which Doug's folder rested was the exact, the *molecularly identical* shade of beige that had covered every horizontal surface in her grandmother's kitchen: the counters, the table, the mysterious shelf above the refrigerator where things went to die—expired vitamins, a ceramic rooster with a chipped comb, a TV Guide from a month in which nothing had been on and which no one had gotten around to throwing away because in her grandmother's house nothing was thrown away, everything was *kept*, everything accrued, objects settled on surfaces and became features of those surfaces the way barnacles become features of a hull, and this, she now understood, was perhaps the origin of her own preservationist impulse, her jams, her orchard, her twelve-dollar jars of quince paste that Brooklyn food writers described as "luminous"—

"It ate then." Doug pushed another photograph across the table but she was looking at the coffee.

Her coffee had arrived. She did not remember ordering it, which either meant the waitress had brought it unbidden—a regional hospitality practice that was itself worthy of a digression she would not, for the moment, permit herself—or meant that she had ordered it during one of the intervals when her attention had been submerged in the beige of the table, which was both here and 1987, which was the counter in her grandmother's kitchen at 14 Birch Lane, where on Saturday mornings the percolator—avocado green, electric, its cord wrapped in a fabric sheath that frayed at the plug end—produced coffee from Folgers grounds stored in a red can, a can she could at this moment feel in her hand, its tin walls yielding slightly under thumb pressure, the plastic lid prying off with a sound like a small revelation, and inside: the grounds, brown-black, their smell rising to meet her face like a landscape seen from an airplane, vast and dark and organized by principles she could not, at eight years old, have named but which she now recognized as the principles of *commodity*: the bean grown somewhere equatorial by hands she had never been asked to imagine, shipped, roasted, ground, canned, trucked to a Shop 'n Save in central Maine where her grandmother bought it with a coupon clipped from the *Bellham Register* and carried it home in a paper bag and opened it in a kitchen where the light, on Saturday mornings, came through a window above the sink and fell across the counter in a quadrilateral of gold that moved, over the course of the morning, from the toaster to the knife block to the place on the counter where the red can sat—
