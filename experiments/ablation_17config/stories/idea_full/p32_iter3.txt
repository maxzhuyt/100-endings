# The Permits

---

You will want to know about the linoleum first, because the linoleum explains everything.

It was laid in 1923 — I remember the year because the men who laid it were paid twice in one afternoon, the price having changed between morning and lunch, and one of them said to me, *Paschke, we are laying floors on a sinking ship*, which I thought was an exaggeration but which turned out to be, if anything, modest. It was the dark green kind, flecked with something meant to look like marble but which looked instead like the skin of a trout, and it ran from the front entrance of the district housing office at Schönhauser Allee 147 all the way to the third-floor records room without interruption, excepting a patch near the second-floor landing where a radiator had leaked in — well, I am getting ahead of myself. The radiator is not important yet.

What I mean to say is that linoleum does not care who walks on it. This is its primary virtue. I have mopped that floor under four governments, five if you count the week in 1945 when there was no government at all and I mopped it anyway because the hallways were filthy and the absence of a state does not excuse filth. The nameplates on the office doors changed. The linoleum did not. Under the Weimar people it said BEZIRKSAMT FÜR WOHNUNGSWESEN on the second-floor suite. Under the National Socialists it said KREISAMT FÜR WOHNUNGSWESEN, and below that, for a period, a smaller sign I did not read because I had learned by then that reading signs carefully was a form of participation, and participation was something I preferred to avoid. Under the Soviets it was a room number only, no words, which was clarifying in its way. And now, under our German Democratic Republic, it says ABTEILUNG FÜR WOHNRAUMLENKUNG, which means the Department for the Direction of Living Space, which is a phrase I have considered many times while mopping and which I believe contains, in its five syllables, everything you need to know about this particular republic and its relationship to the people who live in it.

But you will say: Paschke, you are an old man, you are half deaf — this is true, the left ear is gone entirely, an artillery matter from the first war, not the second, and the right ear operates on a selective basis that my wife, when she was alive, called *suspiciously convenient* — you are a custodian, what do you know about the people in the offices? And I will say: I know everything. A custodian who mops forty years of hallways knows everything in the way a river knows its banks. I do not attend the meetings. I do not read the files. But I am present at six in the morning when the building is honest, when the desks are unattended and the wastebaskets have not yet been emptied, and I am present at seven in the evening when the last person leaves and the hallways hold the smell of what has happened in them all day — carbon paper, anxiety, the particular staleness of rooms where people have explained things to other people who were not listening.

There are two I must tell you about.

The first is Klaus Findeisen, who sat at the third desk in the second-floor suite, the desk nearest the window, though the window faced the courtyard and so provided nothing to look at except the courtyard, which contained a drainpipe and a bicycle that no one had claimed since 1956. Klaus was — I must find the right word, and the right word is not *careful*, though he was careful, and it is not *quiet*, though he was quiet. The right word is *correct*. Klaus Findeisen was a man who had made correctness into a kind of religion. Not the dangerous correctness of a zealot — I have known those; they announce themselves by the angle of their chins — but the correctness of a person who has understood, perhaps from childhood, that the world is a place where things go wrong, and that the only permissible response is to ensure that one's own small corner is in order. He filed in triplicate not because the regulation required it — it did, but that is beside the point — but because triplication was, for Klaus, a form of prayer. A thing that exists in three copies is three times as real. He arrived at seven-forty each morning. He left at five-eighteen each evening, the eighteen minutes being the time required to verify that his desk was clear, his forms were filed, and his chair was pushed in at the precise angle that indicated departure rather than temporary absence. He was thirty-four years old. He wore the same grey jacket every day. The elbows were beginning to shine.

The second is Renate Böhler, who sat two desks away, the desk near the filing cabinets, and who was — but here I must be more cautious, because to describe Renate is to describe something I am not sure I have the vocabulary for, and I am an old man who left school at fourteen and whose vocabulary was acquired not from books but from listening, which is a different kind of education, more thorough in some respects and full of holes in others.

Renate was not beautiful in the way that word is normally used, which is to say she would not have stopped you on the street. She had a broad face and heavy eyebrows and she wore her hair pulled back in a manner that was either practical or severe depending on whose eyes you were looking through. She was twenty-nine. She was, I believe, intelligent in the way that is most inconvenient for women in offices, which is that she was more intelligent than the people she worked for and had learned, as I had learned in my own sphere, to conceal this fact behind a surface of unremarkable competence. She processed housing allocation forms. She did it well. No one remarked on this because no one remarks on things that are done well; they remark on things that are done badly, and Renate gave them nothing to remark on.

What I noticed — and I say this with the authority of a man who has been observing human beings in institutional hallways since before most of them were born — was the following: Renate's posture changed when Klaus entered the room.

It was not dramatic. It was not the kind of thing a younger person would see. The shoulders went back perhaps a centimeter. The hand that had been resting on the desk moved to the paper in front of her, as if work were a form of composure. And there was something about the angle of her head — she did not look at him, that was the point, she very specifically did not look at him, with the kind of deliberate non-looking that is itself a form of attention so concentrated it could burn a hole in the wall.

I noticed. I always notice. It is the one skill that has transferred cleanly across all four — five — governments: the ability to see without being seen seeing. A custodian with a mop is invisible. This is the great secret of my profession. We are the walls. We are the linoleum. We hear what is said in rooms where the door has been closed but not latched, and we do not hear what is said in rooms where the door has been closed and latched, and we know the difference, and we keep the difference to ourselves, because keeping things to ourselves is how we have remained employed through four changes of nameplate while the ambitious people whose names were on those plates were, variously, promoted, arrested, shot, denazified, rehabilitated, re-arrested, and in one case appointed to a fisheries committee in Rostock, which I believe was considered worse than the shooting.

So. Klaus. Renate. The building. The linoleum. The summer of 1961.

I will tell you what happened.

---

It was the first of August, a Tuesday, and the heat was of the kind that makes government buildings into ovens because government buildings are designed by people who do not work in them and who therefore do not know that a south-facing corridor with sealed windows is, in August, an instrument of torture. I had opened the basement windows at five-thirty, which helped nothing but which gave me the feeling of having done something, which is, as I have learned, the primary function of most actions taken inside government buildings.

Klaus came in at his usual time, seven-forty, and I knew something was different because he went not to his desk but to the staircase, and he went down instead of up.

The permits section was in the basement.

I should explain. Our building housed, on its upper floors, the district housing office — the ABTEILUNG FÜR WOHNRAUMLENKUNG, with all its forms and registries and the filing cabinets that Renate tended like a gardener tends a large and unruly hedge. But in the basement, accessible by a separate entrance that smelled of must and pipe tobacco, there was a smaller office that processed transit permits, travel authorizations, and the various permissions that a citizen of the German Democratic Republic required in order to move his body from one location to another. The two offices shared a building but not a staircase, and the staircase was, I had always thought, a kind of metaphor, though I am not a man who uses that word often: the people who managed where you lived were upstairs, and the people who managed where you went were below, and the linoleum connected them whether they acknowledged the connection or not.

Klaus descended. I was mopping the basement corridor — there had been condensation again; the pipes in the basement were, like me, of an age where reliable function was no longer guaranteed — and I saw him approach the service window, which was the third window on the left, beneath a sign that said ANTRÄGE REISE- UND TRANSITGENEHMIGUNGEN in the particular shade of bureaucratic blue that I have come to think of as the color of the state's indifference.

Behind the window sat Frau Gessler. I knew Frau Gessler. Everyone in the building knew Frau Gessler, in the way that everyone in a body knows the location of a kidney stone. She was not unkind. I want to be precise about this because it is important. Frau Gessler was not unkind, in the same way that a turnstile is not unkind: she was a mechanism, and mechanisms do not have intentions, they have procedures, and Frau Gessler's procedures were, like the linoleum, unchanged by the passage of regimes, because the specific procedures changed but the *manner* of procedural adherence did not, and Frau Gessler's manner was that of a woman who had been told, at some formative moment, that the purpose of a rule is to be applied, and who had carried this revelation forward with the serene conviction of a person who has found the one true answer to the problem of human existence.

Klaus stood at the window. I mopped. I was perhaps four meters away, and the left ear, as I have mentioned, is useless, but the right ear is adequate at four meters, especially in a basement corridor where the stone walls produce a mild echo that, I have noticed, favors the higher registers of human speech, such as the register a man adopts when he is requesting something from an authority and wishes to convey that he is worthy of consideration without conveying that he considers himself entitled to it.

"I would like to inquire," Klaus said — and I record the word *inquire* because it was the word he used, not *request*, not *apply for*, but *inquire*, which is the word of a man who has been inside bureaucracy long enough to know that the first approach should always be tentative, that one should never ask for the thing directly but should instead ask whether the thing might, in principle, under certain conditions, be available for asking about — "I would like to inquire about the process for obtaining a one-day transit permit to West Berlin."

Frau Gessler's face did not change. I would not have expected it to. She reached below the counter — I heard the sound of a drawer, a familiar sound, the sound of forms being located, which is in government buildings what the sound of a cash register is in shops: the sound of a transaction beginning — and she produced a document that I could not read from my position but which I later learned was an informational sheet, not an application, because the application could not be released until certain preconditions were met, and the informational sheet existed to inform the applicant of the preconditions, which is to say it existed to inform the applicant that the application was not yet available to him, which is a sentence I have just said and which I believe is the foundational sentence of the German Democratic Republic.

"Category?" said Frau Gessler.

"Family visit," said Klaus. "My mother. She will be seventy on the twelfth of August. She lives on Dudenstraße. In — in the western sector."

He said *in the western sector* as if apologizing for his mother's geography, which I understood, because in those years the location of one's relatives was a matter not of accident but of suspicion, and a mother in the West was a fact that required management, like a chronic illness or a problematic uncle.

"Category III," said Frau Gessler. "Family visit, first degree, limited duration. You will need —" and here she recited, with the fluency of a woman who has said these words perhaps eight thousand times, a list of requirements that I will reproduce as best I can, though I may be conflating this list with other lists I have overheard at this window over the years, because the lists blend together in memory the way the individual tiles of the linoleum blend together into a single unbroken surface of institutional green.

The list was: a completed Form 7b — Application for Temporary Transit Authorization, in duplicate. A current residency confirmation, stamped by the district housing office. A workplace certification confirming employment and return obligation. A written declaration of purpose, specifying the date, duration, destination address, and nature of the visit. A photographic identification of specified dimensions — I believe she said three-by-four centimeters, though it may have been four-by-five; the dimensions, like much else, changed periodically and without announcement. And the signature of a departmental supervisor, which could be obtained only after the other documents were assembled, since the supervisor could not sign what did not yet exist, and which yet was listed as a prerequisite for the assembly of the other documents, a circularity that Frau Gessler recited without any indication that she was aware it was circular, or perhaps with the indication that she was aware and had long since made her peace with circularity as a governing principle.

Klaus listened. I watched him listen. His head moved in small, precise nods, the nods of a man who is accustomed to receiving procedural information and filing it mentally in the correct order. He did not protest. He did not express surprise, frustration, or dismay. He said: "The residency confirmation — that would be stamped by my own office? I work in the housing office. Upstairs."

"The residency confirmation must be stamped by the district housing office, yes," said Frau Gessler.

"I work there," said Klaus again, as if repetition might introduce this fact into the procedure where it had not previously existed.

"The form is available from the residency registry," said Frau Gessler. "Third floor."

"I know the registry. I — " He stopped. He began again. "I process allocation forms. I have never seen a residency confirmation form for transit purposes."

"It is Form 12c," said Frau Gessler. "It is available from the residency registry."

"I will look into it," said Klaus.

"The processing time for a complete application is ten to fourteen working days," said Frau Gessler. "Sincerest wishes for your mother's birthday."

This last sentence was, I believe, genuine. Frau Gessler was not unkind. The mechanism wished him well. The mechanism could not help him, but it wished him well, and in the German Democratic Republic this was, I had learned, the most that one could generally expect from one's encounters with the state: the state could not help you, but some of its individual components, the human ones, the ones with faces and memories and mothers of their own, wished you well, and the wishing was real even though the helping was not.

Klaus went back upstairs. I wrung out my mop. I followed some minutes later, not because I was following him — I had the second floor to do; it was on the schedule — but because the building had a single staircase on the east side and the staircase went where the staircase went.

When I reached the second floor, Klaus was at his desk. He had not removed his jacket, which was unusual. He was looking at a piece of paper that I assumed was the informational sheet. Renate was at her desk, two desks away, processing a form. She was not looking at him.
