# The Peacemaker's Trap

## I.

The driveway was a quarter mile of packed ice, and the Subaru fishtailed twice on the way up. Marco Medina drove with one hand, the other gesturing at trees he apparently expected me to find impressive. "White pines," he said. "Whole ridge is white pine. My mother had a thing." He said *had* in the way Americans said it about the living — casually, without conjugating the loss.

I had been in the country four months. Long enough to understand most English, short enough to still528 hear it as a sequence of deliberate choices. In Finnish, we would not say someone *had a thing*. We would say what the thing was. But Marco's family, I was learning, made an art of inexactitude.

The estate emerged through the pines in stages. First the main house, a sprawling timber-and-stone structure with a wraparound porch that sagged at its eastern corner as if the house were shrugging one shoulder. Smoke from two chimneys. Every window lit. Wreaths on the door, the garage, the wellhouse — wreaths made of real balsam, I noted, not the plastic ones, though the balsam had browned in a way that suggested they'd been hung weeks early, perhaps by someone who wanted to ensure perfection and instead got decay.

Marco pulled alongside three other cars already dusted with new snow and killed the engine. "Welcome to Christmas," he said, then added, under his breath and perhaps not for me, "God help us all."

The front door opened before we reached it, releasing a rectangle of yellow light and the competing frequencies of at least two conversations, a television playing football, and something by Bing Crosby. A woman I didn't know shouted, "Marco, you're late, and I don't forgive you," and then laughed as though lateness and forgiveness were both theoretical constructs she found amusing.

I carried my bag and Marco's second bag. This was understood, in the dynamics of hospitality, as my contribution — the exchange student earns her stay through small labors. I did not mind. It gave me a reason to move slowly, to stand on the porch an extra moment while Marco went inside to embrace the woman who did not forgive him.

That is when I looked north along the ridge.

There were four other cabins visible from the porch — three to the north, staggered along the slope at fifty-meter intervals, and one to the south, set farther back in the pines. All of them were dark. This was not unusual in itself. Vacation properties in the Adirondacks were often seasonal, often empty. But it was December twenty-third, and these cabins were *decorated*. The nearest one had a string of lights along its eave, unplugged but still hanging, and through the window I could see the silhouette of a small tree on a table. A wreath on the door. A sled propped against the railing. The next cabin had stockings visible through a side window, three of them, hung but slack, and a table set — I could see the white circles of plates through the glass, four place settings, as if the family had stepped out between courses and never returned.

No tire tracks. No footprints. No smoke.

It was the kind of detail I might not have noticed if I had been from here, if these mountains meant something personal to me, if my attention had been absorbed by the emotional gravitational field of the house behind me. But I was from Joensuu, and nothing here was mine, and so I saw what was there: a ridge of cabins dressed for a holiday that no one had come to celebrate.

"Lotta!" Marco called from inside. "Come in, you're letting the cold in."

I picked up the bags and went in. But I looked back once more. The southern cabin had a generator beside it, and even from the porch, in the silence between gusts, I could hear it running.

## II.

The Medina-Connollys arrived the way weather arrives — in fronts, with pressure changes you could feel before you could name.

Marco's sister Dana came first, forty minutes after us, with her husband, whose name was either Glenn or Grant (I heard both, from different people, and was never sure which was correct, and eventually it seemed too late to ask). They had three children between the ages of six and twelve who entered the house like a breaking wave and immediately colonized the den. Dana embraced Marco with her eyes closed, held him an extra beat, then said, "You look tired," which I understood, even then, was not about rest.

Then the uncles. Pete arrived alone, though his wife, Karen, was expected later. He was a thick-necked man in an unzipped parka who walked through the front door like he was repossessing the place. He went directly to the kitchen, opened three cabinets before finding the bourbon, and poured himself four fingers without ice. He did not acknowledge me. He barely acknowledged Marco. He stood at the kitchen island and surveyed the preparations that someone had already begun — the roasting pans, the defrosting turkey, the ranked spice jars — with an expression I recognized from my father's face when he inspected a repaired fence, looking for the flaw.

Douglas arrived next, with his partner, Neil, and a cooler full of oysters. Douglas was the youngest of what I now understood to be five siblings — Marco, Dana, Pete, Douglas, and one more I hadn't met. He was loud and immediate and took my hand in both of his and said, "You must be Lotta, Marco told us about you, I'm sorry in advance for all of this." Neil smiled behind him with the particular patience of a person who has learned to find his partner's family anthropologically interesting.

Lisa, the last sibling, came with the snow. By the time her rented SUV climbed the driveway, the flurries had become a steady, determined fall, and she emerged from the car looking like she had been personally offended by the weather. Her teenage son, Alex, stayed in the passenger seat with headphones on until Lisa knocked on his window. He unfolded himself — he was tall, in the way of boys still surprised by their own height — and looked at the house with an expression I can only describe as practiced indifference. Lisa was thin and angular and embraced no one. She said, "The road is a nightmare," and this became the first collective topic — weather as neutral ground, the one subject on which no Medina-Connolly had a grievance.

By five o'clock the house held nineteen people, including partners, children, and me. I was given a room on the third floor, a converted attic space with a sloped ceiling and a twin bed that smelled of cedar and disuse. "Grandma's sewing room," Marco explained. "Make yourself at home." He said this with genuine kindness, and left, and I sat on the bed and listened to the house beneath me — the layered noise of a family assembling, the specific acoustic signature of people who are performing closeness while negotiating distance.

I should say something about the matriarch, since her absence was the most present thing in the house. Helen Medina had died in March. The estate was hers — built by her husband, maintained by her will, held together by her gravitational force. I knew this because Marco had explained it during the drive, in fragments, the way you explain a country's history to a foreigner: selectively, and with the embarrassment of someone who knows the full version is worse. Helen had hosted Christmas on this ridge for thirty-one years. This would be the first one without her. The reunion had been contested — some siblings wanted to skip it, others insisted — and the compromise, if it was a compromise, was that they would come but begin the process of "figuring out the house." I understood this to mean dividing the inheritance. I understood, also, that this was the thing everyone was thinking about and no one was saying.

Helen's objects were everywhere and untouched. Her reading glasses on the mantel. Her coat on the hook by the back door — a red barn coat with a torn pocket that no one had moved in nine months. A handwritten list on the refrigerator: *eggs, lamp oil, call Pete back*. Call Pete back. As if she might still do it. As if someone had left the list there as an accusation or a prayer.

I was cataloguing these details from the stairwell, trying to be unobtrusive, when I heard a voice below me say, "You must be Marco's exchange student."

I turned. The woman on the landing was perhaps sixty, with short silver hair and a fleece vest over a flannel shirt. She carried a clipboard. She was smiling with her whole face — not the performative smile of the other Medina-Connollys, who smiled as a defensive posture, but a smile that seemed to arrive from actual warmth.

"I'm Sheryl," she said. "I'm not a Medina or a Connolly. I married in and stayed after Charles didn't." She said this without bitterness, as a simple fact, the way you might say you lived on a particular street. "You're Lotta?"

"Yes."

"Finnish?"

"Yes."

"I spent a week in Helsinki once, in the nineties. It was dark the whole time and everyone was very honest with me. I loved it." She looked at her clipboard. "I've put you at the adults' table for dinner tomorrow. You're seventeen, right? Close enough. Unless you'd rather sit with the kids. No judgment."

"The adults' table is fine."

"Good. Also, the hot water takes about ninety seconds to arrive in the third-floor bathroom. Don't let it fool you. It's coming." She touched my arm briefly and moved past me down the stairs, already calling to someone in the kitchen about the turkey brine.

Here is what I want to convey, and what I could not have articulated at the time: within the first two hours of the reunion, I watched Sheryl defuse three separate conflicts with the precision of someone disarming the same bomb over and over. The first was a dispute over rooms — Lisa wanted the master bedroom because she had the worst drive, Pete wanted it because he was the oldest present (being older than Marco), and Dana's husband (Glenn or Grant) had already put their bags in it. Sheryl resolved this by producing a schedule she'd made in advance, based on a rotation Helen had apparently used for years, which happened to give the master bedroom to Dana's family this year. The schedule was typed. It had a header: *Mom's Christmas Assignments — Updated 2024*. Whether Helen had actually ever made such a schedule or whether Sheryl had created it whole seemed, in the moment, irrelevant. It worked. It was a document, and documents have authority.

The second conflict was between Pete and Douglas over the oysters. Pete, who had begun his second bourbon, announced that oysters were "not a Medina tradition," and Douglas said they were "a *good* tradition," and the air between them compressed in a way that suggested the oysters were not about oysters. Sheryl stepped between them — physically, not metaphorically — and said, "Pete, your mother served oysters in 2019, I have a photo, and Douglas, we'll do them as a separate appetizer so nobody's committed. Everyone eats what they want. This is America." Both men retreated, which I think surprised both of them.

The third conflict was so small I almost missed it. Lisa said something cutting to Marco about the drive, implying he'd chosen the arrival time to inconvenience her. Marco's jaw tightened. Before he could respond, Sheryl appeared with a mug of coffee made exactly the way Lisa apparently liked it — oat milk, no sugar — and said, "Lisa, this house missed you." That was all. Lisa took the coffee and the moment dissolved.

By evening, I understood the family's internal model: Sheryl was the one who held it together. Sheryl remembered the schedules, the allergies, the grudges, the birthdays, the coffee preferences. Sheryl was not Helen — no one could be Helen — but Sheryl was the closest thing to a functioning center this family had. I liked her. I want to be clear about that. I liked her immediately, and I think she intended for me to.

## III.

The KitchenAid was a copper-finish Professional 600, mounted on the island counter like a small industrial monument. I know this because I was sitting at the breakfast bar when it happened, eating an apple and reading a Finnish novel on my phone, and the machine was directly in my line of sight.

It was the morning of December twenty-fourth. Six people were in the kitchen in various states of preparation — Pete's wife, Karen, who had arrived late the night before and was now asserting her presence through aggressive volunteerism; Dana, who was managing the turkey; Douglas, who was doing something with cranberries; Marco, who was on his third coffee and not helping; and Neil, who was dicing celery with the focus of a man who has learned that kitchens are the safest room during family events.

Pete came in to make a sandwich. He was in everyone's way. Karen told him to move. He said he lived here — which was not true in any legal or practical sense — and she said, "You *summered* here, Pete, don't be grandiose," and someone laughed, and Pete's face went red in the specific way that precedes either a joke or a detonation.

It was at this moment that Karen turned on the KitchenAid.

She had loaded it with the mashed potato preparation — boiled Yukon Golds, butter, cream, the works. Eighteen pounds of it, Dana said later, though in the moment it seemed like more. Karen set it to what she later described as "stir" — the lowest setting, a gentle rotation. What happened was not stir.

The mixer erupted.

I don't know how else to describe the physics. The beater engaged at what must have been maximum speed, and the bowl — which should have been locked — jumped its mount. The entire mass of potatoes became, for approximately three seconds, airborne. The spray pattern was radial, explosive, and targeted with a specificity that felt, even in the chaos, architectural. Pete, who was standing directly across the island, took the primary impact. The potatoes hit him in the chest, the neck, the face. A secondary wave caught Karen's arm and the backsplash behind the stove. A tertiary spray reached the ceiling, where it clung in pale stalactites.

Pete stood motionless for a long second. Potato dripped from his eyebrows. A piece of Yukon Gold slid down his collar.

Then the kitchen erupted louder than the mixer had.

Karen screamed — not in fear but in fury, the scream of a woman who has been humiliated in the precise territory where she intended to be competent. Pete said, "What the *fuck*, Karen," and Karen said, "I set it to *one*," and Pete said, "You obviously didn't set it to one," and Karen said, "Don't tell me what I did," and Dana said, "The bowl wasn't locked, someone didn't lock the bowl," and Douglas, from the far counter, said, very quietly, "This isn't about the bowl."

He was right. It was not about the bowl.

The argument that followed lasted eleven minutes. I know this because I timed it, not out of cruelty but out of the habit I had developed in this country of measuring things I could not otherwise understand. In those eleven minutes, the following alliances shifted: Karen and Pete, who had entered the kitchen as a married unit, exited it as opposing litigants. Karen accused Pete of sabotaging her; Pete accused Karen of incompetence. Dana attempted to mediate, failed, and ended up aligned with Karen against Pete, which reopened a grievance between Pete and Dana dating to, I gathered, their mother's funeral. Marco tried to leave the kitchen and was blocked by Lisa, who had arrived in the doorway to spectate and who told Marco that his "pathological avoidance" was "the reason this family can't have one fucking Christmas."

It was Sheryl, of course, who ended it.

She came in from the mudroom with a bag of backup potatoes — *backup potatoes*, as if she had anticipated this specific disaster — and said, "Okay. New potatoes in forty minutes. Everyone out of the kitchen except me and whoever wants to peel. No discussion of fault until we've all eaten something. House rules."

There were no house rules. Sheryl had invented them on the spot. But the family obeyed, because the family had been obeying Sheryl for years, and because obedience to a competent woman with a plan is the path of least resistance when you are covered in potato.

I stayed at the breakfast bar. Either I was too peripheral to be included in "everyone out" or Sheryl had forgotten me. I picked up a peeler. She looked at me, surprised, then smiled. "Finnish efficiency," she said. "I knew I liked you."
