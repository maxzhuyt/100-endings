# The Amber House

## I.

By the eighteenth month the plaster dust had gotten into everything. It silvered the insides of the perfume bottles that still lined the eastern corridor in their oak-and-glass cabinet, so that what had once been attar of rose and civet paste and a nameless extract shipped from a city that no longer existed now smelled only of powdered calcium and horsehair, the skeleton of the walls expressing itself in the medium of scent. Madame Lešić, who had been the House's parfumeuse before she became its cook, kept the cabinet locked anyway. She said the dust was itself a kind of perfume. She said it was the smell of the building remembering what it was made of.

The fresco in the main salon — a panorama of bodies, sixty or seventy of them, interleaved in a composition so intricate it seemed less like a depiction of ecstasy than a proof of some geometric theorem about the possible orientations of limbs — had been bisected by a mortar crack that ran from the northeast corner of the ceiling to the baseboard beneath the cold fireplace. The crack had the precision of an editorial intervention. It split a woman's painted thigh from the painted hand that rested on it and separated, by three centimeters of exposed lathe, two mouths that had been touching for over a hundred years. On bad nights, when the shelling came from the eastern hills, new tributaries crept from the main fissure and the figures seemed to be slowly pulling apart from one another, as if the fresco were not cracking but exhaling — as if those sixty painted bodies were finally, after a century of frozen congress, releasing one another from their embrace.

No one slept in the main salon anymore. The seven who remained slept in the interior rooms, the ones without windows, the ones that had been designed for a different kind of privacy. None of them called these rooms what they had been called in the years when the Amber House was what it was. They called them by number, or by the color of the remaining wallpaper, or by the name of whoever had claimed the mattress. But the rooms remembered their own purposes the way a body remembers an injury: structurally, involuntarily. The walls of the Blue Room were still upholstered in moleskin, for sound. The Linden Room still had iron rings set into the ceiling joists at precise intervals, though now they held drying herbs and a line of laundry. The room they called the Library — because someone, years before the siege, had papered its walls with pages torn from books in six languages — still locked from the outside.

The shelling had its own calendar. From the east it came in the early mornings, three or four rounds before dawn, percussive and almost metronomic, the artillerists on that side having settled into the rhythm of bureaucrats performing a duty they no longer believed in but could not be seen to neglect. From the west it came unpredictably — sometimes nothing for days, then a concentrated barrage at midday that shook mortar from the joints and sent the refugees into the interior rooms where they sat with their backs against the moleskin walls and breathed through their mouths and did not speak. The two armies were not fighting each other. They had ceased to be interested in each other months ago. What they were fighting, to the extent that they articulated it at all, were the borders of two collapses — two states in the late stages of ceasing to exist, contracting like drying hides, and the Amber House sat precisely on the line where both edges met. It belonged to no country. It belonged to no army. For forty years before the war it had belonged to everyone — to anyone who could pay, or who was beautiful enough to be paid for, or who simply arrived on a Thursday night when the music was playing and the doors were open and the border was just a word for the place where one kind of pleasure ended and another began.

Now it belonged to the seven, and to the dead.

Madame Lešić kept a garden in the courtyard, in the shelter of what had been the fountain. The fountain's centerpiece — a marble satyr whose expression suggested less lust than a kind of tender, goatish confusion — had lost its head to a fragment in the fourth month. She planted beans in the basin. The water had been diverted long ago to a filtration system that Dražen, the engineer, had built from copper bathtub fittings and a repurposed still. The still had been used, in the old days, to produce a plum brandy that the House served in thimble-sized glasses after certain encounters — encounters that Madame Lešić, in her former role, had scented with specific blends designed to be smelled later, in other places, and to summon the memory of what had happened here with the force of a hand on the back of the neck.

The dust had gotten into the brandy, too. What remained of it tasted like chalk and longing.

The Amber House was not amber. It was the color of all old buildings in this part of the world — a faded ochre that the rain had streaked with grey and the shelling had pocked with white craters of exposed stone, so that from the outside, if anyone had been outside to look, it resembled a face marked by some beautiful and disfiguring disease. In the old language, the one spoken before either empire claimed the region, *amber* did not refer to the color. It referred to the resin — the substance that preserves what it traps, that makes permanent what was supposed to be temporary. The House had been named not for how it looked but for what it did. People came here and what they did here was held. The walls knew. The moleskin remembered the sounds. The parfumeuse remembered the scents. And the Chronicler — the Chronicler remembered everything.

## II.

She read in the mornings, before anyone else woke, in the small room off the kitchen that had been the accounts office and was now hers. She read the way she had read for thirty years: with her fingers, the pads moving across the pages with the deliberateness of someone tuning an instrument by touch. The pages were heavy. They were made from a cotton-rag stock that the House had once commissioned from a mill in the south, thick enough to hold the impressions she pressed into them with a stylus of carved bone — a script of her own devising, each character a cluster of raised dots and short scored lines, somewhere between Braille and cuneiform but older in feel than either, as if she had reached past the known systems of tactile writing and found something more archaeological.

The ledger itself was the size of a family Bible. Its cover was calfskin, darkened by decades of handling to a brown so deep it was almost black, and the spine had been rebound twice — once by a bookbinder in the capital who had not asked what was inside, and once by the Chronicler herself, using linen thread and a sailmaker's needle, working by touch in the dark because for her it was always dark and had been since the age of nine, when the fever came and took everything except her hands.

Her name was Zara. She was called the Chronicler because that was what she did, and because names, in the Amber House, had always been provisional. Clients came with their names and left them at the door the way they left their coats. The House gave them other names — temporary names, names that lasted only as long as the encounter — and Zara recorded these names alongside the other things she recorded, so that her ledger was a register of ghosts: hundreds of people identified not by who they were in the world but by who they became in the House's interior, where the rules were different and the self was something that could be laid on a table and examined by candlelight.

What she recorded was specific. Not the acts themselves — those were, in her estimation, banal in their gross mechanics, endlessly repetitive, a limited alphabet — but the *quality of want* that the acts expressed. The hesitation before a touch and where it hesitated. The words spoken involuntarily, the ones that escaped from behind whatever the client had constructed to contain them. The particular way a body oriented itself in the moments before and after — toward the wall, toward the partnering body, toward the door. She had been, in the House's great years, its most essential and least visible functionary: a blind woman who sat in adjacent rooms, who was led through the corridors by Madame Lešić or by one of the others, who listened and smelled and felt the vibrations of the floor and the walls and who later, alone, transcribed what she had perceived into her raised script with the bone stylus, pressing each character into the heavy paper with the care of someone setting jewels.

She did not sell this information. She did not use it for blackmail or leverage, though the contents of the ledger would have brought down governments — had, in fact, survived the fall of two. She recorded because recording was what she did. She was a calculus of other people's need. And over the decades, reading and rereading her own entries, tracing the patterns that emerged across hundreds of encounters, she had arrived at a conviction that she shared rarely and that, when shared, was received with the particular silence that greets either profundity or madness:

That the shape of a person's desire predicted the shape of their death.

Not metaphorically. Not in the vague, symbolic way that a horoscope predicts — not *you will face a challenge* or *love will find you*. Specifically. Structurally. She maintained that the man who wanted to be held from behind and spoken to as a child would die in an enclosed space, facing away from what killed him. That the woman who wanted to be looked at but not touched would die of something she saw coming and could not avoid. That the syntax of lust — the precise sequence of approach, escalation, climax, withdrawal — was a rehearsal, a template, a map drawn decades before the territory became real. She could read a person's entry in the ledger and describe, she claimed, the spatial and procedural architecture of the death that awaited them.

The others treated her the way you treat a priest whose theology you do not share but whose discipline you cannot dismiss — with a deference that was partly respect, partly fear, and partly the pragmatic instinct of people under siege who cannot afford to alienate anyone who claims to understand the structure of what is killing them.

In the mornings, before anyone else woke, she read.

Her fingers moved across the entries for each of the seven refugees. These were newer entries, the raised dots still sharp-edged, the scored lines still deep enough to catch a fingernail. They had not been made from observations through moleskin walls; they had been made from close attention in shared spaces — the kitchen where they ate, the courtyard where they waited out the shelling, the interior rooms where they slept in configurations dictated by fear and cold and the animal need to hear another body breathing. She had learned them the way she had learned everyone: by the evidence of their want.

Dražen, the engineer: a man who desired control but craved its removal. She had heard it in the way he ate — meticulously, obsessively, every portion measured — and in the way he slept, which was with his arms uncovered, stretched above his head, wrists exposed, a posture of such ambient surrender it contradicted everything about his waking self.

Madame Lešić: a woman whose desire had migrated from the body to the task. She wanted to be needed. She performed need-meeting — the cooking, the tending, the rationing — with a fervor that had a familiar cadence, the cadence of someone who had once performed other services with the same thoroughness and who had simply changed the content without altering the form.

Lejla, who was seventeen and had been born in the first year of the war and knew nothing else: she wanted to be surprised. This was the cruelest desire Zara had ever recorded, because in a siege, surprise is always lethal.

Tomislav, the professor of dead languages who had been stranded here while researching an etymology and who had stayed because the alternative was the road: he wanted to be forgiven, though for what, Zara had not yet determined.

Safet, the musician, whose instrument — a sevdah — had been crushed in the ninth month and who now played nothing and spoke rarely and who wanted, Zara knew, simply to be left alone, which in her taxonomy was not the absence of desire but its most concentrated form: the desire for a self unwitnessed, which was the desire for a kind of death.

And the seventh — the woman who had arrived five weeks ago, on foot, from the western side, with nothing but a canvas bag and a letter she never showed anyone and a way of entering a room that Zara had recognized immediately, the way a musician recognizes a chord: she entered rooms as if she already knew their dimensions. As if she had been in them before. As if the Amber House were not a refuge she had stumbled upon but a destination she had been traveling toward for a long time.

Her name, she said, was Aida.

Zara had entered her in the ledger with the bone stylus, pressing slowly, feeling each character form under the pressure. She had recorded the way Aida listened — too carefully, with the specific attentiveness of someone cataloguing. She had recorded the way Aida moved through the House — not the tentative movements of a newcomer but the proprietary movements of someone returning. She had recorded the way Aida smelled: like road dust and soap and, beneath both, something Zara recognized from thirty years of proximity to the mechanisms of desire — the flat, mineral scent of someone who is performing composure over something enormous.

Zara did not yet know what Aida wanted. This was unusual. After thirty years, Zara could typically read a new body within days. Aida's want was either absent — which was impossible, in Zara's cosmology — or concealed with a skill that Zara had encountered only a few times and only in professionals: people who were trained to separate their interior from their surface, the way a smuggler separates a bag's false bottom from its true one.

In the mornings, before anyone else woke, Zara read her ledger, and she paused at Aida's entry, and she traced the sparse characters she had managed so far, and she listened to the sound the House made in the early hours — the building settling, the plaster adjusting, the creak of lathe and timber — and she waited. Not for information. For the specific vibration in the structure of events that would tell her the shape of what was coming. She was, in her own understanding, not a prophet. She was a reader. The text was already written. It was always already written. You simply had to know how to feel the characters.

## III.

The shell that killed Safet arrived at 3:17 in the afternoon on a Tuesday, and it arrived in the way that a sentence arrives — already complete, its grammar fully formed by the time it reaches the ear.

He had been in the Linden Room. This was known because that was where they found him, or what the shell left of him, which was less than it should have been — the round had been small-bore, precise, a sniper's weapon scaled up to artillery, the kind of targeted munition that neither army had used in the general bombardment. The general bombardment was composed of old Soviet-era ordnance that fell in wide, sloppy ellipses and destroyed things incidentally, the way a drunk destroys a room. This was different. This had come through the west-facing window of the Linden Room — the only window in that room, narrow, barred, a window designed to let in light but not sight, so that what happened inside the Linden Room could be illuminated but not observed — and it had detonated at a height and position consistent with the iron ring from which Safet had been hanging his laundry.

Dražen, who understood munitions the way he understood plumbing and wiring and the structural tolerances of old buildings — which is to say, technically, fluently, without emotion — said it was a direct-fire round, probably a recoilless rifle, aimed and not lobbed. He said this meant line of sight. He said this meant someone had been looking at that window and had known what was behind it and had fired not at the building but at the room.

The others received this information in the way they received all information now: at a slight delay, as if it had to pass through a membrane of fatigue before it could register. Madame Lešić crossed herself and began boiling water for tea, which was her response to everything. Lejla sat against the wall with her knees drawn up and her eyes very wide and did not cry because she had been alive for the entire war and crying was a skill she had never had occasion to develop.
