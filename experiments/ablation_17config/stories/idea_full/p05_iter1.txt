# The Carrera Austral

## I.

The email arrived on a Tuesday in March, which was the day I usually reorganized the supply closet at the clinic — replacing tape rolls, counting cold packs, dating bags of paraffin wax — and I mention this because I want you to understand the smallness of the life into which the email fell. Like a stone dropped into a teacup.

The subject line read: *getting the band back together (not a joke) (ok sort of a joke)*.

It was from Tomás.

I hadn't heard from Tomás Guerrero-Hahn in four years, since a conference in Santiago where he'd appeared at my panel on rotator cuff rehabilitation in masters-level swimmers, sat in the back row, and afterward said, "Lena, you look exactly the same," which was a kindness I did not deserve and did not correct. Before that, it had been another three years. Before that, the silence had been so complete I once caught myself checking his name against an obituary database, a thing I will never admit to anyone.

The email was long. Tomás writes like he used to run: in bursts of unsustainable speed followed by long lateral digressions. The gist of it, once I'd parsed the exclamation marks and stripped out the links to YouTube videos of Patagonian river crossings (some of which I watched, and one of which made me close my laptop and stand in my kitchen for a full minute with both hands on the counter), was this: the team was entering the Carrera Austral. All five of them. Ages forty-four through forty-nine. And they wanted me.

Not wanted. Needed. Tomás drew the distinction himself, with the precision of a man who has had a decade to learn which word keeps people from hanging up. "We need you, Lena. Specifically your hands, your tape, and your willingness to watch us destroy ourselves and only say I told you so with your eyebrows."

I read the email three times. Then I opened the supply closet and finished counting the paraffin wax.

I should tell you about their bodies.

Not as they imagine them, or as some race photographer last captured them in 2011, mid-stride on a ridgeline in the Dolomites, faces obscured by sun visors and suffering, all sinew and purpose. I should tell you about their bodies as I know them, which is to say: as tissue, as accumulated compromise, as a record of every bargain each of them has struck with time since I last put my hands on them.

Start with Tomás, because Tomás is easiest. He was always easiest. His left Achilles tendon underwent a partial rupture in 2014 — he told me this over the phone, the one time we spoke that year, describing it with the breezy inaccuracy of a man who has decided his body is someone else's problem. "It's fine, it just pops sometimes." It is not fine. Three weeks before the race, when I finally examined him in person in a rented physiotherapy suite in Coyhaique, I found what I expected: a tendon that had healed thickened and fibrotic, approximately forty percent wider than the contralateral side, with a palpable nodule at the midportion that rolled under my thumb like a marble buried in cable. His calf had atrophied to compensate. He walked with his foot externally rotated eleven degrees — I measured — which meant his knee tracked laterally with every step, which meant his ITB was taut as a bowstring, which meant his hip was doing work his ankle had resigned from. I pressed my thumb into the nodule and he said, "That's just my personality now." I did not laugh. I mapped the adhesion with both hands and felt, beneath the scar tissue, the ghost of the tendon he'd once had — the one I'd taped before a hundred races, elastic and alive, the tendon of a man who did not yet know what a body could refuse to do.

Tomás at forty-six: six foot one, seventy-nine kilograms, beard going white at the jaw. Still handsome in the way of men who were once beautiful and have relaxed into something kinder. His lumbar spine had compressed. He'd lost almost two centimeters of height since 2009, and I could feel the dehydration in his discs when I palpated the spinous processes — instead of the springy resistance of healthy nucleus pulposus, there was a flatness, a stiffness, as though someone had replaced the fluid between his vertebrae with compressed felt. He'd always had exceptional proprioception, a body that knew where it was in space with the confidence of a cat, and this had not left him entirely. He could still balance on one leg with his eyes closed. He just couldn't do it without his face going tight around the mouth.

Santi I assessed next. Santiago Herrera, whom I have known since he was twenty-three and who is the only patient who has ever made me leave the room to collect myself — not because of his injury, which was a tibial stress fracture in 2010, unremarkable in its mechanism, but because of the sound he made when I told him he couldn't race. It was not a cry. It was lower than that. It came from somewhere in his diaphragm, and it was the sound of a man whose identity had just been removed from his body like a bone from a fish.

Santi at forty-four: five foot nine, eighty-three kilograms, up from the seventy he'd raced at. The weight sat in his midsection and thighs. His shoulders, which had once been the narrowest on the team — a source of relentless and affectionate mockery — were now the broadest, thickened by years of compensatory upper-body training after the stress fracture healed badly and he'd decided, unconsciously, that his legs could no longer be trusted with the full project of locomotion. His right tibia, when I ran my fingers along the medial border, still had a palpable callus at the old fracture site, a ridge of remodeled bone that his body had laid down like a seam in metal. The bone was stronger there now than it had ever been. This is the paradox of fracture: what breaks and heals becomes, at the site of breaking, harder than what never broke at all. But the muscles around it — the posterior tibialis, the soleus, the deep flexors that stabilize the ankle in rough terrain — had never fully reactivated. He ran like a man carrying a package he was afraid to drop. The package was his right leg.

He'd also developed a slight tremor in his left hand. He noticed me noticing. "It's nothing," he said. "Caffeine."

It was not caffeine. But I made a note, and I said nothing, and I moved on.

Dag. Dagfinn Larsen, the Norwegian, the quietest of them, whom I always assessed last in the old days because he required the least management and the most patience. Dag did not describe symptoms. Dag waited for you to find them.

Dag at forty-nine: six foot three, eighty-one kilograms, still the leanest of the group. He had the body of a man who had never stopped moving but had lowered the intensity to a simmer — years of cross-country skiing, I guessed, and was right. His cardiovascular base would be the best on the team. But his shoulders told a different story. The right rotator cuff had a partial-thickness tear, either supraspinatus or infraspinatus, probably both; I could feel the crepitus when I passively abducted the arm past ninety degrees, a sound like someone slowly crumpling a paper bag inside his joint. He hadn't been to a doctor about it. He had, instead, done what Dag always did with pain: incorporated it into his daily architecture, routing his movement around it the way water routes around a stone. He reached for things with his left hand now. He pulled on jackets right-arm-first. He slept, I would come to learn, exclusively on his left side, cradling the injured shoulder against his chest like something he was protecting from the world.

"Can you paddle?" I asked. There would be river stages.

"I can paddle," he said.

"For seventy kilometers?"

He looked at me with those pale Norwegian eyes, the ones that had always made me feel like I was being assessed by a fjord. "I will paddle."

I wrote in my notes: *Right shoulder: significant. Will not report pain. Monitor closely. Bring extra kinesiology tape, the good kind, the German one.*

And then there was Ines. And then there was Maren. And I must tell you about them separately, because their bodies were always separate problems, but I must also tell you about them together, because their bodies were never, in all the years I knew them, truly separate.

Ines Darrieux at forty-seven: five foot six, sixty-four kilograms, with the dense, compact musculature of a woman who had once been the strongest pound-for-pound climber on the adventure racing circuit and who now — I could feel it in the first thirty seconds of assessment, in the way her trapezius fibers stood taut as bridge cables from her cervicothoracic junction to her acromion — carried every unfinished thing in her body like luggage she had never set down. She still led with her shoulders when she entered a room. This had always been true. Ines moved through doorways the way a boxer enters a ring: chin tucked slightly, center of gravity forward, as though whatever waited on the other side would need to be met with structural readiness. But now there was a pause. A fractional hesitation at the threshold that I noticed the first time she walked into the suite in Coyhaique, half a second in which her body gathered itself, as though the act of entering a room had become a decision rather than an event. She would have blown through that doorway at thirty. At forty-seven, she paused, and the pause told me more than her intake form did.

Her left knee was the primary issue, of course. ACL reconstruction in 2015, patellar tendon autograft, performed in Lyon by a surgeon whose work I recognized by its signature: the graft was well-placed, the tunnels clean, the fixation solid. The knee was stable. It had healed well. And it had changed everything. The quadriceps on the left side had rebuilt unevenly, the vastus medialis lagging behind the lateralis, which meant her patella tracked slightly laterally under load, which meant her knee ached in a specific weather-sensitive way that she described as "nothing" and I classified as chondromalacia patellae, early-stage, progressive, the kind of thing that doesn't end your career in one dramatic moment but erodes it like water on limestone, slowly, until one day you realize the shape of the stone has changed entirely.

She had compensated by developing an extraordinary gluteal firing pattern on the left side — her body's attempt to protect the knee by rerouting the work — and the result was visible: a slight asymmetry in her stride, the left hip dropping two or three degrees less than the right at mid-stance, a hitch so subtle that no one but a physiotherapist would see it. I saw it. I had been watching Ines Darrieux move since she was twenty-six years old. I could have identified her stride from a satellite photograph.

"How's the knee?" I asked, because I had to ask, though I already knew.

"Fine," she said. And then, after a pause that was the verbal equivalent of that hesitation in the doorway: "It tells me things. About the weather."

I put my hands on the knee. I felt the joint line, the slight effusion that lingered like a conversation that hadn't finished, the warmth of low-grade inflammation that was so chronic it had become architectural. Ines watched me, and I felt her watching, and I felt what I always felt when I put my hands on Ines: the particular weight of being trusted by someone who trusts almost no one.

"It's going to tell you a lot of things in Patagonia," I said.

"I know."

And Maren. I have to tell you about Maren.

Maren Visser at forty-five: five foot ten, seventy-one kilograms, a body I had once described in my professional notes as "the most efficiently organized musculoskeletal system I have encountered in fifteen years of practice," and which I now — eleven years after last examining her, sitting across from her in that same rented suite where the light through the window was gray and Patagonian and unkind — described in my notes as follows:

*Significant bilateral patellar tendinopathy. Right hip: reduced internal rotation, likely early labral degeneration. Thoracolumbar junction: restricted extension. Cervical spine: bilateral facet irritability C4-C6. General muscle tone: reduced from previous assessments. Cardiovascular status: self-reported, unverified, likely adequate. Patient's dominant movement pattern has shifted from hip-driven to lumbar-driven. This is compensatory. This is not sustainable for the duration of the Carrera Austral.*

But this is what the notes don't say. The notes don't say that when Maren walked into the room, Ines looked at the wall. The notes don't say that Maren's hands, which I remembered as the stillest hands on the team — hands that could perform fine-motor tasks at altitude while hypothermic, that could tie knots in the dark, that could paddle a kayak in surf conditions with a metronome precision that made the rest of the team look like they were fighting the water — now moved continuously. She adjusted her watch strap. She smoothed the hem of her shirt. She picked up a roll of tape from my table, tested its weight, set it down, picked it up again. Her fingers had developed a restlessness that was not pathological exactly but that spoke of a body that no longer had a project, that had been purposeful for three decades and had, in the last eleven years, lost the organizing principle that held all that purpose in place.

When I examined her, I put my hands on her hip and felt the crepitus immediately — a grinding, gritty sensation that radiated through my thumbs and told me that the cartilage in the joint was no longer a smooth bearing surface but something roughened, something beginning its slow translation from tissue to bone. I internally rotated the femur and she flinched — a small, controlled flinch, the kind of movement a person makes when they have been flinching alone for a long time and have learned to keep the radius of the flinch as small as possible, so that no one will feel obliged to acknowledge it.

"How long has the hip been like this?" I asked.

"A while."

"How long is a while?"

"Maybe four years."

"Have you seen someone?"

"I'm seeing you."

I looked at her. She looked back. And in that look was the entire history of a relationship that had been conducted almost exclusively through my hands on her body — the hours I had spent mobilizing her joints, lengthening her fascia, reading the narrative of her training and her racing and her living through the text of her tissue — and I understood something I had always known but never permitted myself to articulate, which is that this woman had not been touched by someone who understood her body in eleven years, and that this was a different kind of injury than the hip, and could not be treated with the same techniques, and I was not qualified to treat it, and I was going to try anyway, because what else was I for.

I taped her hip. I gave her exercises. I said the things a physiotherapist says.

What I did not say: *You are all going to break out there, and I will not be able to put any of you back together in the way that matters.*
