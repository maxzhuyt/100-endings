# The Tunguska Corridor

## I.

The standard-issue field notation device for temporal cartography is the Keeler-Brandt Model 4 recorder, a steel cylinder roughly the size and weight of a flare gun, fitted with a rotating drum of chemically treated paper that produces a continuous seismographic trace of local chronological pressure. You depress the lateral switch to mark a waypoint. You depress it twice to mark an anomaly. Three times logs a casualty. The paper feeds at a rate of four centimeters per hour under stable conditions. Under unstable conditions, the paper feeds at whatever rate the local timestream dictates, which is why you also carry a grease pencil and a standard Bureau notebook with waterproof pages. The notebook is considered the primary record. The Keeler-Brandt is considered supporting evidence. We were told this on the first day of certification and again on the last day. I have found, in two years of fieldwork, that the notebook is more useful and the Keeler-Brandt is more honest.

I am — I was — Junior Temporal Cartographer Lena Vasquez, pay grade TC-4, assigned to Survey Corps, Northeast Corridor Division. The work of temporal cartography is infrastructure work. We do not explore. We do not make contact with local populations. We chart stable transit routes through time-corridors the way surveyors once mapped rail grades through mountain passes: measuring the pitch, marking the hazards, establishing where the roadbed can hold and where you will lose your crew to a slide. Someone else built the passes. Someone else will use them. We measure the distance between those two facts and write it down in Bureau-standard notation, and the notation goes into a file, and the file goes into an archive, and the archive makes the next insertion three percent safer than ours was, and the one after that three percent safer than that, and so on, and this is what we mean when we talk about infrastructure.

Our squad leader for the Tunguska insertion was Ellison.

Officially: Senior Logistics Coordinator Diana Ellison, reassigned to field command eight months prior following what the Bureau called a departmental restructuring and what the division called a shortage of people willing to walk corridors that had killed the last team sent through. She had not, as far as any of us knew, commanded a field survey before. She had run supply chains. She had coordinated depot schedules across six corridor zones without, reportedly, a single lost shipment. Someone in the division had apparently decided these skills were transferable. Ellison did not, to my knowledge, dispute this assessment. She did not, to my knowledge, comment on it at all.

The pre-insertion briefing was held in a module tent at Forward Operating Base Kirensk, which sat in a gravel clearing on the west bank of the Lena River in what the Bureau designated as Anchor Year Null — our present, the fixed point from which corridor time was reckoned. It was raining. The tent smelled of nylon and coffee that had been on the heating element since the prior shift. There were seven of us: Ellison; Kowalski, the forward scout; Caruso and Ng, both mid-grade cartographers like me only a year more senior; Petrov, a Russian national and our local-terrain specialist who spoke to none of us except when asked a direct question; Singh, the corridor technician responsible for the stabilization equipment we were hauling; and myself. We sat on folding chairs. Ellison stood at a folding table.

The brief was eleven pages, Bureau-standard formatting, and Ellison went through it with the compressed efficiency of someone who has read a document so thoroughly that presenting it aloud is a formality conducted for the benefit of slower readers. The Tunguska corridor was anchored to a fixed point in eastern Siberia, late June, 1908 — eleven days before the event the historical record would call the Tunguska explosion, though the Bureau's temporal surveys had established that the explosion was less an event than a boundary condition, a marker at the end of the corridor beyond which local time became incoherent. The corridor itself was old, as corridors went. It had been charted twice before. Both surveys had returned usable data. But over the preceding fourteen months, the corridor had begun hemorrhaging what the brief called "localized chronological storm activity" — zones of radical temporal acceleration that moved through the corridor space like weather fronts, untethered from any fixed position, eroding the route the earlier surveys had marked.

Our task was this: march the corridor route to the stabilization point, a fixed-geometry node approximately forty-one kilometers from the insertion terminus. Singh would calibrate the node using equipment the Bureau had spent, I was told, a meaningful portion of its annual budget to develop. Once the node was calibrated, the corridor's chronological weather would, theoretically, attenuate. We would return along the charted route. The expected duration was sixty hours. The window before the corridor's anchorage decayed to the point of stranding us was seventy-two hours, which left what the brief called an "acceptable margin."

Ellison went through all of this. She answered Caruso's question about storm frequency data. She answered Singh's question about the calibration equipment's weight distribution for the march. She did not answer Ng's question about what had happened to the last survey team because the brief did not contain that information and Ellison did not speculate. What Ellison did, while the rest of us were reading our copies, was annotate hers. I was sitting to her left and I could see the margins. She was writing in a small, exact hand — printing, not cursive — and the notes were not summary notes or questions. They were specific: distances between waypoints recalculated in meters rather than the brief's kilometers, weight limits for each team member's load rebalanced against the march pace she intended to set, timing intervals for storm-watch rotations broken into fifteen-minute increments. She had done this math before the briefing. I could tell because she was not calculating. She was transcribing. The pen moved without pausing.

I noticed, also, that the supply allocation she had requisitioned for seven people over sixty hours was wrong. Not short. Heavy. She had packed ration units for what I estimated, counting the crates Singh was already complaining about having to carry alongside the calibration equipment, to be at least ninety hours. Perhaps more. At the time I interpreted this as the instinct of a supply-chain person — the same instinct that would make a quartermaster over-pack a shipment because shortfall was the failure mode she had spent her career being punished for. It was fussy. It was the mark of someone who did not yet understand that on a corridor march, every extra kilogram was a tax on your legs and your time, and time was the one resource the corridor was actively taking from you. I did not say this to Ellison. I was a TC-4. She was the squad leader. I made a note of it in my field book because the notation protocol requires you to log anything that deviates from the standard loadout, and I logged it the way I logged everything: as a fact observed, a line of data, a number that did not yet belong to a story.

Ellison closed her copy of the brief. She aligned its edges with the edges of the folding table — a small, precise gesture that I would later recognize as characteristic, the habit of a person who keeps the world's objects in order because the alternative is a disorder she cannot describe to you and does not intend to.

"We insert at oh-four-hundred local," she said. "Questions on the route, ask them now or carry them."

No one asked anything.

We carried them.

## II.

The corridor mouth opened in a stand of Siberian birch approximately nine kilometers northeast of the Stony Tunguska River, and we stepped through it at 04:00 local time on what the Bureau's chronometric instruments said was the morning of June 19th, 1908, and the first thing I recorded was the smell.

Corridors have a transit scent — an ionized, metallic taste at the back of the throat that experienced cartographers call "the hum," though it is not a sound. It dissipates within minutes of insertion. What replaced it did not dissipate. The air in the birch forest smelled of ozone — the sharp, clean-laundry smell of a lightning strike — layered over pine sap and wet bark and the black, fungal odor of permafrost soil that has recently thawed. These smells did not belong together. Pine sap is a summer smell. The thawing permafrost was a late-spring smell. The ozone belonged to no season at all. I recorded this in my field book: *Olfactory incongruence at site. Multiple seasonal scent-markers present simultaneously. Consistent with localized temporal bleed.* I wrote this calmly. I was doing my job. The pencil was steady and the notation was standard.

The birch trees were pale as bone in the early light, which came from a low sun that sat on the horizon in the manner of high-latitude summer, sourceless and diffuse, casting no shadows worth measuring. The forest floor was a mat of last year's leaves and this year's growth tangled together, fiddlehead ferns uncurling next to stalks already gone to seed, as though the ground could not agree on a month. Petrov, who had said nothing during the insertion, looked at the tree line and then looked at the ground and then said "Da" in a tone that could have meant confirmation or resignation and did not elaborate.

Ellison had the team in march order within four minutes of insertion. Kowalski on point, twenty meters ahead. Caruso and Ng behind him at ten-meter intervals carrying the secondary survey equipment. Then Singh, burdened with the calibration rig, a seventy-kilo frame pack that turned her into a slow-moving geometry of aluminum struts and wrapped cable. Then Petrov. Then me. Ellison walked last, which I knew from the field manual was non-standard — squad leaders typically walked third in a seven-person column — but which I did not question because Ellison had given the order and the order was clear and we were walking.

The forest was quiet in the way that forests are quiet when something is wrong with them. There were birds, but they were wrong: I could hear what Petrov later identified as a Siberian jay calling in a pattern that belonged to its autumn territorial display, and beneath that, the rapid, overlapping chatter of a breeding-season wren, and beneath that, nothing. No insects. The air was warm — warmer than June in this latitude should have produced — and the warmth had the quality of a held breath, the way air feels in a sealed room with too many bodies. I logged the temperature from my kit thermometer: 22°C at 04:40 local. I logged the birds. I logged the absence of insects. The Keeler-Brandt on my belt was producing a trace that I checked every ten minutes, and the trace showed low-amplitude oscillation — the corridor's baseline instability, a gentle sine wave that meant the local timestream was bending but not breaking. Not yet.

We had been walking for forty-seven minutes when Kowalski called a soft halt and raised his left fist.

I did not see the storm arrive. I saw Kowalski see it. He was standing at the edge of a small clearing where the birch thinned and the ground dipped toward a streambed that was dry and wet at the same time — water pooled in some depressions and not in others, as though the stream couldn't decide whether it had rained last week. Kowalski was facing northeast. His head turned, tracking something. His body shifted the way a body shifts when it is calculating whether to step left or step right. He chose neither. He chose forward, one step, an instinctive lean into the space ahead of him, the way a scout moves when he wants a better look at terrain that seems passable.

What I saw next, I saw in sequence, and I will record it in sequence, because that is the notation protocol and because sequence is all I had.

A distortion moved through the tree line. It was visible the way heat haze is visible above summer asphalt: a bending of the light behind it, a shimmer that made the birch trunks flex and warp as though seen through old glass. It moved. That is the critical datum. It was not a fixed zone. It traveled across the clearing at — I estimated later, reconstructing from my own frozen count — roughly three meters per second, the speed of a person walking with purpose. It was perhaps six meters wide. It made no sound. The air ahead of it was still. The air inside it was not visible because the distortion was the air.

Kowalski's forward step carried him into the leading edge.

What happened to him took approximately four seconds. I am certain of this because the Keeler-Brandt's trace, when I examined it later, showed a four-second spike of catastrophic amplitude — the needle slamming to the edge of the drum and back — and because I was counting. I was counting because the field manual says that when you observe a chronological anomaly you log its duration, and I was, God help me, logging.

In the first second, the skin of his hands changed. I was close enough — perhaps twelve meters — to see the texture shift: a tightening, a drawing-in, the way leather looks when it dries too fast. His knuckles became prominent. The backs of his hands went from the brown, weather-roughened skin of a forty-year-old man who worked outdoors to something thinner, spotted, the tendons rising like cables under wax paper.

In the second second, his hair turned. It did not gray in the way hair grays over years, root to tip, a slow tide of pigment loss. It went white the way a bulb goes white when you over-expose a photograph — all at once, a bleaching, total and instant. His posture changed at the same time. His shoulders, which had been square and set in the way of a man carrying a pack he was accustomed to, rolled forward. His spine curved. The pack straps slid outward on shoulders that were suddenly narrower than they had been.

In the third second, he dropped to his knees. The movement was not voluntary. His legs folded the way legs fold when the muscles can no longer support the skeleton — a mechanical failure, not a decision. His hands went to the ground. I could see his face in profile. The jaw had loosened. The skin under his chin hung. His eyes, which I had seen that morning in the tent at Kirensk — steady, pale blue, the eyes of a man who had walked nine corridors and come back from all of them — his eyes were open and they were the same color but they were set in a face that belonged to a man forty or fifty years older than the one who had stepped forward two seconds ago.

In the fourth second, he fell sideways. The weight of the pack pulled him. He lay on the ground among the birch leaves and the half-uncurled ferns and he did not move. His chest rose once — a slow, labored inflation, the breath of a body that had been old for decades and was exhausted by the effort of continuing — and then it did not rise again.

The distortion passed over him and continued through the far tree line and was gone. The clearing was still. The streambed was still. A Siberian jay called once, the autumn call, from a branch that was producing spring buds.
