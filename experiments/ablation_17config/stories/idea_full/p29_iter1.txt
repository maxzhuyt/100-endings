# The Unsent Message

## I.

The storm had been building since four o'clock, heaping itself in bruised columns above the Absarokas, and by the time it crossed the river flats and found Elk Bend it was the kind of storm that makes horses refuse fences—low, electrical, carrying the smell of iron filings and distance and something else Cordelia Sax would later describe to no one as the smell of a door left open to a room she had not known was there.

She was at her key. She was always at her key. The Cheyenne line had been pushing cattle futures since half past six and she was transcribing with the particular detachment the work demanded, her fingers translating the dots and dashes into the felt rhythm of language the way a pianist no longer sees the score but only hears the music arriving through her hands as though the hands themselves were listening, and the message that evening was as dull as money always is when you are only its instrument: *BEEF STEERS FOUR TWENTY FIVE COWS THREE FIFTEEN STOCKERS FIRM STOP.* She wrote it out in her telegraph copperplate on the yellow flimsy, and outside the window the sky went white.

It did not come through the window. Or perhaps it did. Later she would argue both sides to herself without resolution, the way one argues with a dream whose logic was irrefutable while you were inside it and absurd the moment you try to drag it into daylight. What she saw—what she registered in the half second before it reached her—was a sphere of light approximately the size of a croquet ball, luminous and pale blue and trembling at its edges with a kind of furred instability, as though the air around it could not decide whether to contain it or flee. It moved slowly. That was the thing she could never make anyone understand, had she ever tried to make anyone understand, which she did not. It *drifted.* It crossed the room the way a soap bubble crosses a parlor—wobbling, stately, inevitable—and it found her at the key the way water finds the lowest point in a field, not with violence but with the serene indifference of a physical law completing itself. She had time to lift her hand from the brass. She did not lift her hand from the brass. The sphere touched the key and her fingers and the current passed through her and the last thing she heard before the floor hit the back of her skull was a sound like every wire in the territory speaking at once.

She was unconscious for what the clock on the wall, when she could focus on it, told her was eleven minutes.

She woke on the floor. Her fingers were burned in a pattern that followed the pads and whorls where she had gripped the key, the blisters already rising in the shape of the contact points, so that her right hand looked as though it had been branded by the machine itself. Her hair smelled of copper. Her teeth ached. And in the space where the silence of the empty office should have been, there was noise.

Not noise. Not yet. First it was clicking. The familiar chattering of Morse, but layered—dozens of transmissions running simultaneously on the same wire, a pileup of signal that any competent operator would recognize as catastrophic crosstalk, as a switchboard failure, as the sound of a system overwhelmed. She pressed her burned palms to the floor and pushed herself upright and the clicking sharpened, separated, began to sort itself into distinct streams the way a river braids into channels when it hits a gravel bar, and each channel carried—

Language.

Not Morse. Not the rhythmic abstraction of dots and dashes she had spent seven years learning to hear as words. Actual language, arriving fully formed, unkeyed, unsent, as though someone had bypassed the transmitter entirely and fed the message straight into the wire of her auditory nerve.

*—the way her ankle turns when she steps off that porch, the left one, the way the boot—*

*—if he would only once put his hand on the back of my—*

*—her mouth and the sugar on it from the—*

*—his collarbone where the shirt opens and I could put my—*

*—she doesn't know I watch her through the window of the—*

It was not one voice. It was not twelve. It was every mind within—she would later determine, with the grim precision of a surveyor walking and counting and mapping the boundaries of a territory she had not requested—a quarter mile of where she stood. And what those minds were carrying, what they were transmitting on the hidden frequency that ran beneath every bland transaction of speech and gesture and daily performance, was *want.*

She heard the night clerk at the Elkhorn Hotel imagining the widow Farris unlacing her boots—not quickly, not in the manner of a scene already in progress, but slowly, with the excruciating care of a man who had refined this particular fantasy over months, who knew exactly which boot came off first (the left), who had given the imaginary widow specific dialogue (*You can stay if you're quiet*), who had furnished the room with details the real widow's room almost certainly did not contain: a blue quilt, a candle guttering in a brass holder, the smell of rosewater, a sound she made in her throat. He was constructing an entire architecture of intimacy in the sovereign privacy of his skull, and Cordelia heard it the way she had heard a thousand transmissions from Cheyenne: clearly, involuntarily, and with the professional detachment that lasted approximately four seconds before the next signal overwhelmed it.

She heard the stable boy at Patterson's livery—fifteen, barely shaving—dreaming (he was asleep, she realized; this was a literal dream leaking through the wall of unconsciousness into her reception) of the Dietrich rider who had come in that afternoon on a paint horse, the one with the broken nose and the scar through his left eyebrow. The boy's dream was compositionally simple, almost tender in its lack of structure: just the collarbone, repeating. The ridge of it beneath brown skin. The hollow at its base where a pulse would be visible. The boy's desire had the quality of a daguerreotype, all surface and light, and it was so plainly the first real wanting of his life that Cordelia felt something buckle in her chest before the next channel overtook it.

She heard the pastor's wife.

Cordelia had known Mrs. Eldon Bryce for six years. She had sat beside her at the quilting circle. She had listened to her opinions about the arrangement of hymns and the impropriety of face paint and the spiritual dangers of the Thursday dance hall. And now she heard Mrs. Eldon Bryce composing, with a fluency and specificity that would have been impressive in a French novel, an encounter with the pharmacist's hands. The pharmacist was Heinrich Goss, who was sixty and smelled of camphor and had a tremor in his left hand that made his script nearly illegible. None of this mattered to the transmission. Mrs. Bryce's fantasy was granular, relentless, topographic: the exact pressure of Heinrich Goss's thumbs along her spine, the scrape of his calluses on her hip, the way his breath would sound against the back of her neck, the words he would say (in German, which Mrs. Bryce did not speak but which her desire had apparently taught itself for the occasion). The pharmacist's tremor, Cordelia realized with a lurch of comprehension she did not want, was not an impediment in Mrs. Bryce's imagination but the *point*—it was the vibration, the involuntary shaking, that Mrs. Bryce craved, the loss of fine motor control that stood for the loss of all control, the body surrendering its governance, the hand that could not hold itself still and therefore could not hold itself back.

Cordelia vomited into the wastebasket beside her chair. The messages did not stop. She wiped her mouth with the back of her burned hand and the pain flared and the signals flared with it, as though pain were an amplifier, and she heard three more transmissions layered atop one another—a woman wanting rain, a man wanting his dead brother's wife, a child wanting nothing more than to be held but wanting it with a force that registered as a scream in the frequency Cordelia could not close—and she stood and she locked the office and she walked out into the storm.

It was still raining. The rain was a relief because it was only rain.

She walked home. Home was a rented room above the dry-goods store, four blocks east and two blocks north, and in the course of that six-block walk she mapped the borders of her new country without meaning to. Passing the Elkhorn Hotel: the night clerk's signal surged, went vivid, acquired the specificity of direct proximity (every detail of the imagined blue quilt became crisper, more saturated, as though she were walking toward a fire and feeling the heat intensify). Passing the jail: a new signal, someone in the drunk tank dreaming of peach orchards and a woman's wrist, the dream confused and sun-drenched and thick with the smell of fermentation. Turning onto Second Street: the pastor's wife faded. By the time Cordelia reached her own door, the signals had not stopped but they had thinned, spread, become the kind of ambient murmur you could almost mistake for the sound of your own circulation if you held very still and did not breathe and did not think about the fact that the murmur had grammar.

She sat on the edge of her bed. She did not light the lamp. In the dark, with the rain finding its way through the warped sash of her window, she held her burned hand in her unburned hand and listened to the town wanting, wanting, wanting—all of it muffled now by distance, all of it still legible, all of it addressed to no one who could hear it except a woman sitting on the edge of a bed in a rented room wondering whether the ball lightning had killed her and this was what death was: other people's longing, forever, without respite, without the ability to look away.

She did not sleep.

Toward dawn, when the desires dimmed—not because the town had stopped wanting but because exhaustion had banked the fires to coals, because even longing observes the circadian—she held her hand up to the gray light and studied the burns. They were already beginning to scar. The pattern was precise: dots and dashes. Her hand had been branded in Morse.

She could not read what it said. The message was on her skin, in her own hand's handwriting, and she could not read it. She put her face into the pillow and laughed until the laughing became something else, and then she lay still, and the town began to wake, and the frequency rose, and the day's transmissions began.

## II.

Three days later she went back to the office. She told herself this was necessity, which it partly was. The line to Cheyenne would not operate itself, and Mr. Burch, who owned the franchise, had already sent a runner asking after her health. She told herself the burns had healed enough to key, which they had not, quite—the blisters had broken and wept and been wrapped in clean cotton and they throbbed in time with her pulse, a secondary heartbeat in her fingertips—but she could work through the pain or rather beside it, the way you walk beside a river that is running too fast to cross.

What she did not tell herself, because she could not yet afford the admission, was that she went back because the telegraph office was the only place in Elk Bend where the noise made *sense.* In her room she was merely a receiver. At her key she was an operator. The distinction was everything.

She had spent three days learning the mechanism. Not the origin—she would never understand the origin, the way you never understand why one particular piano string snaps during one particular étude—but the *operation.* She could modulate. She had discovered this on the second morning, when the breakfast-hour surge of appetites and resentments and small involuntary fantasies had risen to a volume that made her press her cotton-wrapped hands over her ears (it made no difference; the signal did not arrive through her ears) and in her desperation she had done what she did at the key when the lines were crowded: she had narrowed. She had focused. She had followed a single strand of signal out of the tangle the way you follow a single voice at a party where everyone is speaking at once, and the rest had dropped—not away, never fully away, but down, to a murmur, to the wash of static behind the signal, to the hiss of the carrier wave.

She could tune.

So she returned to the office and she opened the shutters and she tested the Cheyenne line (it was clear, mundane, the signal traveling through the copper in its familiar mechanical rhythm, perfectly indifferent to the second kind of signal she could now hear) and she filed two messages for the bank and one for the general store and she was beginning to feel, if not normal, then at least *functional*—a machine performing its designated task—when the door opened and Deputy Walt Yarrow came in with a message for the territorial marshal's office in Laramie.

She had prepared for this. In the three days of silence and self-tutelage she had prepared for many things, but she had prepared for this most of all, because she was not a fool and she knew her own frequency and she knew that whatever the lightning had done to her it had not burned away the signal she herself had been carrying for three years, the one she had never keyed, never transmitted, never sent.

He came to the counter. He was wearing the same canvas coat he always wore, the one with the tear at the left elbow he had never mended, and his hat was pushed back on his head in the way that meant he was not on formal business but on the kind of business that was only technically business, the kind that gave him a reason to stand at her counter, and his eyes were brown and his teeth were not straight and the scar on his jaw from the Dietrich altercation last October was fading to a thin white line she wanted to touch with the tip of her finger, and she had prepared for this.

"Morning, Cordelia."

"Morning, Walt."

"Heard you took ill."

"Touch of something. I'm fine."

He placed the message on the counter. His fingers on the paper. His fingers were blunt and clean and the nails were trimmed short and there was a callus on his right index finger from the trigger guard and she had catalogued these fingers so many times in the privacy of her own mind that seeing them now felt redundant, felt like rereading a passage she had already committed to memory, and she reached for the paper and her bandaged hand crossed the plane of the counter where his hand rested and she felt the faintest disturbance of air between their skins and she was prepared for this but she was not prepared for this:

*—the steam, God, the steam and the smell of the lye and she pushes it back, she pushes the hair back with the back of her wrist because her hands are wet, they're always wet, and the tendons in her wrist, the way they move under the skin, like something alive beneath the surface—*

The signal hit her the way a rogue wave hits a boat that has been riding the swell competently—from underneath, from the direction you cannot brace against.

*—and if I came in with a shirt, if I just brought a shirt to be pressed, she'd take it and her fingers would touch the fabric where my body had been and I'd watch her hands on the cloth and I wouldn't say anything, I never say anything, but she'd turn to the basin and the light from the back window would come through the steam and I'd see her—*

Walt Yarrow was dictating a message to the territorial marshal. Something about a livestock theft report, dates and descriptions and brand markings. His voice was the surface wire, steady, official, transmitting on the public frequency. And beneath it, louder than his words, more vivid than the room in which they stood, was the second wire—the one that had been running since he walked in, the one he did not know she could hear—and on that wire was Mei-Lin Zhou.

Mei-Lin Zhou, who ran the Chinese laundry on Placer Street. Mei-Lin Zhou, who was small and precise and whose English was accented and formal and who kept roses in a window box behind her shop, roses that had no business surviving a Wyoming summer and yet did, roses the color of blood and of silence. Mei-Lin Zhou, whose wrists—whose *wrists,* specifically, the tendons and the motion of them—occupied a place in Walt Yarrow's interior life that Cordelia had believed, until eleven seconds ago, was hers.

She transcribed the livestock report. Her burned fingers moved on the key. The pain was useful; it gave her something to attend to that was not the sound of the man she loved imagining another woman in exhaustive, devastating, irreversible detail.

*—and the roses, she grows them
