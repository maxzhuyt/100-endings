# Hardin's Gulch

THE STOVE NEEDED blacking and the window on the north wall had been cracked since August, a seam of newsprint stuffed into the gap where the cold came through. Edith Crale stood at the pine table she used as a desk and divided a sheet of butcher paper into four unequal rectangles with a straightedge and a pencil that wanted sharpening. The arithmetic lesson would cover long division again. The younger Fenn girl still reversed her remainders, writing them above the dividend rather than beside it, and the only way to fix the habit was repetition of a particular and patient kind.

She set the pencil down and looked at the second row, third seat. "Your sevens, Colm. Your sevens still lean to the left when you rush. Watch the crossbar." She held up an invisible slate to show him. "You see? Flat across. Like a shelf. A seven is a shelf for the number that sits on top of it."

The seat was empty. All the seats in the second row were empty. She lowered her hand and placed it flat on the desk and held it there until she could feel the grain of the wood against her palm, each groove a fine certain line, and when the grain was fully present she picked up the pencil again and resumed ruling the butcher paper.

Four rectangles. One for each student remaining.

Outside, finches moved along the roof edge in a stuttering line. Frost had come early to the gulch this year, and the aspens on the south ridge had turned all at once, a single night's work, the kind of autumn that arrives not as a season but as a verdict. The ore cart track that ran from the Perdition lode to the stamp mill was visible from the schoolhouse window, a brown cut through the sage, and at its terminus the mill itself sat with its jaw open and its teeth still. It had been silent for eleven months.

---

Edith walked to Schreiber's General Store at half past ten because the chalk was down to nubs and because walking was better than sitting in a room where the cold found every seam. The road from the schoolhouse was three hundred yards of packed dirt and wheel ruts, and she passed two houses with their doors nailed shut and a third where Mrs. Olmstead's chickens still pecked at the frozen ground in the yard, though Mrs. Olmstead herself had gone to Butte in September. The chickens did not know this. They maintained their schedules.

Schreiber's was warm. The stove in the corner was fed with pine and the air smelled of linseed and the particular staleness of bolted flour kept too long. Walter Schreiber stood behind the counter with his ledger open, but he was not attending to it. He was witnessing.

At the counter, Colm Pressfield held a pen over a deed of transfer and was speaking to the man beside him in a voice so steady and so mild it might have been describing the weather. The man beside him was Eustis Greer, who had worked the Nightjar claim for nine years and whose wife had taught Sunday school in the same room where Edith taught Monday through Friday. Greer's hand was on the counter, not on the pen, and his face had the flattened expression of a man performing an arithmetic he has already completed in his head and found correct and does not wish to complete again.

"Mister Greer, if you need another day," Colm said.

"I don't need another day."

"The offer stands either way. I want you to know that."

"I know it."

Greer took the pen. He signed. Schreiber signed beneath him as witness. Colm blotted the document with a small square of felt he kept in his vest pocket for this purpose and folded the deed along its crease and placed it inside a leather case that held, Edith could see, many other folded documents of identical dimension.

"Miss Crale," Colm said, and when he turned to her his face opened into a warmth so thorough and so unperformed that it altered the geometry of the room, drew the proportions inward toward him the way aeli draws a current of air. He was thirty-one years old. He was taller than his father had been and broader through the chest and his hair was darker, almost black where his father's had been the brown of creek-bottom stones, but his mouth was his father's mouth exactly, the same downward curve at the corners that made even his smiles look as though they were being pulled from deep water.

He was twelve, and the composition on his desk was titled "Uses of Silver in the Industrial Arts," and his handwriting had improved so markedly in three months that she had written in the margin, in her careful vertical script, *Colm — this is the work of a mind that refuses to be ordinary. I am proud of you.* He was twelve and his father had been gone for six days and he had not spoken except when called upon and even then only the minimum of syllables, and she had kept him after school and given him tea with too much sugar and told him that the men would find his father, that Thomas Pressfield knew the Bitterroots better than anyone in the gulch, and the boy had looked at her with eyes that were not a child's eyes and said, "Yes, ma'am," and nothing else.

He was thirty-one and his hand was on the leather case and his smile was genuine.

"Miss Crale, you look well. Are you keeping warm up at the school?"

She reached toward his face. Her fingers stopped an inch from his jaw. She was going to correct the smudge of ink on his cheek, the smudge he always got when he wrote with his left hand curled around the pen in that peculiar grip she could never quite train out of him. Her fingers hung in the air.

"Edith," Colm said gently. He did not step back. He tilted his head slightly, the way one adjusts to accommodate a gust of wind. "It's all right."

She lowered her hand. She felt the counter under her other palm. Wood grain. Certain lines.

"Chalk," she said. "I need chalk."

"I've got three sticks behind the register," Schreiber said. "Last of it. I can order more but the freight wagon's not reliable anymore."

She bought two sticks and left the third for a future she did not examine. At the counter, paying, she stood where Eustis Greer had stood. There was no trace of him in the wood. The transaction had left the surface of the counter no different than it had been before, and this seemed to her a failure of material, that pine could not hold the impression of a man signing away nine years.

Colm held the door for her. His courtesy was complete and unironic and as she passed him she smelled coffee and leather and the faint mineral trace of land recently walked — sage and alkali dust and the cold of high places.

"Will you come to dinner this week, Miss Crale? I'm at the hotel. The dining room still serves on Thursdays."

"I have lessons to prepare."

"Of course you do," he said, and his smile carried no condescension, only the fond, uncomplicated admiration of a student who has never stopped believing his teacher is the finest person he has known.

---

Martin Ordway had been the assayer for the Perdition and Nightjar lodes for fourteen years and he kept his records in a ledger bound in sheepskin that he'd brought from a previous post in Leadville. The records were meticulous. In November of 1891 he had tested the last six samples pulled from the Perdition's deepest lateral drift and found silver content of eleven ounces per ton, a figure that represented not a decline but a collapse, a geological sentence handed down without appeal. He had written the number in his ledger and underlined it once and closed the book and sat for some time in his assay office with his hands folded on the cover.

He told the mine owners. They did not resume operations. He told no one else, because no one else asked, and because the silence of the stamp mill had already told the town everything his numbers could confirm.

When Colm Pressfield began making offers on claims that spring, Ordway understood the logic immediately and could not find fault with it. The silver was finished. The claims had no mineral value. Colm was offering forty, sometimes fifty dollars for rights that any honest assessor would price at ten or fifteen, and Ordway, who was an honest assessor by both training and temperament, recognized in these numbers something he could only call *decency.* A man paying more than he owed for something worth less than its owner believed. This was not predation. He could not say what it was instead, but it was not predation.

He said as much to his wife, who listened and said nothing, and later to Deputy Hargreaves at the post office, who listened and said less.

Charles Hargreaves had served as deputy under two marshals and a period of no marshal at all and his understanding of the law was practical rather than philosophical. He had watched eleven families leave Hardin's Gulch since March. He had watched Colm Pressfield purchase nine claims, two town lots, and the timber rights to the eastern ridge. He had reviewed the documents when asked by Schreiber, who served as the town's informal notary and who wanted, Hargreaves understood, someone official to tell him whether anything was wrong.

Nothing was wrong. The prices were fair. The prices were, in several cases, generous to the point of implausibility. The sellers signed voluntarily. No threats had been made, no duress applied, no promises of future value extended. Colm Pressfield was purchasing, at above-market rates, the remnants of a town whose economic basis had been removed by geology. Hargreaves could not name this as injustice. He could not name it at all. He filed the copies and said nothing more to Schreiber, who understood the silence as confirmation and stopped asking.

Margaret Foy had run the boarding house on Placer Street for seven years and she had sold her claim — the half-interest in the Nightjar her husband had left her — to Colm Pressfield on a Tuesday afternoon in October for sixty-five dollars, which was forty dollars more than she had expected and twenty dollars more than she needed to buy passage to Helena, where her sister kept a rooming house and had written twice to say there was a room if Margaret wanted it.

She told the Olmstead chickens, because there was no one else on the street to tell.

"He was always the smartest boy in that school," she said. "Everyone said so. Edith certainly said so. He'd have been something fine if his father hadn't — well." She scattered the feed. "He's something fine anyway, I suppose. You don't offer sixty-five dollars for a claim worth nothing unless you're either a fool or something else, and that boy was never a fool."

The chickens ate. They did not distinguish between the feed and the interpretation.

Colm himself, in the hotel room he rented by the week, sat at the writing desk with the leather case open and arranged the deeds in two stacks: those already filed with the county recorder and those awaiting filing. The stacks were orderly. He numbered each deed on its outer fold in the same small, precise handwriting that Edith had once spent three months correcting from a backward slant to a vertical one. He did not look at the names on the deeds. He looked at the numbers and the legal descriptions, the metes and bounds, the sections and ranges, the geographical coordinates of a place that was becoming, deed by deed, *his* in a way it had never been his father's.

This thought did not occur to him in these words. It did not occur to him as a thought at all. It was present the way the scar on his left hand was present — from a fence nail in his ninth year — visible if examined, functional if not.

---

The claim at the eastern base of Sawyer Ridge had been Thomas Pressfield's, and after Thomas Pressfield's disappearance it had reverted to the county and been sold at auction for twelve dollars to a speculator from Missoula who never visited it and let the assessment work lapse. Colm purchased it from the speculator's estate for thirty dollars in August. He did not file it with the others. He kept it in the inner pocket of his coat, separate.

He walked the claim on an evening in late October when the light was the color of old brass and the sage was silver-gray and the ridge above him held the last of the day's warmth like a hand holds a stone taken from a fire. The claim markers were still standing — hand-cut pine stakes with the tin tags wired on, T. PRESSFIELD stamped into the metal with a letter punch. He touched one. The tin was cold.

His father had walked out on a morning in February with a rifle and a story about trap lines on the north fork. This was not unusual. Thomas Pressfield ran trap lines every winter and was usually gone two or three days and returned with marten or fox and sometimes nothing at all and either way said little about the trip. On this occasion he did not return on the second day or the third. On the fourth day Martin Ordway and two men from the Perdition crew went up the north fork and found the trap line but not the man. They looked for two more days. The snow came. They came back. In March, when the snow receded, a second party went and found nothing further. In April, Edith Crale took the boy into her quarters at the back of the schoolhouse and enrolled him formally as a ward of the school district, which was a legal fiction — there was no school district, only Edith and a building the town let her use — but the document she drafted, in her careful vertical script, was sufficient to give the arrangement an official appearance, and no one questioned it, and the boy stayed.

Colm remembered the quarters. The cot was narrow and she had given it to him and slept on a pallet on the floor and when he protested she said, "I am the teacher, and the teacher decides who sleeps where, and this is a lesson in accepting generosity, and you will accept it." He was twelve. He accepted it. He slept on the narrow cot for two years and wrote his compositions at her desk by lamplight and she corrected them in the margins and returned them and he corrected the corrections and returned them again and this was the process, this circulation of pages between them, this call and response of pencil and ink, that constituted his education.

He kept the compositions. They were in the leather case, beneath the deeds, in a waxed envelope he had bought in Helena for the purpose of keeping them. The paper was cheap foolscap and some of the sheets had yellowed to the color of old teeth and the fold-lines were soft with handling, though not recent handling. He had not taken them out in years. He knew what they said. He knew what she had written in the margins. He did not need to reread the words because the words were part of the architecture of his thinking, as much a part of him as the scar on his hand, and to reread them would be to examine the architecture rather than inhabit it, and he was a man who preferred to inhabit.

One composition, written in his thirteenth year, was titled "The Purposes of a Town." He had argued, in the earnest and somewhat grandiose style of a boy who had recently discovered the essays of Emerson, that a town existed not for its ore or its timber but for the *web of obligations between its people,* and that when the web was broken — by departure, by death, by the failure to search — the town had already ended, even if its buildings stood. Edith had written in the margin: *This is a serious thought written by a serious mind. I do not entirely agree. A town is also its buildings, its stove, its cracked window. The material world is not a metaphor for the human one — it is the human one. But you must keep thinking this way. The world needs people who think about what holds things together.*
