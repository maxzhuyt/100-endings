# The Lakeshore Knife Story

## I.

The lake had a way of holding the town's reflection a half-second longer than physics should have allowed, so that on still October evenings when the water lay flat as poured tin and the dock-lights came on one by one along the esplanade like a congregation finding its hymnals, you could look down from the footbridge and see two towns — the one above, which smelled of woodsmoke and the bruised sweetness of wild apples rotting in the Halsey orchard, and the one below, which was the same town in every particular except that it trembled, it wavered, it could be undone by a stone dropped from a child's hand, and it was, in every way that mattered to this story, the truer portrait. Garnet Lake. Fifteen hundred souls in the off-season, fewer each year, the young ones leaving for cities the way water leaves a crack in winter — slowly, then all at once, then not at all, because what remains has frozen into the shape of its leaving. The town had a Main Street with two dead storefronts and a hardware store that sold ammunition. It had a public library built in 1937 with funds from a logging baron's guilty widow, and the library had a children's room with a mural of otters that no child under twelve could look at without feeling, in some inarticulate way, that they were being loved by the building itself. It had a dock — the dock, the one that matters, the one the reader will return to as the hour turns — and the dock was made of cedar planks gone silver with decades of frost and thaw, and the nails that held the planks were square-headed and original, and between the planks you could see the water four feet below, black and wood-tannin brown, and the water moved beneath you as you walked the dock's length in a way that made you feel you were not walking on a fixed thing but on a thing that had merely agreed, for now, to be still.

There were three birthdays that week. October the nineteenth, all three of them, an accident of conception that the town had long since metabolized into tradition: Dara Kinsella, Bex Yolanda Maud Achilles, Tomás Reyes-Quinn. Eighteen. The age, in Garnet Lake, when the town gave you a knife.

It is necessary to say what kind of knife and why, because the tradition is the argument and the argument is already the ending, though no one inside the story knows this yet. The knives were handmade by Oren Salk, who had been the town's bladesmith since before the word *artisan* arrived in Garnet Lake on the bumpers of out-of-state Subarus, and who worked in a shop behind the fire station where the anvil-ring carried across the water on certain mornings like a church bell from a denomination no one had joined deliberately. Oren made each knife to the recipient's hand — he measured the span of the palm, the length of the fingers, the distance from the base of the thumb to the tip of the index in full extension, and he forged a blade whose handle fit that hand and no other, so that the knife was, in a sense the town found beautiful, *a portrait of the person who would hold it*. The blade was always the same: four inches of high-carbon steel with a convex grind and a spine thick enough to baton kindling. A practical knife. A knife for cleaning fish, for whittling tinder, for cutting the braided dock-lines that swelled in rain and would not yield to fingers alone. Every child in Garnet Lake grew up knowing the knife was coming the way children in other places knew about driver's licenses or prom — it was the visible seam between childhood and what the town, in its slow and certain grammar, called *after*. At the birthday dinner, or the birthday party, or in some cases just the kitchen table with a parent's hand shaking slightly, the knife was presented in a cedar box lined with oiled cloth, and the birthday child drew it out and held it, and the blade caught whatever light was nearest — a candle, a dock-lamp, the flat gray of an October morning — and the child saw their own face in the steel, warped and miniaturized, and the town said, without saying, *You are one of us now, and what we are is people who carry sharpened things and call it love.*

Seven teenagers would be at the dock that Friday night. Three of them would receive their knives. By Saturday morning the clinician from the county seat would document the way the lake caught the blue strobe-light of the first responder's truck, and the water would hold that blue a half-second too long, as it held everything — the trembling, truer portrait — and four of the seven would be past holding. But that is later. That is the story the narrator already carries in its mouth like a stone, tasting its weight, choosing when to open. For now it is Wednesday, two days before the party, and the town is the thing that matters: the gorgeous, ordinary, irreversible fact of a place that hands its children blades and believes it is handing them belonging, that wraps violence in cedar and calls it ceremony, that mistakes the capacity to cut for the capacity to survive. The wild apples fall. The water holds. The dock waits.

Everything in Garnet Lake is patient.

## II.

The kiss behind the boat shed had happened eleven days ago, and Dara Kinsella had replayed it four hundred times — she had not counted, but the narrator, who has access to the machinery behind her machinery, puts the number there to illustrate the scale — and each replay was not a memory but a *revision*, a draft of herself being improved, the way a student rewrites an essay not to capture what happened but to arrive at what should have.

What had happened: Niall Ochoa had been sitting on the overturned hull of Ernesto Goya's retired canoe, smoking a cigarette he did not enjoy because he was seventeen and had not yet learned that the gesture of smoking and the pleasure of smoking are two currencies that do not convert. Dara had come around the corner of the boat shed carrying a sweater she did not need, which she had retrieved from Bex's car as a pretext for walking past the place where she knew — had confirmed through three separate acts of digital surveillance — Niall would be. He had looked up. The looking-up was the hinge. In life, in the actual moment, his face had done nothing extraordinary; it had merely rearranged itself from private blankness to social acknowledgment, the way any face does when another person enters its range. But in Dara's four hundred drafts, the looking-up was a *revelation* — his eyes finding her across the blue shadow of the boat shed, his cigarette forgotten, his posture shifting in its moorings, and the whole weight of his attention falling on her like the beam of the Garnet Lake lighthouse on the promontory, which had been decommissioned in 1994 but which every local still saw sweeping the dark on certain nights, as though light could leave a habit in the air.

They had kissed. This was true. His mouth had been smoky and dry and slightly open before hers reached it, which meant — she had decided through careful adjudication — that he had been *about* to kiss her, that the impulse had originated in him, that she had merely closed a distance he had already halved. She told herself this version with the authority of someone citing precedent. She told it to the bathroom mirror at 6:15 each morning while the light was still gray and the lake through the window was the color of an unwritten page. She told it to herself between classes, during the nine-minute passing period at Garnet Lake Regional High, where the hallways smelled of floor wax and the particular desperation of fluorescent lighting, and where Niall Ochoa walked thirty-eight feet ahead of her on the path between the science wing and the arts annex, his backpack riding low on his hips, his shoulders carrying the specific slouch of a boy who would be handsome at twenty-five but at seventeen was still negotiating the unevenness of his own growth, and she watched the back of his neck — the vertebra at the top of his spine, the small dark hairs — and each day's watching was a bead added to the rosary, and the rosary was not a prayer for something to happen but a prayer that what had already happened would keep meaning what she needed it to mean.

What she needed it to mean was this: that she, Dara Kinsella, who had been for the first seventeen years of her life a person of furious and concealed feeling — whose interior was a cathedral of longing that no one had entered, because no one had known where the door was, because she had hidden the door even from herself and then raged at the world for not finding it — that she was now, at last, a person who was *wanted*. Not liked. Not tolerated. Not included the way the town included everyone, with the bland democratic warmth of a potluck where all contributions were thanked equally and none was singled out for praise. *Wanted.* In the specific, annihilating, you-and-no-other sense that she had learned from songs and novels and the one film she had watched nine times in which a woman stands in rain and a man crosses a street and everything before that crossing is preamble and everything after is life.

The narrator knows what Dara does not: that Niall Ochoa kissed her because she was there and because the evening was blue and because he had been, that day, refused by someone else — a refusal he would never mention to Dara or to anyone, because it had not mattered to him the way his kisses mattered to her, because he moved through the world with the unexamined confidence of a person whose reflection had never once made him wince. This is not cruelty. It is not even carelessness. It is simply the asymmetry that the narrator observes without judgment: that for Niall the kiss was a comma, and for Dara it was the book.

And the narrator knows one thing more, the thing that matters for the story to come: that this hunger — this electric, devotional, consuming need to be *seen* by another person, to feel one's own existence confirmed by the quality of an external gaze — was not unique to Dara. Every teenager who would stand on the dock Friday night carried some version of it. The need to be known. The need to be witnessed in the secret room. Bex carried it in her dead languages, whispering Sumerian conjugations into her pillow like love letters to a civilization that could not reject her. Tomás carried it in his ceilings, painting only surfaces that required him to look up, because looking up was the posture of supplication and supplication was the only honest relation he knew to beauty. Even Niall carried it, though his version was so well-armored in ease that it passed for its opposite. They all carried it — the open door, the unlocked window, the place where the right words, said by the right voice, could enter and fill the room.

The killer knew where every door was.

But it is still Wednesday. Dara is in her bedroom, cross-legged on the quilt her grandmother made from factory-salvage corduroy in seven shades of brown, and she is rehearsing Friday: how she will stand on the dock, how the knife will be placed in her hand, how Niall will see her holding it — transformed, adult, no longer the girl who hid the door but the woman who flung it open — and how the night will be the night she has been writing in her mind since the boat shed, the night when the rough draft becomes the final, the night when she is, at last, the protagonist.

The narrator does not interrupt. The narrator, who already knows about the blood on the cedar planks, lets Dara dream.

## III.

Friday.

They arrived at the dock between seven and eight, in that October hour when the sky is the color of a bruise discovering it is also a sunset, and the lake held each of them twice — once above, in the careless fact of their bodies, and once below, in the trembling portrait that the water made of them because the water could not help it, because holding was what the water did.

Seven.

Begin with **Bex**, because Bex arrived first, as Bex arrived first to everything on the theory that punctuality was a form of reverence and reverence was the minimum the world deserved. She wore a dress the color of stormlight and boots that were too large for her because they had been her mother's and she had put newspaper in the toes, and in the left boot's newspaper there was a vocabulary list in her handwriting — twelve Sumerian words for types of silence, because the Sumerians had understood, four thousand years before Garnet Lake existed, that silence was not the absence of language but its most populated room. Bex collected dead languages the way other people collected stamps or resentments: with methodical, obsessive, embarrassing love. She could conjugate *ĝen* — to go — in its marû and ḫamṭu forms, and she had once spent an entire Saturday afternoon weeping at the Garnet Lake library because she had found a tablet transcription in which an Ur III scribe had made a spelling error and corrected it, and the correction was visible, and the visibility meant that four thousand years ago a person's hand had hesitated, and the hesitation was still legible, and Bex could not explain to anyone why this made her feel less alone, so she stopped trying and simply carried the feeling the way she carried the word-lists: folded, hidden, pressed against her skin like a compress against a wound she could not name. She was seventeen. In two hours she would be eighteen. She was so alive.

**Tomás** came next, carrying a canvas the size of a hubcap that he had painted that afternoon and could not bear to leave in his bedroom, because the paint was still wet and leaving wet paint alone felt to him like leaving a conversation mid-sentence. The canvas was meant for a ceiling — his bedroom ceiling, which was already covered in seventeen other paintings, overlapping and layered so that the surface above his bed was a palimpsest of color and gesture, a record of every time he had looked up and found his own inadequacy and painted over it. This new one was a study of lake-water from below — what a fish might see, what a drowned thing might see — the surface a lens of bent light above, the sky beyond it warped and inaccessible and beautiful the way all inaccessible things are beautiful. He painted only ceilings because he believed — and this belief was the architecture of his seventeen years, the load-bearing wall — that the only honest art was art that required you to look up, that demanded the posture of surrender, chin raised, throat exposed, eyes searching a surface that would never come closer no matter how long you looked. He had not told anyone this. He had told no one that when he painted he cried, not from sadness but from the specific overwhelm of trying to make a physical thing do what a feeling does, and that the crying was the proof that the painting was working, and that he would rather die than be seen crying, and that this contradiction — the need to be witnessed and the terror of being witnessed — was the engine of every canvas he had ever stretched. He held the wet painting carefully, one hand on each side, his fingers smudged the color of deep water, and he was so alive.

**Eli Voss** arrived with a six-pack of the craft beer his older brother bought him — IPA, too hoppy, the bitterness a performance of adulthood the way Niall's cigarette was — and Eli was the one who told jokes that landed two seconds late, the delay an artifact of his processing, because Eli listened to everything twice: once for content and once for music, and the second listening was where the humor lived, in the rhythm rather than the meaning, and his friends had long since learned to wait for his laughter the way you wait for the second beat of an echo. He kept a notebook of sounds he wanted to remember: the specific groan of the dock under Oren Salk's weight, the way Bex said *Sumerian* with the accent on the second syllable instead of the third, the noise the lake made against the pilings on a windless night, which was not silence and not lapping but something he had written down as *the sound of water remembering that it used to be rain*. He was seventeen. He had never been kissed by anyone, and he wanted this so badly that he had developed an allergy to wanting, a counter-program that intercepted every desire before it reached his face and translated it into irony, so that what the world saw was a boy who joked about everything and cared about nothing, and what the boy contained was a seismograph of caring so sensitive it registered the vibration of a dock-plank under a friend's foot. He was so alive.
