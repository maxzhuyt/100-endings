# The Sommerfest Job

## I.

The argument about the village's name had been going for eleven minutes, which meant they were exactly on schedule, because the argument about the village's name was the argument they always had when they were early and anxious and needed something to chew on that wasn't the job.

"It's *Kirchtal*," said Ludo from the driver's seat, punching the second syllable like he was trying to kill it. "Hard *t*. No diphthong. You're adding a diphthong that doesn't exist."

"I'm not adding a diphthong, I'm acknowledging the regional palatalization that anyone with even a passing familiarity with Upper Bavarian dialect would—"

"Soren." Nora didn't look up from the detonator she was calibrating. "Soren, I need you to hear me when I say this: no one on this planet has a *passing* familiarity with Upper Bavarian dialect. People either have no familiarity, or they have your familiarity, and there is nothing in between, and both categories of person find you equally exhausting."

Margaux watched all of this from the back of the rented van—a white Sprinter with Bavarian plates and a magnetic sign on the side reading FESTZELTVERLEIH GRUBER, which Soren had sourced from an actual tent-rental company in Augsburg that had closed for insolvency the previous March, because Soren did not believe in cover identities that could not survive a phone call, even though no one was going to make a phone call, because they were robbing a church in a village of four hundred people, not infiltrating the BND. This was the thing about Soren. He was the best lockpick she'd ever worked with and the most annoying human being she'd ever met, and these qualities were not separate. The same compulsive precision that let him feel a pin tumbler's false set at a quarter-millimeter tolerance also compelled him to correct your German in the middle of an argument about your German.

"The palatalization," Soren continued, because he had never once in his life registered a social cue that wasn't engraved on a lock cylinder, "is documented in Kranzmayer's *Historische Lautgeographie des gesamtbairischen Dialektraumes*, which I realize none of you have read, because none of you read, full stop, unless you count Margaux's insistence on working through Bernhard during jobs, which I would characterize less as reading and more as—"

"Masturbation," offered Hélène from the passenger seat, without turning around. "Intellectual masturbation. That's the word you're looking for."

"I was going to say *affectation.*"

"Masturbation is funnier."

"Is it? I've always found it a rather limp metaphor. So to speak."

Margaux let the Bernhard comment pass. She let most comments pass. This was, depending on who you asked, either her greatest professional asset or evidence of a personality disorder. She was currently watching the village resolve itself through the van's rear windows as Ludo brought them down the valley road—a compression of butter-colored houses and dark timber and window boxes trailing geraniums the red of arterial spray, all of it backed by the extravagant green of the foothills and capped by a church steeple that rose above the roofline with the quiet authority of a thing that had been there longer than anything else and intended to remain. The golden hour light was doing what golden hour light does to Bavarian villages, which is to make them look like something a dead painter invented to sell the concept of peace to anxious Protestants.

She cataloged the steeple's height—thirty meters, give or take. Slate roof. Louvered openings on four sides, which meant four-directional sight lines, which meant she'd have the entire village square and all three exit roads in view. Good. She cataloged the roads themselves: north toward the valley pass, east toward the highway, south through the forest. Three ways out. Three was enough. Three was more than enough.

"Can we please," said Ludo, turning onto the main road that would take them to the staging point behind the Gasthof, "talk about the job instead of Kranzmayer's phonological opinions?"

"Kranzmayer was a dialectologist, not a phonologist, but yes, let's talk about the job." Soren pulled a tablet from his bag and opened the floor plan he'd been refining for three weeks. "The reliquary is displayed in the north transept, east wall, in what the diocese inventory describes as a 'period-appropriate vitrine,' which I take to mean the case is old and the locks are worse. Silver-gilt, circa 1480, attributed to a Nuremberg workshop but almost certainly Augsburg—the finial work is Augsburg, I'd bet my hands on it—containing what the Church claims are the finger bones of Saint Koloman, which the Church is welcome to go on claiming because we are not being paid for finger bones. We are being paid for the reliquary itself, which Desmond's client has valued at—"

"One point four," said Margaux. It was the first thing she'd said in twenty minutes, and she said it the way she said most things: flatly, precisely, as if placing a card on a table. "One point four million, which after Desmond's commission and expenses is seven hundred and seventy thousand, which split five ways is one hundred and fifty-four thousand each, which is insulting for a cross-border job with a bespoke entry plan and a demolitions component, but which we all agreed to because none of us has worked in four months and Nora's custody lawyer charges six hundred euros an hour."

A silence opened in the van. It was the specific silence that followed whenever someone made the mistake of mentioning Nora's custody lawyer or, more broadly, the chain of events that had begun in Bruges three years ago with a forged Cranach and ended with Nora's husband possessing both the Cranach and primary custody of their daughter, the intermediate steps of which involved Hélène in ways that no one was permitted to discuss and everyone constantly discussed.

"I charge six hundred euros an hour," Nora said calmly, "because I win. Unlike some people's authenticators."

Hélène's shoulders went rigid. "The Cranach was flawless. Your husband's appraiser was—"

"My *ex-*husband."

"Your *ex-*husband's appraiser was a savant. No one could have anticipated—"

"You used zinc white in the ground layer. *Zinc white.* In a painting supposedly from 1529. The man didn't need to be a savant. He needed to own a blacklight."

"That," said Hélène, and her voice had the particular silky quality it took on when she was about to say something that would ruin the operational atmosphere for the next two hours, "is a *lie* that Gustav—"

"Bruges is closed," said Margaux. "Bruges is a closed topic. We agreed."

"You agreed," Hélène said. "*I* was told."

"And yet here you are, not discussing it. The system works." Margaux opened her own bag, checked the binoculars—Zeiss Victory, 10x42, bought with her cut of the Istanbul job and worth more than the Bruges Cranach, real or forged—and then checked the comms kit, five earpieces, five channels, encrypted and frequency-hopping, also courtesy of Istanbul and also more competent than anyone in the van except possibly Soren. "Run it back for me. Whole thing. Start to finish."

Ludo pulled the van behind the Gasthof and killed the engine. Through the walls of the van, Margaux could hear the first sounds of the Sommerfest assembling—brass instruments being tested, the resonant thock of wooden stalls being bolted together, children's voices oscillating between excitement and crisis. Ludo turned in his seat. He had the flat, unworried face of a man whose job was to sit in a vehicle and be ready, and who had never yet encountered a situation that could not be resolved by sitting in a vehicle and being ready. He was wrong about this, but his wrongness had not yet cost them, and his calmness was, in its way, a kind of value.

"Nineteen hundred hours," Ludo said. "Sommerfest parade starts. The whole village is in the square. Soren enters the church through the east transept door during the loud bit—"

"The *Festzug,*" Soren said.

"—during the *loud bit.* Hélène runs distraction in the square. General tourist chaos. I hold the van here, engine running, facing north. Margaux takes the bell tower, covers all four quadrants, calls movements. Nora handles the display case—"

"I handle the display case if the case is the approach," Nora said. "The specs suggest there may be a lower access point. A crypt or subchamber. The provenance file from Desmond includes a builder's survey from 1987 that shows a void space beneath the north transept. If that's real, and if the reliquary can be accessed from below, we go through the crypt instead, which means no exposure in the nave, no risk from foot traffic, and a more interesting problem for Soren's fingers."

Soren flexed his hands unconsciously. Margaux watched him do it. She watched everyone. This was what she did, what she had always done, what she had been doing since she was six years old and had realized that other children did not sit in the corner of the schoolyard mapping the social dynamics of games they could have joined. Watching was the first thing she'd ever been good at and—despite what Hélène might say about the Bernhard—the only thing she'd ever truly loved.

"If we go through the crypt," Soren said slowly, "I'll need Nora with me. Real-time assessment. If the lower locks are older than the display case—which they will be, if this is genuinely a medieval subchamber—then we're dealing with a different discipline entirely. Ward locks, possibly. Or something bespoke. I'll want her for the mechanism and for the extraction. The reliquary will be heavy."

"So the two of you go down," Margaux said. "Hélène stays in the square. Ludo holds the van. I hold the tower."

"And if things go wrong?" Nora asked.

"Define wrong."

"Discovered. Interrupted. Delayed past the twenty-two-minute window."

"Then we abort. Soren and Nora out through the east transept, same door as entry. Hélène out through the crowd—she's a tourist, she walks away. I come down from the tower and I walk to the van. Ludo has us on the north road in under a minute. We're in Augsburg by midnight. Desmond gets a phone call. We don't get paid. We live."

"Charming," said Hélène. "The Margaux Vidal motivational speech. *We don't get paid. We live.* Stirs the blood."

Margaux said nothing. She was watching, through the gap between the van's rear doors, a group of villagers carrying something across the square—a long wooden trestle, or possibly a barricade, though that second reading occurred to her and was dismissed in the same instant. Why would it be a barricade? It was a table for the festival. Villages carried tables. She noted the way the men carrying it moved—not with the shuffling negotiation of people cooperating on an awkward lift, but with a synchronized fluidity that suggested rehearsal, or repetition, or something so deeply habitual it had passed beyond coordination into instinct. Impressive, for a table. She filed it and let it go.

"Comms check in five minutes," she said. "I need to get to the tower before the square fills up. Everyone clear?"

Four voices, four variations of assent, four people she would not choose to sit next to at dinner and could not imagine replacing. Nora, whose demolitions work was as precise as her grudges were permanent. Soren, who could open a seventeenth-century Chubb lock by touch and could not open a conversation without making someone want to hit him. Hélène, who could become anyone for ninety minutes and could not be herself for five without starting a war. And Ludo, who was possibly the most reliable driver in the western European underworld and who had once, in a moment of uncharacteristic personal disclosure, confessed to Margaux that he found the sound of a well-tuned diesel engine "emotionally sufficient," a phrase she had thought about with upsetting frequency in the three years since.

She climbed out of the van, crossed herself for no reason she could have articulated, and walked toward the church.

## II.

The climb was 127 steps, which she knew because Soren had counted them on the floor plan and she'd read Soren's notes because she always read everything, and also because she counted them herself as she climbed, because counting was something her body did when her mind was doing something else, and her mind was doing something else.

The staircase spiraled clockwise—standard medieval construction, designed so that a right-handed defender descending could swing freely while a right-handed attacker ascending could not. Margaux was not a medievalist, but she had robbed enough churches to have opinions about staircase geometry, and this one struck her as unusual in a way she couldn't immediately name. The steps were too even. Too well-maintained. Stone stairs in a church this old should be concave from centuries of feet, worn into saddles at the center. These were flat. Not new—the stone was old, the walls were old, the smell was old (damp limestone, dried mortar, the sweet-rot undertone of ancient wood)—but *maintained* with a care that exceeded what you'd expect from a village parish with a declining congregation and a diocese that had been consolidating rural properties since the nineties.

Someone was keeping this staircase in good repair. Someone climbed it regularly enough to care.

She filed this alongside the synchronized table-carriers and continued climbing.

The bell chamber was exactly what the floor plan had promised: a square room, open on four sides through louvered wooden shutters, dominated by two bronze bells that hung in a timber frame so massive it looked like it had been built to restrain something rather than support it. The bells were old. Much older than the church. Margaux was not an expert, but the green-black patina and the style of the casting marks put them pre-Reformation at minimum. One of them had inscriptions around the rim that she photographed out of habit and would later show to Soren, who would go pale in a way she would not understand at the time.

She swung the shutters open on the south face first—the square. The Sommerfest was assembling below her with the orderly enthusiasm of a community that had been doing this for a very long time. Stalls selling Bratwurst and Steckerlfisch and Lebkuchenherzen were completing their setup along the square's perimeter. A temporary stage had been erected near the fountain. The brass band—twelve players in lederhosen and dirndls, median age sixty, competence radiating from them like heat—was arranged in a semicircle finalizing warm-up scales that bounced off the surrounding facades in ways that made the square a resonating chamber. Children were running in patterns that appeared random but weren't, quite. Old women in embroidered aprons were stringing the last of the flower garlands across the entrance to the square—enormous ropes of cornflowers and daisies and something red she couldn't identify from this height, woven so thickly they were almost opaque.

South: clear. She could see the road out through the forest, framed between two rows of linden trees, open and empty. Good.

East shutters. The road to the highway. A gentle downhill grade, two switchbacks, then the bridge over the creek and the straightaway to the autobahn junction. Currently unobstructed. A few parked cars along the verge—festival attendees from neighboring villages, she assumed. One hay cart sitting at the first switchback, being loaded or unloaded by three men who were working with the same frictionless coordination she'd noticed in the square. She noted their positions. She noted the hay cart's orientation—lateral, across the road, though not fully blocking it. Eighty percent, maybe. A car could get past. Probably.

North shutters. The valley pass road, their primary exit route, where Ludo's van was already positioned. Clear. Open. A maypole had been erected about a hundred meters up the road at a widened section that served as a secondary gathering area, and a group of young women in dirndls was marking out the dance circle with ribbons.
