# The Stutters

## I.

The 6:04 pulled away from the Grafton depot eleven seconds early because Darya Koss had been driving the 71 route for nine years and she knew that eleven seconds bought back the left-turn cycle at Merchant and Figg, which in February ran thirty seconds long on account of the school-zone reprogram that Public Works still hadn't synced with Transit. You learned this or you didn't. You carried it in your body or you were always late.

She had the seat adjusted the way she'd spent two years adjusting it: ratcheted one notch past the factory stop, a rolled-up hand towel wedged against the lumbar curve because the county wouldn't spring for ergonomic backs and her union rep said the grievance was "in process," which meant it was in a drawer. The mirror was angled to catch the rear bench and the first three rows of the articulated section. Her travel mug sat in the cupholder she'd duct-taped to the dash partition after the original one cracked off in the cold snap of 2019. Lemon ginger. No sugar. She was not a person who needed things to be sweet.

Grafton to Riverside Terminal: forty-one stops, sixty-eight minutes if the Figg light cooperated, seventy-two if it didn't, eighty on the first Monday of the month when the Social Security office on Pruitt disgorged a slow tide of people who needed the kneeling step and who sometimes needed, more than the kneeling step, someone to say *No rush, take your time, I've got you*. She had them. She always had them.

The 6:04 was the workers' bus. Elena Aguilar boarded at Grafton and Third with her lanyard already around her neck, badge photo facing inward because she was superstitious about strangers seeing her picture—she'd told Darya this once, three years ago, in a confiding murmur, and Darya had never mentioned it again, which was its own form of care. Benny Tomaszewski got on at Halyard Street smelling like the instant oatmeal he ate in the car on the drive to the stop because his wife had left in October and breakfast had become a problem he solved with maple-brown-sugar packets and a spoon he kept in the glovebox. Darya knew this because she could smell it—the particular humid sweetness of microwaved oats—and because one morning she'd said "Maple?" and Benny had looked at her like she'd performed a magic trick and then laughed, really laughed, the kind that comes out of lonely men like a dog let off a leash.

She knew Terri Gaines at Polk, who carried two phones and always held the newer one face-down. She knew Marvell at the Civic Center stop, fourteen years old and already carrying the oversized portfolio that meant he'd gotten into the arts magnet, who sat behind the rear doors and drew on his knees and never once played music out loud. She knew the woman she privately called Velvet because the woman wore a crushed-velvet headband in a different color every day and had never, in four years of boarding at Elm Terrace, spoken a single word to anyone, though once she had touched Darya's wrist while depositing her fare and the touch had been so deliberate, so clearly communicative, that Darya still thought about it.

Forty-one stops. She knew them the way the devout know the stations of an observance: each one a small, obligatory pause that gave shape to the passage between. The hydraulic sigh of the doors. The particular rattle of the fare box when someone dropped coins instead of swiping. The way the engine note changed on the grade up Belmont Avenue, deepening into a sound she thought of, though she would never have said this to anyone, as the bus bearing down and trying. The way, at certain hours, the light came through the windows in laterals and the interior of the bus became briefly beautiful—aluminum and vinyl and scratched plexiglass made cathedral by nothing more than the angle of the sun and the presence of thirty people who had somewhere to be.

She loved it. She would not have used the word. She would have said she was good at her job. But the difference between being good at a job and loving it is mostly a matter of what you notice when you don't have to, and Darya noticed everything.

The 6:04 was on time. The Figg light cooperated. The morning opened the way every morning opened—with the ordinary, magnificent fact that a metal box full of strangers trusted her to carry them through the city and set them down, alive and undamaged, where they needed to go.

## II.

It happened first on a Tuesday in March, between the Halyard Street and Polk Avenue stops, at approximately 6:23 a.m., though Darya would not write down the time until later because at the moment it happened she did not yet know that anything had happened, only that something had felt wrong the way a missed step feels wrong—the foot expecting ground that isn't there, the half-second of freefall before the brain catches up and says *You're fine, you just miscounted*.

Terri Gaines was standing in the aisle, one hand on the overhead bar, talking to the woman beside her about a leak in her kitchen ceiling. The conversation was unremarkable. The overhead light was the same institutional yellow it always was. The bus was moving at its usual post-stop pace through the stretch of Halyard that ran parallel to the rail yard. And then Terri's jacket—her quilted navy jacket, which Darya had seen five days a week for three years—was red.

Not gradually. Not like a trick of light. It was navy and then it was red the way a television is one channel and then another: a cut, not a dissolve. And in the splice—in the seam between the navy and the red—Darya felt something arrive in her head that was not her own thought.

*She's going to ask me about the water stain again and I swear to God if she does I will*—

Then the jacket was navy. Terri was laughing about the leak. The sentence in Darya's head stopped as if someone had pressed a thumb over the mouth of it. The woman beside Terri said something about calling the landlord and Terri rolled her eyes affectionately and pulled the cord for Polk.

Darya checked the mirror. Checked the road. Checked the mirror again. Her hands were at ten and two, where they always were. The bus was doing twenty-two in a twenty-five. The Polk stop was forty meters ahead. She signaled, decelerated, opened the doors. Terri stepped off without looking back, the way she always stepped off, and the navy jacket moved down the sidewalk until it rounded the corner and was gone.

Darya drove. She thought: *I'm tired*. She thought: *I didn't sleep well*. She thought about the jacket and could not, in fact, remember if it had been navy or red before the shift—whether the red was the intrusion or the correction. It had been navy afterward. She was almost certain it had been navy before. But the *almost* was new. She'd never had to qualify her certainty about something she'd seen on the bus. The bus was where she saw things clearly. That was the whole point of her.

She made the next five stops without incident. She made the left onto Belmont and felt the engine deepen and trusted it the way she always did.

At the Civic Center stop, Marvell got on. He was wearing his portfolio strap across his chest and had his earbuds in and did not look up as he swiped his student pass. He moved toward his usual seat behind the rear doors. And as he passed the third row, the overhead advertisement panel—the one running a digital loop for a dental practice—blinked. The dentist's name changed. The phone number gained a digit. The smile in the photograph widened by a fraction that should have been imperceptible but wasn't, not to Darya, not to her eyes which had looked at that same advertisement every morning for six weeks.

And again, in the blink, a thought arrived that was not hers:

*—draw him the way he really looks when he's scared not the way he—*

Then the sign was the sign. The dentist's name was the name it had always been. The smile was the same professional, laminated smile. Marvell sat down and opened his portfolio and began to draw on his knees with the concentration of a boy who has found the one thing in the world that answers back.

Darya drove. Her hands were steady. The taste in her mouth was like licking a battery—metallic and wrong, a taste that had no business being in a mouth that had just sipped lemon ginger tea. She swallowed. She made the Belmont crest and started the descent toward Riverside. The light was coming through the windows in laterals. The bus was briefly beautiful. She noticed this because she always noticed this, and because noticing was, for the first time in nine years, not a comfort but a warning.

She was seeing too clearly. She was hearing things that were not sounds. She was a woman driving a bus and the world had hiccupped around her, twice, and twice it had sealed itself back up as though the hiccup had never happened, and the seal was perfect except for the residue it left in her—the half-sentences, the battery taste, the knowledge that navy had been red for one frame of a film nobody else was watching.

She pulled into Riverside Terminal four seconds early. She opened the doors. She said "Have a good one" to fourteen people who deserved it, and she meant it, and she sat in the empty bus for ninety seconds before starting the return trip because she needed the silence the way she needed air, though she could not have said what it was the silence was supposed to cure.

## III.

The notebook was a pocket Moleskine, graph-ruled, purchased for $4.99 from the drugstore on Clement Street the following Saturday. She chose graph paper because she'd read somewhere that engineers preferred it, and what she needed—she decided this while standing in the stationery aisle holding it, aware of how small and ordinary a gesture this was—was an engineer's relationship to the problem. Grid lines. Data. The insistence that what could be measured could be managed.

She began with a system. Date, time, location (by stop number or, if mid-route, by nearest cross street), duration (her best estimate—fractions of seconds, most of them), and phenomenon (her worst language for what she'd seen). The first entries were cautious:

> *3/14, 6:23a, b/w Stop 7–8. Jacket color shift (navy → red → navy). Intercept: partial sentence, female voice, irritation re: repeated conversation. Duration: <1s. Taste: metallic.*

> *3/14, 6:31a, Stop 12 (Civic Center). Ad panel text/image shift. Intercept: partial sentence, indeterminate source, re: portraiture/fear. Duration: <1s. Taste: metallic, fainter.*

She called them *stutters* because the word was right. Reality had stuttered. It had caught on something—a burr in the film, a scratch in the groove—and for a half-beat the underlying track had bled through before the needle righted itself and the song went on.

Within a week she had filled four pages. Within two weeks she had developed a shorthand:

> **S** for stutter (the visual glitch—the coat, the sign, the shifted detail)
> **I** for intercept (the thought that rode in on the stutter's back)
> **C** for correction (the moment reality smoothed over, leaving no visible trace except the trace in Darya)

She began to sort them. She discovered categories. She named them with the grim, fond efficiency of a woman who names stray cats she can't afford to take in:

**Flickers**: visual only, no intercept. A lamppost moved six inches to the left then back. A passenger's hair, brown for one frame, then gray, then brown again. No thought arrived. Just the seam showing, briefly, like a hem coming undone.

**Bleeds**: full stutter with intercept. The real payload. A visual glitch accompanied by a caught thought—always fragmentary, always mid-stream, always concerned with something the thinker had apparently decided not to think, or been prevented from thinking, or had thought and then been relieved of by whatever mechanism ran the corrections. These were the ones that stayed with her. These were the ones that tasted like copper and rust.

**Soft Edits**: no visual glitch at all—just the intercept, arriving without a perceptible seam, as if the correction had gotten smoother, faster, almost seamless, but still not fast enough to outrun Darya's attention. These frightened her most. They meant the system—she had started thinking of it as a system—was adapting. Getting better at hiding. But not, yet, better enough.

She graded the intercepts on what she called, in her private lexicon, the **Wince Scale**, which ran from 1 (petty, venial, the kind of thought you'd be embarrassed to admit but not ashamed) to 5 (the kind of thought that, if spoken aloud, would empty a room).

Most were 1s and 2s. A woman looking at the teenage boy across the aisle and thinking *—bet he ends up just like his father, you can see it in the—*. A man staring at his own reflection in the window and thinking *—told her I was at the—*. The small, squalid negotiations of interior life, the ugly rough drafts that people revise into something presentable before they let the thought complete itself. Except these people weren't revising. The revisions were being done *to* them. The stutter was the moment before the editor's pen touched the page, and Darya was reading the first draft.

There were 3s. She logged them carefully. A regular—a man she'd always liked, a retired letter carrier who smelled of cedar and always asked about her day—who, in one stutter, had directed at the young woman in the wheelchair a thought so reductive, so casually annihilating, that Darya had gripped the steering wheel as if the bus had hit a pothole. The correction came. The man smiled. He held the door button for the woman in the wheelchair as she exited. He was kind. He was kind and he had also, for one unedited instant, not been kind at all, and Darya did not know which fact to file under *truth*.

She logged a 4 once. She did not write down the content. She wrote only: *4. Won't say. Correction immediate.* She underlined *immediate* twice.

She never logged a 5. She was not sure whether this was because no one on the 71 route had ever thought something that terrible, or because the system edited 5s so fast that even she couldn't catch them, or because she was, unconsciously, refusing to look.

But not all the intercepts were ugly.

This was the thing she had not expected, the thing that complicated the taxonomy and resisted the Wince Scale entirely and sat in her chest like a stone she couldn't cough up.

A woman at the Elm Terrace stop—not Velvet, someone else, someone Darya didn't know well—had been looking at her sleeping toddler in the stroller and in the stutter Darya caught: *—love you so much it makes me sick, it actually makes me feel sick, like I'm going to die from it, like it's going to—*

Correction. The woman's face smoothed. She adjusted the stroller's canopy with a calm, competent hand and boarded the bus and sat down and was fine. She was fine, because the correction had taken the rawness—the animal, annihilating, almost pathological tenderness—and replaced it with something manageable. Something a person could carry to work without weeping on the bus.
