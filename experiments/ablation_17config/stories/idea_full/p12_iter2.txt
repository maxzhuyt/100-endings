# The Republic of Magdalena

## I.

We came in from the north on the second day of October with three mules and a cask of water that had started leaking somewhere around the Cerro Prieto and by the time we reached the fort had lost a third of itself to the ground behind us. I told my uncle about the cask and he said it would be fine. I told him we were down to maybe twenty gallons and he said there would be water at the fort. There was no water at the fort.

What there was: four adobe walls, three of them mostly standing, and the fourth reduced to a hip-high ridge of crumbling mud brick that a man could step over without adjusting his stride. No roof. There had been vigas once — you could see the sockets in the wall tops where the beams had seated, spaced about two feet apart, each hole now home to some species of wasp that had better claim to the place than we did. Mesquite had come in through the collapsed north wall and established itself in what had been, according to my uncle, the central courtyard. The mesquite was ten or twelve feet high. It had been there a while. One of the remaining walls bore scorch marks that ran from the ground to about the height of a man's chest, and above the scorch marks someone had painted an eagle, or what I took to be an eagle. The paint had gone to salmon and the shape was ambiguous. It could have been a hawk. It could have been a turkey. It had one wing raised and one wing folded and it faced east, or possibly it had originally faced west and the adobe had sloughed away in a manner that reversed it. My uncle said it was the seal of the Republic. He said it faced south, toward Mexico City, as a gesture of defiance. It did not face south. I had a compass in my kit and it faced east-northeast and I said nothing about it.

We picketed the mules in what shade the south wall threw and I unloaded the packs while my uncle walked the perimeter with his hands clasped behind his back like a man inspecting a property he was considering purchasing. He went around twice. He tested the standing walls by pressing his palm flat against them and pushing, the way you'd test a fence post, and the walls did not move, and he seemed both pleased and unsurprised, as though the Republic's infrastructure had vindicated his long faith in it. Then he chose a spot along the east wall, in the shade, and unrolled his blanket there, and I hauled the remaining water and the cook box and the panniers full of dried beef and flour and coffee through the gap where the north wall should have been and stacked them in the courtyard near the mesquite.

Nobody else was there.

My uncle had told me we'd been summoned. He'd used that word — *summoned* — and he'd said it with the particular weight of a man who wanted it understood that the summoning had been mutual and urgent, that the others needed him, that this reunion had been years in the negotiating and had at last achieved the necessary critical momentum. He had shown me the letters. They were real letters, written by real men, and they did contain language about the desirability of one final gathering, but they also contained language about sore backs and the cost of travel and the difficulty of crossing the border now that the border meant something, and if you read them with a compositor's eye — which is the only eye I have — the enthusiasm in those letters was of the type where each man matched the previous man's tone precisely, escalating nothing, retreating from nothing, everyone holding the temperature exactly where the last man had set it so that no one would be guilty of either proposing or refusing. The gathering had convened itself the way weather does. No one had caused it. No one could have stopped it. No one actually wanted it.

I laid out my own bedroll against the west wall, as far from my uncle as the geometry allowed. When the others arrived they would do the same. I knew this because I knew these men, or rather I knew my uncle, and they were all my uncle.

The sun went down behind the broken wall and the sky turned the color of a bruise and the fort filled up with blue shadow and I made a fire in the courtyard from mesquite deadfall and cooked beans and my uncle ate without comment and afterward sat smoking and looking at the walls as though the walls were looking back and the two of them were having a conversation from which I was excluded.

I am a typesetter. I was then and am now. I set type for the *St. Louis Demokrat* and before that for the *Daily Missouri Republican* and I have been doing this work since I was fourteen and my hands know it the way a blind man's hands know a familiar room. I mention this because it is the only thing about me that matters to this story. A typesetter sees error. That is the whole skill. You read with the part of your brain that expects words to be correct and simultaneously with the part that knows they won't be, and you hold both readings at once, and the gap between them is where the work lives. I have spent my life in that gap. I do not say this with pride. It is a occupation and it has made me the way it has made me and the way it has made me is inconvenient at gatherings like this one, because I cannot hear a man tell a story without typesetting it in my head, and the errors jump out at me like a wrong-font letter in a column of clean Caslon, and once you have seen a wrong-font letter you cannot unsee it, and you cannot un-hear a lie that doesn't set clean.

## II.

Colonel Dunlevy came first. He came from the west, which was the wrong direction if he'd traveled from Tucson the way my uncle said he would, and he came on a horse so old and swaybacked that the colonel's boots nearly dragged the ground on both sides, and he came alone, and he came slowly. My uncle saw him from a quarter mile out and shaded his eyes and said, "That'll be Dunlevy," and stood up and tucked his shirt in and ran his fingers through what remained of his hair.

I watched the colonel approach. It took a long time. The horse was in no hurry and the colonel was in less. When he reached the fort he sat for a moment looking up at the walls the way a man looks at a building where someone has recently died, and then he dismounted, and the dismounting was a process that took its own long time — left foot out of the stirrup first, a pause, a hand on the cantle, another pause, a controlled slide that ended with both feet on the ground and both hands still on the saddle and a stillness that lasted long enough for me to understand he was waiting for his knees to accept the new arrangement. Then he turned and my uncle was there and they shook hands and clapped each other's shoulders and the colonel said, "By God, Clell, you old bastard, you look just the same," and my uncle said, "Dunlevy, you son of a bitch, I'd have known you anywhere," and both statements were so remote from the truth available to any functioning pair of eyes that they could only be understood as incantation — ritual words whose purpose was not to describe but to consecrate, to establish that the past tense in which these men knew each other still had jurisdiction here.

The colonel had brought his own whiskey, two bottles of it, wrapped in an old shirt in his saddlebag. He had packed one change of clothes. He had packed for two days. My uncle had packed for a week.

When my uncle went to see about the colonel's horse I was left alone with the colonel for ten minutes, and in those ten minutes the colonel told me his knees were no good, that the ride had been longer than he remembered, that he had almost not come, and that he was glad he had because the others — he said "the others" the way you'd refer to a congregation — had been writing about nothing else for three years and it would have been wrong to let them down. He said this while lowering himself onto a stack of adobe bricks with the care of a man handling something fragile. Then he looked at the fort and said, "Smaller than I remember."

Harlan Pace came next, five hours later, from the south, which meant he had crossed the river somewhere downstream and ridden up through Mexican territory, which meant he had risk enough in him for that but I would learn that this was the last of his risk and he had spent it getting here. Pace was thin in the way of men who had once been wiry and were now just reduced. He rode a good horse, better than Dunlevy's, and he dismounted without difficulty but then stood holding the reins and looking at the fort as though confirming a suspicion. My uncle shaded his eyes. "That'll be Pace," he said. The same tucking of the shirt. The same fingers through the hair. Pace came through the gap in the north wall and there was handshaking and back-clapping and my uncle said, "Harlan, by God," and Pace said, "Clell, you old bastard, you haven't changed a bit." The words were not identical to Dunlevy's but they were set from the same case.

Pace had packed for three days. He had brought no whiskey. When my uncle went to help with the horse, Pace told me his back was no good, that he had almost not come, and that he was glad he had because the others had been so keen on it.

"Your uncle especially," he said. "He's been writing me about this for years."

"Has he," I said.

"Oh, yes. Wouldn't let it rest."

Dr. Holbrook arrived near dusk, from the east, on a mule, carrying a medical bag that was either very old or very new — I could not tell because the leather was uniformly black — and wearing a coat too heavy for the climate, buttoned to the neck. He dismounted the way Dunlevy had, slowly and with strategic pauses, but where Dunlevy's difficulty was in the knees, Holbrook's was in the hips, a stiffness that turned his legs to a single rigid unit so that he came off the mule like a plank being tilted against a wall. "That'll be the doctor," my uncle said, and went through the procedure again, the tucked shirt, the smoothed hair, the handshake, the back-clap, the exchange of lies about who had and hadn't changed. Holbrook said, "I almost didn't make it — the crossing was a bastard," and then, when the others were out of earshot, told me his hips were no good and he had almost not come and the others had really wanted this.

"Pace especially," he said. "He's written me four letters about it this year alone."

I said nothing. I was beginning to see the shape of it.

The last to arrive was John Royce Burke, who came the next morning from the north, the same direction we had come, riding a horse I recognized as rented because it had the particular dull resignation of a livery animal. Burke was the youngest of the five. He was fifty-three. He was also the only one who had brought a gun, a Colt Navy revolver in a holster so stiff with age and disuse that the leather had molded itself shut around the weapon like a fist and I doubted whether the gun could be drawn without cutting the holster off. He wore it on his right hip. It was the first thing Dunlevy looked at when Burke dismounted and there was a silence of about half a second in which everyone looked at the revolver and then looked away, and then the handshaking commenced, the back-clapping, the you haven't changed, the same words set in the same order from the same depleted case of type.

Burke had packed for four days. He had brought whiskey — a single bottle — and also a ham, which he presented with the ceremony of a man producing a gift he had worried about, and my uncle said it was a hell of a ham, and Dunlevy said it was, and Pace said he hadn't seen a ham like that in some time, and Holbrook said nothing about the ham because he was looking at the gun.

Alone with me, hauling his saddle into the shade, Burke said the ride had been rough on his shoulder — an old wound, he said, from the campaign, meaning the Republic — and that he had almost not come.

"But the others," he said.

"Yes," I said.

"They've been after me for years."

"So I understand."

He looked at me then, or rather he looked at the space just beside my face, the way men do when they want credit for eye contact without the labor of it.

"Your uncle most of all," he said. "Clell's the one who put this together. Wouldn't give it up."

"He told me you summoned him," I said, and I should not have said it — it was a compositor's error, setting the truth where custom called for a blank space — but Burke only blinked and said, "Well, it was mutual."

Five men in five corners of a roofless fort. Each performing arrival. Each confessing reluctance. Each locating the motive force in the others. The gathering existed the way the Republic had existed — because everyone believed everyone else believed in it, and to test the belief would have been to dissolve the thing entirely, and none of them was willing to be the one who dissolved it, because the man who dissolved it would have to look at what was left, which was five aging men in a ruin in a foreign country with bad knees and stiff hips and a revolver that couldn't be drawn, and the looking would be very bad.

I cooked. I watered animals. I said nothing.

## III.

The fire that first night was mesquite, which burned hot and fast and smelled of the desert, and I kept it fed while the five of them arranged themselves around it in a loose semicircle, each man on his own bedroll or saddle, each holding his own cup — Dunlevy and Burke drinking whiskey, the others drinking coffee — and the talk began the way such talk begins, with small reconnaissance: questions about weather, routes, the state of the river crossing, the quality of the mules, the availability of supplies in Altar and Caborca, all the safe subjects that allowed each man to display competence without touching anything that mattered.

Then Clell — my uncle — cleared his throat.

"You know," he said, "riding in here, seeing the old place — it put me in mind of how we first came down. Dunlevy, you remember that ride from Guaymas."

"I do," said Dunlevy.

"Three days without decent water."

"Four," said Dunlevy.

"Three days," said my uncle, "and every one of us green as grass except Burke here, who'd done some prior service, and the rest of us not knowing a picket line from a clothesline, and yet when we reached this place and saw the potential — I said to myself, now hold on, Clell, you don't want to get ahead of yourself here, these men are fired up and somebody has to be the steady hand —"

I poured myself coffee. I had heard this part.

At Thanksgiving, in St. Louis, at the long table with the good cloth, my uncle told this story differently. At Thanksgiving my uncle was the visionary. At Thanksgiving my uncle was the one who saw the valley and said, "Boys, this is it, this is where we plant the flag," and the others had to be persuaded, cajoled, rallied. I had heard the Thanksgiving version nine times. I had set it in type in my head so many times that the plates were worn. In the Thanksgiving version there was no steady hand. There was only prophecy.

Here, at the fire, my uncle was the moderate. The reluctant statesman. The man who counseled patience while hotheads — he did not name them, but his eyes moved to Burke — pressed for the declaration.

"I said, now, boys, let's think about this clearly," my uncle continued. "A republic is not a thing you declare on a Tuesday and undo on a Wednesday. I wanted to be sure we understood the obligations. The, the — what would you call it —"

"The gravity," said Holbrook.

"The gravity. That's right. I wanted to be sure the gravity was felt."

Burke was looking at the fire. Pace was looking at his hands. Dunlevy was nodding, and the nod had the quality of a man keeping time to music rather than agreeing to facts.

My uncle talked for perhaps twenty minutes. His Republic was a thing of considered deliberation, of parliamentary procedure, of men who had studied their Locke and their Montesquieu (my uncle has not read Locke; I know this because I gave him a copy in 1866 and found it ten years later in the same place I had left it, with the pages uncut).
