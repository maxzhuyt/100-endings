# Maintenance

---

## I.

Jonah was saying something about the water bill—or the gas bill, one of the ones that arrives in the blue envelope—when the rain reversed.

I need to be precise about this. I wasn't looking at the window. I was looking at the bill, or at Jonah's hands holding the bill, or at the coffee ring on the counter from his mug, which he'd set down without a coaster the way he always does, and the part of my brain that tracks coaster violations was doing its quiet, tireless work. So when he stopped talking mid-syllable—not mid-sentence, mid-*syllable*, the second half of *kilowatt*—I looked up at his face first, not the window.

His mouth was open. Not in an expression I'd seen before. Not surprise exactly. More like the expression you'd make if someone asked you to locate a new emotion on a chart that didn't have a slot for it.

"Mar," he said.

I looked at the window.

The rain was falling normally. It was a Tuesday in November, 4:47 p.m. by the microwave clock, and the rain was coming down at the angle rain comes down when there's a mild crosswind from the east, which there was. Everything about the window was ordinary. I have confirmed this with myself many times since.

"It went up," Jonah said.

"What went up?"

"The rain. All of it. It went up."

He was gripping his coffee mug with both hands. The bill had fallen to the floor. He was looking at me the way people look at the only other person in a room where something impossible has happened, which is to say he was looking at me as though I were the last credible surface in the universe and if I didn't *hold* he was going to fall through.

"For how long?" I asked, because that is evidently what I do. Something rips a hole in the ontological fabric of a November afternoon and I ask for the duration.

"Six seconds. Maybe six seconds."

"You counted?"

"I don't know. It *felt* like six seconds. It felt—" He set the mug down. On the counter. No coaster. "It was like watching a video in reverse. Except it wasn't a video. And only the rain. The cars were still going the right way. The people were still walking forward. Just the rain. Going up."

I looked at the window again. Rain. Down. Correct.

"Okay," I said.

"Okay?"

"Do you want me to say it's not okay? I didn't see it, Jonah."

"I know you didn't see it."

"Then right now it's your word against"—I gestured at the window, the street, the entire apparatus of functioning physical law—"everything."

He nodded slowly. He picked up his coffee. He sipped it. It was still warm. I know because I touched the mug later, when he'd gone to the bathroom to splash water on his face, and it was still warm, and I held my hand against it for a long time.

Nothing else happened that evening. We ate the leftover pasta. We watched forty minutes of something on television I can't remember. We went to bed. The rain continued in its customary direction throughout.

That was the worst part—how obediently everything behaved afterward, as though the world had composed itself before company arrived and was now sitting with its hands folded, daring us to mention what we'd seen.

What *he'd* seen. I keep doing that. What he'd seen.

I am aware that this is the central problem. I have been aware of it from the first sentence. I am reconstructing events from Jonah's reports, from my own peripheral observations, and from the detailed, time-stamped, increasingly unhinged document I began keeping on my phone twenty-six hours later, and if you are concerned that this makes me an unreliable narrator, I want you to understand: so am I. I have been concerned about that every minute since this started.

But I'm the only one writing it down.

## II.

The first one came through the east wall of the apartment at 6:12 p.m. on Wednesday.

I need to describe this carefully because the word *through* is doing too much work. It didn't pass through the wall the way ghosts pass through walls in films—no shimmer, no translucence, no brief, tasteful superimposition. It came through the wall the way a person walks through a waterfall, except the wall was still *there*. The drywall didn't break. The paint didn't crack. The entity simply occupied the space where the wall was, and then the space where the wall wasn't, and the wall had the frantic, confused look of matter that has just been told it was optional.

Then the drywall cracked. Then the paint buckled. Then a four-by-six-foot section of our living room wall fell in slabs to the floor, as though reality had checked its notes and retroactively applied the physics that should have been active the whole time.

The entity itself—

I'm going to use the word *entity* because what else can I use. It wasn't a creature. It wasn't a machine. It was approximately eight feet tall and made of—the closest analogy is television static, except three-dimensional and damp-looking, like a cloud of visual noise that had been rained on. It had no face. It had no center of gravity. It moved the way an inflatable tube man moves outside a used car lot: flailing, overcorrecting, every limb (if those were limbs) swinging ninety degrees past its intended position like a drunk trying to pass a field sobriety test at a hundred times normal scale.

It hit the coffee table.

The coffee table exploded.

Not dramatically. Not with fire. It just stopped being a coffee table and became instead a large number of coffee table components distributed across the living room at speed. A leg hit the television. The glass top went through the window Jonah had been looking through the day before when the rain defected.

The entity didn't notice. It didn't notice the way a crane doesn't notice a traffic cone. It swung—lurched—*overcorrected* toward the center of the room, one static-arm pinwheeling through the bookshelf, and the books rained down with a sound that was almost comic, that *was* comic if you weren't present and couldn't feel the displacement of air from something enormous and indifferent moving through a space built for humans.

I was on the kitchen floor. I don't remember getting to the kitchen floor. I was standing by the stove and then I was flat on the linoleum behind the island, cheek pressed to a patch of dried pasta sauce I'd missed mopping, watching under the gap between the island and the floor.

I could see Jonah's feet. He was by the couch, backed against it, and the entity was swiveling its bulk toward him with the slow, seeking motion of a satellite dish finding a signal.

Then Jonah's feet weren't there.

The couch was there. It had been a two-seater. Now it was—

A three-seater.

It was immediately, obviously wrong. The couch had always been small, slightly too small for the room, and now it was comfortable, generous, exactly the size a couch should be if it had been a couch its entire life and had never been anything else. The cushions were the same gray-blue fabric. The legs were the same cheap birch from the same cheap furniture store. But there were three seats where there had been two, and the middle one—

I am trying to describe this without the language of horror because it wasn't horror, not yet. It was awe. It was the awe of watching a rabbit become a hat, or a hat become a rabbit, and understanding that the distinction between the two had never been structural. It had only ever been an agreement.

The entity swept its static-limbs across the room. I felt the air move. I pressed my face lower against the linoleum. It swung toward the couch and paused.

It stayed there.

For four seconds—I counted this time, I was counting now and I would keep counting, I would count everything—it stayed there, something like attention radiating from its centerless body toward the couch. The couch sat there. Three seats. Gray-blue. Ordinary.

The entity moved. It lurched away, knocked a lamp into the wall hard enough to dent the plaster, swung through the *west* wall—and this time the drywall came down simultaneously, as if the building had learned its lesson—and was gone.

Two holes.

One in the east wall, one in the west wall, each approximately four by six feet and shaped like nothing I can describe. Not shaped like the entity, because the entity had no consistent shape. Shaped like the *absence* of the entity, which is different. Shaped like the architectural record of something that had never committed to a geometry.

My ears were ringing. The apartment smelled like ozone and drywall dust and the peculiar burnt-sugar smell of a shorted fuse.

The couch rippled.

I don't mean the cushions moved. I mean the *fabric* of it ran like water, and Jonah was there, on his hands and knees, gasping, naked from the waist up because his shirt had—I don't know what his shirt had done. Merged with the upholstery. Become a throw pillow. It was gone.

He was shaking so hard I could see it from the kitchen.

"Did it—" he said.

"It left," I said. "It's gone."

"Did it see me?"

"I don't think it—I don't think *see* is the right—"

"Mara."

"No," I said. "I don't think it found you."

He nodded. He kept nodding, the way people nod when the motion is no longer communicative and has become merely a way to keep some part of the body moving because if everything stops moving at once you'll die.

I pulled my phone from my back pocket. My hands were shaking but not as badly as his. I opened a new note.

*Wed, 6:12 PM,* I typed. *Entity entered through east wall. Approx 8 ft tall, visual static composition, no discernible anatomy. Damage: coffee table (destroyed), bookshelf (toppled), east wall (hole, ~4x6 ft), west wall (hole, ~4x6 ft), one lamp, one window. J. shifted into couch (2-seat to 3-seat, dur. approx 30 sec). Entity did not detect. Departed west. Smell: ozone + drywall + caramelized sugar(?). J. intact. Shirtless.*

I looked at what I'd written. It was insane. It was the most precise thing I'd ever committed to a document.

I typed: *My hands are shaking.*

Then I deleted that, because it wasn't data.

Then I typed it again, because maybe it was.

## III.

We couldn't stay in the apartment. Obviously. Two wall-shaped holes and a destroyed living room do not constitute shelter, or if they do, they constitute the kind of shelter that answers questions you didn't want the building inspector to ask.

Jonah wanted to go to his sister's. I said no. I said, and I was already thinking this way, I was already thinking in terms of *exposure vectors* and *pattern integrity*, that we couldn't go somewhere associated with his name, his history, his social security number, the paper trail of a life that had been, up until forty hours ago, a perfectly adequate simulation of permanence.

I said: *We need to stay moving.*

I did not say: *I need to keep watching you.*

I did not say: *If I stop watching, I won't be able to prove any of this happened, and if I can't prove it happened, it might un-happen, and if it un-happens, then the world is even less real than the rain suggested, because at least the rain only violated physics, whereas un-happening would violate the one thing I have left, which is sequence.*

We walked. It was 7:40 p.m., dark, cold in the way November gets cold in this city—not dramatic, just mean. Jonah was wearing my oversized cardigan because his shirt had become part of our couch's permanent collection, and he looked absurd, the sleeves ending three inches above his wrists, and I loved him with a ferocity that felt like a head injury.

We walked twelve blocks. He was jumpy. Every sound—a cab horn, a pigeon launch, a man coughing in a doorway—made him flinch and I could see the flinch travel through his body like a wave, and at the crest of each wave there was a flicker, a *suggestion*, as though his outline was briefly negotiable. His left hand went wooden for a half-second—I mean literally wooden, the grain visible, the knuckles becoming knots—before snapping back to skin.

"You need to eat," I said, because I didn't know what else to say, and because it was true, or at least it was the kind of true that belongs to the ordinary world where people eat when they're shaking and the act of eating means they are still people.

We went into a restaurant. Italian. Brightly lit, crowded, the kind of place with red-checked tablecloths and candles in chianti bottles and an owner who calls everyone *friend*. Jonah didn't want to be in public. I could see it in his posture—the hunch, the way he kept his hands in the cardigan pockets, the way his eyes tracked every diner as though each one might be made of static. But I pushed, because I thought normalcy might help. Because I thought he needed to be in a room full of people being people, eating and complaining and existing in the thoughtless, luxurious way that people exist when they have no reason to suspect their existence is anything other than guaranteed.

I was wrong.

We were seated at a table near the window. I sat facing the door. Jonah sat facing me, his back to the room, and I could see his shoulders pulling in, his whole body contracting into as small a target as possible.

We ordered water. We opened menus. Thirty seconds of something that felt almost like before.

Then I felt it before I saw it. A pressure change—the same ozone taste, the same electromagnetic cringe of the air. Through the front window I saw a shape moving down the street, wrong-shaped and wrong-sized, a second entity or the same one, barreling along the sidewalk with the lurching, pinwheeling gait I already recognized, sending a fire hydrant skidding into a parked car without slowing down, without noticing, without understanding that fire hydrants and parked cars were things that existed at its scale.

"Jonah," I said.

He felt it too. I could see the panic hit him like voltage—his eyes went wide, his skin went pale, and then his skin went *wrong*, a ripple passing over his face like wind over water.

"Don't," I said, but it was already happening, and the word *don't* was as useful as a throw pillow against a tidal wave.

He stood. He jerked his chair back. The entity was at the restaurant window now, its static-body pressing against the glass, and the glass was holding because glass doesn't understand that it shouldn't be able to hold, and ignorance is a kind of strength.
