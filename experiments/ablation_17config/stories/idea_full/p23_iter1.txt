# This Year

## I.

The patient is a fifty-three-year-old male presenting with end-stage hepatocellular carcinoma, diagnosed eight months prior, with metastasis to the peritoneum and left lung confirmed by CT in October. He was referred to hospice by Dr. Amina Hassoun at St. Luke's after electing to discontinue sorafenib following two cycles. The referral notes describe the patient as "compliant and realistic about prognosis." Insurance is through his employer. Emergency contact is listed as a nephew, Brian Cowell, relationship confirmed by patient during phone intake.

I parked in a loading zone because the lot behind his building was chained and the street was permitted. When I buzzed his unit he said, "Third floor, sorry about the stairs." He hadn't mentioned the parking, not yet. That came when he opened the door, before he said hello. "I should've warned you about the lot, they chain it on weekdays, I'm sorry about that." He was standing in the doorway of a studio apartment at 40 Delancey, Unit 3R, and he was the color of a legal pad.

Dennis Raymond Cowell. Date of birth: March 14, 1971. Weight according to his last outpatient visit: 141 pounds, down from a reported baseline of 185. He looked lighter than 141 to me, but I didn't have a scale yet. I'd bring one Thursday.

"Come in, come in." He stepped back and gestured at the room as if it were larger than it was. It was not large. A twin bed against the far wall, made up tight with a flat sheet and a brown commencement-style blanket, hospital corners. Army or habit, I didn't know yet. A folding metal chair, the kind you'd find in a church basement, positioned next to the bed and angled toward a television that sat on two milk crates, one white, one blue, stacked and aligned carefully. A window with plastic blinds, half open. Lino floor, clean. The smell was Lysol and, beneath it, something hepatic—sweet and faintly rotted, the metabolic odor of a liver in failure. You learn that smell. It doesn't leave a room even when the window's open.

"Sit down, please." He meant the folding chair. He lowered himself to the edge of the bed with the careful, breath-held motion of a man whose abdomen has become a foreign country—any sudden movement and the fluid shifts and the pain blooms somewhere new.

I told him I'd stand for now, that I needed to move around the apartment as part of my assessment. He nodded like that made sense. It did make sense. It was also a way of not taking his only chair.

Medication reconciliation: Oxycodone 5 mg every four to six hours as needed for pain, ondansetron 4 mg as needed for nausea, lactulose 20 g twice daily, spironolactone 100 mg daily, furosemide 40 mg daily. All bottles present, all current, organized on the kitchen counter in a line—not in a pill organizer, just in a row, labels facing out, like products on a shelf. I counted the oxycodone. The count was right. He was either taking them as prescribed or not taking them and tracking the count. I've seen both.

"Pain level today, Dennis? Scale of zero to ten."

"Three. Maybe a four when I get up too fast."

His abdomen was distended and firm under palpation. Mild to moderate ascites. Skin jaundiced, sclera markedly icteric. Bilateral pitting edema in the lower extremities, 2+. Blood pressure 98/62. Heart rate 88, regular. Respirations 18, unlabored at rest. He held still for all of it with the patience of someone accustomed to being handled by institutions, medical or otherwise—DMV patience, waiting-room patience, the posture of a man who has spent a lifetime in lines.

The kitchen was a three-foot strip of counter with a two-burner stove and a half-size refrigerator. I opened the fridge as part of my nutritional assessment. A rotisserie chicken, partially eaten, still in its plastic clamshell. The sell-by date was five days past. Mustard, ketchup, a bottle of Italian dressing, a half-empty jug of store-brand cranberry juice. The cranberry juice was recent. Everything else looked archaeological—condiments from the old world when he still ate things that required condiments. I noted the protein deficiency concern and the need for supplemental nutrition consult.

"Do you have someone who shops for you, Dennis?"

"I get by. Brian drops off the chicken sometimes."

Brian. The nephew. The emergency contact. Drops off a rotisserie chicken sometimes.

I finished the intake. I went over the plan of care, the visit schedule, what to expect as things progressed. I explained the comfort kit—the medications we'd leave in the home for emergencies, the morphine and the Ativan and the atropine drops. He listened carefully and nodded at appropriate intervals. He asked one question: whether I'd need to park in the loading zone every time or if he should talk to the landlord about the lot chain. I told him I'd figure it out.

"Thank you for coming," he said. "I know it's a drive."

It was eleven minutes from the office.

"Thank you," he said again, at the door. And then he apologized a second time for the parking.

I sat in my car and opened his chart on my laptop. Under "Home Environment" I typed: *Patient lives alone in studio apartment, adequately maintained. No safety hazards identified. No adaptive equipment currently in use. Recommend bedside commode and shower chair for next visit. Patient is alert, oriented x4, cooperative, and appears to manage ADLs independently at this time. Limited social support identified. Emergency contact is nephew, involvement appears limited. Patient is a good historian and appropriate in affect.*

I closed the chart. I sat there a moment longer. Then I pulled out of the loading zone and drove to my next patient, a seventy-eight-year-old woman in Bridgeport whose family had built her a hospital room in the living room and rotated in shifts so she was never alone.

## II.

Third visit. A Tuesday. Dennis's abdomen was larger and his ankles were worse and I was adjusting the diuretic with the physician's verbal order when the buzzer sounded. Dennis pulled himself up from bed and pressed the intercom. "Yeah."

"It's Brian."

Dennis buzzed him in without saying anything else, then lowered himself back to the mattress edge. He ran a hand over his head—no hair left, not from chemo but from years of shaving it to the scalp, a choice that now, with the weight loss and the jaundice, made him look monastic and too young at the same time, a sick child playing at being an old man. He looked toward the door and composed his face into something neutral.

Brian was forty, maybe forty-two. Barn jacket, clean boots, a digital watch. He entered the apartment without knocking because the door was unlocked—Dennis had unlocked it between the buzzer and Brian's arrival, a small act I noticed because everything Dennis did in that apartment was small and deliberate and oriented toward someone else's convenience. Brian carried a manila folder and nothing else. No food. No bag. Just the folder, held at his side like a clipboard.

"Hey, Uncle Dennis."

"Hey, Bri. This is my nurse. She's—she's here Tuesdays and Thursdays."

Brian looked at me and nodded—not dismissively, but with the particular blankness of someone who has decided that everything in this apartment belongs to a category he will deal with only once. "Thanks for taking care of him," he said. I told him of course.

Brian did not sit on the bed. He did not pull the folding chair closer to Dennis. He remained standing and opened the folder on the kitchen counter, two feet from the row of medication bottles, and began.

"So I talked to the landlord. He says the lease has a sixty-day notification clause, but you're month-to-month now anyway so we just need thirty days' notice after—when the time comes." A brief stall on the phrasing. "Do you know if you've got a security deposit on file?"

"Should be. First and last, I paid when I moved in. 2004."

"Okay. And the—he mentioned a laundry key? For the basement unit?"

"On the hook by the door. There's a building key too. Same hook."

Brian wrote something in the folder. He asked about the car—Dennis had a car? Dennis had a car. It was parked at his sister's house, had been since September, since he couldn't drive anymore. Title was in the glove box. Brian wrote this down. He asked about bank accounts. One checking, one savings, both at TD, both with Brian listed as beneficiary per the paperwork Dennis had done in November. "At the bank on Grand?" Brian asked. "The one by the AutoZone?" Dennis confirmed. He asked about a will. Dennis said there was one, notarized, in the filing box under the bed, along with the DNR and the power of attorney and the insurance card and a copy of his birth certificate.

"You're organized," Brian said, and it was the first thing he said that wasn't a question or a logistics entry. It hovered in the room for a moment, available for warmth, but neither of them reached for it. Dennis looked at a spot on the floor near Brian's boots and said, "I had some time."

Brian closed the folder. He tapped its edge against the counter twice, squaring the pages. He looked once around the apartment—a scan, an appraisal, not a farewell—and said he'd be in touch about the rest. "You need anything?" he asked, already re-shouldering whatever he'd put down to come here.

"I'm good, Bri. Thanks."

"Okay." He turned to me at the door. "Is there a number I should have? In case—for the medical stuff?"

I gave him my card. He studied it, slid it into the folder, and left. His boots were quiet on the stairs, then gone.

Dennis sat on the bed for a while. The room was very still. I finished recording the diuretic adjustment in my notes and dated the new medication label and I let the silence hold because silence, in this work, is often more useful than language. People fill it when they're ready or they don't fill it. Either way you have to let it exist.

"Can I get you some water?" Dennis asked. He was looking at me with an expression of mild concern, as though I'd been the one enduring something.

"I'm fine, Dennis. Thank you."

He picked up the remote from beside his pillow and turned on the television. A game show. Wheel of Fortune or something shaped like it—the bright colors and the applause and the wheel ticking through its numbered slots. He turned the volume down to a murmur, just enough to populate the room with human sound, and settled himself carefully against the wall behind his bed. His eyes were on the screen but not tracking. The television was not entertainment. It was a weather system—something to fill a room's climate so the room would stop asking questions.

I packed my bag. I confirmed Thursday's visit time. He thanked me. He did not mention Brian's visit. He did not mention the lease, the laundry key, the car at his sister's house, the filing box under the bed that contained the organized paperwork of his own administrative death. He watched the wheel spin and the letters appear and the contestants guess at words, and when I closed the door behind me I could still hear the faint, tinny applause leaking into the hallway, filling nothing.

## III.

I found the cake on a Thursday.

I was looking for the supplemental nutrition shakes I'd ordered through the medical supply company—they'd been delivered to the building's front entrance Tuesday, and Dennis said he'd brought them inside, but when I checked the counter and the fridge, there were no shakes visible. I opened the upper cabinet, the one above the stove, and there it was.

A supermarket sheet cake in a clear plastic clamshell, the kind with a hinged lid and a barcode sticker on the side. White frosting. A piped blue border. No writing, no name, no message—just the decorative edge and a blank white field in the center where the bakery worker would normally pipe "Happy Birthday Whoever" or "Congrats Grad" or "We'll Miss You Donna." The frosting had hardened and cracked in places like old paint. The clamshell was slightly fogged with condensation that had long since dried into ghostly streaks. I picked it up. It was light. The cake inside had begun pulling away from the walls of the container, shrinking as it dried, leaving a gap of stale air around the edges.

The barcode sticker had a date. It was from March.

I put it back. I found the nutrition shakes in a plastic bag behind the milk crates, where Dennis had stored them out of what I think was an instinct not to clutter the counter—his counter, his kitchen, his life, always kept clear, always maintained as if for inspection by someone who never came.

Dennis was on the bed, propped up today because the ascites was pressing on his diaphragm and lying flat left him short of breath. I'd brought a second pillow, foam-wedge, from the supply closet at the office. He'd accepted it with the gravity of someone receiving a gift they hadn't requested and weren't sure they deserved. He was wearing a gray T-shirt with a small embroidered logo on the chest—the kind grocery store employees wear under their smock vests. The shirt was clean and too large now, hanging off shoulders that had narrowed since October.

"Dennis, when's the last time you had a real meal? Something more than the shakes."

"I had some soup. It was—I don't know. Last week, maybe."

"What kind of soup?"

"Chicken noodle. The can kind. I used to keep a lot of it here but I let it run out."

"Do you want me to add some to the next grocery order?"

"That's okay. I'm not—I don't have much interest in it."

This was expected. The appetite goes early with hepatic cancers. The body begins refusing fuel in stages, each refusal a border crossed. First the solids, then the soups, then the shakes, then the sips of water, then nothing. You can chart the trajectory. I've charted it dozens of times.

"The cake in the cabinet," I said. I didn't plan to say it. It came out the way things sometimes come out in this work—you spend an hour inside someone's silence and then a detail pushes through.

Dennis looked at me. A very slight rearrangement of his face, not alarm, not shame. Something closer to being caught at something he hadn't realized he was doing. "Oh," he said. "That."

"It's been there a while."

"Yeah. I should probably throw it out. I just—" He stopped. He looked at the television, which was off. He looked at the window. "I work at Garvey's. The grocery store on North Main. Or I did. Thirty-one years."

"Thirty-one years."

"Started in '93. I was twenty-two. Stock and receiving, first. Then I got moved to bakery-deli when Manny retired. I did that for—must be twenty years now."

I sat in the folding chair. I didn't take notes. There are moments in this work where the chart can wait. The patient is talking. You let them talk.

"You know how many cakes I've written on? The custom orders. People call in the morning, they want a quarter sheet, they want buttercream, they want it to say"—he gestured with one hand, a piping motion, and I saw his wrist, thin and bruised from a blood draw I hadn't ordered—"'Happy 40th, Marco,' or 'Welcome Home, Sis.' The birthday ones are most of it. Weekends you're doing fifteen, twenty birthday cakes. Saturday is the big day. Everyone's born on a Saturday, apparently."

He smiled. It was a real smile, the kind that comes when you're describing competence.

"I got fast at it. The writing. You have to get the pressure right or the letters blow out, get too fat. I had good pressure. Manny taught me before he left—he said the trick is you breathe out slow while you're piping, same speed as the bag, so your hand and your lungs are doing the same thing. I could do a full 'Happy Birthday' in one pass, no lifting."

"That sounds like a skill."
