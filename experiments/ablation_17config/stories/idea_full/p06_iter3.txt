# The Evaluation

---

## I.

**FORENSIC PSYCHIATRIC EVALUATION — PRELIMINARY NOTES**
**Case No. 81-­4297**
**Subject: BACHMEIER, Marianne Louise**
**Evaluator: Dr. R. Brandt**
**Date of Assignment: 19 March 1981**
**Referral Source: Landgericht Lübeck, Third Criminal Chamber**

---

The file arrived on a Tuesday, in an interdepartmental envelope the color of weak tea. I note this because I have since been asked, by colleagues and once by a journalist I declined to speak with, when I first became aware of the case. The answer is that I had been aware of it the way every person in the Federal Republic had been aware of it — through newspaper headlines, through arguments at dinner tables I was not seated at, through the sound of the name *Bachmeier* spoken on radio broadcasts I half-heard while shaving. Aware the way one is aware of weather in an adjacent city. Informed and untouched.

The envelope contained a letter from Judge Ewald Henkel, a sixteen-page prosecutorial summary, a thinner defense brief, a manila folder of police photographs I will describe in a moment, Grabowski's prior psychiatric evaluations (there were four, spanning eleven years, authored by three different colleagues — one of whom I had studied under), and a VHS cassette tape in a black plastic case with a strip of masking tape on which someone had written, in blue ballpoint, *Gerichtssaal 6.III.81*.

I read the prosecutorial summary first. I will reproduce its relevant passages not because the facts are unfamiliar — they have by now been published so widely that I expect no reader requires my recitation — but because the language interests me. The language has become my subject.

*On 6 March 1981, at approximately 10:15, during the third day of criminal proceedings against Klaus Grabowski (b. 1940, Lübeck) for the murder of Anna Bachmeier (b. 1973, d. 1980, Lübeck), the defendant's mother, Marianne Bachmeier, produced a .32 caliber Beretta Model 70 semi-automatic pistol from her handbag and discharged it seven times in the direction of the accused. Three rounds struck Grabowski in the back, causing injuries from which he died during transport to Universitätsklinikum Lübeck. Bachmeier was immediately restrained by court officers. Upon arrest, she made the following statement: "I wanted to shoot him in the face, but I only got his back."*

I read this paragraph four times. Each reading increased its strangeness. Not the act — the act was by then already familiar to me, already smoothed by repetition into a kind of cultural furniture. What struck me was the word *produced*. The prosecutorial style demanded it: she *produced* a pistol the way one *produces* a document, an exhibit, a piece of evidence. As though the Beretta were a footnote she had been keeping in reserve. As though the courtroom were functioning precisely as designed, and this was simply the next item to be entered into the record.

I turned to the police photographs. I will not describe them at length. I am not interested in performing a sensitivity I may or may not possess. The relevant photograph, for my purposes, was not of Grabowski's body on the courtroom floor — though I examined that image with the attention my training requires — but of the gallery seating. Row three. A handbag, dark leather, standing upright on the wooden bench, its clasp open. Behind it, slightly out of focus, the back of the bench in row two. On the wood, a gouge — old, not related to the event, a mark left by someone's ring or buckle years before. I do not know why I noticed it. I note it here because my training has taught me that the details we are drawn to before we know why we are drawn to them are often diagnostic.

I then read the defense brief, which was competent and unmemorable, and then the four prior psychiatric evaluations of Klaus Grabowski.

I will summarize them in the order they were conducted.

**1969, Dr. F. Hammerstein, Schleswig-Holstein State Psychiatric Facility:** Grabowski, then twenty-nine, was evaluated following his first conviction for the sexual assault of a minor. Dr. Hammerstein diagnosed antisocial personality disorder with paraphilic features — specifically, pedophilia, exclusive type, attracted to females. He recommended indefinite institutional commitment. He noted in his conclusions: *The probability of recidivism, in the absence of long-term structured intervention, approaches certainty.*

Grabowski was sentenced to six years. He served four and a half.

**1974, Dr. M. Lauterbach, Forensic Outpatient Services, Lübeck:** Assessed as a condition of supervised release following a second offense — indecent exposure to a child — Dr. Lauterbach's evaluation was briefer and more clinical. He noted that Grabowski had "voluntarily submitted" to surgical castration during his second incarceration. ("Voluntarily" appeared without quotation marks in the original. I have added them.) Dr. Lauterbach concluded that the castration, combined with outpatient supervision, "substantially mitigated" the risk of future contact offenses. He recommended continued release with quarterly check-ins.

**1977, Dr. M. Lauterbach (same):** A follow-up evaluation, conducted after Grabowski's general practitioner prescribed testosterone replacement therapy for symptoms including fatigue, depression, and bone-density loss — symptoms, that is, of the castration that was the sole basis for his supervised release. Dr. Lauterbach noted the hormone therapy. He noted that testosterone replacement would, pharmacologically, reverse the effects of castration on sexual drive. He noted this in a single subordinate clause in the fourth paragraph of a six-paragraph document. His recommendation was unchanged: continued release, quarterly check-ins. He described Grabowski as "cooperative" and "future-oriented."

**1979, Dr. K. Weiss, Independent Forensic Assessment, Hamburg (defense referral):** Conducted after Grabowski was investigated — investigated but not charged — for an incident involving a nine-year-old girl in his apartment building. Dr. Weiss's evaluation was the longest and, in my professional judgment, the most thorough. It was also the most disturbing, though not for the reasons one might expect. Dr. Weiss identified the hormonal reversal, documented Grabowski's resume of predatory behavior with precision, and concluded that Grabowski "continues to present a significant risk to children in unstructured community settings." His recommendation was residential treatment in a secure facility.

Two pages later, in an addendum dated six weeks after the primary evaluation, Dr. Weiss noted that his recommendation had been reviewed by the Lübeck court and declined on procedural grounds. The supervised release conditions remained in effect.

I have reproduced the evaluative history at this length because it is, in a sense, the history of my profession as applied to this case. Four psychiatrists. Eleven years. A clear consensus, even among the more permissive assessors, that Grabowski was dangerous. And the machinery — the courts, the release boards, the supervisory apparatus — processed their findings and produced, as its output, a man living unsupervised within four hundred meters of a children's playground.

Anna Bachmeier was seven.

I turned off the desk lamp. I inserted the VHS cassette into the department's player, a Grundig unit on a rolling cart that was typically used for training videos. The monitor was small, fourteen inches, and its color balance leaned toward green.

The footage was not, as I had expected, from a fixed court camera. It was a news crew's tape — West German television, ARD or ZDF, I do not remember which — filming the third day of the Grabowski trial for a segment that would never air as intended. The image was unsteady. The camera was pointed at the defendant's bench, where Grabowski sat with his back partially to the gallery, his attorney beside him, a uniformed officer to the left. The audio was poor. Someone — a judge, a prosecutor — was speaking, but the words were garbled by the room's acoustics and the microphone's position.

Marianne Bachmeier was not visible.

Then she was.

The camera did not find her immediately. In the first frame of her appearance she was already standing, already in motion, and the camera swung to catch up with the sound — because there had been a sound, not a shot, not yet, but something, a shifting of weight, a collective intake of breath from the gallery that preceded the event and which the microphone caught as a low, oceanic murmur. The camera found her in profile. Dark hair. A coat. One arm extending. The handbag was not visible — it had been set down or dropped, I could not tell.

The first shot was inaudible on the recording. What I heard was a percussive distortion, a clipping of the audio, and then a woman's scream from elsewhere in the gallery, and then the second and third shots in rapid sequence, and then a chaos of motion — bodies rising, falling, the camera swinging wildly, a flash of the ceiling, the oak paneling of the courtroom wall, then the floor, then nothing. The tape ran on for another forty seconds of heaving, unfocused footage before cutting to black.

I rewound. I watched again. This time I ignored the sound and focused on her arm. The extension was not hurried. She was not lunging. The arm came up and forward with a steadiness that — I noted in my first clinical impression, which I wrote immediately after — was "inconsistent with a dissociative or fugue state, and more consistent with rehearsed or purposeful motor action." Even with the tape's poor resolution I could see that her wrist did not waver. Three shots. The arm came down. She did not fire the remaining four rounds in the magazine.

I rewound. I watched again.

This time I watched her face. The resolution was insufficient for detailed analysis. I could make out no expression. The face was a pale oval, turned in profile toward Grabowski, and it did not change — not during the shots, not after, not when the officer reached her. I noted: *"Affect flat throughout recorded event. No visible startle response to report of own weapon. Consistent with either extreme dissociation OR extreme resolve. Diagnostic differentiation will require direct clinical interview."*

I rewound. I watched again.

I do not know how many times I watched it. The department logbook, which I was required to sign when using the equipment, indicates I checked out the Grundig at 4:20 p.m. and returned it at 6:55 p.m. Two hours and thirty-five minutes. The tape's relevant footage lasted eleven seconds.

I can account for this discrepancy clinically. Repeated viewing of traumatic material is a standard forensic technique, used to identify micro-expressions, sequential details, and behavioral markers that are not apparent on first exposure. There is nothing unusual about an evaluator spending extended time with evidentiary footage.

I note, however, that my clinical notes from the fourth or fifth viewing contain the following entry, which I initially wrote and then struck through with a single line — insufficiently, as it remains legible:

*~~The arm. Steady. How was she so~~*

Below that, in smaller script:

*Administer MMPI-2, SCID-II, and Rorschach per standard forensic battery. Schedule first clinical interview no later than 25 March.*

I locked the file in my desk drawer, returned the Grundig to the departmental closet, and drove home. My wife asked about my day. I said it had been routine. At dinner I found I could not hold my water glass without a slight, persistent tremor in my right hand — the hand I write with, the hand that would hold the pen in Marianne Bachmeier's presence. I attributed it to fatigue. I did not note it in the file.

I am noting it now.

## II.

Karl Möller visited me on the Thursday before my first scheduled session with Bachmeier. I had not invited him. He appeared at my office door at half past ten with two cups of coffee from the faculty lounge, which he carried with the ease of a man who has never doubted his welcome.

Möller was the senior forensic consultant to the Third Criminal Chamber. We had served together on an evaluation panel in 1977 — a diminished-capacity case involving an arson — and he had been, on that occasion, thoroughly competent. He was perhaps fifty-five, with a face that seemed designed for reassurance: a broad brow, reading glasses he wore on a chain, a slight softness to the jaw that suggested patience. His hands, I remember, were very clean. The nails filed round. He set the coffee on my desk and seated himself without being asked, crossing one leg over the other, and he smiled, and the smile was the kind you give to a younger colleague whose work you respect but whose judgment you are not yet certain of.

"You've received the Bachmeier file," he said.

"Monday." 

"And you've reviewed the materials?"

"In full."

"Good. Good." He sipped his coffee. "Then you know it's not a simple case."

I said I was still forming my initial impressions.

"Of course. Of course. I wouldn't presume to steer your assessment. You know that, Richard." He paused. I did know that. I also knew that the sentence *I wouldn't presume to steer your assessment* is only ever spoken by someone who is about to attempt precisely that. "But I thought it might be useful to share some context. The landscape, so to speak. Things that don't appear in the file."

I told him I would appreciate the context.

What followed was a monologue of approximately twenty minutes, delivered in the warm, unhurried cadence of a man addressing a graduate seminar. Möller spoke about the political climate. He spoke about the public mood — the rallies outside the courthouse, the letters to the editor, the opinion polls showing that seventy percent of West German citizens believed Bachmeier should be acquitted or given a suspended sentence. He spoke about the pressure on the judiciary, the upcoming state elections, the tabloid headlines. He spoke about what he called "the institutional optics" — his phrase, not mine — and what it would mean for the court if the psychiatric evaluation concluded that Bachmeier had acted with full premeditation and unimpaired capacity.

"No one is asking you to fabricate a finding," he said. He held up one hand, palm out, as though I had accused him. I had not spoken. "No one would dream of it. You are scrupulous, Richard, everyone knows this. What I am saying — and I say this as a friend, not as a consultant — is that in a case like this, the evaluator has a degree of interpretive latitude. The diagnostic categories are broad. 'Diminished capacity' is not a binary. There is a spectrum. And a finding that locates her — even partially — within that spectrum would serve everyone. The court. The prosecution. The defense. And, frankly, Bachmeier herself. Because the alternative, Richard, the alternative is that she is found fully competent and fully deliberate, and then we are in the territory of a murder conviction, and then this woman who has already lost her child—"

He stopped. He had the grace, at least, to hear himself. He looked at his coffee.

"The finding should reflect the clinical reality," he said, more quietly. "That's all I'm saying. But clinical reality, in a case like this, is not uncomplicated. There's room. That's all I'm saying. There's room."

I asked him whether he had reviewed the prior evaluations of Grabowski.

"I'm familiar with the history, yes."

I asked him whether he was familiar with the 1977 Lauterbach evaluation specifically — the one that noted the testosterone replacement and recommended no change in supervision.

He recrossed his legs. "Lauterbach was operating within the guidelines at the time."

"The guidelines."

"Forensic practice has evolved, Richard. You know that. We all know that. What was considered adequate supervision in the mid-seventies—"

"Four hundred meters."

He paused. "I'm sorry?"

"Grabowski lived four hundred meters from the Fröbelplatz playground. After the hormone replacement. After Weiss's recommendation for secure residential treatment was declined. He lived four hundred meters from where Anna Bachmeier played."
