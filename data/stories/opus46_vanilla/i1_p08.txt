# Stolen Moments in Roma

The coin hadn't even hit the water.

Ben was watching it arc — a perfect parabola, really, the kind of trajectory he might have calculated on a napkin if anyone had asked — when the world became noise. A Vespa engine screaming like a furious cat, the shriek of rubber on wet travertine, and a voice, female, loud, delivering what sounded like either a prayer or a string of profanity so elaborate it could have been poetry.

He stumbled backward. The guidebook — *Fodor's Rome, 14th Edition*, dog-eared to page 203, "Baroque Fountains and Their Hydraulic Engineering" — flew from his hands and landed spine-up in a puddle.

The motorcycle stopped close enough that he could feel the heat of its engine through his khakis.

"*Ma sei completamente pazzo?*" The rider yanked off her helmet. Dark hair spilled out, chaotic, alive. She had a face like a Renaissance painting that had gotten into an argument with modernity and lost — sharp cheekbones, a crooked nose, eyes so dark they swallowed light. "You stand in the *middle* of the street reading a *book*?"

"I — that's — this is a pedestrian area, actually," Ben managed, pointing vaguely at a sign he hoped existed.

"Everything in Roma is a pedestrian area. Also everything is a road. You learn this or you die." She looked down at his guidebook drowning in the puddle. "Ah. *Fodor's*. This explains."

"I'm terribly sorry about — well, you nearly hit *me*, technically, but I do apologize for —"

"You apologize to the person who almost kills you." She tilted her head, studying him like a specimen. "English."

"British."

"Same sadness, different rain."

He laughed before he could stop himself. She noticed. Something shifted behind her eyes — a door opening a crack.

Her Vespa was a battered navy Piaggio, the kind that Roman couriers rode like they were auditioning for their own funerals. Along the left flank ran a spectacular scratch — a jagged lightning bolt of exposed metal through the paint, bordered by a smear of what appeared to be bright tangerine orange, as though she'd sideswiped a traffic cone or a sunset at high speed. She caught him staring at it.

"Don't ask," she said.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were composing the question in your head. I could hear it." She swung off the bike and picked up his guidebook, shaking water from its pages with a grimace. "Page 203. Fountains. You came to Roma to read about fountains while standing in front of one?"

"I'm an academic. It's — it's sort of what we do."

"This is the saddest thing anyone has ever said to me, and a man once cried into my pasta." She thrust the ruined book back at him. "I'm Alessandra."

"Ben."

"Okay, Ben." She said his name like she was tasting something she hadn't decided about yet. "You want to see Roma? The real one? Not —" she flicked the guidebook — "this autopsy?"

---

He should have said no.

He said no, actually. He said, "That's very kind, but I have a lecture to prepare on Bernini's use of negative space —"

She was already handing him the spare helmet.

---

Trastevere, 11:47 a.m. She took corners like the laws of physics were suggestions. Ben held on to her waist — *her waist* — and tried to remember the Italian word for "slower," which was *più lento*, which he shouted twice, which she ignored twice.

They stopped at a market stall where an old man sold artichokes the size of fists. Alessandra spoke to him in rapid dialect, laughing, gesturing. Ben understood perhaps one word in five. While the vendor turned to bag their selection, Alessandra's hand drifted — casually, almost absently — across a display of small ceramic saints. When her hand returned to her side, it was empty.

Or appeared to be.

Ben blinked. She met his gaze with an expression of absolute innocence.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing. Did you just —"

"I just *what*?"

"Nothing."

She bit into a raw artichoke leaf and grinned.

---

The Aventine Keyhole, 1:15 p.m. She pushed him toward the door of the Priory of the Knights of Malta.

"Look through."

He looked. Through the tiny keyhole: a perfect, impossibly framed view of St. Peter's dome, floating at the end of a green corridor of hedges. He exhaled.

"That," she said quietly, standing close enough that he could smell her perfume — something sharp, citrus, slightly dangerous — "is not in your book."

"It's actually on page 178."

She punched his arm. He deserved it.

---

A café near Campo de' Fiori, 3:30 p.m. She ordered for him without asking. Two espressos, two cornetti, a small carafe of something amber that turned out to be grappa and nearly dissolved his soft palate.

"So," she said. "Professor. What are you running from?"

"I'm not running. I'm on sabbatical."

"Same thing. Different grant money."

He turned his cup in its saucer. "A marriage. Well — the end of one. Eighteen months ago."

"Ah." She didn't offer sympathy. She offered him the second cornetto. It was better.

"And you?" he ventured. "What do you run from?"

"Standing still." She stole a sugar packet from the next table — literally, while its occupant was mid-sip — and pocketed it without breaking eye contact with Ben. "It's a medical condition."

---

The Ponte Sisto, sunset. They walked. He didn't know when walking had replaced the Vespa, only that at some point she'd parked it and they'd continued on foot, and the absence of velocity had made the city louder and softer simultaneously.

She stopped at the center of the bridge. The Tiber below was gold and filthy, beautiful the way only ruined things could be.

"You're staring," she said.

"Sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be honest. Why are you staring?"

"Because I can't figure you out."

"Good." She leaned on the railing. The stolen ceramic saint — San Cristoforo, patron of travelers — appeared in her hand as though conjured. She placed it on the stone ledge between them. "A gift. For surviving today."

"You *did* steal it."

"I *liberated* it. There's a difference. He was unhappy on that shelf."

Ben picked up the small figure. It was warm from her pocket. "Alessandra."

"Hmm?"

"I haven't prepared my lecture."

"No."

"I don't care."

She looked at him then — really looked, the mischief in her face settling into something unguarded, almost startled, as though she'd set a trap and caught herself in it. The moment hung. A scooter whined across the bridge behind them. Someone somewhere was singing.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Same fountain. Don't stand in the road this time."

She walked away without waiting for an answer, one hand raised in a wave that might have been a dismissal or a promise. He watched her go — quick stride, leather jacket, chaos incarnate — until she turned a corner and Rome swallowed her whole.

He looked down at San Cristoforo in his palm.

He was in terrible, wonderful trouble.
