# The Weight of Iron and Laurel

The dream dissolved the way it always did—not all at once, but in fragments, like ash lifting from a dying fire. My mother's face. The smell of barley porridge in a clay pot. The particular blue of the Thracian sky in autumn, so different from the pale, hazy wash that hung over Rome like gauze over a wound.

I opened my eyes to the familiar dark of the cell.

Not a cell. They called it a chamber. The lanista was particular about that. "You are athletes," he would say, pacing before us with his vine staff tucked under his arm like a centurion, "not prisoners." But the door locked from the outside, and the window was the width of my forearm, and the distinction between athlete and prisoner seemed to live only in his mouth.

I sat up on the straw pallet and pressed my bare feet against the stone floor. Cool. Always cool, even in summer, even when the streets above baked and the fountains ran warm and the city stank of its own excess. Down here in the ludus, beneath the belly of the Flavian Amphitheatre, the earth remembered some older, cooler world.

My left shoulder ached. It had ached since the Dacian's shield rim caught me there six days ago—a bruise that bloomed black and yellow across my skin like a grotesque flower. Pavo, the Greek physician who tended us, had packed it with a poultice of crushed comfrey and vinegar. The vinegar smell still clung to me. It mixed with the deeper smells of the ludus: sweat soaked into wood, the iron tang of old blood in the sand of the practice yard, olive oil rubbed into leather, and beneath it all, the faint sewage breath that rose from the drains whenever the wind shifted.

I reached beneath my pallet and found the small carved figure I kept there. Hercules. Not the polished marble Hercules of the temples, draped in his lion skin with one perfect foot forward, but a rough thing I had whittled myself from a scrap of oak, its features worn smooth by years of handling. I held it in my palm and closed my eyes.

*Hercules Invictus. You who labored. You who were enslaved and yet became divine. Remember me.*

It was not a prayer exactly. It was more like a conversation with someone who understood.

I tucked the figure into the fold of my subligaculum and stood.

---

The morning meal was taken in the common hall—a long, vaulted room where forty-three of us sat on stone benches and ate from wooden bowls. The barley porridge was thick, sweetened with dried figs and mixed with ash, which Pavo insisted strengthened the bones. It tasted of chalk and resignation. Beside me, Corax—a Gaul with arms like mooring ropes and a laugh that could shake dust from the ceiling—was telling anyone who would listen about the woman who had thrown her stola into the arena after his last fight.

"Silk," he said, holding up a fig as though it were evidence. "Tyrian purple at the hem. A senator's wife, I'd wager my teeth on it."

"Your teeth aren't worth wagering," muttered Ferox from across the table. He was Numidian, quiet, with a scar that split his left eyebrow into two pale arches. He had killed eleven men in the arena. We did not speak of this. We did not speak of the number for anyone. It was an unwritten law among us, as sacred as any decree the Senate had ever passed.

I ate slowly, letting the sounds of the hall wash over me. Somewhere a man was humming. Spoons scraped against wood. A guard's hobnailed sandals clicked on the stone corridor outside. These were the sounds of my life now—had been for seven years, since the slavers pulled me from the wreckage of my village and sold me to a trader in Thessalonica, who sold me to a lanista in Capua, who sold me to the ludus here in Rome. Each transaction recorded on paper, stamped and filed, as orderly as the Empire itself. Rome was nothing if not organized in its cruelties.

The Emperor Domitian had recently declared himself *Dominus et Deus*—Lord and God. The guards whispered about it. Even the lanista seemed uneasy, though he would never say so aloud. There was a tension in the city I could feel even from down here, a tautness like a bowstring drawn too far. Senators disappeared in the night. New statues appeared in the forum, golden, enormous, Domitian's face rendered with the serene confidence of a man who believed his own divinity. I had seen true divinity—or what I imagined it to be—in the way lightning split the sky over the Rhodope Mountains when I was a boy, in the way my mother sang while she worked, in the carved Hercules beneath my hand. It did not look like Domitian.

After the meal, we were led to the practice yard for morning exercises. Not combat drills—those came in the afternoon. This was stretching, lifting, running in tight circles around the perimeter of the sand court. The physician watched from the colonnade, noting which of us favored a limb, which of us breathed too hard. We were investments. A trained gladiator cost as much as a small farm. They kept us alive the way a merchant keeps his best amphorae intact during transport—not out of love, but out of arithmetic.

I ran, and the morning light came down through the open roof of the yard in long golden columns, and the sand was soft beneath my feet, and for a few moments I could almost forget. The body has its own memory, its own joy. Muscles warming, lungs filling, the rhythm of footfall and breath—these things belonged to me. Not to the lanista, not to the Emperor who called himself God, not to the crowd that would scream my name or curse it.

I touched the oak figure at my hip.

*Hercules. You who carried the world and set it down again.*

*Teach me how to set it down.*
