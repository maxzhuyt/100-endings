# Love in the Limelight

## Chapter 4: Shelter

The bell above the door hadn't rung properly in three years—it produced more of a consumptive rattle—but it did its job. Elin Pryce looked up from her inventory spreadsheet to find a man pressing his back against her door like he was barricading a castle.

He was wearing sunglasses indoors. In Llandeilo. In November.

"We're closing in twenty minutes," she said.

"Perfect. I only need ten." He pulled the sunglasses off, and she felt a small, annoying detonation somewhere behind her sternum. She knew that face. Everyone knew that face. Rhys Maddox—the Rhys Maddox—was breathing hard in her shop, peering through the gap in her window display of secondhand Penguins.

"There's a fire exit through the back if you've murdered someone," she said, returning her eyes to the spreadsheet.

"Nothing so dramatic. Just two blokes with cameras who've been following me since the Texaco on the A40." He stepped away from the door cautiously, as though it might swing open and swallow him. "You'd think rural Wales would be safe."

"You'd think."

He waited. She could feel him waiting. She'd seen enough interviews to know the routine—the rakish half-smile, the conspiratorial lean, the assumption that everyone within a five-metre radius was already a little bit in love with him. She added a number to her column. Carried the one.

"So," he said, drifting toward the counter. "This is a lovely shop."

"You haven't looked at it."

"I'm looking now."

"You're looking at the door."

He laughed—a real one, surprised out of him. "Fair point. I'm Rhys, by the way."

"I know who you are. Welsh Literature is against the back wall if you're feeling patriotic. Travel is by the window. Self-Help is in the middle, which is probably where you should start."

He put a hand over his heart. "Wounded."

"You'll recover. You're very resilient. I read it in *Vanity Fair*."

He leaned both elbows on the counter, close enough that she caught something expensive and woodsy rising off his jacket. Her pen stopped moving. She forced it to start again.

"And you are?"

"Elin. This is my shop. Those are my books. That's my cat on the biography shelf—don't touch him, he bites—and those are my closing hours on the door, which you didn't read."

"Elin," he repeated, as though tasting it. "Are you always this welcoming, or am I special?"

"You're not special. You're hiding in a bookshop. There's a difference."

Something flickered across his face—quick, nearly imperceptible—and she felt a twinge of guilt. She busied herself straightening a stack of bookmarks she'd already straightened twice.

He wandered. She pretended not to watch. He moved through the narrow aisles with an odd, almost boyish attentiveness, running his fingers along spines, pulling volumes out and reading back covers. Not performing. Actually reading.

"You've got a first edition Chatwin," he called from Travel.

"I know. It's not for sale."

"Everything in a shop is for sale."

"Not that."

He appeared at the end of the aisle, holding the book with both hands like something sacred. "Why not?"

She hesitated. "It was my mother's. She carried it across half of Patagonia in 1987, came back sunburned and heartbroken and full of stories. She said it was the only trip where she felt like herself instead of someone's wife or someone's mother. Just—herself." Elin shrugged, the gesture too tight. "She died four years ago. I can't open the book without smelling her hand cream. Nivea. The one in the blue tin."

He placed it back on the shelf with exaggerated care, adjusting its position until it was exactly as he'd found it.

"My mam kept every cinema ticket from every film I was ever in," he said quietly. "Stuck them in a scrapbook with those little photo corners. Even the terrible ones. Even *Nightfall 3*."

"*Nightfall 3* was atrocious."

"It really was." He smiled, but it was a different smile now—thinner, less practised. "She's still alive, my mam. She's in Swansea. I don't visit enough. Every time I go, she asks if people are being kind to me, and I never know what to say because—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Because the honest answer is that I've arranged my entire life so that everyone is kind to me, and I still don't believe any of it."

The shop was very quiet. Rain had started against the windows, soft and persistent, the way it always rained in Llandeilo—like the sky had made a commitment.

"That," Elin said slowly, "is the first honest thing you've said since you walked in."

"Noticed that, did you?"

"The charm offensive was hard to miss. You deploy it like a weather system."

"And you're what—waterproof?"

She met his eyes. Mistake. They were absurdly, unfairly green, and worse, they were looking at her with something that wasn't performance. Something tentative and searching and entirely too real for a rainy Tuesday.

"No," she said. "I'm just stubborn."

The rattle-bell convulsed. They both flinched. A teenage girl walked in, saw Rhys, and dropped her phone.

"Oh my *God*—"

"Right," Elin said briskly. "Out the back. Go left, past the bins, and you'll hit Church Street."

He grabbed his sunglasses. At the stockroom door, he turned.

"Can I come back? When I'm not fleeing?"

"Shop's open ten to five. Closed Sundays."

"That's not what I asked."

Her cheeks burned. The teenager was still frozen by the doormat.

"Bring coffee," Elin said. "I take mine black."

The grin he gave her was ridiculous—wide and unguarded and nothing like the one on the billboards. Then he was gone, out through her stockroom, past her bins, into the Welsh rain.

Elin exhaled. The cat on the biography shelf yawned.

"Shut up, Owain," she said, and closed the spreadsheet she'd stopped reading ten minutes ago.
