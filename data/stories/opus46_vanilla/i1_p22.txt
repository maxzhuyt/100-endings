# Prison Strings

A dim overhead light flickers in the prison corridor at irregular intervals. Each pulse casts the concrete walls in sickly yellow, then abandons them to shadow, then yellow again—a stuttering heartbeat for a place that feels barely alive. Jolyne Cujoh sits on the edge of her cot, unwinding a thin thread of string from her fingertip, letting it coil on the floor like a sleeping snake. She's been in this facility for three days, and something is wrong.

Not prison-wrong. She knows prison-wrong. She's lived prison-wrong. This is different.

The inmates here don't talk. They move through the halls with glassy, satisfied expressions, performing their routines with mechanical contentment. They smile when they eat. They smile when they sleep. They smile when the woman with the auburn hair walks past them on her morning inspection, clipboard in hand, heels clicking a rhythm that every prisoner seems to sway toward like flowers tracking the sun.

Jolyne doesn't sway.

"You must be the new transfer."

The voice comes from the end of the corridor—soft, warm, the kind of voice that wraps around you like a blanket before you realize it's smothering you. Jolyne looks up. The woman stands just inside the flickering light's reach, her ringed yellow eyes catching each strobe with an unsettling stillness. Auburn hair, neat and professional. A tailored suit that doesn't belong in any prison Jolyne has ever seen. A smile that reaches her mouth and stops dead.

"Makima," the woman says, extending a hand. "I'm the facility's rehabilitation director."

Jolyne doesn't take it. She lets the string curl back into her finger, her green eyes narrowing. "Rehabilitation. Right. Is that why everyone in here acts like they got lobotomized?"

Makima's hand lowers without a trace of offense. "They're at peace. Isn't that what rehabilitation means? Finding peace with your circumstances."

"Peace." Jolyne stands, and she's shorter than Makima but carries herself like she's eight feet tall. "Lady, I've been in prison before. I've seen broken people. Those aren't broken people out there. Those are *empty* people."

The light flickers. In the half-second of darkness, Jolyne swears Makima's smile doesn't move at all—as though it's painted on.

"You're perceptive," Makima says. "I read your file. Jolyne Cujoh. Framed for murder. Father largely absent. You have every reason to be distrustful." She tilts her head, and those ringed eyes perform something that resembles sympathy. "I understand."

"You don't understand shit about me."

"Perhaps not yet." Makima steps closer. The air pressure in the corridor seems to shift, something heavy and invisible pressing against Jolyne's chest. "But I'd like to. Your ability—Stone Free, isn't it? The power to unravel yourself into string. To deconstruct your own body and reconstruct it at will." Her eyes brighten with genuine interest—the first real emotion Jolyne has seen from her. "That's remarkable. A being that can't be bound because it *becomes* the binding."

Jolyne's blood goes cold. "How do you know about my Stand?"

"I know many things about the people in my care."

The thread is already out. Jolyne doesn't remember summoning it, but Stone Free's string laces between her fingers in a razor-thin web, and she feels the Stand materializing behind her—humanoid, powerful, ready. "Let me make something real clear," Jolyne says, her voice dropping low. "I don't know what you are. But I know what you're *not*. You're not a prison director. You're not a counselor. And you're sure as hell not someone who *cares*."

Makima regards her the way a person regards a dog that has learned an unexpected trick.

"You're right," she says simply.

The admission hangs in the air like smoke.

"I'm the Control Devil." Makima says it the way someone might say *I'm a Capricorn*—lightly, without weight, as though the horror of it is someone else's problem. "Every person in this facility belongs to me. They gave themselves willingly. All it takes is the right pressure applied to the right desire. Everyone wants to be relieved of their freedom, Jolyne. Freedom is exhausting."

"Bullshit."

"Is it?" Makima steps closer again, and the pressure intensifies. Jolyne feels it now—a compulsion crawling up her spine, whispering that it would be easier to kneel, to stop fighting, to let someone else decide. "Your father left you. The justice system failed you. Every institution meant to protect you became a cage instead. Wouldn't it be simpler to choose the cage? To love it?"

Jolyne's hands tremble. The string vibrates.

Then she laughs.

It's sharp and loud and entirely inappropriate, and it cracks the atmosphere like a rock through glass. "You think *daddy issues* are gonna work on me? Lady, I punched my way through a maximum-security prison, fought a priest trying to reset the goddamn universe, and nearly died for a father who couldn't say 'I love you' without a Stand doing the talking." She whips the string forward—it lashes around a ceiling pipe, pulls taut, and Jolyne launches herself backward, out of Makima's radius, landing in a crouch ten feet away. "If there's no Mickey, this shit ain't Disney. And you? You're not even a convincing cartoon villain."

Makima stares at her. The smile is gone. What replaces it is worse—a blank, porcelain stillness, those ringed eyes unblinking.

"Interesting," Makima murmurs. "You can't be controlled because you've already chosen what to live for. The string doesn't just deconstruct—it *connects*." A pause. "I'll enjoy finding what happens when I cut those connections one by one."

"Try it," Jolyne snarls, Stone Free blazing behind her.

The light flickers. When it returns, Makima is gone.

But the pressure remains—faint, patient, coiled around the edges of the corridor like a leash not yet tightened. Jolyne stands in the stuttering dark, string wound between her fingers, heart hammering, and knows with absolute certainty:

This prison has no walls she can punch through.

The war will be slower than that.
