# The Weight of Names

## From the Field Notes of Genly Aran-Tai, Second Envoy of the Ekumen to the World Called Dust

I had been on Dust eleven days when I broke protocol.

The formal reception at the Hall of Lineages had lasted six hours. Six hours of sitting cross-legged on basalt while the Tall Ones—they called themselves the Anakhim, the children of giants—recited their genealogies in voices that resonated somewhere below my sternum. Across the hall, separated by a channel of running water that was either decorative or symbolic or both, the Kort sat in their own silence. They were small, dense-boned, dark-adapted. Miners' children. Eight generations in the deep shafts had made them something new.

Two subspecies. One world. The briefings had been very clear about the nature of the tension: resource allocation, territorial governance, the usual failures of unlike bodies to recognize like minds. The briefings had been wrong about nearly everything else.

I left through a service corridor. No one stopped me. The Anakhim guards, who stood nine feet tall and moved with the slow ceremonial gravity of herons, did not seem to register my departure. I was neither tall enough to be one thing nor short enough to be another. I was merely foreign.

---

The market district of Ur-Kaleth descended in concentric terraces toward the old mines. The architecture was stone—everything on Dust was stone, calciumite andite and basite, the whole world a geologist's fever dream—and every surface bore carvings. I stopped at the first terrace wall and ran my fingers over the glyphs. A woman with wings. A man holding fire. A serpent coiled around a tree whose roots plunged downward into something that might have been water or void.

Prometheus. Icarus. Genesis.

Old Earth. Old myths, carried here in the seed-ships and carved into the bones of a new world, then slowly misremembered into something true.

At a stall where dried fungi hung in ropes like garlic, a Kort woman sold me a cup of broth. She had to reach up to hand it to me, and I had to crouch, and we met somewhere in the middle, laughing at the awkwardness. Her eyes were enormous, pupils wide as coins. She saw in the dark. I asked her, in my careful textbook Dustine, what the carved serpent meant.

"That's the Opener," she said. "It broke the world so the light could get in."

A child pressed against her leg—but the child was tall, thin-limbed, pale. An Anakhim child. Or half. The woman touched the child's hair with unselfconscious tenderness.

The briefings had not mentioned this.

I walked further. I saw more. Kort and Anakhim eating together at low tables. A tall boy carrying a small old man on his shoulders through a crowd, both of them arguing about the price of lamp oil. Graffiti on a terrace wall in two scripts, intertwined: *one root, one root, one root.*

The tension was real. I did not doubt that. But it was not the whole architecture of this place, any more than the serpent on the wall was only a serpent.

---

Command's rebuke came through the ansible relay at 0300, which meant someone had been watching. The language was procedural. The meaning was not. *You are not there to understand. You are there to represent.*

In the morning, they gave me my task: approach the Minister of Science, one Hazh Tennor of the Anakhim administrative lineage. A political posting, Command noted. Not a scientist. Handle with appropriate caution.

---

The Minister received me in a chamber cut deep into the second terrace. The walls were carved with the Dust Genesis cycle: the ships descending, the first shafts dug, the Splitting—that moment, half-remembered, when one people became two. Above the lintel, Prometheus again, flame in hand, but here his face was a skull.

Hazh Tennor sat behind a stone desk bare of everything except a single cup of water. She was Anakhim-tall, but old, bent at the shoulders like a wind-shaped tree. Her eyes were the palest grey I had ever seen in a human face.

"So," she said. "The offworlder who walks alone."

"I wanted to see the city."

"You wanted to see what we had not arranged for you to see." She lifted the cup, drank, set it down. "That is either honest or tactical. Which?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously. If you are honest, I can work with you. If you are tactical, I can work *around* you. But I need to know which tools to use."

I said nothing. She watched me with the patience of stone.

"You saw the market," she said. It was not a question. "You saw the mixed families. You think perhaps we have exaggerated our difficulties to the Ekumen. That we perform division for political advantage."

"I think the situation is more complex than my briefings suggested."

"Complexity." She smiled. It did not reach her eyes. "The Ekumen loves that word. It means *we do not wish to act.* Tell me, Envoy—when your briefings described two subspecies in conflict, did they mention that I have Kort grandchildren? Did they mention that the Splitting was not natural divergence but engineered? That someone, three hundred years ago, *chose* this for us?"

The room changed temperature.

"No," I said.

"No." She placed both hands flat on the stone desk, over carvings so old they were almost smooth. "The myths on our walls are not decoration. They are warnings. Someone stole fire. Someone opened the world. Someone made us *different on purpose*, and we have been trying to find our way back to each other ever since, and your Ekumen sends me a child who thinks a walk through a market constitutes understanding."

She stood. Even bent, she towered.

"Come back tomorrow, Envoy. Bring better questions."

I left through the carved doorway, under the skull of Prometheus, and I had no questions yet—only the old, hard weight of knowing how little I knew.
