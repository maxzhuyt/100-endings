# The Weight of Green Stone

The morning gun woke me as it always did — not with surprise but with a slow, spreading dread. My hammock was damp. Everything in Canton was damp. The Pearl River breathed its warm rot through every timber of the *Andromache*, and I lay there feeling the first familiar ache settle into my knees like rust into iron.

I found the jade monkey in my trouser pocket before I found my boots.

Midshipman Cole caught me at the gangway, his face pink and earnest in that way I'd grown to loathe. "Lieutenant Merrick, sir — Captain Elliot's asked for the harbour survey by—"

"By four bells, yes. I'm going ashore to take soundings at the Whampoa anchorage. I'll need the full watch."

"Shall I assign men to accompany—"

"I'll use local oarsmen. Cheaper and they know the channels." I was already descending. My thumb worked the smooth belly of the figurine, tracing the groove where the monkey's tail curled against its haunch. Some previous owner had rubbed the same spot — the stone was paler there, almost white. "Tell the Captain I'll have his figures tonight."

Cole's "Aye, sir" followed me down to the sampan like a dog I couldn't shake.

---

The factory district stank of tea and rendered tallow and the particular sweetness of overripe lychee sold from baskets along Thirteen Factory Street. I moved through the press of compradors and lascars and Parsee merchants, my uniform drawing glances I no longer bothered to interpret. A British naval officer walking alone into the Chinese quarter at half seven in the morning. Let them wonder.

Old Suen kept a paper shop on Hog Lane — at least that was what the painted board said. I pushed through the hanging strips of dried cuttlefish that served as his door curtain.

"Ah, Doih-wai." He used my Cantonese title, the one his regulars had given me. *Lieutenant*. He said it the way you'd say *old friend* or perhaps *old fool*. "Early today."

"I need to see Brother Yao."

Suen sucked his teeth. He was sorting joss paper into stacks, his fingers black with ink. "Brother Yao is not happy with you."

"Brother Yao is never happy. That's his particular gift."

"You owe him forty dollars, Doih-wai. Macao silver."

"Which is precisely why I need to see him." My knees had begun their deeper complaint — that grinding, liquid sensation as though the joints were filling with hot sand. I pressed the jade so hard its carved features bit into my palm. "Tell him I have a proposition. Not money. Better."

Suen studied me with the flat, commercial appraisal of a man weighing tea. Then he shuffled to the back.

---

Brother Yao conducted business from a room above a congee shop, cross-legged on a platform bed, a cat the colour of ash sleeping against his thigh. He was perhaps fifty. A scar divided his left eyebrow into two pale wings.

"The naval officer who borrows and borrows." He spoke passable English when it suited him, pidgin when it didn't. "Sit."

I sat. The cat opened one eye.

"Forty dollars is a small debt," I said. "I can erase it and earn credit besides. I know the patrol schedules for the next month — every cutter, every watch rotation between Lintin Island and the Bogue forts. I wrote them."

Yao said nothing for a long time. Downstairs someone was chopping something fibrous and wet against a board — a steady, metronomic sound that seemed to originate inside my own skull.

"You wrote them," he repeated.

"I can also rewrite them. Shift a patrol window — two hours, perhaps three. Enough for a fast crab-boat to make the passage without inspection."

"And if your captain discovers this?"

"He won't. Elliot trusts me. I'm decorated." The word tasted like copper. "Crimea. Well — not Crimea. The coast of Syria. I took a ball through the shoulder at Acre." I don't know why I said it. Yao didn't care about Acre.

He stroked the cat. "Two taels of Patna. Good quality. And your debt is clear."

"Three taels. And a pipe now. Here. I can't—" My hand was trembling. I flattened it against my thigh and felt the jade monkey press between flesh and bone like a third knuckle. "I can't do the work you need with my hands like this."

Yao looked at my hand. He looked at it the way a physician might — not with pity but with clinical interest in the progression of a disease.

"One pipe. Then you bring me the schedules before the evening tide."

---

The boy who prepared the pipe was twelve, perhaps thirteen. He rolled the bead of paste over the lamp flame with a practised, almost tender rotation, and I watched the dark pearl of it swell and glisten and I thought of absolutely nothing. That was the gift. The boy inverted the pipe over the flame and I drew, and the smoke entered me the way the river entered the sea — without boundary, without argument.

The ache left my knees. It left my shoulder where the Syrian ball had torn through. It left the place behind my eyes where Elizabeth's last letter still lived, the one that said *I do not know you in your letters anymore*.

I lay on the wooden platform and the sounds of Canton came to me scrubbed clean of meaning — the chopping downstairs, a woman singing in a dialect I couldn't name, the creak of sampans on the river. The jade monkey rested warm in my closed fist, and for twenty minutes I was the man I had been before I became the man I was.

Then the smoke thinned. The world reassembled itself — its debts, its schedules, its captains waiting for surveys I would never complete.

I sat up. I asked the boy for paper and ink.

I began to rewrite the patrols.
