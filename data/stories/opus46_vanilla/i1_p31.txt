# Gone South

## Chapter Two: Bourbon Street Breakfast

The saxophone is doing something unholy to a Coltrane tune and I'm standing on a sidewalk in New Orleans with eleven dollars in my pocket and last night's eyeliner smudged halfway down my face, probably looking like exactly what I am: a sixteen-year-old who made a series of choices.

Good choices. I think. Ask me again in an hour.

The morning air is thick—not Tennessee thick, which is like breathing through a damp washcloth, but something else entirely. Something that smells like river water and fried dough and old beer and jasmine all at once, and I can't decide if it's disgusting or the most alive thing I've ever inhaled. My stomach clenches. I haven't eaten since a gas station hot dog somewhere in Mississippi, which was already a questionable life decision at the time and is now staging a full revolt.

The sax player is a Black woman in her sixties, maybe seventies, seated on an overturned bucket outside a shuttered bar. Her case is open on the sidewalk, scattered with coins and a few crumpled bills. She catches me staring and doesn't stop playing, but something shifts in her eyes. Recognition. Not of me specifically—of the *type*. The lost kid type. The nowhere-to-go-at-7-AM type.

I keep walking.

Okay, Taylor. Think. You are in a city you have never been to. You have no phone because you left it on the kitchen counter so Mom couldn't track you. You have no plan beyond the word *away*, which felt like a complete sentence twelve hours ago but is starting to look like a fragment.

But God—*God*—I'm not in that house. I'm not sitting across from Dad's silence, which was worse than yelling, so much worse, that cavernous quiet where he just looked at me like I'd told him I had a terminal illness. I'm not listening to Mom on the phone with Pastor Rick, her voice all cracked and hushed, like she was reporting a death. *Taylor's confused. Taylor needs guidance. Taylor needs—*

Taylor needed to leave. So Taylor left.

I find a bench in Jackson Square and sit down and press my palms against my eyes until I see colors. The cathedral looms across the plaza, white and impossible, and tourists are already milling around with their beignet bags and their maps. I wonder what I look like to them. Probably invisible. Teenagers are furniture in places like this.

"You gonna cry or you gonna eat?"

I drop my hands. There's a kid standing in front of me—my age, maybe a year older, brown-skinned, wire-thin, with bleached-blond twists pulled up in a knot and a oversized Saints jersey that hangs almost to their knees. They're holding out half a beignet, powdered sugar drifting off it like snow.

"What?"

"Simple question. Cry or eat. I recommend eat. Crying's a waste of calories."

I take the beignet. I eat the beignet. It is, without exaggeration, the best thing I have ever tasted, and I say so with my mouth full, and the kid grins.

"Café Du Monde. I didn't buy it, before you get all grateful. Five-second rule from a tourist table." They sit down next to me without asking. "I'm Mika."

"Taylor."

"Cool. You running from something or running to something? Because this city eats both kinds alive, but it's polite to know which flavor of fucked you are."

I almost laugh. Almost. "Running from."

"Yeah." Mika stretches their legs out, crossing battered Jordans at the ankles. "Same. Eight months ago. Atlanta. Don't recommend going back."

I want to ask why. I don't ask why. There's a code to this, I'm realizing—a grammar of runaways that I don't yet speak but can feel the shape of. You don't ask. You offer, or you don't.

Mika offers. A little.

"My mom's boyfriend had opinions about how I should dress. And act. And exist." A shrug, performatively casual, but their jaw tightens. "So I developed an opinion about how far away from him I should be. Turns out the answer was four hundred miles."

I nod. The lump in my throat is back.

"You got somewhere to stay?" Mika asks.

"No."

"Money?"

"Eleven dollars."

"Damn." They whistle low. "Okay. There's a shelter on Rampart, but they ask questions and sometimes they call people. There's also a spot I know under the Claiborne overpass, bunch of us stay there, it's—" They pause, choosing words carefully. "It's okay. It's not *good*, but it's okay. You gotta watch your stuff. And there's this guy Marcus who says he helps kids find work but don't talk to Marcus. You understand what I'm saying?"

"I understand what you're saying."

"Good." Mika looks at me—really looks—and I see them clocking the smudged eyeliner, the chipped black nail polish, the way my hair falls across my face like a curtain I'm hiding behind. "You trans?"

My whole body goes rigid. Fight or flight, and I've already used up my flight.

"Hey. *Hey.* Relax. I'm not—" Mika holds up both hands. "I'm nonbinary. I'm literally asking because I know a clinic on Elysian Fields that does informed consent and they have a youth program. If that's something you need."

The air leaves me. All of it. I sit there on that bench in Jackson Square with powdered sugar on my fingers and the cathedral blazing white in the morning sun and a stranger who just became the first person to say the word *trans* to me without it sounding like a diagnosis, and something cracks open in my chest that I can't close.

"Yeah," I whisper. "That's something I need."

Mika stands, extends a hand.

"Then come on, Taylor. Let me show you how to survive down here."

I take their hand. The saxophone is still playing somewhere behind us—distant now, curling through the humid air like smoke, like a question, like the first notes of a song I don't know yet but am desperate to learn.

I came south to disappear. Maybe instead I'll become.
