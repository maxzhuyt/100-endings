# The Sisyphus Column

*Published weekly in The Tartarus Herald & Underworld Advertiser*

---

The boulder was at the forty-yard mark, which Sisyphus had come to think of as "mid-morning." He'd scratched a notch there centuries ago. It was a good place to pause, lean the shoulder in, and catch up on correspondence.

"Right," he said. "What have we got?"

Crow ruffled himself importantly and produced a letter from beneath one wing, which was a trick Sisyphus had never asked him to explain.

"First one's a repeater," said Crow. "Signed 'Drowning in Thessaloniki.'"

"Ah, her. Read on."

---

**Dear Sisyphus,**

**I wrote to you last month about my mother-in-law, who keeps rearranging my kitchen when she visits. You told me to let her have one cabinet. Well, she's taken three. She's also started on the garden. My husband says I'm overreacting. Am I overreacting?**

**— Drowning in Thessaloniki**

---

*Dear Drowning,*

*You are not overreacting. You are, if anything, underreacting, which is often the same thing viewed from the other side.*

*The cabinet advice stands, but I should have been clearer: the cabinet must have a lock. Not because your mother-in-law is a villain — she isn't, she's frightened that her son's life has a room she can't enter, and she's trying doors — but because a boundary that can't be felt isn't a boundary. It's a suggestion. And suggestions, in my experience, roll downhill.*

*As for your husband: he is not saying you're overreacting. He is saying "please don't make me choose." Tell him, gently, that you're not asking him to choose. You're asking him to build a fence. Fences, as the poets say, make good neighbours. They make even better in-laws.*

*Yours in solidarity,*
*Sisyphus*

**[Crow's note: She's going to need a bigger fence.]**

---

The boulder slipped two yards while he was dictating, which was about average. He put his back into it and recovered the ground. Somewhere above, a vulture circled with the pointless optimism of its kind.

"Next," he said, at the fifty-yard mark.

---

**Dear Sisyphus,**

**I have worked at the same trade for eleven years. I'm good at it. People say I'm good at it. I feel nothing. My friend quit her job to paint watercolours and says she's "never been happier," but she also borrowed money from me twice last month. Is passion a luxury? Should I just be grateful?**

**— Numb in Corinth**

---

*Dear Numb,*

*Gratitude is a fine quality, but it makes a poor anaesthetic.*

*Your friend is happy. She is also broke. These are both true, and neither one cancels the other, and I mention this only because people in your position tend to use other people's failures as permission to stay put.*

*You don't need to quit. You don't need to paint watercolours. But I'd ask you this: in eleven years, has the work ever surprised you? Not pleased you — surprised you. There's a difference. Pleasure confirms what you already know about yourself. Surprise means you're still being introduced.*

*If the answer is no, the problem isn't the trade. It's that you've stopped letting it ask anything of you. Take on the task that frightens you. Volunteer for the job nobody wants. If, after that, you still feel nothing — then yes, it may be time to go looking. But go looking with savings, not loans.*

*Warmly,*
*Sisyphus*

**[Crow's note: I once ate the same beetle eleven years running. No regrets. Beetles are consistent.]**

---

"You've never eaten the same beetle twice," said Sisyphus. "Beetles don't last that long."

"It's called *artistic licence*," said Crow.

The boulder reached the sixty-yard mark and began its familiar negotiation with gravity. Sisyphus leaned harder. His calves, which had long ago passed through pain into a kind of geological patience, did what they always did.

"Next letter. The short one."

---

**Dear Sisyphus,**

**How do I tell my wife I don't like her cooking?**

**— Cowardly in Argos**

---

*Dear Cowardly,*

*You don't.*

*You tell her you'd like to cook together on Thursdays. Then you cook something well. Then you let Thursday become the thing you do. In six months, she will either improve from proximity or — and this is the more likely outcome — you will discover that her cooking was never the problem, and that what you actually wanted was to stand next to her in the kitchen more often.*

*If it genuinely is the salt, buy a better salt and leave it where she can see it.*

*Practically yours,*
*Sisyphus*

**[Crow's note: I have eaten things that were already dead for a week. Perspective.]**

---

The boulder crested the seventy-yard mark. The summit was, as always, theoretical. Sisyphus had made his peace with theoretical summits long before the first letter arrived, carried by a confused Crow who'd been aiming for Athens and missed by several planes of existence.

"One more," said Crow. "This one's... colourful."

---

**Dear Sisyphus,**

**My lover is also my boss. My boss is also my best friend's ex. My best friend is also my landlord. I realise, writing this down, that my life has become a Greek tragedy. How do I untangle this without losing everything?**

**— Anonymous (PLEASE)**

---

*Dear Anonymous,*

*The good news is that Greek tragedies require a chorus, and you haven't got one yet, so there's still time.*

*Move out first. I cannot stress this enough. You cannot negotiate with anyone who controls where you sleep. Once you have your own door, the rest becomes merely complicated rather than impossible.*

*Then tell your best friend. Not because you owe her a confession, but because secrets are heavier than they look, and they get heavier the longer you carry them, and eventually they will come back down on you regardless of how far up you've pushed them.*

*After that, sit with your lover — in a neutral location, not the office — and ask the only question that matters: "What are we building?" If the answer is clear, build it. If the answer is a long silence, you have your answer.*

*You will not lose everything. You will lose some things. That's not a tragedy. That's a Tuesday.*

*With genuine hope,*
*Sisyphus*

**[Crow's note: I'd have led with "stop doing that" but apparently I lack nuance.]**

---

The boulder rolled back to the bottom at 4:47 pm, as it always did. Sisyphus walked down after it, unhurried, rolling his shoulders. The light in Tartarus was the colour of old bronze, and the evening was, by the standards of eternal punishment, rather pleasant.

"Same time tomorrow?" said Crow.

"Same time tomorrow," said Sisyphus, and placed his hands on the rock, which was still warm from the day.

It was, he reflected, nice to have a second thing.
