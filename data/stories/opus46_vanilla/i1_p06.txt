# Carnival of Dangerous Intentions

**Coney Island, New York — July 1937**

---

She stopped just before reaching the shooting gallery set up along the boardwalk. It was near dusk, and the carnival lights were buzzing on — sodium tubes sputtering to life in uneven intervals, so that the faces of passersby seemed to appear and vanish in strobed fragments. A woman's laughing mouth. A child's wide eye. The greased knuckles of a barker gripping his cane.

*His locks.* The words flashed through her mind again, like an intruder. Such an old-fashioned turn of phrase that kept surfacing, and something else — dangerously compelling — quickly suppressed, lest she question why she had returned here at all. This place where she had promised to meet the stranger — the young man with the obsidian curls and even darker eyes.

She didn't even know his name.

The shooting gallery cracked and popped. Tin rabbits shuttled along their wire track, and a boy no older than twelve pumped the lever-action with furious concentration. Behind him, the ocean exhaled its low, indifferent roar.

"You came back."

His voice arrived before she could locate him — low, unhurried, carrying the faintest accent she still couldn't place. Mediterranean, perhaps. Or merely the affectation of someone who had taught himself to speak carefully. He stepped from beside a painted column where a mermaid peeled in long strips of blue and green. The sodium light caught his jaw, then lost it.

"I almost didn't," she said. A lie. She had left her apartment on Flatbush Avenue two hours early and walked the long way to punish herself for her own eagerness.

"Almost." He smiled. "That's a word that does a lot of work, isn't it?"

*Don't smile back.* She smiled back.

He wore the same dark shirt from the night before, open one button too many at the throat. No jacket, despite the respectability the hour demanded. His sleeves were rolled to the elbow, forearms brown and corded, and he held a cigarette between his first and second fingers in the European fashion — not the American pinch. She noticed this because she noticed everything about him, cataloguing details the way one inventories the contents of a room one might need to escape from.

"Walk with me?" he asked.

"Which direction?"

"Does it matter?"

"It might."

He tilted his head, studying her with an amusement that bordered on something more proprietary. "East," he said. "Toward the Parachute Jump. Unless you're afraid of heights."

"I'm not afraid of heights."

"What are you afraid of?"

The question landed with unexpected weight. The sodium lights flickered — a long, arrhythmic pulse — and for a moment his face was entirely in shadow, only the ember of his cigarette marking where he stood. *I'm afraid of exactly this,* she thought. *Of standing in the dark with a man whose name I haven't asked because asking would make this real, and real things have consequences, and I have spent twenty-six years being exquisitely careful about consequences.*

"Cotton candy," she said. "The way it dissolves. Something that substantial shouldn't just disappear on your tongue."

He laughed — a genuine, startled sound — and offered his arm. She took it. His skin was warm even through the fabric of her sleeve.

They walked east. The boardwalk thinned as the main carnival receded behind them. Here the lights were fewer and more erratic, buzzing and snapping like trapped insects. Shadows pooled between the support beams beneath them, and through the gaps in the planking she could see the sand below, colorless in the failing light.

"You're not from Coney Island," she said.

"No one is from Coney Island. People wash up here."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer I'm giving you tonight." He flicked the cigarette over the railing. It spun, a tiny orange comet, and disappeared. "Ask me something else."

*Who are you? What do you want? Why did you speak to me last night as if you already knew me — as if you had been waiting, specifically, for me to walk past that particular booth at that particular hour?*

"What do you do?" she asked instead.

"I convince people of things."

"A salesman."

"Something adjacent."

She stopped walking. The Parachute Jump loomed ahead, its skeletal tower half-lit, riders shrieking as they plummeted in controlled descent. The sodium light above them popped, went dark, then surged back brighter than before, and in that flare she saw his face fully — the sharp, almost severe architecture of it, the heavy brows, the mouth that seemed designed for both cruelty and tenderness in equal measure. He was watching her with absolute attention, the way a jeweler watches a stone he is deciding whether to cut.

"You're doing it now," she said quietly. "Convincing."

"Is it working?"

*Yes.* The word was immediate and unwelcome. She felt it in her sternum, a warm, spreading pressure, as if something locked had been struck precisely at its weakest point. She thought of her mother's voice — *a woman's reputation is a glass vessel* — and of the apartment on Flatbush Avenue with its single bed and its single window and its single life, and how she had chosen all of that singleness deliberately, had built it like a fortress, and how this man with his dark curls and his unnamed profession and his cigarettes held in the European fashion was standing at the gate as if he had always known exactly where the gate was.

"It's getting late," she said.

"It's eight o'clock."

"Late for me."

He stepped closer. Not touching. Just close enough that she could smell the tobacco and salt air and something underneath — warm skin, cedar, a sweetness she couldn't name. The light flickered again, and his shadow merged with hers on the bleached wood of the boardwalk.

"Same time tomorrow?" he murmured.

She should say no. She knew the grammar of safety, could conjugate it in her sleep.

"Same time tomorrow," she said.

He smiled — slow, knowing, and not entirely kind — and the sodium light above them went out for good.
