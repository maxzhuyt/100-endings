# The Azra Gambit

## Chapter Seven: The Invitation

The transfer vessel's air filtration system hums with a persistent mechanical drone — the kind of sound that burrows into the inner ear and stays there, like a memory of something unpleasant. Arthur Neegan sat in a moulded acceleration couch that had been designed, he suspected, for a man of considerably different proportions, and watched the rust-coloured marble of Mars recede through a porthole no larger than a dinner plate.

He had not packed a bag. They had not suggested he needed one.

There had been six of them at his door — seven, if you counted the one who stayed in the crawler and kept the engine running. They wore the dove-grey tunics of the Antares Diplomatic Corps, which was rather like saying a shark wore the ocean. The Corps had not engaged in anything resembling diplomacy since the Ceres Compact of '43, and everyone in the colonies knew it. They carried sidearms with the casual indifference of people who rarely needed to use them, which was, Arthur reflected, considerably more frightening than if they'd pointed them at his chest.

The squad leader, a woman with close-cropped silver hair and the flat, appraising eyes of a card player, had smiled and said: "Mr. Neegan, your presence has been requested at a consultation. We've prepared transport."

"And if I decline the consultation?"

"Then we will have prepared transport unnecessarily, and I will have to file a Form 7-G with Colonial Administration explaining the expenditure." She paused. "I very much dislike filing Form 7-Gs, Mr. Neegan."

He had gone with them.

That had been four hours ago. The vessel — a fast courier, military-grade, with none of the ponderous bulk of the commercial haulers that crawled between Mars and the orbital stations — had cleared Martian atmosphere in under nine minutes. Now it was making for something beyond the orbit of Phobos, though no one had told him what.

Arthur pressed his thumb against the porthole's cold rim and thought about his claim. Sixteen hectares of oxidised basalt in the Meridiani lowlands, purchased for a sum so modest it had barely registered on his account ledger. He had come to Mars to mine iron and manganese — honest metals, boring metals, the kind of metals that built things and held them together. He had not asked for what lay beneath them.

The Azra vein, according to his own survey readings, extended at minimum three kilometres along the sub-surface fault line and ran to a depth his equipment could not accurately measure. The largest confirmed deposit on Mars. Possibly the largest anywhere.

He was still contemplating the sheer, stupid improbability of it when the vessel docked.

---

They brought him through a pressurised umbilical into a station he did not recognise — not one of the registered orbital platforms listed on the Colonial Transit Authority's charts. The corridors were clean and wide and lit by panels that simulated a warm terrestrial daylight, which was a small luxury that spoke of large budgets. The air smelled faintly of cedar, which was an absurd thing to smell in space, and therefore deliberate.

The room they led him to was furnished with a rectangular table of actual wood — teak, if Arthur was any judge — and two chairs. One wall displayed a real-time projection of the Martian surface, the Meridiani basin centred and annotated with topographic overlays. His claim was highlighted in amber.

The man who waited for him was perhaps sixty, though the precision of modern geriatric medicine made such estimates unreliable. He was slight, neat, with the kind of face that seemed designed to be forgotten immediately after seeing it. He wore no uniform, no insignia, no ring of office. His hands rested on the table, fingers loosely interlaced.

"Mr. Neegan," the man said. "Sit down, please. My name is Laszlo Pell."

Arthur sat. "I don't know that name."

"No. You wouldn't." Pell's voice was quiet, modulated, the voice of a man accustomed to rooms where he did not need to raise it. "I understand you've had a rather eventful month."

"I filed a geological survey with the Colonial Mineral Authority. That's all I did."

"Yes. And within seventy-two hours, you received acquisition offers from Consolidated Martian Resources, the Shen-Dai Group, and" — Pell consulted nothing; the list was in his memory — "the Terran Defence Procurement Office. Independently. Simultaneously. That must have felt somewhat overwhelming."

"It felt like being circled."

"An apt metaphor." Pell leaned back. The chair did not creak; it was too well made for that. "Let me be direct, Mr. Neegan. I represent an interest that prefers not to be named at this juncture. We are not a corporation. We are not, strictly speaking, a government. We are — let us say — a coordinating body, concerned with ensuring that certain destabilising technologies do not concentrate in the wrong hands."

"And you get to decide which hands are wrong."

"Someone must." Pell said this without irony, without defensiveness, as a man might observe that someone must empty the waste recyclers. "You are a mining engineer. You understand extraction rates, yield curves, refinement processes. So you understand what a deposit of the size you've reported means in material terms."

"I've done the arithmetic."

"Then you know that whoever controls that vein controls the production bottleneck for every Azra-dependent propulsion system and weapons platform currently in development across four governing bodies. You are sitting on the fulcrum of an arms race, Mr. Neegan. The fulcrum does not typically enjoy the experience."

Arthur said nothing for a moment. Through the projection wall, Mars turned slowly, indifferent.

"What are you offering?"

Pell smiled — a thin, careful expression, like a line drawn with a ruler. "Protection. Infrastructure. Extraction rights managed under a joint-oversight framework with guaranteed royalties paid to you in perpetuity. In exchange, you grant us primary allocation authority over output distribution."

"You want to decide who gets the Azra."

"I want to ensure that *everyone* gets enough, and that *no one* gets too much." Pell unfolded his hands and placed them flat on the teak. "The alternative, Mr. Neegan, is that within six months, you will have no claim, no rights, and very possibly no life. Mars is not a gentle place for men who own things that empires want."

The filtration system hummed. Arthur looked at the amber outline of his sixteen hectares and thought, not for the first time, about iron and manganese — honest metals, boring metals.

"I'll need to see the terms," he said.

"Of course," said Laszlo Pell. "Take all the time you need."

He did not say how much time that would be.
