# Overthinking It

The projector squeaks.

Okay. He's walking over. He's walking over right now. Ethan Park is carrying his notebook and walking toward this desk because Mrs. Hadley just said "partners" and pointed at the two of us, and I have approximately four seconds to decide what my face should be doing.

Smiling is obvious. Smiling says *I've been waiting for this.* But not smiling says *I don't want to be your partner,* which is so much worse, so I need something in between. Something that says *Oh, this is a perfectly neutral development that I have no strong feelings about.* A half-smile. Casual. Like I'm remembering something mildly funny that happened earlier.

He's pulling out the chair.

I did the half-smile. I think. It might have been more of a twitch. Can a person's mouth twitch and have it look natural? Maybe he didn't see. He's arranging his notebook. He's—

"Hey, Noor."

"Hey."

Good. That was good. One syllable, appropriate volume, no voice crack. I've been practicing saying "hey" instead of "hi" because "hi" sounds eager and "hello" sounds like I'm answering a telephone in 1987. "Hey" is the correct word. I'm almost sure I delivered it correctly.

The projector squeaks again as Mrs. Hadley flips to the slide about the ecosystem project. Biome dioramas. Three weeks. Thirty percent of our grade. I already know everything on this slide because I read the syllabus the first day and also looked up the assignment rubric on the class website last night, but I can't say that because it would make me sound—

"So I guess we should pick a biome?"

He's looking at me. He has this way of looking directly at people when he talks to them, like full eye contact, which is theoretically a normal human behavior but feels like standing under a heat lamp. His eyes are brown. Not interesting-brown, not poetic-brown, just brown, and I hate that I've noticed the exact shade because that's the kind of detail a person catalogs when they're too far gone, and I am not too far gone. I am a rational person who happens to have a slightly elevated heart rate in the presence of one specific classmate.

"Yeah," I say. "Sure."

*Yeah sure?* That's my contribution? I have a 4.0 GPA. I read *Cosmos* by Carl Sagan over winter break for fun. I once explained cellular respiration to my mother using an analogy about bank transactions that she said was "genuinely illuminating," and the best I can produce right now is *yeah sure,* like a golden retriever that learned two words.

Say something substantive. Say something about biomes. You know things about biomes.

"Maybe the tundra?" I offer, and immediately I want to die, because why the *tundra*? Nobody picks the tundra. The tundra is the biome of someone who's trying too hard to be different. He's going to think I'm one of those people who says obscure things to seem interesting. I should have said rainforest. Everyone says rainforest. Rainforest is safe.

"Oh, cool," he says. "That could be sick actually. Like permafrost and stuff?"

He said *cool.* About the tundra. That might be a pun. Should I acknowledge the pun? If it was intentional and I acknowledge it, we share a moment. If it was unintentional and I acknowledge it, I'm the weird girl who points out accidental puns. The risk-reward ratio is unfavorable. I will not acknowledge the pun.

"Yeah, the permafrost layers are really interesting because of how climate change is—" I stop. Am I lecturing? I'm lecturing. I can feel myself becoming That Girl, the one who turns a casual conversation into a TED Talk, and I can see the exact future where he tells his friends *She just started talking about permafrost layers like it was a podcast* and they all laugh, not meanly, but in that way where you become an anecdote.

"Because of what?" he asks.

"Just... it's changing a lot. The permafrost. There's stuff happening with it." I have just described one of the most significant ecological crises of our time as *stuff happening with it.* Somewhere, Carl Sagan is weeping among the stars.

The projector squeaks. Mrs. Hadley is explaining the material list. I should be writing this down, but Ethan's arm is on the desk and his sleeve is pushed up and there's a pen mark on his wrist where he was apparently drawing something during the lecture, and I'm trying to figure out what it is without looking like I'm staring at his arm. It's a small square. Or a robot face. I can't tell. I will never know and I need to accept that.

"Want to split the research?" he says. "I can do the animals if you want to do the, uh, ground stuff."

*Ground stuff.* He said *ground stuff.* That's — okay, that's endearing. He doesn't know the word for soil composition or geological substrate, and instead of pretending, he just said *ground stuff,* and something in my chest does a thing that I'm choosing not to name.

"Sure, that works." I'm writing in my notebook now, which is good because it gives me somewhere to aim my eyes. I write *TUNDRA* and underline it twice, then worry that two underlines looks obsessive, so I add a third to make it look intentional, which obviously makes it worse.

He leans over to see what I wrote, and his shoulder is close enough that I can smell his laundry detergent, or maybe his shampoo, and I'm furious at myself for even registering this information because I am not a person who *smells people,* except apparently I am, and—

"Your handwriting's really neat," he says.

"Thanks."

Don't say anything else. That was fine. *Thanks* was perfect. Don't elaborate. Don't explain that you practiced your handwriting in sixth grade using a YouTube calligraphy tutorial. Don't—

"I practiced it," I say.

Why. *Why.* Who admits to practicing handwriting? What is wrong with me?

He grins. "That's kind of awesome, honestly."

The projector squeaks.

I write down the due date, and my hand is steady, and I don't say anything else for a moment, and the silence feels like something I could almost live inside.
