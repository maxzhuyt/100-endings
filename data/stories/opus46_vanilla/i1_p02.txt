# The Lighthouse Keeper

---

**12 March 1953**

Dear Mam,

Well I am here. The boat left me on the rock with my trunk and two crates of paraffin and that was that. The sea was rough coming over from Oban and I was sick twice which is no way for a keeper to start his posting. The Principal Keeper is a man called Fergusson who has been here eleven years if you can credit it. He showed me the light and the mechanism and the sleeping quarters and then he went to his room and shut the door and that is about all the conversation we have had.

The rock is called Dubh Artach which means Black Rock in the Gaelic. It sits fourteen miles out in the Atlantic off the coast of Mull. There is nothing here but the tower and the sea and the gulls and Fergusson.

I will write when I can. The relief boat comes every fortnight if the weather holds.

Your son,
Callum

---

**28 March 1953**

Dear Mam,

Thank you for the shortbread. Fergusson ate most of it without asking which I suppose counts as sociability from him.

You asked what I do all day. I clean the lens. I trim the wicks. I polish the brass. I log the weather. I wind the mechanism every thirty minutes through the night. Between times I sit in the kitchen which is round like everything here because the whole place is a stone tube and I look at the wall.

I am not complaining. The pay is good and it is honest work. But I will say this. The sound of the sea never stops. You would think you would get used to it but you do not. It is always there underneath everything like a second pulse.

Give my regards to Uncle Dougal and tell him I have not yet been washed away.

Callum

---

**19 April 1953**

Dear Mam,

A storm came through on Tuesday that lasted three days. The waves broke clean over the tower. Seventy feet up and the water still hit the lantern glass. The whole building shook and I am not ashamed to say I was frightened. Fergusson sat in his chair reading a western novel as if he was in a parlour in Edinburgh.

I asked him afterwards if he was not scared and he said, "The tower has stood since 1872. It will stand tonight." And that was the end of that.

I have been having trouble sleeping. Not from the watches but from the silence between them. You would think silence would be restful but it is not the right kind.

Callum

---

**7 June 1953**

Mam,

Sorry I have not written. I have been in a dark mood and did not want to pass it on to you. The days are very long now with the summer light and there is less to do because the mechanism needs less winding. More time to think which is not always a gift.

Fergusson told me something last night. He said he had a wife and a boy in Fraserburgh and the boy died of diphtheria in 1943 and the wife would not see him after that. She blamed him for being away at the light. He said this while looking at the sea and not at me and then he went back to his novel.

I think he told me because he could see I was struggling. It was a kindness though a rough one.

Callum

---

**14 August 1953**

Dear Mam,

Thank you for telling me about Jean Cargill asking after me. I do not know what to make of that. She never spoke two words to me when I was in Oban. Maybe absence does something to a person's memory. Makes you more interesting than you were.

I have started reading. Fergusson has a shelf of books, all westerns and one copy of Kidnapped which is better than the westerns. I read it twice. Stevenson knew about lonely places.

Yesterday I watched a basking shark pass the rock. Its fin was the size of a door. It moved so slowly through the water. I stood at the rail for an hour watching it go. It was the most beautiful thing I have seen here. I wanted to tell someone and there was only Fergusson and he was asleep so I am telling you.

Callum

---

**3 October 1953**

Mam,

The autumn gales have started and the relief boat could not come for three weeks. We ran low on tea and tobacco and fresh water. I will not pretend it was easy. There were two days when I could not speak to Fergusson or he to me. Not from anger. From something else. The walls close in on you. The round rooms get smaller.

But then the boat came and we had fresh bread and letters and tobacco and it was like the world remembered we were here.

I have been thinking about what you said about coming home. I want to finish the year. I need to know I can do this.

Callum

---

**20 December 1953**

Dear Mam,

It is Christmas week and Fergusson and I decorated the kitchen with some tinsel the relief crew brought. It looks daft. A stone room sixty feet above the Atlantic with three strands of tinsel and a candle. But we sat and had our meal and Fergusson raised his mug of tea and said, "To absent friends," and I said it back and we sat there quiet for a while and it was not a bad silence. It was the other kind.

I have decided I will come home in the spring when my posting ends. I will not sign on for another year. But I am glad I came. I know that now. I learned something here though I cannot put a name to it yet. Something about what you can bear and what bearing it does to you and how another person sitting quiet in the same room can be enough.

Tell Jean Cargill I will be home in April if she wants to not speak two words to me in person.

Happy Christmas, Mam.

Your son,
Callum
