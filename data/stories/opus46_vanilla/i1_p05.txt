# Chapter Two: The Cabin That Breathed

That night, the lodge felt smaller than JT remembered.

The four of them sat around the table—JT, Mike, their father, and Uncle Harlan, who'd driven up separately and arrived smelling like cigarettes and something sour underneath—eating canned stew heated on the propane burner because the electric range had quit sometime during the summer and nobody had bothered to fix it. The overhead light buzzed at a frequency that lived right behind JT's eyes. He pushed a chunk of potato around his bowl and said nothing.

His father was talking about the morning's hunt in that low, authoritative tone he used when he was constructing a version of events he could live with. They'd tracked a group of cows through the eastern ridge. They'd seen sign of a big buck but lost it in the brush. The usual. Nothing about the clearing. Nothing about the way he'd stood there with his hand outstretched like a man reaching for something in a dream. Mike nodded along, adding details that felt rehearsed, and JT understood with a cold, quiet certainty that neither of them remembered. Whatever had happened out there, it had slipped from their minds like water through a crack in the floor, and he was the only one left holding it.

"You were quiet out there today," his father said, not quite looking at him.

JT shrugged. "Just taking it in."

"Taking it in." His father repeated the words flatly, the way he did when he found something insufficient. "You're not here to take it in, JT. You're here to hunt."

"Leave him alone, Frank," Uncle Harlan said, though there was no real conviction in it. Harlan's eyes were on the window, or rather on the darkness beyond it, which was absolute. No moon. The trees were just a wall of black pressing against the glass. "Boy's allowed to have an off day."

His father grunted and went back to his stew.

Later, while Mike and Frank argued about something inconsequential—truck maintenance, or maybe politics, the two being roughly interchangeable in their household—JT found himself standing beside Harlan on the porch. The cold was sharp and total, the kind that made your lungs feel brittle. Harlan lit a cigarette, the flame from his lighter throwing his face into brief, harsh relief. He looked older than JT remembered. Thinner. Something had gone out of him since last season, some essential vitality, and what remained was a man who looked like he was waiting for something he'd rather not name.

"Harlan," JT said carefully. "You ever hear about a cabin out past the north ridge? Old one. Abandoned."

Harlan didn't answer right away. He took a long drag, and the cherry of the cigarette flared and dimmed, flared and dimmed, like a slow heartbeat.

"Where'd you hear about that?"

"Around. Some guys at the gas station in town were talking. Said there was a place out there people don't go anymore. Said something lives in it."

"Something." Harlan exhaled smoke through his nose. "People say all kinds of things up here. Gets dark early, gets cold, gets lonely. Minds fill in the blanks."

"So it's real, though. The cabin."

Harlan looked at him then, and JT wished he hadn't asked, because there was something in his uncle's eyes that wasn't dismissal. It was recognition. The look of a man who'd been carrying a stone in his pocket for years and had just heard someone describe its exact shape and weight.

"There's a structure out there," Harlan said quietly. "Old trapper's place, maybe. Maybe older. Your grandfather showed it to me once when I was about your age. Walked me out there and then walked me back and never said a word about it again." He paused. "The door was open when we found it. I remember that. Open, in the middle of November, with snow on the ground. And it was warm inside. Not warm like a fire had been burning. Warm like—" He stopped. Crushed the cigarette under his boot. "You don't need to go out there, JT."

"I wasn't planning on it."

"Good." Harlan put his hand on JT's shoulder, and the grip was too tight, held a beat too long. "Because I'll tell you something my father never told me, and that's that some places up here aren't empty. They're just patient."

He went inside after that, letting the screen door slap shut behind him, and JT stood alone in the dark listening to the woods. The silence was immense and somehow textured, layered, as if it were composed of countless small silences stacked on top of one another—the silence of snow not yet fallen, of branches holding still, of animals watching from distances he couldn't calculate. And beneath it all, so faint he might have been imagining it, a sound like breathing. Not his own. Slower. Deeper. The rhythm of something vast and resting, something that inhaled the dark and exhaled it back a shade darker.

JT gripped the porch railing until the wood bit into his palms. He told himself it was the wind. He told himself it was the lake, a mile east, shifting under early ice. He told himself a lot of things in the space of those few minutes, and none of them stuck, because the sound wasn't coming from any direction he could point to. It was coming from everywhere. It was coming from the spaces between the trees, from the ground under his feet, from the cabin he'd never seen but could suddenly picture with nauseating clarity—a low, dark structure with its door hanging open, warm air spilling out into the freezing night like an invitation, like a mouth.

He went inside. Locked the door. Lay in his bunk staring at the ceiling while his brother snored above him, and he did not sleep for a very long time.

In the morning, he found tracks in the frost outside the porch. Not boot prints. Not animal prints. Something in between—long, narrow impressions that started at the tree line, approached the lodge in a straight, deliberate line, and stopped directly beneath the window where he'd been standing.

They did not lead back.
