# Útiseta

I want to say I didn't believe any of it. That feels important to establish, even now—especially now—though I'm no longer sure it's true.

Astrid came for me at eleven. She threw a pebble at my window like we were in some American movie, and I climbed out the way I'd learned to that summer: left foot on the drainpipe bracket, right foot on the woodshed roof, drop to the grass. My parents slept like the dead. They'd been sleeping like the dead since we moved north, as though the village air were a sedative. I envied them that.

She was standing at the edge of our garden with a rucksack over one shoulder and her grandmother's wool blanket folded under her arm. The blanket was the color of old bone. I remember that specifically because I'd asked about it once and Astrid had said, "Farmor dyed it herself. She said the color had to be right." I hadn't asked right for what.

We walked north along the gravel road, then cut through the birch wood where the trees grew so close their branches wove together overhead like fingers lacing. No torches. Astrid said light would be wrong. She said that word a lot—*wrong*—always with the same flat certainty, as though she were reading from instructions I couldn't see.

"You can't eat after sundown," she'd told me that afternoon. "And you have to carry something iron. And you can't look behind you on the way there. Those are the rules."

"Whose rules?"

"The rules."

I had a railway spike in my jacket pocket. Astrid had given it to me. She said her grandmother kept a coffee tin full of them. I didn't ask why.

---

The burial mound was in a clearing beyond the birch wood, a long hump of earth that the village called *Kungshögen* though no king was buried there. No one knew who was buried there. The municipality had put up a small sign at some point—*Fornlämning, Skyddad enligt lag*—but someone had shot it with a rifle and the text was barely legible. Astrid spread the bone-colored blanket on the crown of the mound and sat down cross-legged. She patted the space beside her.

"Now we wait," she said.

"For what?"

"For what comes."

I sat. The wool was coarse and smelled of lanolin and something else, something vegetal and dark, like roots pulled from wet earth. Below us the mound sloped away into shadow. The birches at the clearing's edge were pale verticals, like bars.

"Farmor used to do this," Astrid said. She wasn't whispering. Her voice was conversational, which somehow made everything worse. "She'd sit out on the mound when she needed to know something. Who had stolen a cow. Where a missing person had gone. She said the dead know everything that happens in the parish because they're still *in* the ground. They feel footsteps. They hear conversations through the soil."

"That's insane," I said.

"She was right every time. Ask anyone."

I didn't want to ask anyone. I wanted to go home. But I also wanted Astrid to think I was brave, or at least not a coward, which I understand now are very different things.

She told me to close my eyes. I closed them. She told me to press my palms flat against the earth on either side of the blanket, so I was touching the mound directly. The grass was cold and slightly damp. Underneath it the ground felt solid and dense in a way that seemed deliberate, like packed muscle.

"Ask a question," Astrid said. "In your head. Not out loud. Something you actually want to know."

I asked whether Marcus Lindgren liked me. I'm not proud of that. Of all the questions you could pose to the ancient dead, I chose a boy. But I was fifteen, and he had smiled at me twice in the corridor outside the chemistry lab, and that had felt like enough to build a life on.

For a long time there was nothing. Wind in the birches. My own breathing. Astrid's breathing beside me, slow and measured, almost performative. I was about to open my eyes and tell her this was stupid when I felt it.

A vibration. Low and sourceless, rising through the earth into my palms. Not a tremor—something more rhythmic than that. A pulse. Slow, deep, and absolutely steady, as though the mound itself had a heartbeat and I had just placed my hands against its chest.

I opened my eyes. Astrid's were still closed. Her lips were moving slightly but no sound came out.

"Astrid."

She didn't respond.

"Astrid, do you feel that?"

The pulsing grew stronger. Not faster—just *more*. It climbed through my wrists and into my forearms, a subsonic thrum that I felt in my teeth, in the hinge of my jaw. The air above the mound had changed. It was heavier. Warmer. It pressed against my face like breath.

Then the church bell struck.

One single tone, from the village church three kilometers south, rolling across the flat country and arriving clean and whole. Midnight. But there was no midnight service. There was no bell ringer. The church had been automated for years—Christmas and Easter and Sunday mornings, nothing else. I knew this. Astrid knew this. Everyone knew this.

Her eyes opened.

"We should go," she said. Not frightened. Not calm either. Something in between that I had no name for then and still don't.

We left the blanket. We walked back through the birch wood and I looked behind me twice and Astrid said nothing about it. At my garden gate she gripped my arm hard enough to bruise.

"Don't tell me what you asked," she said. "Don't tell anyone. That's the rule."

I never did.

Marcus Lindgren moved away two weeks later. His father took a job in Gothenburg. It was sudden. No one expected it.

I don't think that means anything. I want to say I don't think that means anything.
