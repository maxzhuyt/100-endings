# Hell is Other Demons

The hellhound was chewing on the throne again.

Lucifer Morningstar, Prince of Darkness, the Adversary, Lord of Lies and Sovereign of the Bottomless Pit, pressed two fingers against his left temple and tried to focus on the quarterly soul-intake projections while the sound of iron being gnawed filled the throne room like a dentist's drill made of smaller, angrier dentist's drills.

"Marchosias. *Marchosias.* Stop it."

The hellhound—three heads, each worse than the last, breath like a sulfur refinery on a hot day—paused, regarded Lucifer with six baleful eyes that held absolutely no remorse, and resumed chewing.

There was a wobble now. A definite wobble. The Throne of Perdition, forged in the fires of rebellion, hammered from the celestial iron of broken angelic swords, seat of infernal power since the Fall itself—wobbled. Like a café table in Montmartre.

Lucifer made a note on a sticky pad. *Get throne leg fixed. Again.* He underlined "again" three times.

"My Lord?" Beelzebub hovered in the doorway, literally, his wings making that irritating wet sound. "The Department of Ironic Punishments is requesting additional funding for the social media influencer wing. Apparently the existing infrastructure of mirrors that show only truthful reflections is—"

"Approved."

"—insufficient for the volume of—really? Just like that?"

"I'm in a generous mood, Beelzebub."

"You don't look generous, my Lord. You look like someone who hasn't slept in four hundred years."

"I don't require sleep. I'm an archangel."

"Former."

"Thank you. Was there anything else, or did you just come to annotate my ontological status?"

Beelzebub hesitated. This was never good. Beelzebub was many things—pestilent, obsequious, deeply committed to middle management—but hesitant was not typically among them.

"She's here," he said.

The air in the throne room changed. Or perhaps it didn't. Perhaps Lucifer only imagined it changed, which amounted to the same thing when you were the one who'd invented imagination's darker applications.

"Where?"

"The west antechamber. She says she left a cardigan."

"She doesn't wear cardigans."

"No, my Lord."

They looked at each other. Marchosias chose this moment to wrench free a small piece of throne leg and swallow it with three simultaneous gulps.

---

Lilith was examining a tapestry depicting the Fall of Man when Lucifer found her. She was examining it the way one examines a photograph from an old holiday—fondly, critically, with a certain proprietary nostalgia.

"That's not how the serpent looked," she said, without turning around.

"Artistic license."

"The serpent was more... elegant."

"I'll pass your notes to the weaver. He's in the Pit of Conditions and Terms, level seven. I'm sure he'd welcome the creative feedback."

She turned. Lilith, first wife of Adam, first woman to say *no* and mean it cosmically, looked exactly as she always looked: like a question that was also the answer to a different, more dangerous question. She was wearing a black silk blouse. No cardigan.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You left a cardigan."

"I left nothing. I came to collect something."

"What?"

She didn't answer. She walked past him, trailing one finger along the stone wall, and he followed because he always followed, which was ironic for someone whose entire mythology was built on refusing to follow, but irony was the foundational element of Hell, more so than fire or brimstone, which were frankly overrated.

They ended up in the kitchen. Hell had a kitchen because Lucifer had spent a century in Lyon in the 1700s and had opinions about sauce. Lilith opened the refrigerator—black, enormous, covered in magnets from places that didn't exist on any earthly map—and retrieved a bottle of something old and red.

"That's mine," Lucifer said.

"Everything here is yours. That's your whole problem." She poured two glasses. "You keep everything. You let nothing go. You sit on that wobbling throne—"

"The hellhound—"

"—in that empty hall, processing *paperwork*, Lucifer. The Morningstar. Doing quarterly projections."

"Someone has to run Hell."

"No one *has* to run Hell. That's the point. That's the *entire* point. You chose this."

"As opposed to what? Going back? Asking forgiveness?" He took the glass. "You know what He's like."

"I know what *everyone's* like. I was married to the first man. I've met the whole collection."

They drank. The wine tasted of regret and blackberries, which were essentially the same flavor profile.

From the throne room, distantly, came the sound of iron being chewed.

"You should get rid of that dog," Lilith said.

"He was a gift."

"From whom?"

"You. Solstice, 1347."

"Ah." She looked into her glass. "The plague year. We were getting along that century."

"We were."

Silence. Not the silence of Hell, which was never truly silent—there was always screaming somewhere, like tinnitus but moral—but the silence between two people who had known each other since before the concept of "knowing" had been invented.

"The cardigan is in the second bedroom," Lucifer said. "The one overlooking the Lake of Fire."

"I told you, I don't—"

"Black cashmere. You left it after the equinox."

She set down her glass. "I'll get it, then."

She didn't move.

"The throne needs fixing," he said.

"It does."

"I don't suppose you know anyone good with iron."

"I might." She refilled both glasses. "I'll have a look at it. After I find this cardigan that doesn't exist."

"Naturally."

"This isn't me coming back."

"Of course not."

"This is about the cardigan."

"Understood."

Marchosias padded into the kitchen, laid all three heads on Lilith's knee, and whimpered adoringly. She scratched behind six ears with the practiced efficiency of someone who had, whether she admitted it or not, been here before and would, whether she admitted it or not, be here again.

The throne wobbled on in the empty hall, listing slightly toward the place where something necessary had been chewed away.
