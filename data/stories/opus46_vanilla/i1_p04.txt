# My Apocalypse Buddy

The thing about the end of the world is that it doesn't have the common decency to kill you outright.

Midas sat cross-legged on the floor of what had once been a Jiffy Lube, studying a map he'd drawn on the back of a flattened cereal box. It was not a good map. It contained such helpful annotations as "probably wolves" and "that place where I got diarrhea" and, in one corner, simply a skull with a question mark.

"We need water tablets," he said.

Chewy stared at him from the workbench with the glassy, unblinking optimism of something manufactured in a Shenzhen factory to delight children. The animatronic koala had once, presumably, danced and sung and said things like *I wuv you* when you squeezed its paw. Now it did nothing of the sort. One eye was missing. Its fur had the texture of a dishrag that had seen combat. Something in its chest cavity occasionally clicked, like a tiny mechanical heart that refused, against all engineering probability, to fully die.

Midas squeezed its paw anyway. Force of habit.

*Click.*

"Right," he said. "Agreed."

This was their dynamic. Midas talked. Chewy clicked, or didn't. Midas interpreted freely. It was, he reflected, the healthiest relationship he'd ever had.

He'd found the graffiti yesterday on the underpass near the dead river—bright red paint, fresh enough to smell, which meant someone out there still had both paint *and* opinions. The message read:

**EDEN GROVE — 12K NORTH — FRESH WATER — NO CANNIBALS (VERIFIED)**

That parenthetical "verified" did a lot of heavy lifting. Midas had encountered other optimistic signage in his travels. "SAFE ZONE AHEAD" had once led him to a building full of feral cats and a man who believed he was the reincarnation of a petroleum company. "FREE FOOD" had, technically, been accurate—the food had been free because it was raccoon.

But water tablets. They needed water tablets. And hope, that most dangerous of post-apocalyptic commodities, had a way of making twelve kilometers sound reasonable.

He stuffed Chewy into the side pocket of his rucksack, head poking out like a furry, lopsided periscope, and set off north.

---

The first four kilometers were uneventful, which should have been a warning.

The fifth kilometer introduced the bridge, which introduced the gap in the bridge, which introduced Midas to the concept of jumping further than he had ever jumped before. He made it. Barely. One boot didn't. He watched it tumble into the ravine with the quiet dignity of a man who now owned exactly one shoe.

"We're fine," he told Chewy. "Shoes are a luxury. Humanity walked barefoot for millennia."

*Click.*

"Don't be negative."

The seventh kilometer brought the dogs. Not wolves—he'd been wrong about the wolves, which was the only piece of good luck he'd had in months. These were dogs in the way that a thunderstorm is weather: technically accurate, fundamentally unhelpful as a description. They were large. They were many. They had the lean, focused look of animals who had read the social contract and eaten it.

Midas climbed a telephone pole with a speed that surprised both himself and the dogs. He clung there for two hours, one-shoed, while they circled below with the patient professionalism of debt collectors.

Chewy's remaining eye caught the sunlight.

*Click.*

"I am *not* throwing you as a distraction."

Eventually, the dogs got bored, or received a better offer, or simply decided that a man clinging to a telephone pole lacked the caloric dignity they deserved. They left. Midas climbed down, collected several new splinters in places splinters should not exist, and pressed on.

---

Eden Grove, as it turned out, was real.

It was also on fire.

Not metaphorically. Not in the poetic, "civilization is burning" sense that people had favored in the early days. Literally, actually, enthusiastically on fire. Someone had built something lovely here—Midas could see the bones of raised garden beds, rain catchment systems, a structure that might have been a communting hall—and someone else, or possibly the same someone in a different mood, had put it all to the torch. Recently. The smoke was still thinking about which direction to drift.

Midas stood at the tree line, barefoot on one side, and watched.

"Well," he said.

*Click.*

In the ashes near what had been the entrance, he found a crate of water purification tablets. Thirty-six of them, sealed, untouched. Beside the crate, written in the same red paint as the underpass, someone had added a postscript:

**CANNIBALS (UNVERIFIED)**

He took the tablets. He took a singed but functional boot from the wreckage that was two sizes too large. He did not investigate further. Some mysteries were best left to people who had more shoes and fewer things to live for.

---

The return trip took nine hours because he got lost, which was impressive given that "south" is not a complicated concept. The too-large boot gave him a blister the size and disposition of a small angry planet. At one point, it rained—not the gentle, cleansing rain of renewal, but the horizontal, vindictive rain of a sky that had taken the apocalypse personally.

He limped into the Jiffy Lube at dusk, soaked, bleeding from the splinter places, and deposited Chewy on the workbench with the tenderness of a man placing a child in a crib.

He lined up the water tablets like tiny white soldiers.

Thirty-six. At two per week, that was eighteen weeks. Eighteen weeks of not dying from the water, which was as close to long-term planning as the end of the world allowed.

"Productive day," he said.

Chewy said nothing. The click mechanism, perhaps waterlogged, perhaps finally surrendering to entropy, was silent.

Midas squeezed the paw again.

*Click.*

He exhaled.

"Same time tomorrow, then."

Outside, the rain continued its grievance. Inside, a man and a broken toy sat in the dark and planned for a future that, against all evidence, they had not yet given up on.
