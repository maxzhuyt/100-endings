# The Morska Conspiracy

## Part II: The Underhold

The door gave with a sound like a man's last breath—slow, wet, reluctant. Beyond it, the underhold opened into a vaulted chamber of sweating stone, its ceiling lost somewhere above the reach of the gaslight sconces that lined the walls in tarnished brass. The air tasted of rendered fat and old iron. Whale-oil lanterns hung from chains at intervals, their flames burning that particular shade of jaundiced amber that meant the fuel was cheap, cut with tallow, the kind that left a film on the lungs.

Aya stepped through first. She always stepped through first. That was the unspoken arithmetic of leadership—you purchased loyalty by spending your own skin before anyone else's.

The chamber was not empty.

Twelve figures stood arranged in a loose crescent before the iron reliquary at the far wall—the great Scythan lockbox, ancient and indifferent, its surface crawling with etchings that predated the city by centuries. And at the centre of the crescent, seated in a high-backed chair that had no business being in a sewer, was Maren Drost.

The guild leader wore her arrest like a woman wears last season's perfume—faintly, and with contempt for anyone who still noticed. Her coat was buttoned to the throat, dark wool over darker leather, and her hands rested on the pommel of a cane-sword whose blade, Aya knew, was whale-bone composite edged with Kovari steel. The bodyguards flanking her were not the theatrical sort. They were the quiet sort. The sort who had done enough killing that it no longer required facial expressions.

"Close the door, Kael," Maren said. Not to Aya. To someone behind them.

The door groaned shut. A lock engaged with the finality of a coffin nail.

Katra swore—a single, bitten syllable. Simeon said nothing, which was worse.

"Three rats," Maren said, "in my cellar. And one of them wearing a very pretty mask."

Aya's mind became a corridor of doors, each one a calculation. *She was never arrested. The raid was theatre. Which means she knew. Which means someone told her. Which means—*

"You'll note," Maren continued, her voice carrying the worn patience of a schoolmistress addressing disappointing children, "that I have not yet asked you to kneel. I find kneeling performative. Undignified for all parties." She tilted her head. "I would, however, like to see a face. Yours, specifically. The one in the bone mask."

Two of the quiet men stepped forward. Aya felt Simeon tense beside her, felt Katra's hand drift toward the gutting knife at her hip.

"Don't," Aya murmured.

"Aya—" Katra started.

"*Don't.*"

The men took her arms with professional indifference. One of them reached for the mask—carved cetacean bone, pale as grief, modelled after the old Scythan death-priestesses. Aya had chosen it for its symbolism. She was regretting that now. The ties came loose. The mask came away.

The silence that followed had weight.

Maren Drost leaned forward in her chair. The lantern light found her face—weathered, sharp, a topography of old decisions—and something moved across it that might have been surprise, or might have been satisfaction, or might have been the particular expression of a woman watching a suspicion crystallise into fact.

"Well," Maren said softly. "Lady Aya Vesserine. Daughter of the late Countess Vesserine." A pause, measured as a physician's dose. "The hanged witch's girl."

The words landed where they were meant to land. Aya felt them in her sternum.

"Maren—" she began.

"You will address me as Drost. You have not earned the intimacy of my Christian name, Lady Vesserine. You have earned rather the opposite." Maren stood. The cane-sword tapped once against the stone floor. "Kael. Disarm the other two."

It happened quickly and without drama. Katra surrendered her knife with a look that promised future violence. Simeon gave up his pistol and his blackjack and, after a pointed look from Kael, the garrotting wire sewn into his collar.

*Think. Think. The artifact doesn't matter now. Survival matters. What do you have that she wants more than your death?*

A distant foghorn sounded three times in the harbour beyond the guild walls. The sound penetrated the stone like a hymn through cathedral doors—low, mournful, absolute. Three blasts. A vessel in distress, or a warning of fog. Either way, an omen.

"You staged your own arrest," Aya said. "To smoke out challengers."

"I staged my own arrest," Maren agreed, "to smoke out *you*. The others were incidental. You've been climbing my ranks for eleven months wearing that dead woman's face, and you thought I hadn't noticed the smell of immateria on my operations?" She said the word *immateria* the way one might say *plague*. "Half my men won't enter the eastern warehouse anymore. They say the shadows move wrong. They say they hear breathing in empty rooms. That is *your* doing, girl."

"Then you know what I'm capable of."

"I know what your mother was capable of. And I know how she ended. Rope and a short drop on Gallows Quay, with half the Admiralty watching." Maren's eyes were steady. "You are perhaps thirty seconds from a similar conclusion, administered privately."

"Kill me," Aya said, and her voice held, "and you kill the most valuable hostage in the Vesserine estate. My father sits on the Maritime Council. My brother commands the *Leviathan*-class dreadnought *Absolute*. You murder me in this cellar, you lose leverage that is worth more than anything in that reliquary."

Maren studied her. The lanterns guttered.

"And if I ransom you? Your family learns their precious daughter has been running with thieves and practising outlawed magick. The scandal alone—"

"Would destroy them. Yes." Aya swallowed. The taste of whale-oil and dread. "Which means they will pay *anything* to keep it quiet."

Maren Drost smiled. It was not a kind smile. It was the smile of a woman who had just been offered a new instrument and was already considering where to cut.

"Sit down, Lady Vesserine," she said. "Let us discuss your price."
