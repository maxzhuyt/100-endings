# Hacker Mindset

The hostess seats us at table seven. Corner booth, sight lines to both exits, one camera above the bar running firmware I could identify from here if I squinted. Stop it. *Stop it.*

"This is nice," Rachel says, and she means the restaurant, but I'm already cataloging: open Wi-Fi network named "Trattoria_Guest" — no captive portal, no client isolation, guaranteed. The POS system is running on the same network because I can see the printer behind the bar sputtering out tickets on the same SSID. One ARP spoof and I'd have every credit card number flowing through this place like—

*Stop. It.*

She's smiling at me. She has a great smile. Focus on the smile.

"I love Italian," I say, which is true and also the most boring sentence ever constructed in English. I deserve to be alone.

But then — salvation. She sets her bag down, and there's a keychain. A keychain with a little plastic Jace Beleren hanging off it, scuffed and faded like she's carried it for years.

"Is that Jace?"

Her eyes light up. Actually light up. Not performatively, not politely — the real thing. Dopamine hit. Genuine recognition flooding her features.

"You play?"

"I played. I *play*. I mean—" Smooth. Very smooth. "I run a blue-black control deck that has ended friendships."

"Oh, you're a *monster*," she says, and she's laughing, and something in my chest unclenches that I didn't know was clenched.

For twenty minutes I am a normal human being. We talk about the Innistrad block. We argue about whether Counterspell should ever have been reprinted. She tells me she once won an FNM with a janky homebrew mill deck and I believe her because her eyes do this thing when she's proud — they narrow slightly, chin lifts, micro-expression cluster consistent with genuine confidence rather than fabrication. I'm profiling her. I can't not profile her. But at least I'm enjoying it.

"I started playing in college," she says. "University of Florida, go Gators. My roommate taught me freshman year."

*University of Florida. First school attended. Security question number three on most banking platforms.* I take a sip of water and drown the thought.

"My mom thought it was satanic," she continues, laughing. "Classic Sandra move."

*Mother's maiden name? No — mother's first name. Sandra. But maiden name is often derivable from—*

STOP.

"She came around when I explained the math involved. Mom's a retired math teacher, so."

*Mother's occupation. Sandra. University of Florida. I now have two common security question answers and a potential social engineering vector and I hate my brain, I hate it, I hate it.*

She's telling me about her first pet, a cat named Biscuit who destroyed her parents' couch in their house on Maple-something Street — and I am not going to remember that, I am going to let it flow through me like water, I am going to be a person who hears a cute cat story and thinks *cute cat* and not *first pet's name, childhood street, challenge questions for Gmail recovery.*

"Biscuit was a menace," I say, and she laughs again, and the laugh is worth more than every credential I've ever captured.

The food arrives. It's good. She's good. This is good.

Then her phone buzzes on the table.

She picks it up, tilts it slightly toward me — not intentionally, just the natural angle — and swipes her unlock pattern.

Z-shape. Four nodes. No backtracking.

My soul leaves my body.

It's one of the nine most common unlock patterns. Literally in the top ten. Academic paper by Marte Løge, 2015. Seventy-seven percent of patterns start in a corner. Hers starts top-left, because of course it does, because most people are right-handed and top-left is the natural starting position and she has just shown me the digital equivalent of a house key under a doormat.

I try to hold it in. I physically clench my jaw. I look at my pasta. I think about Jace. I think about mill decks. I think about anything other than—

"You should change your unlock pattern."

The words are out. They're in the air. They exist in the physical world now, vibrating between us at the speed of ruining everything.

She blinks. "What?"

"Your unlock pattern. It's a Z-shape. It's — statistically it's one of the most common patterns, and you swiped it at an angle where anyone sitting across from you could—" I see her expression shifting, the openness contracting, and I'm watching it happen in real-time like a system going into lockdown, ports closing one by one. "I'm sorry. That's — I do this thing where I—"

"Did you just *watch me unlock my phone?*"

"Not on purpose. I mean — yes, technically, but it's not—" I'm breached. Defenses down. No playbook for this. "It's like how you probably can't look at a number without factoring it. Your mom's a math teacher, you probably grew up—"

"Don't bring my mom into this."

Fair. Completely fair.

Silence. She puts her phone face-down on the table. New perimeter established.

I set my fork down. "I used to do security work. Penetration testing. Finding weaknesses in systems. The problem is, my brain doesn't turn off. I see patterns, vulnerabilities — in networks, in software, in..." I gesture vaguely at the restaurant. "Everything. It's not that I *want* to hack your phone. It's that I can't not notice the door is open."

She's studying me. Evaluating. I can't read her expression, which is terrifying and also, somehow, attractive. An unreadable system. Encrypted.

"That sounds exhausting," she says finally.

"You have no idea."

She picks up her phone. Unlocks it — turning it deliberately away from me this time — and does something I can't see. Then she sets it down and picks up her fork.

"I changed it. Happy?"

"Was it to an L-shape? Because that's the second most common—"

"Eat your pasta, hacker boy."

I eat my pasta. She tells me about a Phyrexian Obliterator deck she's been theorycrafting. I tell her it's evil and beautiful. She says thank you.

I think this might be going okay.

I don't look at the Wi-Fi network once.

Well. Twice. But only twice.
