# Gunpowder Rendezvous

The jukebox in the corner of the Yellow Flag—or whatever this place calls itself, some waterfront dive in the armpit of Southeast Asia—keeps spitting out the same scratchy rock track, guitars grinding through blown speakers like a car wreck that won't stop happening. Spike Spiegel doesn't mind. He's heard worse. He's *been* worse.

He sits at the bar nursing something brown and cheap, a cigarette balanced between his lips, smoke curling up past mismatched eyes. One real, one fake. Both tired. The bounty file sits folded in his jacket pocket: some arms dealer named Kovacs, worth three million woolongs if delivered breathing. Spike needs the money. The Bebop's fuel cells are running on fumes and prayers, and Jet's going to kill him if he comes back empty-handed again.

The door opens. Humid air rolls in carrying the smell of diesel and river rot.

She walks in like she owns the place, or like she's planning to burn it down and hasn't decided which yet. Black crop top, denim cutoffs that border on theoretical, combat boots that have actually seen combat. A dragon tattoo coils up her right arm, and the twin Berettas holstered at her thighs catch the neon light like jewelry.

Spike watches her in the mirror behind the bar. She watches him watching.

"Rum," she tells the bartender. "The whole bottle. And whatever he's drinking, cut him off."

"We know each other?" Spike asks, not turning around.

"No." She drops onto the stool beside him, close enough that he can smell cordite and sweat. "But I know what you are. You've got that look. Bounty hunter."

"And you've got that look. The one that says you're about to make my night significantly worse."

She grins. It's not friendly. "Revy. People call me Two Hands."

"Spike Spiegel. People call me broke."

The jukebox track ends and starts again, the same opening riff, the same grinding chorus. Revy pours rum like she's trying to drown something that learned to swim a long time ago. She drinks, wipes her mouth with the back of her tattooed hand, and fixes him with a stare.

"Kovacs," she says.

Spike's cigarette pauses halfway to his lips. "What about him?"

"Don't bullshit me, cowboy. You're here for the same reason I am. My crew got the contract two days ago. Dutch sent me to collect."

"Funny. My information says he's *my* bounty."

"Your information is wrong."

"My information is never wrong. It's just occasionally optimistic."

They drink. The silence between them has teeth. Spike studies her in peripheral vision—the way she holds the bottle with her left hand, keeping her right free, fingers loose and ready near the Beretta. Professional. He respects that. He also notices the scar tissue on her knuckles, the kind you get from hitting things that don't give, and the faint shadows under her eyes that no amount of attitude can hide.

She catches him looking. "See something you like?"

"Seeing a lot of things. Haven't decided what I like yet."

"Smooth." She takes another pull of rum. "Here's how this works. I find Kovacs first, he's mine. You find him first—" She shrugs. "Won't happen."

"How about we work together? Split the bounty."

"I don't split anything."

"Neither do I, usually. But three million's better than a funeral."

The jukebox stutters, skips, catches the same verse again. A drunk at a corner table knocks over his glass. Nobody flinches except the bartender.

Revy leans closer. She smells like gunpowder and bad decisions. "You any good with that piece you're hiding under your jacket?"

"I get by."

"Getting by gets you killed."

"Already died once," Spike says, and means it. "Wasn't that impressive."

Something shifts in her expression—recognition, maybe. The look of someone who understands what it means to keep breathing out of spite rather than hope. Her eyes drop to his mouth, then back up. The moment holds like a pin in a grenade.

"Fine," she says. "One night. We find Kovacs together. Then we figure out who keeps the money."

---

They find Kovacs in a warehouse by the docks. The firefight lasts eleven minutes. Spike counts. Revy doesn't bother counting because she's too busy turning the arms dealer's security detail into modern art. He watches her move—both Berettas singing, brass casings catching light like thrown coins—and thinks she might be the most dangerous person he's ever been attracted to.

She catches him staring again when it's over, Kovacs zip-tied and whimpering between shipping containers. "You fight like you don't care if you live," she says, breathing hard.

"You fight like you're angry at the bullets for not going faster."

"Maybe I am."

---

The motel room smells like mildew and regret. They'd agreed: neutral ground to wait for extraction. One room. Cheaper that way.

Now Revy sits on the bed with a Beretta in her lap, and Spike leans against the door with his Jericho loose at his side, and the air between them is so thick with want and wariness that it's practically visible.

"One of us could just take the bounty," Revy says quietly. "Shoot the other. Simple math."

"Could," Spike agrees. He lights a cigarette. His hands are steady. "But I don't think that's why you haven't holstered that gun."

"No?"

"No. I think you're scared of what happens if you put it down."

Her jaw tightens. Her finger rests outside the trigger guard—disciplined even now. "You don't know a goddamn thing about me."

"I know you haven't slept in days. I know you drink like you're trying to forget something that won't stay forgotten." He exhales smoke toward the water-stained ceiling. "I know the feeling."

The jukebox from the bar is still playing in his head, that same scratchy track, grinding on and on.

Revy stands. Steps closer. The Beretta stays in her hand but points at the floor.

"Whatever happens, happens," Spike murmurs.

"Shut up," she says, and doesn't shoot.
