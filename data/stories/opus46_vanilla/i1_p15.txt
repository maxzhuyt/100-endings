# Therapeutic Boundaries

The diagram on the wall has no skin. That's what I keep coming back to—the écorché figure with its flayed musculature rendered in clinical reds and blues, every fiber labeled in Latin I once couldn't pronounce and now know like prayer. *Latissimus dorsi. Infraspinatus. Supraspinatus.* The supraspinatus is the one that betrayed me. A tendon the width of my index finger, and it took everything.

"Whenever you're ready, Mara."

Declan's voice comes from behind me, near the counter where he's warming gel between his palms. I'm sitting on the edge of the treatment table in a sports bra and compression shorts, and the paper beneath me crinkles every time I breathe. The room smells like eucalyptus and isopropyl alcohol and something underneath both—his soap, maybe, cedar and clean cotton—that I've been trying not to catalog for three weeks.

I'm not ready. I'm never ready.

"Ready," I say.

He comes around to my right side, and I fix my gaze on the diagram's exposed deltoid—three heads of muscle splayed open like a textbook flower—because looking at the wall is safer than looking at him. Declan Voss, who I last saw eight years ago at the national training center in Colorado Springs, when he was the best middle-distance runner in our cohort and I was the best hurdler and neither of us had yet discovered the specific ways the world would break us down. He'd blown his Achilles at twenty-four. Clean rupture. I remember the sound it made—a muffled pop like a champagne cork underwater. He'd screamed exactly once, then gone quiet in a way that frightened me more.

Now he has a doctorate in physical therapy and a practice with west-facing windows and I have a shoulder held together with suture anchors and the remnants of a career I can't stop reaching for like a phantom limb.

"Let's start with passive range. I'm going to take your arm through flexion. You tell me when you hit a five."

His left hand comes to rest on the posterior aspect of my shoulder—fingertips spanning from the spine of the scapula to the acromion, stabilizing the joint. His right hand cups my elbow from beneath. The contact is professional, moderate pressure, and it sends a current through me that has nothing to do with pain and everything to do with the fact that I haven't been touched with any kind of care in seven months.

He lifts my arm forward and up. Zero degrees, thirty, sixty—the movement is smooth, his hands guiding the humerus through its arc while his left fingers monitor the scapula for premature rotation. At ninety degrees, the first wire of discomfort threads through the anterior capsule.

"Three," I report.

He continues. One-ten. One-twenty. The wire becomes a cable, tightening.

"Five."

He holds me there. My arm is overhead at roughly one-thirty degrees of flexion, and I can feel the reconstructed supraspinatus tendon loading, the surgical site singing its high thin note of protest. His left hand has shifted—thumb now pressing into the upper trapezius, which has seized reflexively, a muscular flinch I can't control.

"You're guarding," he says. "Try to let the trap release."

"I'm not guarding."

"Mara." Quiet. Patient. The same voice he probably uses on every patient, but I knew him when his voice cracked calling splits at altitude, when he cursed in Afrikaans under his breath during ice baths, and the familiarity is its own kind of wound. "Your upper trap is rock-hard. Breathe into it."

I exhale. The muscle releases one reluctant degree. His thumb finds the new slack and presses deeper, a slow sustained pressure into the belly of the muscle, and the sensation blooms—not pain exactly, but its neighbor. That deep ache of tissue yielding under informed hands. My eyes close involuntarily.

"Good. I'm going to add some external rotation here. Same arm position."

He keeps my arm elevated and begins rotating it outward, his right hand guiding from the elbow so my forearm sweeps laterally like a gate opening. This is where the repaired labrum lives, where the anchors sit drilled into bone, and the stretch reaches into the joint with a specificity that makes my breath catch. Twenty degrees of external rotation. Thirty.

"Four," I manage, but my voice has thinned.

"I can feel your pec minor pulling," he says, and his left hand migrates from the trapezius down and forward, fingertips sliding beneath the lateral edge of my clavicle to find the pectoralis minor where it anchors to the coracoid process. The heel of his hand rests against the upper slope of my breast and we both know it and neither of us acknowledges it. His fingers press upward into the taut cord of the muscle and I make a sound—involuntary, guttural—that doesn't belong in a clinical setting.

His hands still.

One breath. Two.

"You okay?"

"It's tender there." Which is true. It is also true that his hand is on my chest and his face is close enough that I can see the small scar bisecting his left eyebrow from a fall on a wet track in 2016 and that his breathing has changed—shorter, shallower—in a way I recognize because I spent years reading competitors' bodies for signs of fatigue and what I'm reading now is not fatigue.

"I can work around it," he says.

"No. It needs work. Keep going."

He resumes the pressure. I open my eyes and stare at the diagram—the pectoralis minor illustrated in isolation, its serrated origin along ribs three through five, its insertion at the coracoid process exactly where his fingers are pressing now. The figure on the wall has no skin, no nerve endings, no memory of what it was. It is pure function, labeled and understood.

I envy it so much I could weep.

"Breathe, Mara."

I breathe. His hands move. The shoulder yields its slow, grudging degrees.

Outside the west-facing windows, the sun is going down, and neither of us mentions it, and neither of us stops.
