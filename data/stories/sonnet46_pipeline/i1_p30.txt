# Útiseta

I'll tell you what I know for certain.

I know that Astrid's boots were red—rubber, the kind sold in the entryway of petrol stations, hanging from a peg between ice scrapers and synthetic scarves. I know the mound was older than the village, older than the church that stood at the village's center like a reprimand. I know a bell rang once at midnight on a Saturday in November, though the church held no Saturday service and the pastor, as I confirmed by telephone the following Monday, had been in Umeå for the week attending a synod on rural congregation management.

Everything else I hold more loosely.

I am thirty-eight years old. I work in municipal planning, which is a profession organized entirely around what can be verified, mapped, cited in the record. I have colleagues who do not believe in anything they cannot put in a spreadsheet. I have, largely, become one of them. This is what I tell myself when I think about that night—which I do less often now than I used to, but which I have never once stopped doing entirely.

Something comes back sometimes. Not a memory exactly, more the edge of one. The smell of frost-stiffened grass and turned earth moving through the ventilation system of an office building or a train station, arriving without cause, leaving the same way. When it happens I sit very still at my desk until it passes, and then I get back to work.

---

We moved to Fröstvik in August of 1994, when I was fifteen and my father had accepted a position with the forestry authority that my mother described, through a fixed smile she deployed for exactly this kind of announcement, as a tremendous opportunity. Stockholm became memory. My bedroom window, which had looked out onto a courtyard with a lime tree, became someone else's bedroom window. I became the kind of girl who eats lunch alone, who has memorized the precise crack patterns in institutional ceiling tiles because looking up is a way of not looking around.

I should say something true, here, about how that felt. It felt like being faintly erased. Like watching Stockholm recede from the back window of a rented van, understanding that the city did not know you were leaving it, that you were not large enough to leave a shape behind. I had friends there. I had a geography I understood. I had a self assembled carefully over fifteen years in particular streets and particular rooms, and the self was not transportable, as it turned out. It was load-bearing. Without its walls it simply came apart.

Fröstvik was a town of four thousand people in a province that aspired to fewer. The school was one building for all ages. The forest came to the edge of everything—the parking lots, the churchyard, the football pitch—like a crowd pressed to a barrier, watching.

Astrid Björk found me in October. She sat down across from me in the canteen with her tray and her pale, evaluative eyes, and said, without preamble or introduction, that she knew something I didn't.

She was right. She was always right about that. It was the whole of her.

---

The midnight games had been going on for a month before she mentioned the útiseta.

They were her games, old ones, inherited: her grandmother had taught her, and her grandmother's grandmother before that, back and back through a lineage that dissolved into a period predating literacy in that part of Sweden. Astrid said this flatly, without mysticism, the way she said everything—as information rather than performance. You got the sense that she was genuinely uninterested in whether you found it impressive.

The games were small things, mostly. Writing a name on paper and reading it aloud three times over moving water. Standing at a crossroads at three in the morning and listening for something in the static of wind and branch that might be addressed to you. Astrid knew the rules of each one—the specific requirements, the prohibitions—with the confidence of someone who has been shown how to do something by a person who was shown by a person before her. There was no invention in it. That was what distinguished her from other fifteen-year-olds who claimed to practice magic: she wasn't making anything up. She was following instructions.

I told myself it was sophisticated rebellion. I told myself the real attraction was simply belonging—that I would have followed Astrid Björk into any darkness that winter, because she was the only person in Fröstvik who had chosen to see me. This was probably true. It is also true that something else was happening, something I didn't have language for then and have been failing to find language for ever since.

---

She described útiseta to me on the walk there. Her voice was even, pedagogical.

"It means sitting out. You go to a place where the dead are. You sit. You keep silent. You receive."

"Receive what?"

"Whatever comes," she said. As though this were obvious. As though the question revealed something slightly embarrassing about me.

The mound was twenty minutes into the forest on the eastern edge of town, a low rise in the earth no more than four meters across. I had passed it on the road a dozen times without understanding it as anything, filing it away as geological accident, the land shrugging. Astrid had known it her whole life. When we arrived she touched its side the way you touch a door before opening it—a question, or a courtesy—and then moved to the top and began clearing frost-stiffened grass from a small flat area with the side of her boot.

She took the preparations from a school notebook. Blue ink. Her handwriting was very small, precise, horizontal even on unlined pages.

No iron. Sit facing north. No speaking once begun. She had made me leave my watch at home because of the metal in the clasp. I had laughed at this when she mentioned it, and she had waited for me to finish laughing before confirming, without adjustment or self-consciousness, that she was serious.

"My grandmother was very clear about the iron," she said now, not looking up from the notebook.

"What happens with iron?"

She looked at me then, her pale eyes calm and unanxious. "It offends them," she said.

I did not ask who she meant. I understood, in some compartment I had not consciously opened, exactly who she meant.

---

We sat.

The cold came into me quickly—it was November, the ground hard since mid-October—but there was something else beneath the cold, something that had its own agenda separate from air temperature or exposure. A quality to the stillness. The forest was dark at twenty meters and absolute dark at thirty, and somewhere in that absolute dark, about forty meters to the east, the treeline went quiet in a specific way. Not the quiet of nothing. The quiet of a large animal that has stopped moving and turned to look at you.

I looked at Astrid. She had not turned her head.

I decided she hadn't noticed. I have since stopped believing that.

In the silence my mind moved where it wanted, unmoored. It moved to my bedroom in Stockholm—not abstractly, but specifically: the lime tree in the courtyard below, which would have gone gold in October, which was going gold right now without me to see it, which would go bare before I returned and then I would have missed the whole year of it. The thought arrived with a weight I hadn't expected. I was fifteen years old and I missed a tree. I missed the particular quality of afternoon light that came through the window in the last hour before dark, and I missed my desk beneath that window, and I missed the self I had been at that desk—the self that was still continuous, still coherent, still in possession of a geography it understood.

I was thinking about this, sitting on top of the dead, when the bell rang.

---

One stroke, clean and carrying, from the direction of the village. I felt it in my back teeth. I felt it in the base of my sternum. The sound moved through the forest without scattering—it arrived all at once, intact, and then passed into the trees behind us and into the ground beneath us and into whatever was beneath the ground.

Astrid's eyes were open and she was looking at the mound beneath us as if she were reading text printed on the inside of the earth. She did not startle. She did not change her expression. But her breathing changed—became slower, more deliberate, more spacious—the breathing of someone receiving information through a channel the rest of us can't locate.

The echo dispersed. The forest was extremely still.

"Did you—" I said, and she raised one finger without turning her head. Not a gesture of annoyance. A reminder. The rules.

I stopped.

What followed I cannot characterize with confidence, and I have tried more times than I will admit.

There was a pressure in the ears, the kind that comes with rapid altitude change, as if something in the immediate atmosphere had altered its weight. The frost on the grass around the mound appeared to darken, though there was no moon that night and I have never been able to account for what I saw it by. My hands were in my lap and I became aware of them there, of the small ordinary fact of them, and I became aware with the same quality of attention that we were sitting on top of people. Not remains. People. And that the people—whatever remained of what makes people people—were not absent.

I understood, then, with the pre-rational certainty of a dream, that the bell had not been for us. That it had functioned as an announcement, and that we were the subject of the announcement. That something in the earth below us was becoming aware of us the way you become aware of something when someone has pointed at it for you.

Astrid touched my arm once, lightly.

It meant: now we go.

---

We did not speak on the walk back. The rules had not been formally lifted, and neither of us tested them. I walked behind her—her red boots leaving their prints in the frost, the trees passing on either side—and I thought about nothing. I was not capable of thought. I was only capable of walking.

At the edge of the forest she stopped and turned back to look at the dark we had come through. I turned with her. There was nothing to see. The trees stood. The dark between them held its shape.

"Well," she said.

"Well," I said.

It was the most honest conversation I have ever had.

---

On the walk home, after, I understood several things that had been true for some time.

That Astrid had not told me what tonight was. Not the full of it. She had given me the frame—the name, the mound, the instructions—but she had withheld the stakes, whether by design or by the assumption that true stakes do not require announcement. That she had done it before, many times, alone, and that her composure throughout the evening was not the composure of bravado but of someone who knows a place and knows its customs. That I had been brought along as something—I could not have said what. As company. As witness. As an additional offering of presence and attention, placed on that mound like a second candle.

That I had agreed to something whose terms I had not read, the way you hold a pen above a signature and think: it can't matter much, and sign.

That the agreement had been registered.

---

I am in Stockholm now. I have been back since 1996, when my father's contract ended and the tremendous opportunity concluded, without ceremony, in February. We drove back the same way we had come and the city appeared ahead of us through the windscreen and I remember thinking that it looked smaller than I had left it. That I was looking at it differently. That something had changed the aperture.

I have a flat. I have a job. I have a window that looks out onto a courtyard, but there is no lime tree—only concrete, painted grey, with a bike rack and a recycling station. I have been trying, for more than twenty years, to establish myself as a person organized around the verifiable. I am largely successful at this.

Fröstvik is too small for satellite coverage detailed enough to locate the mound. I have checked. I have looked at the maps for longer than I intend to admit, tracing the eastern forest road, looking for a rise in the terrain that might correspond to what I sat on. I cannot find it. I do not know what I would do if I could.

Here is what I know. The bell rang. We both heard it, Astrid and I, which means it was not interior—it moved through the same air we were breathing, left the same trace in us both, the way a shared fact does.

Astrid's boots were red and left two small prints in the frost beside the mound, and my boots left two larger ones beside hers.

Here is what I don't know. Whether anything sat with us, there in the dark above the dead. What it understood about me from the sitting—about my loneliness, about the tree I missed, about the particular shape of the absence I had arrived there with. Whether what registered the agreement is still holding it. Whether there is a door, somewhere, that opened when the bell rang, and whether I walked back through it or left it ajar behind me.

I hold all of that loosely.

It took me a long time to learn how.
