# Sisyphus Gives Advice

The mountain, as mountains go, was not particularly dramatic. It had the requisite steepness, a reasonable complement of loose shale, and a summit that managed to look both achievable and, once you got close to it, subtly further away than it had appeared. The boulder—granite, approximately the size of a very ambitious garden shed—was currently about two-thirds of the way up.

Crow sat on a nearby outcropping, sorting envelopes by a system that appeared to involve smell.

"Next one," said Sisyphus, without breaking stride.

Crow tore the envelope open with his beak, spread the letter flat against the rock with one talon, and read:

---

**Dear Boulder & Beyond,**

*My husband has started leaving his socks on the bathroom floor. Not just beside the hamper. On the floor. I've asked him seventeen times. I've tried being nice. I've tried being less nice. I've tried leaving his socks on his pillow. Nothing works. I am starting to wonder if I married the right person. Please help.*

*— Unraveling in Uttoxeter*

---

Sisyphus considered this for a moment, adjusting his grip near a patch of shale that had been giving him trouble since the fourteenth century.

"Tell her: the socks aren't the problem."

Crow picked up the quill. *Obviously,* he muttered, but wrote it down.

"The socks are the flag for a problem, which is that she's stopped believing he hears her. Seventeen requests means she's been tracking. She's been building a case." He grunted as the slope steepened past what the gradient had any right to be. "Ask her: when was the last time she told him something was bothering her *before* it had happened seventeen times?"

He found his footing and resumed.

"And ask him—gently, through her—whether he knows she's keeping count. Men often don't. It isn't stupidity. It's a failure to understand that the bathroom floor is a vote. That every sock is a small ballot he thinks is blank."

Crow scribbled. He considered the letter. He added, in smaller letters at the bottom of the draft: *[Ed. note: Crow would also leave the socks. Crow does not apologize for this.]*

"What was the next one?"

"Give me a moment." Crow was already tearing.

---

**Dear B&B,**

*I work for a man who takes credit for my ideas in meetings. I've tried speaking to him directly. He said I was "too sensitive." I can't afford to leave the job. I have two cats and a mortgage and a mother who rings every Sunday to ask if I've met anyone nice. What do I do?*

*— Invisible in Inverness*

---

"Mm." Sisyphus reached the summit. The boulder arrived with him, teetered at the edge with what he privately felt was unnecessary theatricality, found its conviction, and began its return journey. He watched it go the way a baker watches bread prove—without attachment, but with professional interest in the process. A new crack had opened on the left-hand face. He noted it.

Then he started back down.

"The manager is a problem she cannot solve directly, so she needs to make herself less solvable," he said, matching his descent to a comfortable pace. "Documentation first. Paper trail. After every meeting, send a follow-up to the group—'As I mentioned earlier'—timestamped, attributed. This makes theft logistically complicated without requiring a confrontation she can't win." He stopped to examine a piece of loose shale, decided it would be a problem in three days, and moved it off the path. "Confrontation requires leverage. She doesn't have leverage yet. Act accordingly."

Crow wrote this down.

"The mother is a separate question. She might try answering 'Not yet, but I'm enjoying the search,' which is both true and completely unanswerable. It's very hard to argue with someone who appears to be content." He reached the boulder. Set his hands against it. "As for the cats—the cats are probably fine. Cats are very good at not requiring anything particular from you. They have mastered this."

Crow recorded the cat observation with the focused expression of someone who finds it accurate.

*[Ed. note: Crow has met cats. Crow maintains this was their fault.]*

He riffled through the bag for the next letter. It smelled of something he initially took for cedar, then revised to mean a different kind of wood entirely—the indoor kind, the kind that furniture becomes, the kind that surrounds a person who has been in one place long enough for it to start feeling like the only place.

---

**Dear B&B,**

*I'm 34. I have a good job, good friends, a nice flat. Everyone keeps telling me I should be happy. I am not miserable, exactly. I just don't know if this is it. Did I already choose, without meaning to? Am I just living inside a choice I made when I was twenty-two and didn't know anything, and now it's too late to unchoose it? Is this what it feels like? Is everyone walking around feeling like this and simply not saying so?*

*— Waiting for Something in Winchester*

---

Sisyphus said nothing for a while. The boulder demanded attention near the upper third, where the angle changed and the shale gave way to something more reliable, and he gave it his full attention. Then the slope leveled out a little, and he spoke.

"Tell her," he said, "that the feeling she's describing isn't failure. It's a gap between who she is now and who she hasn't yet tried being." He measured his breath against the incline. "The gap feels enormous because she's standing inside it. That's the nature of gaps. You can't see their actual size until you've crossed enough of one to turn around."

He adjusted his grip.

"Lives don't announce themselves. They accumulate direction—the way a path looks like nothing in particular while you're on it, and then you look back and see that it was, in fact, a path all along. She hasn't chosen wrong. She's just in the middle, which feels like standing still." A pause, while the summit resolved itself overhead. "The practical thing—not the philosophical thing, the practical thing—is to pick one thing she's been meaning to try and try it actually, this week, not eventually. Not because it will be the thing. Because trying things is the only mechanism by which anyone finds out what the thing is. You cannot think your way to it. You have to move."

He reached the top. The boulder and he regarded each other briefly in the way of very old acquaintances who have long since exhausted small talk.

"Add," he said, "that good friends and work that doesn't hollow her out are not consolation prizes. They're infrastructure. Don't tear up the infrastructure while you're still working out the destination."

He set his hands and started back.

Crow wrote carefully, slowly, as if the letter deserved it. He read it back to himself without speaking. Then, in the margin, smaller than usual:

*[Ed. note: Crow has no notes. Crow is going to go think about some things.]*

---

The sun had made its own professional assessment of the day and begun its descent. Sisyphus watched it with the mild appreciation of someone who has seen approximately eleven thousand of them and found each one, on the whole, adequate.

The bag was nearly empty. Crow stood at the edge of the outcropping and checked the outgoing letters—three of them, folded, addressed in Sisyphus's handwriting, which was perfectly legible and slightly archaic in its formality, as if he'd learned to write at a time when handwriting still meant something and saw no reason to update. He tucked them under his wing.

"That the last one?"

Crow riffled through the bag. Found one more. Held it up.

"Save it," said Sisyphus. "For tomorrow."

Crow considered disputing this on principle, then didn't.

He took three running hops off the outcropping and lifted into the air—outgoing letters in his feet, wings catching the thermal that formed every evening along the eastern face, reliable as anything on the mountain. Below him, the hill stretched down to the world, where people were sitting down to write letters in which they apologized for bothering someone with something probably small, please ignore this if you're busy, it's just—

And then the thing they actually meant.

The boulder, behind him, was rising.

Sisyphus had his shoulder to it in the way he'd had his shoulder to it for longer than most of the world's languages had words for time. His feet knew the path. His hands knew the stone. The crack on the left-hand face caught the last of the light and made something almost like a feature of itself, a particular quality the boulder had acquired and would carry, now, everywhere it went.

Which was here. Which was always here.

The mountain was quiet except for the sound of effort, which after a while is not so different from the sound of anything else that continues.
