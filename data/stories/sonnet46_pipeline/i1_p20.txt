# Gunpowder Rendezvous

The bar is called something in Thai that the sign is too burned-out to read, and it doesn't matter. What matters is that it's open, it's dark, and the bourbon is cheap enough that the label is embarrassed to have letters on it. Spike is three drinks into not thinking about anything when the jukebox finds its groove — some scratchy rock song with a guitar line that stumbles over itself like a man leaving a fight — and stays there, repeating, because nobody has fed it anything else and nobody is going to.

He lights his third cigarette off his second and watches the door.

It's a habit. Not paranoia, exactly — paranoia implies you think something is coming for you specifically, and Spike has been mostly at peace with what's coming for him for a few years now. He watches the door because watching the door is what keeps the eyes busy while everything else goes quiet, and quiet is the only thing he's actually after tonight.

He finishes the cigarette. Lights another.

The bartender doesn't talk to him because the bartender is sensible.

The jukebox trips over itself and starts back at the top of the same track.

She comes in the way trouble usually does: without announcing itself, looking like it owns the floorspace, wearing almost nothing. Tank top. Short shorts. A dragon coiling up her right arm in ink dark enough to read from across the room. Two guns on her hips the way other women wear earrings — not accessories, more like a decision she made about herself some time ago and has never revisited.

She scans the room. Professionally. Her eyes move the way his do — cataloguing exits, weight distributions, hands below the bar. They land on him and stop.

He doesn't look away. Neither does she.

She crosses to him. Drops onto the stool beside his — not *next* to him, more like adjacent to him in the way two weather systems are adjacent, each one aware of what the other can do. She signals the bartender with two fingers. She doesn't take her eyes off the middle distance. Neither does he.

"Nakamura job," she says. Not a question.

He exhales slowly toward the ceiling. "Yep."

"I'm not splitting it."

"Neither am I."

Her drink arrives. She takes it without looking at the bartender. The jukebox finds its stumble in the guitar line and begins again.

---

Her name, he gets eventually, is Revy. He'll find out later it's Rebecca underneath it, though neither name seems to have anything much to do with her — they're both signage for a building that would mean what it means regardless of what you called it. She drinks with the focus of someone competing, and she talks the way a person talks when every sentence is priced against the possibility of what comes next: economically, with sharp edges left un-filed, the subordinate clauses cut. He smokes and watches her the way he watches most things — the easy eye and the other one, the one that doesn't entirely agree with the world about depth and distance.

They talk around the bounty. They establish, without agreeing to anything, that Nakamura is being cautious, that the windows are bad on the east side of his building, that he moves with two men who are serious and one who isn't. They don't agree on approach. They don't argue about it particularly hard either. The negotiation keeps losing thread.

"You always this quiet?" she says.

"When the alternative is listening to myself, yeah."

Something crosses her face. He can't tell if it's a laugh or contempt. He gets the sense she might not be sure either.

"Where'd you come from?" she asks.

"Around."

"Very helpful."

"Mars," he says. "Roughly."

This time it's  more clearly a laugh — brief, involuntary, quickly reclaimed by the expression she prefers. She refills her glass. He smokes.

"You?" he asks.

"Around," she says.

"Roanapur."

She looks at him directly for the first time in a few minutes. "You know it?"

"I've heard things about it."

"Things."

"Colorful things."

She considers him. He gets the impression she is deciding how much of what she usually is she needs to be right now, specifically, with this one — whether the full performance is warranted or whether something can be set down for twenty minutes without the ceiling caving in.

The jukebox decides for her. It stumbles back to the start of the track. She fills her glass again.

---

When it happens, it happens with no preamble, because violence has no interest in building toward itself — it's already there, behind everything, just waiting for someone to step back and let it through.

Two stools down, a man has been arriving at a conclusion for the last half hour, and he arrives at it now: he is owed something by the bartender, and the something is money, and the method of expressing this conviction is his hands around the man's collar across the bar. He reaches. He grabs.

Revy's right gun is out before his fingers close.

Spike is already standing, already at the angle he needs, the Jericho level — and she registers this without looking at him, and he registers her without looking at her, because neither of them needs to look, their peripheral vision is not peripheral in any meaningful sense.

The man behind the two stools looks at two gun barrels pointed at the general territory of his face.

He lets go of the bartender.

He sits back down.

He is very still.

Spike holsters. Revy holsters. They turn back to the bar and look at each other.

He's seen fast before. He's been fast before. What he's watching for — what he's always watched for, even before he knew he was watching — is the quality of it. Whether a person has speed as a trait or speed as a language. Whether it's something they do or something they *are*, all the way in, no performance in it because there's no time for performance and so you see what's actually built into the person at the level that can't be chosen.

She had moved clean. No showboating in it. No arc wasted. Function, just function, the gun as a logical conclusion to a premise the situation supplied.

He knows what that looks like. He recognizes it the way, he imagines, one specific kind of damage recognizes another specific kind of damage when they share a room.

"Jeet Kune Do," she says. Also not a question.

"Something in the neighborhood."

She looks at him for a moment. "You're good."

"You're better."

The jukebox begins its track again.

---

They don't leave.

That, more than anything else, is what it is.

Every reasonable stopping point passes and neither of them takes it, and neither of them names what they're doing because naming it would make it something they'd have to decide about. So they order another round instead. And then the one after that.

"You ever get tired of it?" Revy says.

"Of which part."

"I don't know." She turns her glass in a slow circle against the bar top. "The running numbers. The chasing someone until you find them and then they're just a person standing in a room and you're just a person who found them and somehow that's supposed to be the ending." She drinks. "I don't mean the work. I mean whatever you're telling yourself the work is for."

He doesn't answer right away. He's thinking about the best way to not answer, and she waits, which surprises him a little.

"I stopped telling myself it's for something," he says. "It's mostly easier."

"That's bleak."

"Realistic."

"That's what bleak people say."

He exhales smoke. "I died. Already. Few years back." He says it the way he'd say he lost a wallet — matter-of-fact, mildly inconvenient in retrospect. "Past that point, mostly I'm just interested in seeing what happens."

She is very still for a moment.

She's trying to determine if he is performing, he can see her trying — searching the claim for the seams, the place where an ordinary person would have dressed it up in something. She doesn't find it. He watches her not find it.

"I haven't died," she says. The glass turns another circle. "I've been—" She stops. "There were a few years that were close enough that I'm not sure the technical difference matters."

"Yeah," he says.

"Yeah." She drinks. Her voice when she comes back is harder than it was, louder by about fifteen percent, which is how he knows something just got close enough to scare her. "Anyway. Nakamura's running out of town in forty-eight hours, we know that, so we need to—"

"Sure," he says.

"You didn't let me finish."

"You were going to say we need a plan."

She looks at him. Something behind her eyes is not angry, exactly. It is the expression of someone who is not used to being read and is working through whether that's a problem.

"We need a plan," she says.

The jukebox starts over.

---

The motel is two blocks east and one block south of the building where Nakamura's been sleeping, which is the reason they give each other for ending up in the same room. The reason is functional. It holds up, rationally. It is the kind of reason that is doing a great deal of work and both of them know it and neither of them says so.

The room is small. One window, the curtain not quite closing all the way. Street neon pushes in at the gap — pink and cold, breathing.

The bed has a polyester cover in a geometric pattern that probably had a color at some point and has since decided to be mostly beige. One chair. One lamp that lights only the corner it's in. The jukebox from the bar is not audible, but Spike can still hear the shape of the track somehow, the way a song stays in you after the room goes quiet.

Revy is drawing the approach on the notepad by the phone — south entrance, rotation of the two serious men, estimated window — and talking through it, asking him where he'd want to stand, which he answers, and they find an agreement faster than he expected and slower than she expected, and both of them note this and say nothing about it.

He moves to check the window.

She registers the shift in his position — registers it the way she registered the gunplay, in some part of herself that is always calculating geometry, that is always mapping the room and running the numbers on where everybody in it is and how fast they could cover the distance between positions and to what end. She goes still. He goes still.

They arrive at the same conclusion at the same velocity.

Both guns out.

Both guns level.

He's looking at her down the barrel of his Jericho. She's looking at him down the barrel of something that is absolutely not regulation anything. The neon breathes pink across her collarbones, across her arm, the dragon lit in cold intervals.

It's very quiet.

Through the wall — very faintly — some muffled signal from two blocks west that might be the jukebox beginning its track again.

He doesn't lower his gun.

She doesn't lower hers.

He can feel the exact shape of the room. The distance between her and the window. The distance between her and him. The particular quality of the air when two people with this amount of mutual regard and mutual suspicion and mutual — something else, something that doesn't have a useful name — occupy a space this small with nowhere to go and nothing to say that the guns aren't already saying more honestly.

*I don't trust you.*

*I don't trust anyone.*

*I haven't trusted anyone in long enough that I've stopped being sure I remember how.*

*You move like someone who's had things taken from them and decided to become the kind of person things don't get taken from anymore.*

*I know that decision.*

*I made that decision.*

*I don't know what to do with someone who made the same one.*

Neither of them says any of it.

Then she laughs. Short. Surprised, he thinks, by the laugh itself — like it arrived before she could think about whether to let it. She doesn't lower her gun. He doesn't lower his.

The neon breathes.

The track plays on and on.
