# Caribou Season at the Lodge

## I.

The lodge looked exactly right.

That was the first thing JT noticed, pulling the truck up the rutted access road with the spruce pressing in close on both sides, their shadows long in the late afternoon—the way the cedar shingles had gone that particular shade of silver, the way the porch sagged twelve degrees off level on the eastern end, the way the single light in the kitchen window threw a rectangle of yellow onto the gravel that looked, had always looked, like something spilled. He'd been coming here since he was nine years old. He knew the place the way he knew his own palm, the creases and the scar tissue included.

He sat with the engine ticking and looked at it.

His father was already inside—had driven up the day before to get the woodstove going, air the bunks out, run through the usual opening rituals of the season. Mike would be in by suppertime. JT was the last one here, same as always. He had a specific and longstanding relationship with arriving last at things.

He got out of the truck and stood in the cold. Late October and already the air had that mineral edge, that particular North Ontario smell of cold rock and dark water and something vegetal beginning its long submission to winter. He breathed it in. He'd been looking forward to this, or had told himself he'd been looking forward to it, for months.

The trees leaned.

He blinked. Looked at the line of spruce along the property's northern edge, black against the pale sky. They were not leaning. Trees did not lean. He was tired from the drive, the four hours of highway followed by the forty minutes of gravel and the final fifteen minutes of access road that wanted very badly to be a riverbed. His eyes were doing what tired eyes did.

He pulled his duffel from the bed of the truck and went inside.

---

His father was at the kitchen table with a cup of tea, going through a topo map with one finger tracing the cut-lines, and he looked up when JT came through and said, "There he is," in the voice he reserved for JT specifically—not warm exactly, but something adjacent to warm, something that had learned warmth the way a person learns a second language and could deploy it accurately without quite feeling it from the inside.

"Dad."

"Long drive?"

"Long enough."

His father nodded, looked back at the map. JT stowed his gear and poured coffee and they sat together with the stove going orange and the shadows thickening outside, and it was fine. It was ordinary. His father talked about the weather window, the pressure system tracking in from the northwest, the herd movement reports he'd gotten from a man he knew in Cochrane. JT listened and drank bad coffee and looked, when his father wasn't watching, at his father's hands around the mug.

Broad hands. Thick at the knuckle. Hands that had dressed elk and split cords of wood and, once, held JT's eight-year-old head under water in a lake in Algonquin while he thrashed, because swimming required thrashing before it required technique, and thrashing was how you learned.

*You're all right*, his father had said when he let him up. *You're all right. See?*

JT looked away from the hands.

Outside, the trees were not leaning. He could see the tops of the nearest spruce through the kitchen window, black against a sky that had gone dark green at the edges, and they were perfectly vertical, perfectly still. There was no wind.

He told himself that was a good sign.

---

## II.

The next morning.

Cold enough that their breath went up in columns as they moved single-file through the spruce, JT behind his brother, Mike behind their father, the way they'd always walked this country—their father leading with that unhurried North Country pace, the pace that could cover fifteen miles without appearing to hurry, Mike fitting himself to it the way Mike always did, and JT bringing up the rear and watching his footing.

The bull stepped into the cut-line forty minutes out.

It was a cow, actually—his father's tag was for a cow—a mature animal in good condition, already in winter coat, head up and facing them from maybe a hundred and twenty meters. JT stopped. He watched the animal through the trees.

It had heard them, certainly. Smelled them. The wind was wrong and they were not especially quiet and the animal had every sense God put into a prey animal and was looking directly at them as if to confirm what its nose had already told it.

It did not move.

His father raised the rifle slowly, the way his father did everything—with ceremony, with a kind of considered gravity, as though hurrying a thing that didn't need hurrying was a form of disrespect. The scope came up. The seconds stretched. JT watched his father's back, the stillness in his shoulders, the long exhale.

The shot went. The animal went down.

Not sideways, not pitching forward the way an animal will when the shot takes them right—it folded. Precise, almost vertical, the way something folds when it lets go. Like water leaving a shape.

They walked up to it in the snow.

JT had dressed out a dozen animals in this country. He knew the look, the weight of them, the way the steam comes off immediately in cold air. He walked up and looked at this one and something moved through him from the soles of his boots up through his chest and sat at the base of his throat.

The animal's eye was open. Clear, dark, still. Not glazed the way they glaze, not rolling back—just open, and looking at something, and whatever it was looking at was not the sky.

"Clean," Mike said, and meant the shot.

"Yep," their father said, and knelt, and the work began.

JT watched the eye for a moment longer. Then he looked away and watched the trees. *Always safer that way*, he told himself, which was a phrase his father had said to him once about something else entirely and which he had repurposed, over the years, to cover a great many situations his father had never intended it for.

---

## III.

It was on the third morning that JT went looking for Mike and didn't find him.

Mike had said he was heading for the eastern promontory, where the cut-lines opened into muskeg and the caribou liked to cross in early light. JT wanted to walk with him, had thought while lying in his bunk in the gray pre-dawn that maybe on the walk he would say something about the cow, about the way it had folded, about whether Mike had noticed—but Mike had gone out before JT was dressed, and JT followed an hour later and quartered north on the chance of intercepting him at a mid-point.

He heard them from maybe sixty meters.

Voices, he thought first. Then he understood it wasn't voices. It was silence of a particular kind, the silence that has substance and pressure, the silence of something happening that hadn't been arranged to be seen.

He came through the black spruce and stopped without deciding to stop.

The clearing was small, maybe thirty feet across, a natural break in the canopy where the light came down in a flat gray column. The buck was in the middle of it. He was an enormous animal, a rack like bare hardwood, and he was standing still with his head slightly lowered, and his eyes—

JT's father was three feet from it.

His brother stood to the left and slightly behind, rifle hanging at his side, barrel to the ground. His face was turned toward the buck and his expression—JT looked at his brother's face and did not recognize it. Not absent. Somewhere else. The way people look when they're listening to music they've heard before and love and don't hear anymore because loving it is now just part of how they exist.

Their father's arm was rising.

Slow. Not reaching for anything, not the way you reach for a thing. The way water rises to a level. His hand came up and out, palm open, fingers loose, toward the dark muzzle of the buck, and the buck lowered its head further—almost to his hand, almost—

JT's father's lips were parted. His eyes were bright in a way that JT had no word for and didn't want one.

JT stood in the trees and did not move and felt something he would not, later, be able to name—not quite fear, though fear was part of it. Something closer to what you feel when you walk into a room and realize you have interrupted something private, something between other people, and the interruption is the violation even though you hadn't known.

He watched his father's hand hang in the air an inch from that dark muzzle.

He watched his brother's face.

He took one step backward into the trees, and then another, and then he turned around and walked, quietly and with great care, back the way he had come. His face was hot despite the cold. He told himself there was an explanation. He told himself there were several. He picked the most reasonable one and filed it away behind silence, the same place he'd put the cow's open eye, and he walked back to the lodge and poured himself the first of what would be several cups of bad coffee and sat at the kitchen table and looked at his hands until they steadied.

---

## IV.

The abandoned cabin sat maybe three-quarters of a mile northeast of the lodge, deeper in the spruce than JT had wandered in years, and he wouldn't have gone near it at all if his bootlace hadn't snapped clean through on a root.

He'd been following the cut-line back toward camp when it happened, pitching him forward into a crouch with one hand braced against a birch trunk. He cursed quietly, the sound swallowed immediately by the trees the way sound always got swallowed out here—not muffled but *consumed*, like the woods were hungry for it. He stood, took stock. He had a spare lace in his left breast pocket. He'd need to sit somewhere to thread it.

He looked up.

The cabin was just there, between two black spruce close enough to touch each other at the crown. He hadn't been walking toward it. He was fairly certain of that. He stood with his weight on his good boot and told himself it didn't matter, it was just a building, and the building was just lumber going soft with rot, same as it had been for thirty years by his father's telling.

*Old Henriksen's place*, his father called it when he called it anything, which wasn't often. *Man walked out into the bush one November and didn't come back. Wife cleared out. Nobody wanted it after that.* His father had a way of delivering those sentences—flat, tamped down like tobacco in a pipe—that meant the conversation was finished.

JT walked toward the cabin.

The door was not locked, which he'd expected. What he hadn't expected was for it to be open. Not ajar. Not swung wide on a broken hinge. Precisely, deliberately open—a gap of maybe eight inches, as though someone had stepped out recently and hadn't bothered to pull it shut behind them.

He stood in the doorway and did not go in.

Inside: a woodstove with its iron door hanging. A bunk frame stripped to bare metal. On the floor, something that had been a wool blanket once and was now a flat gray shadow of one, compressed into the boards by years of damp. The smell was deep and cold, mineral and animal at once, the way a deer smells when you dress it out in cold weather—that intimate reek of something interior exposed to winter air.

There was a tin cup on the windowsill.

JT stared at the tin cup for a long time. He could not have said with any certainty whether it had always been there, whether some other hunter had used the place for shelter and left it, whether it meant anything at all. His mind offered him several reasonable explanations in a row, the way a magician fans a deck—*pick one, any one, they're all equally good*—and he took the most reasonable and turned away from the doorway and went back toward camp, threading his spare lace in while he walked, feeling the whole time like something behind him was watching with that particular quality of attention that wasn't curiosity or hunger but something closer to *recognition*.

---

## V.

His father and Mike were already back when he got in. The kitchen smelled like woodsmoke and salt pork. Mike was cleaning his rifle at the table with the same methodical patience he'd had since he was fourteen, running the bore brush through in slow measured strokes. Their father sat in the camp chair by the window with a mug going cold in both hands, looking out at the tree line.

Neither of them looked up when JT came through the door.

He stood in the mudroom peeling out of his jacket and thought about the clearing. Thought about his father's hand, trembling, reaching toward that dark muzzle. He thought about Mike's eyes—glassy, somewhere else.

"Anything?" Mike said, without looking up from the disassembled action.

"Tracks," JT said. "Lot of sign up by the cut-line. Nothing I could put in front of a shot."

Mike nodded. Their father didn't move.

JT hung his jacket and went to the stove and poured himself coffee that had been sitting since noon, the kind of coffee that was more of a geological process than a beverage by that point. He took a long swallow and looked at the back of his father's head—the thin place at the crown, the gray working through the brown—and thought: *say something*. His mind produced the line with absolute clarity: *Dad, what happened out there this morning? What was that buck?*

He said: "Cold snap coming. Probably overnight."

His father made a sound that was agreement, or acknowledgment, or just the noise a man makes when he's somewhere else inside himself and the outside world requires some kind of response.

Mike set the cleaned barrel down with a soft click. "Somebody been in that old Henriksen place, you figure?"

JT went very still. He turned from the stove slowly.

"Why?" he said.

Mike shrugged, squinting down the bore. "Saw smoke from up that direction when I was coming down off the ridge this morning. Early, before you were all up. Probably nothing. Deer hunters from the next township, maybe."

"Probably nothing," JT agreed.

His father finally turned from the window. His expression was ordinary, tired, the same expression he'd worn every evening of every hunting trip JT could remember, and yet something in the set of his eyes—

JT looked away first.

---

The dinner was salt pork and canned beans heated over the camp stove, and bread that had gone from fresh to acceptable to geological, and his father told the story of the bull moose he'd called in at Jubilee Lake in 1987, which JT had heard seven times and Mike had heard more. His father was a good storyteller in the way that men who don't talk much are sometimes good storytellers—the words, when they came, came precisely, with nothing extra, the way a good shot goes.

JT watched his father's face while he told it.

He watched for something. He wasn't sure what. Some seam in the ordinary expression, some evidence of what he'd seen in that clearing—the parted lips, the too-bright eyes, the hand going out toward a thing no reasonable man goes toward. He found nothing. His father ate his beans and told his story and poured himself a finger of rye when the dishes were cleared and sat in the camp chair and looked at the stove, and there was nothing there that wasn't supposed to be there.

Mike laughed at the ending of the story, the way Mike always laughed—surprised, like he'd forgotten the ending and then remembered it. Their father's mouth moved in something that wasn't quite a smile but occupied the same general territory.

*What happened this morning*, JT thought, staring into his mug. *What was that animal, and what were you doing, and why did you forget the rifle?*

He said: "Good shot on the cow yesterday, Dad."

"Wind cooperated," his father said.

The stove ticked. Outside, snow had begun, fine and dry, audible only as a faint persistence against the window glass.

---

He lay on his bunk that night and listened to his father and brother breathe themselves down into sleep with the ease of men who carried no particular weight that evening, and he stared up at the ceiling where the shadows from the wood stove moved across old knots in the pine boards, and he thought about the tin cup on the windowsill.

He had not touched it.

He was somewhat relieved by that.

Outside, far off but distinct, something passed through the brush—not crashing, not fleeing, but moving at its own considered pace through the dark, the way something moves when it already knows exactly where it's going and has no reason to hurry.

JT closed his eyes.

He told himself he'd sleep. He told himself tomorrow they'd take a bull and pack out and drive south with the windows cracked and the heater blasting and everything would smell like pine and blood and ordinary distance, the way it always smelled on the way home. He told himself there was nothing in the forest that was waiting for anything.

He was almost sure that was true.
