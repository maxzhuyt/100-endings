# Gone South
### Chapter One: The City That Care Forgot

The truck doesn't slow down to drop me off. It just stops being worth my while to stay in it, and I get out, and then it's gone, and I'm standing on a street corner in New Orleans at nine in the morning with forty-three dollars and a backpack that smells like I've been wearing the same clothes since Tuesday, which I have.

Which I have, because I have.

I stand on the corner and do what needs doing. Backpack: still there, zipper still closed, nothing missing. Cash: forty-three dollars folded into the front pocket of my jeans, which I confirm by pressing my hand flat against my thigh like I'm checking for a heartbeat. Phone: dead. Time: the clock on the bank building across the street says 9:07, and the light has that early yellow quality that means the heat is only getting started.

The heat here is a different animal than Tennessee heat. Back home it's dry and mean and knows exactly what it's doing to you. This heat is something else—thick and damp and borderline *affectionate*, like it wants to wrap around you and will not be refused. I'm sweating through my shirt within thirty seconds of standing still. My jeans feel like a bad decision. Everything about my body feels like a bad decision right now, which is not a new feeling, just a newly specific one.

*Okay*, I think. *You're here. This is the part where you figure out what here means.*

I don't know what here means. I know what there meant, and I left it, and now here I am in the city that care forgot with forty-three dollars and the particular vertigo of someone who hasn't slept more than two hours at a stretch since Monday night and who has spent those waking hours sitting in the passenger seats of strangers' trucks watching Tennessee become something else entirely and trying to decide which fact about the last forty-eight hours is the one that matters most.

None of them feel like they matter most. All of them feel like too much.

There's a saxophone playing somewhere down the block. I can hear it over the traffic, over the tourists taking photographs of everything, over the general *loudness* of this place, which is louder than anywhere I have ever been—not loud like a concert, loud like a living thing, loud like the city itself is producing sound from its own animal lungs. The saxophone cuts through all of it. Playing something slow and gorgeous and deeply unbothered. Playing like it has no interest in being interrupted by the ambient chaos of approximately ten thousand people doing ten thousand separate things in close proximity to each other.

I resent it immediately. Which I notice, and note, and keep walking.

---

I walk because standing still makes me feel like a target and also because I don't know what else to do with a body that has too much adrenaline and nowhere in particular to put it. My legs know how to walk. I let them.

The French Quarter is doing the absolute most. Wrought iron everywhere—railings, balconies, fences—all of it worked into elaborate shapes, vines and flowers in metal, impractical and beautiful and somehow slightly overripe, like everything here is one afternoon past its peak in the best possible way. The buildings are painted colors that would look wrong anywhere else: ochre, coral, a green that is almost too green, a pink that has made its peace with being a pink. Balconies dripping with ferns and potted things. A doorway with a mosaic set into the threshold. A window box full of flowers whose names I don't know.

Everybody has somewhere to be. Or something to perform. Or someone to do it all for. Two women in matching sundresses are photographing each other against a turquoise wall. A man in a suit is talking into his phone with the urgency of someone whose problems are expensive. A group of what I think might be a bachelorette party is moving down the sidewalk as a single brightly colored organism, laughing at something one of them said.

I walk through all of it and it does not part for me and it does not notice me and this is both terrible and something of a relief. Nobody here knows what happened Monday. Nobody here is making the face my mother made. Nobody here is doing the brisk, businesslike walk to another room that my father did, not toward me, just away, away from the table and the dinner and the thing I had said that was now hanging in the air over the cooling food.

*You have forty-three dollars,* some useful part of my brain offers, by way of redirecting. *You have not eaten since yesterday afternoon. These are the facts that require action.*

These are the facts. Yes.

I keep walking and the saxophone gets louder.

---

I turn a corner without meaning to—just following the sound, I realize, which is the kind of thing I would normally resist—and there it is. An older man in a white linen shirt, jacket despite the heat, standing on the sidewalk with his eyes closed and his saxophone producing something that sounds like it's coming from somewhere lower than his lungs. A scattering of bills in the case at his feet. People walk around him without stopping, the way you learn to walk around beauty that isn't asking you for anything.

I stop walking. My feet do it. I don't make the decision.

The music is beautiful the way a thing is beautiful that you don't want to need. Of course it's beautiful, I think—of course everything here is performing at you, of course you can't go ten minutes without the city producing something that makes you feel something you haven't got the resources to feel right now. I think this very clearly and with real irritation, looking at the saxophonist and his closed eyes and the way his shoulders move, and then underneath all that thinking I notice that something in my chest is doing something complicated and unwelcome, something that wants to be acknowledged, something that is sick of being suppressed.

I tell it to wait.

That's when I see her.

She's sitting against the building two doors down from the musician. My age, maybe a year older. Dark hair pulled up rough like she did it fast and has not thought about it since. Cutoff shorts, a faded blue T-shirt, a pair of sneakers with the laces worn thin. She's got a backpack between her feet, and she's keeping both shoes touching it—not leaning against it, touching it, the way you keep contact with something you've learned to monitor.

She catches me looking and doesn't look away. Most people look away when you catch them. She doesn't. She just looks back, steady and assessing, and in her eyes I see the same thing I know is in mine: *I'm cataloguing you before you can catalogue me.*

She's been here longer than a morning. I know this the way I can't entirely explain: something in the way she's sitting. The quality of her stillness. The absence of tourist alertness, that craning-outward quality of someone taking in a new place. She's not taking anything in. She's watching it all go past from somewhere just behind her own expression.

"You look lost," she says.

"I look fine," I say, which is so obviously false that she actually smiles—just briefly, just the corner of her mouth.

"Where'd you come from?"

"Tennessee."

She nods like this completes a thought she was already having. "I'm Dani."

"Taylor."

"You got somewhere to be, Taylor from Tennessee?"

And there it is. The question underneath the question, the one I've been turning over since I got out of the last truck at the state line and into the next one and spent six hours watching Mississippi go past the window: *are you going somewhere, or just gone?* I don't have an answer for either version of the question.

"Not yet," I say.

She moves her bag six inches to the left. I understand this to mean I can sit, so I sit. The pavement is hot through my jeans. The saxophonist shifts into something slower and more minor, a key that feels like late afternoon even though it's barely ten in the morning, and I feel my chest do the complicated thing again and focus on a crack in the concrete instead.

---

"What brought you down?" I ask.

Dani's shrug has architecture to it. Careful load-bearing walls. "Needed a change of scenery."

"Same," I say.

We both let that be enough, because we both speak that language. The language of the partial answer and the courtesy you extend because it's the courtesy you need.

It's quiet between us for a minute—not uncomfortable, just present—and in the quiet the Monday dinner forces itself up again, which is what it keeps doing, what it has been doing since I got in the first truck and stared at the white lines of the highway and tried to keep it from happening. It forces itself up in pieces, non-linear, impressionistic:

*—the particular way the fork went down on the plate—*

*—not anger first. Shock first. Her face doing something I'd never seen it do before, making a shape it didn't have a name for—*

*—and then the shape collapsing into something that looked almost like grief, which was somehow worse than the anger I'd been prepared for—*

*—my father's chair scraping back—*

*—and nobody raising their voice, and nobody raising their voice, and nobody—*

I look at the crack in the concrete. I breathe through my nose. I left, I think. I made a decision and I left and I am here and those are facts I can hold in my hands, solid and real.

"You eaten?" Dani asks.

"Not today."

She pulls a granola bar from her bag without hesitation and holds it toward me. I look at it for a second—forty-three dollars, I think, and my pride, and the fact that I don't know this person—and then I take it, because forty-three dollars and pride are not a combination that survives past the first twelve hours, and I am learning this.

"Thank you," I say.

"Don't." Fast. Like the word was already in her mouth, waiting. Like gratitude is a ledger she doesn't want opened.

I eat the granola bar and think about that *don't*. About how quickly it came. About the woman I buy—*bought*, past tense, I don't live there—*used* to buy granola bars from at the gas station near school who had the same quality to her generosity, something brisk and almost impersonal about it, like the giving had to be completed before anyone noticed.

Something happened to Dani. I can see its shape in what she doesn't say—in the way her eyes move to track anyone who comes too close, in the tightness around her mouth that relaxes slightly when they pass, in that *don't*, in the way she offered the food so fast I barely had time to look hungry. She's been carrying something. She knows the weight of it well enough by now that she carries it evenly, but it's there.

*What are you running from?* I don't say. Because I know the protocol.

Around us, the morning does its morning things. A delivery bike cuts too close to a group of pedestrians. A man sets up a folding table and begins arranging goods on it. The brightly colored organism of the bachelorette party reappears from a side street, still laughing, still moving as a single entity. The saxophonist transitions into something that sounds almost—not quite—like joy, like the approach of joy, like standing outside a house where the party is.

---

"I need to find a bathroom," I say eventually, because this is true and also because I need something concrete to navigate.

Dani stands up without comment, hoists her bag like she's done it ten thousand times, and jerk-nods down the street. "There's a cafe two blocks. They'll let you use it if you buy something. Cheapest thing is a coffee."

She walks with me. Not making a production of it, just walks alongside, like we've established something without quite agreeing to it. I watch how she moves through foot traffic—slight adjustments, no hesitation, reading the stream of people and finding the line of least resistance. She knows this street. She knows which way the crowd moves at this time of day.

The cafe is dark inside and smells like coffee and something sweet and slightly fermented. The woman behind the counter has copper rings stacked on every finger. I buy a black coffee with crumpled bills and she hands me a key attached to a piece of driftwood the size of a forearm and points to the back.

In the bathroom, under the single bulb, I look at my face in the mirror for the first time since Monday.

I look like what I am. Someone who has been in trucks for thirty-six hours. Someone who cried somewhere in northern Mississippi and then stopped and has not started again. Someone who told the truth and then ran from the consequences of it—which is not the same as running from the truth, I have decided, though I'm still working out the difference.

*This is your face,* I think. *This is the one you have.*

I wash it. I change my shirt in the cramped space, pulling the clean one from the bottom of my bag where I put it two days ago thinking I'd need it. I drink some water from the tap. I breathe.

When I come back out, Dani is at the counter with her own coffee. She's paid for it herself. She doesn't mention this.

We take the coffees outside and stand on the sidewalk and drink them, and the saxophonist, from somewhere around the corner, plays the opening of something I almost recognize.

---

We find a doorstep wide enough for two—one of those broad stone steps that seem built for exactly this purpose, for sitting on and watching New Orleans conduct its business—and we sit in the relative shade and I turn my empty coffee cup in my hands and think about the question she asked earlier. *Why are you here?* I gave her a version: needed a change of scenery. She accepted the version because she offered the same.

But the full thing is sitting right behind my sternum and has been since Mississippi and before that since the highway and before that since the scrape of my father's chair, and at some point the pressure of keeping it there starts to cost more than—

Not the full thing. Not all of it.

"My family," I say, which is not a full answer and not a lie. "Something happened with my family."

Dani looks at her cup. Nods. The nod says *I have that category of thing too.*

"They find out something about you?" she says. Careful. The question phrased to take whatever shape I need it to.

I look at the street. At a pigeon investigating something on the pavement. At the tourists going past with their cameras and their sunburns and their complete irrelevance to this moment.

"Yeah," I say. "Something like that."

Dani says: "Well." And then nothing. No follow-up question. No face rearranging itself into the shape of pity or the shape of discomfort or the shape of a door becoming a wall. She just says *well* and lets it sit there, and the saxophonist around the corner is playing something now that goes wherever it wants to go without asking permission, and I think: *I don't know if this is safe.* And then I think: *I don't know if I get to have safe right now.* And the pressure in my chest releases, slightly, like a valve.

"Yeah," I say again.

We sit with that for a while.

---

The afternoon comes on. The light goes from yellow to white to something almost bleached. We move twice—once when a man I don't know exchanges a look with Dani that means something I don't understand yet and she says *come on* and we walk fast around a corner, and once when she spots something—a shift in the crowd, a presence at the far end of the block—and makes a small, unhurried adjustment to our route. I follow without asking. I file it. I'm learning that navigating this city is reading a language I don't speak yet, and Dani is fluent, and for right now fluency is more useful to me than anything else.

"That guy," I say later, when we've settled again near a fountain in a square where children are throwing change into the water and pigeons assert their territory.

"Don't worry about him," she says.

"I'm not asking because I'm worried. I'm asking because I want to know how to read it."

She looks at me sideways and seems to decide something. "He rents spots," she says. "On blocks. If you're on his block without paying he gets disagreeable."

"How do you know whose block is whose?"

"You learn."

"Okay," I say. "How did you learn?"
