# The Barley and the Ash

The straw smells like the man who slept here before me. I don't know his name. I knew his smell — sour, animal, particular to him the way a voice is particular — and then one morning the smell was mine alone, and I did not ask what had become of him.

This is how I know I have been here long enough.

The stone beneath my back has its own cold, different from winter cold, different from the cold of the river I crossed in chains three years ago. That cold wanted to kill me. This cold has simply accepted me. I am not sure which is worse. Across the room Dakarai is praying in his language, which sounds like water over stones. Somewhere a man coughs the cough that has been getting worse since the rains. I breathe in, breathe out, and wait for my body to remember that it is necessary to stand.

I stand. This is what the body requires: instruction, then compliance.

---

Barley porridge with oil. Beans. A measure of the ash-water that the Greek physician insists builds bone. It tastes like defeat ground fine and dissolved in rain, and I have drunk it every morning for three years, and I think perhaps I have come to depend on it the way I depend on other humiliations — because it is mine, because no one else can take the precise bitterness of it from me.

I eat alone in the way you can be alone in a room of forty men. The lanista moves through, counting us the way a man counts coins before a purchase. His eyes are not unkind. That is almost the worst thing about him.

My armor is in good repair. I maintain it the way I maintain everything — without feeling, without neglect. Before I set down my bowl I touch the small figure I keep in the leather fold at the base of my kit. Bronze, worn to smoothness, the club barely distinguishable now from the hand that holds it. Hercules. Son of the highest god and a mortal woman, which made him belong to neither world completely. Enslaved by Hera's madness, enslaved by Eurystheus's cowardice, enslaved to labors that should have destroyed him and instead became the only stories anyone tells.

*You were purchased too,* I tell him, not aloud. *And they still sing you.*

He doesn't answer. He never answers. But the silence he returns is a particular silence, weighted, which is more than most silences offer.

---

The Subura smells like boiled fish and human want. I am sent to collect a debt the lanista is owed by a popina owner near the fuller's — an errand for a man who trusts that my face will accomplish what words might not. He is correct. The popina man's expression when I duck through his doorway is a thing I have learned to read the way I once read weather: calculation moving quickly toward appeasement.

I take the coins. I move back through the street.

A group of men outside a bathhouse are arguing about the games. I hear my name — not my name, the name they have given me, the name painted on the walls in red letters I cannot read but have been told spell something like *The Numidian Shadow* — and I keep walking, because the transaction of being known is one I have learned to complete without looking up.

A child points. His mother pulls his arm down, embarrassed, though whether for pointing at a spectacle or pointing at a person I cannot determine. Perhaps she does not know either.

On the wall beside the fuller's there is fresh graffiti. A man I pass is reading it and laughing. I do not ask. The city is always writing itself messages I am not meant to receive.

---

The notice is posted on the column near the temple of Saturn: white paint on red board, the next games announced, names listed in the order the crowd will receive them. I know my name by its shape now even if I cannot read the letters. I have stared at it enough. It is the fourth name on the list.

I stand in front of it longer than I intend to.

Around me Rome moves at its usual velocity — senators in their litters, water-carriers, a priest leading a goat that is having serious objections to its destination. The goat is not wrong to object. We are alike in this, the goat and I: we know something about where processions lead.

*Is this the labor?* I ask Hercules. Not aloud. *Or is there another after?*

He does not answer, which is also an answer.

There is a thing I do not allow myself to think about in the mornings, when the cold stone and the bitter ash-water keep me distributed among many small sensations. I allow myself to think it now, here, in front of the board with my name on it: I had a sister. She would be eleven by now. When they took me she was eight and too young to understand, and I hope she is still too young to understand, and I know she is not, because eleven is old enough to understand everything. My god was enslaved and survived and became a star. His sister was placed in the sky. I believe this the way I believe the ash-water builds bone — not with certainty, but with the daily discipline of proceeding as if.

I look at the board a moment longer.

Then I go back.

---

It is late when I sit down with my back against the wall and the lamp throwing its small argument against the dark. Far away — close enough to feel in the chest on the right kind of night — I can hear the Colosseum settling on its stones. The sound buildings make when the day has used them and they are only themselves again.

I take out the small worn figure and set him on my knee.

Tomorrow I will eat the barley. I will drink the ash. I will touch this thumb-smooth club and say the particular silence back to him.

Tonight I listen to the stone.
