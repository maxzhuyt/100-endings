# Therapeutic Boundaries

The room smells like antiseptic and synthetic lavender, which is worse than antiseptic alone. When they add the lavender, they've decided the pain needs softening. The treatment table is upholstered in that particular shade of teal that belongs only to medical spaces—the color of informed consent—and above it, mounted at the precise height to surveille whoever lies beneath, hangs a laminated diagram of human musculature. Anterior view on the left, posterior on the right. The figure has no skin. It has been peeled back to its obligations: everything exposed, everything labeled in a font too small to read from the table. *Flexor digitorum superficialis. Semimembranosus. Iliopsoas.* A body reduced to a ledger. I look at it for a long time before the door opens.

The man who enters has his back to me. He's reviewing a clipboard—my intake form, which I filled out in the waiting room sitting next to a rubber tree that had given up—and from behind I know him before I have to know him. The width of his shoulders. The way he carries them, not high and tense the way most people carry theirs near a stranger, but settled, certain, like someone who has never had to choose between his body and his life. I spent four years watching that back on tempo runs, watching it pull away from me on back straights, watching it cut through water at angles I could study but never reproduce. He was simply faster. We both knew. This is the first fact.

He turns around.

His face has the same quality it always had. The quality of someone for whom things have gone largely as expected.

"Rennick," he says.

"Marcus."

He looks at the form. I look at the diagram.

---

He asks me to rate the pain on their numbered scale, the one that starts at smiley and ends at the face that has stopped being a face. I want to tell him that the pain is not the problem. The problem is what I can no longer do with any scale at all, because scales assume the thing being measured is still the same substance it was before. My supraspinatus tendon was torn from the greater tubercle of the humerus. It was surgically reattached. The surgeon showed me the imaging afterward with a kind of professional satisfaction: *look what we restored.* And I looked, and I saw the repair, and I understood that the thing that had been restored was not the thing that had existed before, and that the distinction was everything, and that no one in that room would understand why I was not more grateful.

I catalog what's gone while Marcus uncaps his pen. Not the surface injuries—those have their own timeline, their own PT protocols, their own numbered scales. I mean the other thing. The thing that does not appear on imaging.

I used to know where I was in space. Not in the way most people know—not as a vague orientation, a rough sense of up and down—but precisely, completely, the way a musician knows a piece of music they've played ten thousand times. At the apex of a butterfly stroke, at full extension, I could tell you the exact angle of my elbow relative to the surface of the water. I could tell you the precise moment when my hand had caught enough water to begin the pull. The body was a instrument I had tuned so long that I had stopped being distinguishable from it. There was no Rennick and then the body. There was just the body, knowing.

Now I have to look to see where my arm is. Now there is always a half-second lag between what I intend and what happens. The lag is small. It is the size of a life.

Marcus says he'd like to start with some passive range of motion testing and could I lie back.

I lie back.

---

He places both hands on my shoulder without announcement. One cupped at the posterior capsule, one resting at the clavicular head of the pectoralis, and he begins moving the arm in slow arcs—abduction, flexion, horizontal adduction—watching my face rather than the arm, which is the correct clinical technique and which I find almost impossible to tolerate. His hands are warm. This is worse than if they were cold.

"Tell me when."

When is almost immediately. The arm reaches approximately forty degrees of abduction and there is a sensation like something drawn too tight, a threshold clearly marked by the tissue itself, a wall inside the joint that has no interest in negotiating. Not sharp. That's the thing about this kind of pain. It doesn't spike. It presses.

"There."

He doesn't stop at there. He holds the arm exactly at there, applies two or three degrees of additional pressure, and I breathe out through my teeth. He's mobilizing the posterior capsule—convincing the scar tissue that the movement is survivable, that the world on the other side of the wall is not dangerous, that what happened before will not happen again. I have read the literature on my own injury obsessively, the way the condemned study their sentencing guidelines. I know the terminology. The terminology does not help.

He asked me, while I was still sitting up, whether the injury was competitive or training. I told him competitive: sprint medley relay finals, the senior championships, the one that had mattered. He had looked at the form. He had not said he remembered. Something in the way he moved on made me understand that he had.

"You were on the circuit," he said now, flat, not quite a question.

"Junior. Four years."

He adjusts the angle of the arm slightly, testing a different vector. The piano wire sensation moves.

"I remember the two-hundred IM. You went sub-two-ten at regionals."

"That's gone," I say.

He holds on the edge of the threshold for five seconds that I count individually. Then he lets the arm down, repositions, and takes it through a different arc. He doesn't say anything else about the two-hundred IM. I appreciate this in a way I will not tell him.

---

He asks me to turn face-down. I turn face-down. The wax paper makes its noise.

He takes my right leg, bends the knee to ninety degrees, and begins applying internal rotation at the hip: foot pressing outward, knee fixed, the femur rolling in the acetabulum. I know what this stretches. I can feel all of it separately—piriformis, superior and inferior gemellus, quadratus femoris—which is not how you are supposed to experience your own body from the inside. You are supposed to experience your body as a unified thing, a going-concern, something that moves toward desired ends without announcing its component parts. I experience my body as a debriefing. Every structure giving its report.

The stretch builds along a line I can trace the way a geographer traces a river: from deep in the gluteal fold, threading beneath the sciatic nerve, fanning upward toward the sacroiliac joint. Marcus's left hand is at my ankle, constant, tracking the rotation. His right hand is at my hip, the heel of his palm just lateral to the sacrum, steadying the pelvis against compensation. This contact is anatomically necessary. It is the size of a handspan. I am looking at the floor.

He applies slight additional pressure.

"Breathe," he says.

"I am breathing."

"You're holding it up in your chest. Let it drop."

I let it drop. The muscle resists, holds, resists—and then yields by some increment too small to measure. Not release. Something more reluctant: the tissue admitting, finally, that it has run out of reasons to refuse.

His thumb has not moved from the lateral sacrum. His other hand tracks the rotation with the steadiness of something that does not doubt itself. He said, on his way into the room, that he'd seen the imaging notes, that the shoulder was the primary site but that the compensatory guarding had redistributed strain across the whole posterior chain. He said the hip work wasn't optional. He said it in the manner of someone delivering information, not asking permission. I noticed that I didn't mind.

He eases the leg down. He repositions. He takes the leg again, this time adding hip flexion, and the angle lengthens the hamstring complex: I can feel all three heads of it, the biceps femoris, the semimembranosus, the semitendinosus, which the diagram on the wall has labeled in red. The labels are unnecessary. The distinction between them is legible to me now. Before the injury I would have called all of it simply my hamstring and moved through the world in blissful anatomical imprecision.

The discomfort becomes something more than discomfort. A pressure behind the knee. A deeper pull at the sitting bone. Something spreading into the erectors like heat.

I make a sound that I did not authorize.

It is small and involuntary and gone before I have fully registered making it, and Marcus does not look up. He simply says, "Good," in the same register he'd use to call out a split. Clinical, observational, completely without affect. The tissue released. He felt it through his hands before I felt it from the inside. He knew before I did, which is the most disorienting thing that has happened in this room and something has already been happening in this room.

I turn my face toward the wall. The diagram's anterior view faces me: the figure with its arms slightly raised, as though presenting for inspection. Its chest is opened. Its layers are rendered lovingly, one above the next, each structure given its proper name. It has no face. This is deliberate, I've always thought. The face would complicate the argument.

---

He sets my leg down. He moves to the head of the table and asks me to roll onto my back. He'll finish at the shoulder—a sustained passive stretch of the posterior capsule, the manual hold that is the technical destination of everything we've been doing to get the chain loose enough to tolerate it.

He positions my arm. He takes the elbow with one hand and applies a long-axis distraction through the glenohumeral joint—pulling the humeral head away from the glenoid, opening the space, reducing the compression that has been this joint's alibi for not moving. With his other hand he takes the shoulder into horizontal adduction, slowly, past the point of comfort, past the point where I would stop if I were doing this alone. He holds it there.

It is a sustained hold: thirty seconds, perhaps longer. Not a mobilization this time but a persuasion. The stretch progresses in waves, each wave cresting higher than the last, the tissue approaching and retreating from something I don't have a word for. I look at the ceiling. Then I look at the diagram. Then I look at neither. The window is clouded glass. It diffuses the light outside into something general and gray.

His hands don't shift. The hold is absolutely still, which requires the kind of strength that doesn't announce itself. I know this from competitive swimming, the strength that looks like nothing because it has made itself efficient. He's watching my face. I look at the window.

At some point—I don't experience it as a discrete moment—the muscle stops arguing. There is no pop, no release, only an absence where the resistance was, a door that has stopped requiring force to stay open. He holds another five seconds. Then he lowers the arm. He sets it at my side with both hands and holds it there for a moment, the joint settled, before he takes his hands away.

He writes in the chart. He doesn't say what he's writing.

"We'll add heat next time, before the mobilization. Your fascial restrictions suggest chronic guarding. Longer history than eleven months."

"Twelve years of competition."

"That's what I mean."

He flips to a scheduling page. Morning or afternoon.

"Morning," I say.

"Thursday at eight."

"Fine."

He writes it down. I sit up. The wax paper sounds like it always sounds beneath this particular kind of movement—the sound of something that was meant to be disposable performing its function. My arm moves through the air at my side. I register, involuntarily, that there is more of the arc available than there was when I entered. Five degrees, perhaps. An increment. A fraction of a circle, a specific and quantifiable gift that I notice with the part of my mind that still measures things and despise with some other part that I have not yet identified accurately.

He is already reviewing the next file.

I slide off the table. I reach for my things. The room is exactly as it was when I entered it—the teal, the lavender, the overhead light that doesn't flicker because they have maintained it specifically—and the diagram is where it always was, fixed at its surveilling height. The anterior figure looks toward the door. The posterior figure looks at nothing: its back is turned, its structures labeled and exposed, everything visible from behind.

I walk toward the door.

"Thursday," I say.

He nods without looking up.

I open the door. The waiting room is there on the other side of it with its rubber tree and its numbered scale and its industry of recovery, and I walk into it, and the diagram watches me go.
