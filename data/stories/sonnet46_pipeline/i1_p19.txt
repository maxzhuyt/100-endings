# High Seas and Low Vices

The figurine is already in my hand.

I know this before I know where I am, before the light resolves into the particular grey-yellow of a Canton morning through salt-warped shutters, before the smell of the river asserts itself—low tide, fish oil, the particular rot of a city that has been trading with the world for longer than the world deserves. My jaw is locked. The sheets are wet. My hand is closed around the jade, the small carved boar my mother gave me at Portsmouth when I was twenty-two and had, she said, a future of considerable distinction ahead of me, and this is the first thing I think each morning, not that she said it, but that she was not wrong.

The tremor in my hands is mild this morning. A mercy.

Somewhere, on a desk I have not approached in three days, there are papers with my signature required. A copy of yesterday's drill report—Whitmore's draft, not mine, I have not written my own in some time. A requisition for rope and tar I approved in abstentia, trusting Whitmore to have used correct figures, which he will have done, Whitmore being the kind of man who checks figures twice and sleeps without incident and whose jaw unclenches fully when he sleeps. I know this because I have watched him sleep. I have watched a great many people sleeping in this city, from the particular vantage point of a man who does not.

I do not look at the papers.

I wash my face with water from the basin. In the mirror above it, a naval officer looks back at me with reasonable conviction. He is thinner than last year. His coat fits him as though the coat had not been altered and the man inside it had simply, quietly, given ground. But the coat is pressed. The brass is polished. These things require only habit, and habit is what I have most of.

---

Whitmore finds me on the dock before I find an excuse to be elsewhere.

He is a good officer, Whitmore. Conscientious in a way that requires no effort from him, which is either a gift or an absence, I have never decided. He falls into step beside me with the easy authority of a man who has nowhere in particular he would rather be.

"Lieutenant Merrick." He nods downriver. "The *Andromache* made signals an hour ago. Captain Ellison wants assessment of the storage situation in the lower factory district. He's concerned about the Jardine arrangement—whether we've been sufficiently—" he searches for the word, settles on "—attentive."

The figurine turns in my coat pocket. I become extremely interested in the middle distance.

"The storage situation," I say.

"Specifically the lower warehouses. Whether our visibility there has been—"

"I'll attend to it this morning," I say. "In fact, I'd intended to make rounds in that direction. I have an errand. Something for the registry office, signatures required."

Whitmore nods. He is too well-trained to ask which signatures or for what. This is the beauty of naval protocol, has always been the beauty of it: everyone is too well-trained to ask.

"Good," he says. "Good man." He says it with genuine warmth and I think, not for the first time, that his genuine warmth is the most fatiguing thing I encounter in a given week. It requires a counterfeit in return, which costs me more than the morning already has.

I give him the counterfeit. He accepts it. We part on good terms, as we always do.

Beneath my coat, the figurine turns and turns.

---

The factory district in the morning is all industry and legitimacy—the regulated houses, the East India Company's flag hanging with imperial self-satisfaction, British merchants in British clothes moving between British-signed agreements, the whole elaborate theatre of trade conducted according to rules that everyone understands and that answer, ultimately, to the harbor and the guns moored in it. I know these men. Some of them know me. I move with enough purpose that no one thinks to question my direction, which is, increasingly, the achievement I am most proud of.

Past the American factory. Past the French.

The streets narrow by degrees, which is how Canton works—not a sudden boundary between the regulated and the unregulated but a slow loosening, a gradual diminishment of European signage, a gradual increase of the particular smells that mark a city living in its own logic rather than an imported one. Charcoal and dried fish and canal water. The smell of food cooking in ways I no longer question.

My hands are trembling more now. Not badly. Not visibly, perhaps, to anyone who is not looking for it.

Chen is where Chen always is, in the corridor behind the money changer's on Mao Yi Street, sitting with a cup of tea he never appears to drink, dressed in a dark coat that has seen the better side of twenty years and carries its age with appropriate dignity. He looks up when I approach with the expression of a man who has been expecting me, which he has been, as he always has been, because I am nothing if not consistent.

"Lieutenant," he says, in good English.

"Chen."

"You are looking well," he says.

This is courtesy, not observation. We both understand this.

"I need to speak with your associate," I say.

"Mr. Lin is occupied."

"He'll be less occupied when he knows I'm here."

Chen looks at me for a moment with eyes that do not miss much and never have. Then he stands—unhurriedly, taking his tea with him—and moves to the interior door. The conversation beyond it lasts less than a minute. He returns and holds the door open.

"He is less occupied," Chen says, and something in his voice might be amusement, or might be the absence of it. The two are indistinguishable, with Chen, and I have stopped attempting the distinction.

---

Lin's room is small and smells like paper and lamp oil and the cedar of his account books, which are numerous and meticulously kept. He is a compact man in his fifties who wears his prosperity without advertisement and who has, in my experience, an almost architectural intelligence: he sees the load-bearing elements of any situation immediately and works from them outward. He is pouring tea when I enter, which means he has already assessed the situation and reached his conclusions, because Lin does not pour tea for men he intends to refuse.

"Lieutenant Merrick." He gestures to the chair across from his small desk. "Sit."

I sit. The tremor in my hands is less when I am still, or perhaps I simply notice it less when there are other things to notice.

"I am a little short," I say.

Lin nods. He knew this. He always knows.

"How short?"

I tell him the figure. He receives it without expression, sets his own cup down, opens one of the account books, moves a finger down a column of characters that mean nothing to me, and then closes the book and folds his hands on top of it in the manner of a man who has done his arithmetic and arrived at an answer he finds neither surprising nor particularly interesting.

"There is the matter of the anchor survey," he says. "The naval assessment of the Pearl River soundings, east channel."

I look at him.

"A copy," he says. "Not the original. The survey itself is already three months old. The information in it is known, perhaps, by seven or eight men in this city, of whom you are one."

"It is a naval document."

"Yes," Lin says. "That is why it has a value." He says this without cruelty, without emphasis, as a man states the terms of an arithmetic problem to a student who already knows the answer. "If the information were freely available, it would be worth nothing and I would not be interested in it. If it were unavailable, I would not expect you to provide it. The situation is as it often is—somewhere in the middle."

The jade figurine is hard in my fist inside my coat pocket.

"If it became known—"

"It will not become known," Lin says. "The information will be held for purposes of commercial navigation, not shown to anyone in an official capacity, and will be superseded by the next survey, which I understand will be completed in the spring." He pauses. "I have had this conversation before, Lieutenant. In various forms."

He says this without contempt. That is almost the worst of it—there is no contempt in it, just the acknowledgement of a recurring transaction, filed under its correct category, managed as any other business is managed. I am a line item in his ledger, and he is telling me this, and we both know he is telling me this, and I agree to the terms.

"I'll bring it tomorrow," I say.

"Today would be preferable."

The tremor in my hands decides the question before I do.

"Today," I say.

Lin nods, pours more tea, and slides a sealed envelope across the desk. Inside it, I know, is the difference between what I have and what I need. I know what it costs, and I put it in my pocket beside the figurine, and we drink our tea, and we do not discuss what has been agreed upon, because there is no need to discuss it further: Lin is a man who seals agreements with completion, not elaboration, and I am a man who has nothing useful left to add.

---

The den on Tung Hsing Street does not announce itself.

There is a door in a wall that is indistinguishable from the other doors in the same wall, and behind it a corridor, and beyond the corridor a room—low-ceilinged, oil-lamped, with the particular quality of silence that belongs to a space in which several people are present but not speaking. Six men when I enter today. Two who are there with some frequency. The others I do not know and do not ask about. There is an etiquette to this place as there is to any place where a variety of men require the same thing: you do not look too closely, you do not speak too openly, you recognize your own condition in others without forcing the recognition into conversation.

The proprietress—a small woman of indeterminate age who has never told me her name and to whom I have never offered mine—leads me to the back corner without being asked. She has recognized my usual position. This is either comfort or condemnation, and I have enough practice at not deciding.

She prepares the pipe with the economy of practiced service—the small coal, the needle's work, the specific routine I have watched so many times I could perform it myself, though I do not, because performing it myself would require an admission I still decline to make, as though being served makes me something other than what I am.

I lie back against the low cushion.

The tremor leaves my hands.

What the literary men who have never experienced this will tell you is that it is transcendent, oceanic, a dissolution of the self into something oceanic and warm, a velvet oblivion, a silken forgetting. What they are describing is what their imagination requires it to be, because they are making an argument—for horror or for seduction—and their argument requires the thing to be larger than what it is.

What it is: the end of a particular kind of pain.

The jaw, finally, unclenches.

My hands, finally, are simply hands.

The jade figurine lies in my coat pocket. I am not holding it. This is the only hour of the day when I am not holding it, when the physical compulsion to hold it—as though the stone itself might transmit some small amount of what I need—simply releases, and the figurine is an object again, a gift from a woman in Portsmouth who was not wrong about me and is no longer in a position to see how far wrong has come.

Across the room, one of the men I do not know breathes very slowly. Like a man sleeping who does not need to sleep. Like a man who has found, temporarily, the same answer to the same question, by the same arithmetic.

I close my eyes.

The Pearl River sounds like a river.

---

The afternoon is edged in amber by the time I come out onto Tung Hsing Street, the city loud again in the ordinary way of a city that was always this loud and only becomes bearable when you can hear it from inside a body that is not flinching. I move well. I know I move well—this is one of the hours in which I move well, think clearly, could, if required, have the conversation about the *Andromache* and the lower factory district and the Jardine arrangement with sufficient fluency that Whitmore would notice nothing. The calculation is already underway, in some back corridor of my mind: what tomorrow will cost, what I have just arranged to fund it, what must be managed between now and then.

I am making good time back toward the harbor when I see Reeves.

He is one of mine—was one of mine—an able seaman from the *Blenheim* before the reassignment, a Cornishman who tied every knot to a standard you could inspect without embarrassment. He is carrying a coil of rope across his chest and he is looking down at the street in front of him, and for a half-second I think he will not see me, and I do not slow my pace, and then he looks up.

His face opens.

"Lieutenant Merrick," he says.

The rank arrives like something dropped from a height. I watch it fall.

"Reeves." I stop. There is no alternative to stopping; he is standing in the middle of the street and stopping is what men do when they encounter their superior officers in foreign ports. "Good afternoon."

"Sir." He shifts the rope on his shoulder with the ease of habitual labor and looks at me with the uncomplicated goodwill of a man who has no idea that it requires anything of me to return it. "Didn't know you were in this part of the city, sir. Errand?"

"Yes," I say. "Registry matter. Finished now."

"Right, sir." He nods, satisfied. Men like Reeves are satisfied by the word 'errand.' They believe in errands. They believe in rosters and time-sheets and the honest accounting of an afternoon's location, because for them these are simply descriptions of events that have occurred, and for them errands are genuinely what they say they are.

"Keeping well?" I ask, because it is what I would have asked before, and I am still, in these hours, capable of being what I would have asked before.

"Right enough, sir." He grins, slightly. "Hot, isn't it."

"It is," I agree. "Carry on, Reeves."

"Sir." He moves past me, already returning to whatever arithmetic occupies the afternoon of a man with a clean conscience and a coil of rope.

I do not watch him go. I walk on toward the harbor with the stride of a lieutenant on legitimate business, and somewhere inside the part of my mind the afternoon has freed up for noticing, something briefly sharpens—the gap between the word *sir* as Reeves uses it and what I brought out of Lin's office this afternoon folded in my inner pocket, next to the figurine—and then the sharpness recedes, as it does, as it has learned to, as I have trained it to, and I am simply a man walking toward the harbor in the amber afternoon, moving well, attracting no attention, perfectly functional, on my way.

---

The Pearl River at evening is genuinely beautiful.

This is not the opium speaking; the opium has mostly spoken, is receding now, is leaving the particular quiet aftermath in which I am capable of observing that a river at evening is beautiful without needing anything more from the observation than the observation itself. The light goes red and then slowly grey. Sampans work their way upstream. Someone on the water is burning something aromatic—I cannot identify it, which is the truth, so I do not attempt to.

My quarters face north, away from the water, which I have always considered a poor arrangement.

The papers on the desk look the same as they looked this morning. I sit down in front of them, take up the pen, draw a sheet of blank paper toward me, and then hold the pen without applying it to anything. There are things I could write. Letters that begin in my mind with good sentences and then require a second sentence, and then a third, and the third sentence is always where the thing I am trying not to say surfaces, and I stop.

I put the pen down.

The jade figurine I set on the desktop, and I look at it for a moment—the small carved boar, worn smooth at the haunches from years of handling, first from my mother's handling and then from my own—and it looks back at me with the blankness of an object that has been assigned spiritual significance it was manufactured without.

Somewhere across the harbor, naval signal lamps are blinking things back and forth that I have authority over and interest in, diminished as both are. Tomorrow there is the matter of the survey copy, which I have already decided not to think about until it must be thought about, which is tomorrow. Tomorrow there is the question of what the afternoon will require. Tomorrow the tremors will start before the sun is fully over the river, which I know the way I know the Pearl River's tidal timing—not from study but from the body's own accumulated record.

I pick up the figurine again.

The river makes its sounds
