# Prison Strings

The lights in C-corridor did what they always did: told the truth in intervals.

On, and the concrete was just concrete. Off, and anything could be standing in it. On again, and you saw what the dark had been hiding, or you saw the dark had been hiding nothing, and you couldn't ever quite decide which was worse. Jolyne had been here long enough to stop flinching at the flicker. She'd learned to read the corridor the way you read a person—in the gaps, in what they didn't show you.

She was headed toward the laundry access when she saw the woman.

Not an inmate. That much was obvious before Jolyne could sort out why—something in the posture, something in the absolute stillness. Inmates didn't stand that way. Inmates were always slightly braced, always accounting for the next thing, because the next thing in a place like this was never nothing. This woman stood at the corridor's midpoint like she had chosen the spot cartographically, like she'd surveyed the options and selected the one with the best sightlines, and she was not bracing for anything because she did not expect anything to require bracing for.

Jolyne registered all of this in the half-second before the light flickered back on and she saw the face.

Young-looking. Pretty, in the way that expensive things were pretty, with that same quality of effort so total it had looped back around to appearing effortless. Dark hair pulled back. A coat that had no business being this clean in this place. And the eyes—something wrong with the eyes, something Jolyne couldn't land on before the light went out again and she was standing in the dark with just the afterimage of them.

When the light returned, the woman was looking at her.

Not turning to look. Already looking. Turned as if she'd been waiting for Jolyne to arrive at exactly this point in the corridor and was simply acknowledging that her timing had been correct. She smiled, and the smile was warm and pleasant and involved every part of her face that a smile was supposed to involve.

Jolyne's stomach did a slow, specific thing that had nothing to do with the cafeteria food.

"This block's off rotation on Tuesdays," Jolyne said. "You're not supposed to be down here."

"I know," the woman said. "I was hoping to run into you."

Her voice was even. There was no texture in it, nothing scratched or pressured—it was the voice of someone explaining something simple and kind, a recipe, a set of directions. She said *I was hoping to run into you* the way you said *the bus comes at seven*, which was somehow its own kind of wrongness.

"Then you know my name."

"Cujoh Jolyne." A single nod, more affirmation than introduction. "Stone Ocean. They gave you a number too, of course, but that's administrative."

"And you are."

"Makima." She didn't offer more. Didn't gesture toward a badge or a clipboard or any of the props that people with authority over this place usually carried as proof of it. She just stood with her hands loosely at her sides, looking at Jolyne with eyes that were—

Yellow. They were yellow. That was what was wrong with them. Not quite, not human-yellow, something with rings in it, something that shouldn't have been looking out from a human face, and the light chose that moment to gutter and Jolyne lost them again, stood in the dark for one long second with her jaw set.

"What do you want," Jolyne said. Not a question.

The light came back.

"I wanted to see how you were doing," Makima said, which was so self-evidently a lie that Jolyne almost laughed. "The circumstances of your conviction were unfortunate. The facility here is difficult. You've been—" a small pause, the pause of someone who had already completed the thought before speaking and was deciding how much of the completion to share— "managing, I think. But it must be isolating."

"I'm fine."

"Your father," Makima said, and Jolyne's entire body went for a second somewhere very cold and very still, "is a remarkable person. You must have inherited that from him. The resilience, I mean."

"Don't."

"I only meant—"

"I heard what you meant." Jolyne looked at her straight and kept her voice flat. "Don't."

Makima received this with another small nod, unfazed. Not wounded, not recalibrating even—just acknowledging, the way you acknowledged weather. *It's raining.* She reached up and straightened the collar of her coat with two fingers, though it had not needed straightening, and said, "I've read about your ability. Stone Free." She tilted her head slightly. "Strings from your own body. It must feel like an extension of yourself. Like your nervous system, but visible."

There was something in the way she said it that made Jolyne think of someone looking through glass at a fish.

"Feels like strings," Jolyne said. "Nothing poetic about it."

Makima smiled again. "You're protecting yourself."

"Always."

"That's not what I—" she stopped. Something moved behind her eyes, something brief and mathematical, and then the pleasantness came back down over it like a curtain. "I think we might be able to help each other. If you were open to a conversation."

"We're having one."

"A real one." The word real delivered with a weight that implied all their conversation so far had been a polite waiting room. "I have access to information about the circumstances of your case that you don't. About the people involved. I could also make certain aspects of your situation here considerably easier, in the short term." She glanced at the corridor around them with exactly the amount of distaste a person permitted themselves to show when commenting on something that didn't actually affect them. "This can't be comfortable."

Jolyne was about to say something sharp when she heard it—the sound of too many footsteps coming from the T-junction at the corridor's north end. Not guards. Guards moved differently. This was something else, three of them, and the one in front had the particular rolling walk of someone accustomed to taking up more space than they'd been given and resenting it.

She'd had a problem with Benedetti since the second week. She was about to have it again.

"Hey, Cujoh." He said it like something he was chewing. "You know you're not supposed to be down here Tuesdays."

"Funny," Jolyne said. "I was just saying the same thing to someone."

She didn't look at Makima. Later she would try to reconstruct this and she would not be able to say exactly why—only that looking at Makima felt like giving her something, and this didn't seem like the time for gifts.

Benedetti's two associates fanned out in the classic geometry of people who had decided a thing already and were just finishing the paperwork of deciding it. One of them had something in his right hand. It was the length of a forearm and dense-looking and no guard was coming, no guard came, not down here on rotation days, which was of course why he'd chosen now.

Jolyne felt Stone Free come up in her chest like breath.

It began at her skin—the unspooling sensation, the loose heat of it—and then the strings were in the air, faintly audible, a sound like a high note sustained just below hearing. She moved left, which moved the world according to a geometry she understood and Benedetti did not, and the string went around the wrist of the arm with the object in it and she pulled, and the physics were simple and the result was that he was on the ground and the object was not in his hand anymore.

The other two hesitated. People always hesitated at the strings. There was a moment—she knew it well, had learned to use it—where they looked at the strings trying to understand what they were looking at, and that moment was hers, and she stepped into it and made it count.

It took forty seconds. She counted automatically; she always did. Forty seconds and all three of them were occupying the floor, not seriously hurt, but done, and the object was dismantled in her strings, hanging in segments like punctuation, and her breathing was slightly elevated but controlled.

She let the strings retract. She turned around.

Makima hadn't moved.

She was standing in the exact same position she'd occupied before. Her coat was unruffled. Her hands were still loose at her sides. She was looking at Jolyne with an expression that was warm and pleasant and lit entirely from the inside by something Jolyne could only call hunger, though hunger felt like the wrong word for it, hunger implied a need, and this was more like a collector finally standing in front of the thing she'd been tracking for a long time.

"Forty seconds," Makima said. "And you were watching me for the first few."

Jolyne said nothing.

"The strings are load-bearing too. Not just binding." She said it the way a person said *of course* when they'd predicted something and now found themselves correct. "You've thought through the architecture of it."

"They're strings," Jolyne said. "They do what I need."

"Yes," Makima said. And then more quietly, in exactly the same tone: "Yes, I imagine they do."

The light went out.

In the dark, Jolyne stood very still. She noticed that she could hear Makima breathing, slow and even, not the breathing of someone who had just watched a fight. The breathing of someone who had watched a demonstration. The difference was in where the attention lived.

The light came back.

Makima's eyes were directly on her. The yellow in them was deep and ringed, not an iris exactly, something that went further back than irises were supposed to. Jolyne held the look for one second and then looked away—not down, not evasive, just away, because holding that look felt like putting her hand on something that would then close around it.

*Hers.*

The word surfaced in Jolyne's memory—had Makima said it? When? She pulled the conversation back through the corridor of the last few minutes and found it: a moment she hadn't registered consciously, somewhere during the Benedetti problem, Makima speaking quietly almost to herself. *She'll be herself again in a moment. She doesn't like to be interrupted when she's working.* Not she, referring to Jolyne. Something smaller in the inflection—possessive or maybe something that hadn't evolved a word yet that was worse than possessive.

*She* as in someone who belongs to me.

*She* as in something I maintain.

Jolyne thought about the trial. She thought about the specific quality of her lawyer's smile—how it had been warm and thorough and how every piece of that warmth had been arranged in service of something that had nothing to do with Jolyne, and how she had understood it only in retrospect, only sitting in the transport vehicle watching the city get smaller, only when the shape of it was finally complete enough to see from a distance.

You couldn't see it up close. That was the design.

She was seeing this one up close.

She turned back to Makima and said: "You were watching me in there."

"I was."

"You could have helped."

"Could I," Makima said, which wasn't a question.

"You didn't even move."

"No." She paused, in the deliberate way she paused—not at a loss for words, at a decision about them. "You didn't need it. And I find I learn more from watching someone work without interference." Another pause. "Interference tends to distort the data."

*Data.*

There it was.

The thing she'd been feeling at the edge of this conversation, the shape of it she couldn't see from up close but had been sensing from her peripheral vision the whole time—*data.* Not Jolyne. A data point. A thing being studied with the warmth a researcher brought to a very interesting result.

The light went out for a long second, longer than usual, and in the dark Jolyne stood with her hands at her sides and thought about what she was dealing with. She thought about it efficiently, the way you thought about something when you needed an answer by the time the lights came back. She sorted through what she had: the coat too clean for this place, the voice with nothing underneath it, the eyes built wrong, the word *hers*, the absolute stillness during a fight, the way she'd named Jolyne's father not as personal detail but as lever.

*She understands leverage*, Jolyne thought. *She knows where to put the bar under a person. She knows how much weight to apply before it gives.*

And then the thought just below that, the one she'd been circling: *She's done this before. Many times. And whoever she's done it to—*

The light came back.

Makima was still looking at her. Still warm. Still perfectly, immovably pleasant, in the way of someone who had never once in her life needed to be anything else.

Jolyne looked at her steadily and said nothing. She was counting again, but not seconds this time. She was counting the moves. She was thinking about where the corridor opened and where it narrowed, about what she had and what she didn't, about the eleven feet between them and what eleven feet meant when she didn't know what she was dealing with.

She moved—not away, sideways, a small deliberate adjustment of position—to see what Makima did.

Makima did nothing. Watched her.

Jolyne moved again, closer this time, six feet, and let one string come off her right hand. Just one. Let it hang in the air between them.

Makima looked at the string with those wrong-colored eyes, and something crossed her face too fast to name—not fear, nothing close to fear, something that might have been a kind of delight if delight could be that cold—and she said, softly: "Careful, Cujoh."

"Or what."

Makima smiled. It was the smile she'd been wearing since the beginning—warm, full of specific, calibrated care—and she said: "Or you'll find out what I can do before you're ready to know." A beat. "I'd rather build up to that. For your sake."

*For your sake.* The phrasing of someone doing you a favor. The phrasing of someone with a leash in their hand telling the animal this is for your comfort.

Jolyne retracted the string.

"Okay," she said. "What do you actually want."

"I've told you."

"You told me what you think I want to hear. I'm asking what you want."

Something shifted in Makima's face, too small to call an expression. A tectonic thing, deep and slow. Then: "I want to build something with you. Something with real structure." The word *real* again, with its weight. "Your ability is extraordinary. Your willingness to use it, more so. An asset like that, properly supported, could accomplish—"

"Stop," Jolyne said.

Makima stopped.

"The word contract," Jolyne said. "Were you going to use it?"

A pause. Two seconds, maybe three, during which the light completed a full cycle—on, partial, full on again.

"I might have," Makima said.

"And my father. You were going to come back to him."

"His situation is—"

"Is not something you get to touch." She said it flat and final, a door closing. "That's not a card you have."

"Every card is one I have," Makima said, and it was the first time her voice had had something hard in it, the first time the surface had shown the thing underneath the surface, and she seemed to realize it almost immediately because the warmth came back down over it so fast, like a hand pressed flat over a wound. "I'm offering you something real, Jolyne. Something better than this corridor. Better than counting days and managing—" the gesture toward the three men still on the floor "—this. You're capable of more. I can give you more."

Jolyne looked at her for a long time.

She looked at the coat and the eyes and the smile, and she thought about what it would feel like to say yes. She understood it, the shape of it—how you could feel the yes forming, how the arguments for it were reasonable and the arguments against it were just sensation, just her gut doing its persistent ugly work, just the part of her that had sat in a courtroom and watched someone smile at her and understood later that it meant something she didn't have a word for at the time.

The offer was good. That was the thing about a good trap—the offer was good. The bars were comfortable and the food was adequate and you could tell yourself you'd chosen it, you'd contracted into it, it had been your decision, and maybe by the time you understood what you'd decided you'd have forgotten what deciding used to feel like.
