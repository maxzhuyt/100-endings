# Carnival of Dangerous Intentions

She had told herself she was only walking in this direction.

That was the first lie. The second was that she had come to return the scarf—his scarf, navy wool left behind on the bench where they had spoken four days ago, which she had washed and folded and placed in her bag that morning with the focused deliberateness of someone defusing something. The scarf was in her bag now. She could feel the slight weight of it against her hip.

She stopped just before reaching the shooting gallery set up along the boardwalk. It was near dusk, and the carnival lights were buzzing on. *His locks.* The words flashed through her mind again, like an intruder. Such an old-fashioned turn of phrase surfacing involuntarily—and something else, something dangerously compelling—quickly suppressed, lest she examine why she had returned here at all. She didn't even know his name.

The sodium lamps above the gallery blinked twice, irregularly, making the row of painted duck targets jump and disappear and reappear like things glimpsed in lightning. A man with a cigar was paying for his three shots. A child was licking something red. The ordinary world performing itself with enormous conviction.

Then there he was.

He was standing at the edge of the gallery's light, one shoulder against the wooden support post, and he was watching her with an expression she could not immediately name. Not surprise. He had been expecting her, or something like her, and she felt the specific humiliation of having confirmed a prediction she hadn't known she was making.

He was younger than she'd let herself remember. That was the third lie—she had let herself remember him as somewhat older, somehow safer.

"You came back," he said. Not a question.

"I have your scarf." She reached into her bag before she had finished the sentence, and the haste of the gesture betrayed her. She heard it herself.

He watched her hand without moving toward her. "Keep it."

"I washed it." She heard how this sounded. Domestic. Preoccupied. As if the washing had been a four-day occupation. She squared herself. "It would have been rude to simply keep it."

"And is that what brings you? Manners?"

The sodium light above them chose that moment to go dark entirely, then stutter back, and in the half-second of its absence his face became something else—older, less legible—before the light returned and settled and he was again merely a young man with obsidian curls and eyes the color of something that had no bottom.

"I don't know your name," she said.

"You didn't ask for it last time."

"I'm asking now."

He was quiet for a moment, as if consulting something. Around them the boardwalk sound continued—the rifle shots from the gallery, the calliope from the carousel two stalls over, a woman's high laugh ending abruptly. All of it at a slight remove, as if she were hearing it through water.

"Edmund," he said at last. "And you're—" He paused with a precision that felt intentional, a pause shaped for effect. "—not going to tell me yours."

"Vera," she said, immediately regretting it.

"Vera." He turned it over without smiling. "Roman. Serious." He finally pushed off the post and moved toward the gallery railing, leaning against it with his back to the duck targets, facing her squarely. The scarf hung unacknowledged in her hand. "What is it you actually want to know, Vera? You didn't come back here for manners."

*I came back,* she thought, *because for four days I have been unable to finish a single sentence in my own head without arriving at your face.* She said: "I wanted to ask what you meant. The last thing you said."

"Which was?"

"You said—" She paused. She had rehearsed this. The rehearsal collapsed. "You said, *some arrangements appear accidental.* I wanted to know what you meant by it."

Edmund considered her. The light flickered again, and this time she watched his face carefully during the interruption, looking for something that would help her. She did not find it.

"I meant exactly that." He reached over and, without asking, took the scarf from her hand. His fingers grazed her palm in the process—a contact so brief it could have been accidental, which was precisely its genius. He folded the scarf and laid it on the railing beside him. "Some arrangements that look like chance are something else. Do you believe that?"

"I'm not sure what I believe."

"That," he said quietly, "is the most honest thing you've said tonight."

She knew she should leave. The knowledge was clear, clinical, entirely present in her mind—it sat there like a well-lit exit sign in a dark theater, patient and legible. She saw it. She catalogued it. She did not move toward it.

"There's a place," Edmund said, "about a quarter mile down the boardwalk. They have a piano, and they're not particular about who they serve." He paused. "Or who asks questions."

The calliope resolved itself into something almost recognizable. The child with the red thing was gone. The man with the cigar was gone. The shooter at the gallery had run out of shots.

"One drink," Vera said.

"One drink," he agreed, with the equanimity of someone who knew exactly how these things proceeded—not with walls but with doors, each one opened by the person walking through it.

She picked up the scarf from the railing and put it back in her bag.

He said nothing about this. He only gestured toward the boardwalk.

She went first.
