# Hell is Other Demons

The paperwork had been accumulating since the Merovingian dynasty, and Lucifer had developed a system for ignoring it that he considered, privately, inspired. The oldest files he had simply moved to a lower shelf. The lower shelf had, over centuries, become a sort of geological record of his avoidance—compressed strata of damnation orders, appeal notices, inter-departmental memos from Beelzebub written in a hand that suggested Beelzebub had learned to write by watching someone else do it from a very great distance.

Beneath him, the Iron Throne registered its familiar complaint: the low, rhythmic gnawing that had become, over the last several decades, simply a feature of the room, like the ambient screaming or the smell of sulfur that everyone eventually stopped noticing. Gnaw. Pause. Gnaw. The hellhound—Grim, a misnomer so severe it had looped back around to accuracy—had been working on the throne's left rear leg since roughly the Council of Trent, and whatever structural progress he'd made was not Lucifer's immediate concern.

He signed another form. The form pertained to the eternal assignment of one Gerald Fitch, deceased accountant, condemned for a catalog of offenses that included tax evasion, three counts of insurance fraud, and what the intake form described, in the careful language of official Hell, as *sustained and deliberate misuse of the office communal microwave.* Lucifer considered Gerald Fitch's eternity briefly. He assigned him to the fourth circle and moved on.

"My lord."

The demon in the doorway was new. They were always new. Hell churned through middle management at a rate that reflected poorly on something, though Lucifer had stopped determining what. This one was trying very hard to look competent and succeeding at looking like something that was trying very hard to look competent, which was subtly different.

"The delegation from the Third Administrative District is here regarding the Wrath Assessment backlog—"

"Tell them I'm reviewing their submission."

"They say you've been reviewing it since—"

"Tell them I'm reviewing it *thoroughly.*"

The demon disappeared. Lucifer signed three more forms. Gnaw. Pause. Gnaw.

The Wrath Assessments had been Lilith's domain, technically—not by any formal designation, nothing so legible as that, but by the accumulated weight of her simply doing them because she couldn't watch them go undone. He'd noticed, in a purely logistical sense, that the backlog had begun around the time she'd—around the time the current administrative arrangement had taken effect. He had noted this. He was continuing to note it. At some point the notation would resolve into action.

He signed another form. This one was a formal complaint lodged by a damned soul named Harrison Pemberton, who objected to the temperature of his assigned torment and requested to speak with a supervisor. Lucifer wrote *denied* in the margin, then reconsidered, then crossed out *denied* and wrote *received and under review,* which was, technically, also a form of denial but one that preserved a certain correspondence of tone.

*No one has filed the Wrath Assessments since—*

He filed that thought where he filed the others, on a very low shelf, under significant pressure.

"My lord." Different demon. Or possibly the same demon; they blurred. "Mister Mephistoph—Mephista—Mephist-*o-phèles* is requesting—"

"*Mephistopheles,*" Lucifer said.

"Yes, that's—I said that."

"You said *Mephist-o-phèles.* The stress is—it doesn't matter. What does he want?"

"He's requesting a formal title review. He feels his current designation undersells his—"

"His designation is Grand Duke. Several human poets wrote about him by name. Tell him I said he's doing fine."

"He says the poets were—" the demon consulted a small note— "*dismissive of his contributions to the larger project.*"

"Tell him," Lucifer said, carefully, "that the larger project has not historically solicited his creative feedback."

Gnaw. Pause. Gnaw.

He looked down. The gouge in the left rear leg had deepened perceptibly since this morning. Grim was enormous, ink-black, possessed of three rows of teeth that managed to suggest a fourth, and was currently curled beneath the Iron Throne with the focused industry of something that had a plan. Lucifer regarded the exposed root of the problem—the splinter of iron curling away from the leg like a wood-shaving, except iron—and returned to his paperwork.

He would deal with it tomorrow.

---

She came in the way she always had, which was without announcement and from the wrong direction, as if the primary entrance was a convention she found professionally beneath her.

Grim heard her first. Lucifer noticed this because Grim stopped chewing.

In the sudden quiet, her footsteps were audible across the obsidian floor—not tentative, not aggressive, simply footsteps, with the confidence of someone who has never in their existence questioned their right to be somewhere. Lilith had been walking into rooms this way since she'd first invented the concept of walking into rooms, and several architectural historians in Hell's lower archives had suggested, privately, that she'd possibly invented the rooms as well, just so she'd have something better to walk into.

"Your filing system," she said, before she'd fully arrived, gesturing at the geological shelf without looking at it, "is genuinely worse."

"It's a different system."

"It's a *pile.*"

"It's an accumulative organizational method."

"That's a *pile* with—" she paused. She looked at the throne. At the leg. At Grim, who had emerged from beneath the throne entirely and was now sitting at a height that put him approximately level with her chest, tail moving in a slow and traitorous arc. She did not reach for him. Her expression did not change. He pressed his enormous skull against her hand anyway, and her hand curved around it, just briefly, before she withdrew it.

"Your throne," she said, "is shorter."

"It's always been that height."

"One side of your throne. Is shorter. Than the other."

"The floor is uneven."

She looked at him.

"In that area," he said.

She looked at him for a while longer, and then she looked away, and he felt this as a specific thing, the looking-away, the way you feel barometric pressure in a joint that was once broken. She was carrying something—a case, dark metal, the kind that had sigils on the hinge. Had carried it in. He hadn't registered it consciously until now.

"I left something here," she said.

"I know."

"I'm retrieving it."

"I assumed."

"Don't." She set the case down on the edge of his desk, next to the pile, and began to open it. "Don't do that thing where you say things that mean other things. I'm here for the Seal. The Fourteenth Seal, the one that went—you had it, you said you'd—"

"Fourth cabinet, left side, under the Edicts of—"

"I'll find it."

She crossed the room. He watched her walk past, in the specific way you watch something move when you are trying very hard to simply observe it neutrally and not have this be a thing you are doing. Grim followed her, his nails clicking on obsidian. She opened the cabinet without ceremony, found the Seal apparently without looking, and turned around with it, which meant she turned around and was looking at the space between where she'd been and where she had to return to, which contained his desk, which contained him, and Grim was still beside her, watching with his many-toothed face arranged in what passed for his expression of profound emotional investment.

"You rearranged this cabinet," she said.

"I reorganized it."

"These were in order of Covenant. You've put them in—what is this?"

"Chronological acquisition."

"That's meaningless. If you need the Fourteenth Seal you don't think *when did I get it,* you think *which Covenant governs—*"

"I know where everything is."

"You found the Fourteenth Seal by telling me *fourth cabinet, left side.*"

"Because I knew where it was."

"You knew where it was because the system I built hasn't fully collapsed yet."

Gnaw. Pause. Gnaw.

Grim had returned to his throne. The sound resumed with the comfort of a metronome. Lilith was still standing by the cabinet with the Seal under her arm, and she appeared to be—not reading the room, she wouldn't read a room she'd built, but perhaps making some internal audit of it, the way you note, without wanting to, which things have stayed where you put them.

The throne-gnawing was where you put it, he thought, which was probably not what she was auditing.

"Sit down," he said, before he'd planned to say it.

"I have the Seal."

"I know you have the Seal. The papers for the Third District—I need a second signature. It's a formality."

"You have subordinates."

"I have forty-seven subordinates who can't pronounce *Mephistopheles* and one who keeps filing Wrath Assessments in the wrong column because no one has—because the column labeling system was never—there's a training gap," he said. "In the Wrath Assessment division. I've been meaning to address it."

She looked at him for a slightly different kind of long time than before.

Then she walked to the desk, set the Seal down, pulled a chair out with her foot, and sat.

He produced the papers.

---

They worked without speaking, for a while, in the way they had always been able to work without speaking—two people who had spent long enough in each other's company that silence had textures and they knew which texture this one was. It was the texture of coexistence. Productivity. Not warmth, not exactly, but a kind of thermal efficiency, two bodies occupying a space and conserving it.

"The Third District's petition is redundant," she said.

"I know."

"They're asking for expanded territorial rights that the Second Covenant already granted them. Someone in their administrative apparatus doesn't know the Second Covenant exists."

"Someone in their administrative apparatus doesn't know much of anything."

"You should dissolve the position and rebuild it."

"I've been meaning to."

"You've been meaning to dissolve and rebuild most of Hell for—" She stopped. Made a small adjustment to the angle of a document. "The petition language is almost identical to the one from 1683."

"I processed that one."

"You didn't process that one. I processed that one while you dictated letters to Milton."

"Milton was—"

"I know what Milton was. I was *here.*"

There was a sharpness to the last word that she hadn't intended, or had intended and regretted, or had intended and refused to regret but couldn't stop the small stillness it produced in both of them from happening. Grim's gnawing paused for a moment. Then resumed.

"The Wrath Assessments," Lucifer said, to the paper in front of him.

"I'm not filing your Wrath Assessments."

"I wasn't asking."

"You were building toward asking."

"I was making an observation about a logistical—"

"You've been 'making observations about logistical gaps' since before the Romans had a word for logistics," she said. "You observe. You note. You file the observation on a low shelf and review it periodically." She signed something without looking at it. He noticed she still held a pen the same way—the first two fingers only, the thumb barely involved, as if she'd never quite committed to the instrument. "You've been 'meaning to address' the Wrath Assessment training gap since—when did I leave?"

"We don't need to—"

"It's a logistical question."

He signed something. "Eleven years."

Another document moved between them.

"Twelve," she said. "In March."

He did not say anything. She did not say anything. The pen moved.

*She would have said something, here,* he thought, abstractly, in the way he sometimes annotated his own silences. *She would have said something designed to make it smaller, then something designed to make it larger, then something else designed to make it—*

"The cold spot in the fourth antechamber," she said. "The one by the—"

"Still there."

"You never fixed it."

"It's not—it doesn't affect anything."

"It's cold."

"It's Hell. The ambient—"

"The ambient temperature of Hell is not *cold,* it's you just—" She stopped again. Set her pen down. Picked it up. "It's a simple seal replacement, it would take someone half an afternoon."

"Someone could do it," he agreed.

"Someone specifically on your staff, whom you pay in—whatever you pay them in—"

"Fear, primarily. Some stock options."

She laughed.

He had not been prepared for this, in the specific way that you cannot prepare for something you've been, without admitting it, braced against. It was brief. It was the sound of her catching herself off-guard, which was a sound he had a particular, wholly involuntary physical response to, which he managed by looking at a document that did not require his attention.

"Stock options," she said, already recovering.

"We diversified in the nineties. Everyone was doing it."

"Hell diversified into *stock options.*"

"Several very promising portfolios. Tobacco, primarily. Some pharmaceuticals."

She was looking at him now in the way that meant she was deciding whether to let something be funny, which was a decision she made consciously and that he had always found complicated to watch, because the moment before she allowed something to be funny was the moment she looked most like herself, and.

And.

"I should go," she said.

"You haven't—the Third District documents still need."

"Send them to me."

"You said you weren't—the Wrath Assessments—"

"I said I wasn't filing your Wrath Assessments. I didn't say I wouldn't look at Third District jurisdictional paperwork." She stood. Picked up the Seal. "Send them through Mephistopheles. He'll enjoy the delegation."

"He'll want a new title in exchange."

"Let him have one. Grand Duke is doing him no favors. Give him something with *Infernal* in it. He'll be manageable for a decade."

She was at the edge of the desk now. Grim had emerged again—she hadn't moved toward him, hadn't called him, hadn't made any of the sounds or gestures that would have summoned him, and yet he was there, his vast head at her hip, his tail a slow and eloquent argument. She looked at him. He looked at her with both of his faces, or all several of them, depending on how the light caught him.

"You named this dog *Grim,*" she said.

"He came with the name."

"You could have changed it."

"He knows it now."

She put her hand on Grim's head, for longer this time, and Grim's tail increased its revolution, and Lucifer found a document that required his attention very suddenly.

When he looked up she was halfway across the room.

"The cold spot," he said.

"What about it."

"I'll have someone look at it."

She didn't turn around. She paused, briefly, with her back to him and the Seal under her arm, and he thought she was going to say something, and she thought something about this situation that he was not entitled to, and then she said, over her shoulder—

"Don't let Mephistopheles name the seal replacement project. He'll call it something Latin and you'll have to file it under five different cross-references."

"I know."

"Good."

She left. The wrong direction again. The sound of her footsteps crossed the obsidian record of the room, grew smaller in it, and were gone.

---

He sat on the Iron Throne.

It listed, slightly, to the left—the rear leg measurably shorter now, the balance of the whole enormous thing shifted by fractions. He adjusted his angle without thinking about it, a small lean to compensate, the kind of adjustment you make often enough that it stops being an adjustment and becomes simply how you sit.

Grim returned to him.
