The scooter’s whine cut through the Trevi Fountain’s constant roar like a blade through silk.

Ben had just lifted his guidebook—creased spine, neon sticky tabs like little flags of optimism—when someone’s shadow flashed across the page. A rush of hot air. Rubber on stone. A motorcycle shouldered into the narrow lane of tourists as if the crowd were water and it knew the current.

Ben took one instinctive step back.

The bike’s front tire kissed the cobbles, skidded, recovered—so close his elbow brushed the handlebar. He caught a glimpse of a dented side panel, the scratch painted over in a childish explosion of colors: turquoise looping into fuchsia, a streak of yellow like a lightning bolt. Someone had tried to make damage into art.

“Watch where you—” Ben started, heart punching his ribs.

The rider’s helmet was hooked to the back; her dark hair was pulled up in a rough knot. Sunglasses sat on her head like she’d forgotten they were there. She swung one boot down, steadying the bike without looking frightened, without looking impressed.

“Ma sei cieco?” Her voice was sharp as the water’s spray, slicing through English and German and the fountain’s thunder. Are you blind?

Ben blinked, mouth still open, guidebook held up like evidence in a trial. “I’m— I’m sorry. I didn’t see you. I mean, I did see you, obviously, I—”

She eyed him, then the guidebook, then the way his fingers clenched the edge as if it might anchor him. “You stood in the middle,” she said in accented English that sounded like she’d learned it to argue. “Like… a statue.”

“I was trying not to—” He gestured helplessly at the throng of people and the fountain and the feeling of being swallowed. “I was trying not to get in anyone’s way.”

“You succeeded,” she said, deadpan. “By being everyone’s way.”

A laugh barked out of someone behind them; Ben’s cheeks heated. “I’m truly sorry,” he insisted, British politeness turning into a reflexive apology as though volume could make it sincere enough to undo physics. “I should have moved. Are you alright? Is your— your bike—”

“My bike is always alright.” Her gaze flicked to the colorful scratch/dent, a brief, tight thing in her expression, gone before he could name it.

Ben looked again. The scratch had been made to look intentional, but it wasn’t. It was the kind of mark left by a bad choice.

He tried Italian, because he’d been practicing on the plane, because he’d promised himself he would. “Mi scusi,” he said carefully. Then, reaching for the next phrase, he grabbed the wrong word in his mind like a slippery fish. “Non volevo… disturbarti.”

Her eyebrows jumped. “Disturbarmi?” She leaned closer, amusement brightening her face. “You did not want to *disturb* me?”

“I mean— I didn’t want to— cause trouble.” Ben’s ears felt hot. He heard his own voice, too earnest, too slow for this fast Roman morning. “I’m not very good at—”

“At being alive?” she supplied, and then, because she wasn’t cruel enough to leave it there, she softened it with a grin. “Or at Italian.”

“I’m excellent at being alive,” Ben said, surprising himself, a flicker of irritation finding its way into humor. “I’m just trying to do it quietly.”

For a moment, she paused. Something in her eyes shifted—like she’d expected him to fold, and he’d instead offered a small, tidy rebellion.

“Quietly,” she repeated, tasting the word. “In Rome.”

Ben held her gaze, because if he looked away he’d lose whatever little ground he’d gained. “Yes.”

She laughed then, properly. Not loud, but warm, like sunlight slipping between ancient stones. “Okay, quiet Englishman,” she said. “You need help.”

“I’m managing,” Ben said automatically.

Her eyes dropped to his shoulder bag—canvas, academic-looking, the strap worn soft. Then to his nose, which was turning the particular pink of someone who’d underestimated the sun. Then to the guidebook, bristling with tabs like it was a hedgehog trying to defend itself.

“You are not managing,” she pronounced. She swung her leg over the bike and—without asking—reached out, plucked the guidebook from his hands. The motion was quick, casual, like taking a cigarette from a friend’s mouth.

Ben’s fingers closed on air. “Excuse me—”

She flipped it open, frowned at a highlighted passage. “‘Top Ten Must-See Sights of Rome,’” she read in an exaggerated announcer’s voice, then snapped it shut as if it had insulted her. “Madonna.”

“It’s just a guide,” Ben protested, and then, because the guidebook was his plan and plans were his armor, he added, “I have an itinerary.”

“An itinerary.” Her mouth curled. “You came to Rome to make a list.”

“I came to Rome to—” Ben stopped. The truth was not something he wanted to say aloud in a crowd of strangers. He came to Rome because he’d been offered a fellowship for his research, because he’d postponed living for years, because his life had been a series of footnotes and now he was standing in the sunlight with a book telling him what to feel.

He reached for his guidebook. She raised it just out of reach.

“No,” she said. “Today, I fix your Rome.”

“Today?” Ben echoed, half laugh, half alarm. “Who are you?”

She considered, then shrugged as if names were optional. “Alessandra.”

Ben swallowed. “Ben.”

“Bene.” She tapped the guidebook against his chest, like a baton. “Ben. You are going to stop walking like you are apologizing to the ground.”

“I wasn’t—”

“Yes, you were.” She took his arm—her hand light but decisive, fingers closing around the fabric of his sleeve. The contact was electric in its simplicity. “Come.”

Ben’s feet moved because her momentum pulled him. Or because something in him wanted to be pulled, wanted someone else to make the choice. The fountain’s roar fell behind them as they slipped into a side street where the air smelled of espresso and exhaust.

“Wait,” Ben said, jogging to keep up. “I have— I have plans. I’m meeting someone later.”

“You are meeting Rome,” she said, as if that settled it. She glanced back at him, eyes gleaming. “Do you trust me?”

Ben opened his mouth to say no, because no was the sensible answer. But he saw his own guidebook in her hand, saw the way she moved as if the city belonged to her, and felt, unexpectedly, the weight of his own loneliness.

“I don’t know you,” he said, truthfully.

Alessandra’s smile sharpened. “Then you have nothing to lose.”

Ben heard himself say, “Alright.”

Her grin widened, victorious. She tugged him around the corner into a small café tucked between souvenir shops, its bar crowded with locals knocking back coffee like medicine. The air inside was cool, dark, alive with clinking cups and rapid Italian.

Alessandra leaned in to the bar, fired off an order without looking at a menu. Ben watched, stunned, at how easily she belonged—how the barista’s face lit, how someone shouted her name from the back.

“Caffè?” she asked Ben over her shoulder.

“Yes, please,” Ben said. “Just— just espresso.”

She made a face like he’d asked for boiled water. “You need sugar,” she decided.

“I’m fine—”

“You are English, yes? You are made of tea and sadness.” She took the tiny glass of espresso the barista slid toward her, then another for Ben, and a small dish of sugar packets.

Ben reached for his cup. Their fingers brushed, and he felt, stupidly, like it meant something.

They moved to a narrow standing table. Alessandra tore open a sugar packet with her teeth, poured it into her cup, stirred with a violent little twist.

Ben watched her hands. Quick. Clean. Restless.

A man in a suit bumped the table, muttered an apology. Alessandra smiled at him, easy. Then her hand dipped to the sugar dish again.

Ben’s gaze followed, because he could not help it. He’d spent years training his mind to notice details—archival dates, marginalia, the difference between two nearly identical scripts. He saw her fingers close on a sugar packet and, instead of dropping it into her cup, slide it into the pocket of her leather jacket.

So smoothly it could have been a mistake, except it wasn’t. Her hand came out empty, innocent.

Ben stared at the pocket as if it might confess.

Alessandra caught his expression and tilted her head. “What?”

“Nothing,” Ben said too quickly. “Nothing at all.”

She held his gaze for a beat, eyes bright with a question she didn’t ask. Then she turned away first, as if boredom had won.

Outside again, the sun slapped Ben in the face. Alessandra handed him the guidebook like she’d gotten tired of carrying his conscience.

“Now,” she said, pointing at her bike parked half on the curb like it owned the pavement. “You will come with me.”

Ben looked at the motorcycle. He’d assumed she meant walking—because tourists walked, because walking was safe. The bike looked like speed and trouble. The colorful scratch/dent gleamed in the sunlight, cheerful disguise over something damaged.

“I— I don’t have a helmet,” Ben said, searching for practical objections.

Alessandra reached under the seat, pulled out a helmet, and shoved it toward him. “You do.”

Ben put it on. It was too big; it slid down over his eyebrows, cutting his view into a narrow strip. He fumbled with the strap like it was a puzzle.

Alessandra watched, leaning on the bike. “Dio mio,” she said, not unkindly. She stepped closer, fingers at his chin, fastening the strap with brisk competence.

Her knuckles grazed his jaw. Ben went still.

“Better,” she said, and her voice had gone lower, for half a second, as if she’d noticed his reaction and liked it.

Ben cleared his throat. “Where are we going?”

“Somewhere your book doesn’t know.” She swung onto the seat and patted the space behind her. “Come.”

Ben hesitated. The street was chaos—scooters threading through cars, buses growling, people stepping into the road like they had immortality on their side. He imagined himself as a headline: *British Tourist Dies Attempting Roman Romance With Stranger*.

Alessandra turned her head, one eyebrow arched. “You are scared.”

“I’m cautious,” Ben corrected.

She laughed, a bright sound swallowed by traffic. “Same thing, different outfit.”

Ben climbed on.

The seat was narrow. His knees felt too long. Alessandra’s back was warm through her jacket. He hovered his hands, unsure where to put them.

“Hold,” she ordered.

“I don’t want to—” Ben began, meaning *touch you*, meaning *assume*, meaning *be inappropriate*.

Alessandra reached back, grabbed his wrists, and placed his hands firmly on her waist. “Now you want to fall off?”

His palms settled against her, and the contact sent a bolt of heat through him that had nothing to do with the sun.

“Okay,” he said, voice thinner than he liked.

“Good,” Alessandra said, and then the engine surged.

Rome blurred into a ribbon of light and stone. Ben’s stomach rose into his throat as they shot forward, weaving between a taxi and a delivery van. The air smelled of petrol and basil from someone’s open kitchen window. Wind slapped his face. The fountain’s roar became a memory.

Alessandra leaned into turns with the ease of someone dancing. Ben clung to her waist, his fingers tightening every time a car drifted too close, every time a pedestrian stepped off the curb without looking.

They passed a sign: *ZTL*—restricted traffic zone. Ben saw it and inhaled sharply.

Alessandra didn’t slow.

“Isn’t that—” Ben shouted over the engine.

“A suggestion!” she called back, laughing.

“It’s not a suggestion, it’s a law!”

“Rome is older than laws,” she threw at him, and slipped through the boundary like a fish through reeds.

Ben’s pulse thundered. His mind catalogued risks in neat columns—fine, ticket, accident, police. And yet, beneath the panic, there was a wild, shameful pleasure: the sensation of being carried by someone else’s certainty.

They cut through a narrow lane where laundry hung overhead like flags. They burst into a market that smelled of oranges and fish and hot bread. Alessandra parked the bike with a casual disregard for painted lines, and Ben stumbled off, legs buzzing.

She moved into the crowd like she was made for it. She stopped at a stall of cherries, spoke rapid Italian to the vendor, her hands gesturing, her expression fierce and playful at once. The vendor argued back, equally theatrical.

Ben watched, half mesmerized. She wasn’t just impulsive; she was fluent in this world’s unspoken rules.

Alessandra turned, held out a paper cone of cherries to Ben. “Eat.”

Ben took one. It was dark and sweet, juice running down his finger.

“You like?” she demanded.

“Yes,” Ben said, and meant it more than he meant most things.

“See? Rome,” she said, as if she’d invented the fruit. Then she grabbed his sleeve again and pulled him onward.

They slipped into a church through a side door. The heat and noise of the street died instantly, replaced by cool stone and the scent of wax. Their footsteps echoed. Ben’s breathing sounded too loud in his own ears.

Alessandra slowed without announcing it. Her hand slid from his sleeve, and for a moment they walked side by side without touching.

Light fell through high windows, striping the floor. Ben looked up at frescoes, at gold leaf, at centuries layered like sediment. He felt small in a way that wasn’t unpleasant.

Alessandra stopped near a side altar, gaze fixed on a flickering candle.

Ben waited, unsure if he was supposed to speak.

Her throat moved as she swallowed. The bravado fell away for a heartbeat, leaving something tired underneath.

Ben’s voice came out quieter than he intended. “Is this one of the places your guidebook hates?” he asked, trying to make it light.

Alessandra glanced at him, and the mask slid back into place. “My guidebook,” she murmured, and her mouth twitched. “Maybe.”

They left the church and climbed stairs that narrowed into a passage between buildings. The city opened suddenly onto a rooftop overlook where Rome sprawled in every direction: domes, terracotta, the river snaking like a ribbon of dull silver.

Ben’s breath caught. “Oh.”

Alessandra watched him instead of the view, satisfaction sparking. “Better than Trevi,” she said.

Ben nodded, then, because he didn’t want to be only a recipient of her gifts, he said, “Do you know that the word ‘desiderium’—” He stopped, hearing himself veer toward lecture.

Alessandra groaned theatrically. “No Latin.”

“It’s not—” Ben smiled despite himself. “It’s just… It’s where we get ‘desire.’ It originally meant the feeling of missing the stars. Sailors, looking up, realizing something was absent.”

Alessandra’s expression stilled. For a moment she looked like she didn’t know whether to mock him or listen.

“Missing the stars,” she repeated, quieter.

Ben shrugged, suddenly self-conscious. “It’s a better way to say… wanting.”

Alessandra turned back to the view. Her sunglasses slipped down from her head, and she didn’t push them back up. When she spoke, her voice was deliberately light. “You are strange, Ben.”

“Thank you,” Ben said. Then, because he was learning her rhythm, he added, “You’re fast.”

She laughed, a small puff of sound. “Yes.”

They descended again, and as they did, Alessandra’s phone buzzed in her pocket. Once. Twice. A third time, insistent.

She pulled it out, glanced at the screen, and something in her face tightened. Not fear exactly—more like irritation edged with something sharper.

Ben noticed because Ben noticed everything.

Alessandra’s thumb hovered over the screen, then she shoved the phone back into her pocket without answering.

“Everything alright?” Ben asked.

“Everything is always alright,” she said, and the echo of her earlier line sounded like a lie now.

They passed a police car idling at a corner. Alessandra’s shoulders went rigid for a fraction of a second. Her gaze flicked toward the motorcycle parked down the street—toward the colorful dent. Her mouth pressed into a line.

Ben followed her eyes. “That scratch,” he said gently. “Did someone hit you?”

Alessandra’s laugh came out too quick. “Did you become my mother?”

“No,” Ben said. “Just… curious.”

She leaned closer, her smile sharp again, defensive as a blade. “Curiosity is dangerous. Especially for English tourists with sunburn.”

Ben should have let it go. He didn’t. “You ignored that call,” he said softly. “And you looked at the police like they were—”

“Like they were police,” she snapped, and then, abruptly, she turned away. “Come. You are thinking too much.”

Ben followed, but something had changed. The day’s lightness now had a seam in it, a thread he could feel under his fingers.

Later—midday heat pressing down, sweat drying salty on Ben’s neck—Alessandra led him into a small museum courtyard. A sign at the entrance listed hours and rules. Ben barely read them; he was still catching up.

Alessandra, of course, didn’t slow.

They slipped past a velvet rope that clearly meant *do not enter*. Ben’s stomach tightened.

“Alessandra,” he hissed. “We can’t—”

“Yes, we can.” She glanced back, eyes glittering. “Unless you want to go back to your list.”

Ben looked at the rope. Looked at the empty, shaded corridor beyond it, leading to a small garden where orange trees grew in pots. No guards in sight. A harmless trespass, perhaps.

His heartbeat thudded. He heard his own voice in his head, prim and disapproving. He also heard something else: the whisper of missing stars.

He lifted the rope and stepped under it.
