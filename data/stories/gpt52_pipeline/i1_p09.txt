The darkroom door sighed shut behind me, and the hallway noise—some distant laughter, a vending machine swallowing coins—died like someone turned down the world.

Red light pooled over everything. It was the kind of red that made skin look like clay and time feel thicker. The safelight’s hum was steady, a low electrical purr over the drip of the wash sink.

I flipped the fan switch on out of habit, even though the fan always sounded like it might quit. Chemicals sat capped and labeled in my handwriting from last week: DEV, STOP, FIX. Three shallow trays waited like open mouths. The enlarger stood in the corner, tall and black, its head angled down at the easel as if it had been left mid-thought.

“Hey,” a voice said softly.

Elliot was already here, half absorbed into the shadows by the door, like he’d been printed too lightly. His clothes were layers of thrift-store intention—black lace peeking at his wrists under an oversized cardigan, a thin chain glinting at his throat when he moved. His hair was dark and cut to fall in his eyes, and under the red light I couldn’t tell if his lipstick was real lipstick or just the way the light treated his mouth.

He held a plastic sleeve of negatives with careful fingers, like it was something fragile that could bruise.

“You’re early,” I said, because it was easier than saying I’d been thinking about this room since dinner. About being alone with him in it.

He shrugged one shoulder. “Couldn’t sleep.”

I set my bag down and moved into my usual rhythm. Timer plugged in. Tongs lined up on the edge of the counter. Paper box tucked under the table, closed tight. My hands knew the choreography, and my voice followed.

“Okay. We’ve got… what, an hour before campus police decides we’re criminals?” I tugged on a pair of nitrile gloves and snapped them at my wrists. “Let’s make it count. What did you bring?”

Elliot stepped closer, and his silhouette sharpened into details: a small scab on one knuckle, a faint smudge of ink on his thumb. He slid the negatives onto the counter, angled so they wouldn’t catch the safelight too directly, even though he knew better than that.

“I have the series from the river,” he said. “The one with the broken dock and the reeds.”

“Good.” I leaned in, my shoulder nearly brushing his. In the red, the negative strips looked like thin bones. “We’ll pick two frames and do test strips. You want to start with yours or mine?”

His eyes flicked to me. I couldn’t read them under this light, and that made me talk more—fill the blank with competence.

“Mine are probably overexposed,” I added, already reaching for the enlarger’s drawer.

“They’re not,” he said, quiet but certain. “Your midtones are consistent.”

It landed like a compliment that had been thought through.

“Look at you,” I said, half-laughing. “Critique mode.”

He huffed something that might’ve been a laugh too, then took the negative carrier from my hand before I could fully take it out. He did it smoothly, not like he was grabbing control, just like he knew where his hands belonged.

“I can load it,” he said.

I should have let him without comment. Instead, I heard myself say, “I know. I just… tend to—” to take over, to steer, to be the one with the plan. That sentence tasted too revealing, so I replaced it with, “—tend to be faster.”

His mouth tilted. “You are.”

And then he slid the negative into place with such precision I felt my own confidence rearrange itself. He wasn’t passive. He wasn’t waiting for me to pull him along. He was… deliberate. Like every choice had weight.

We worked with minimal talk at first, the kind of quiet that felt practiced. I adjusted the enlarger height; he aligned the easel. We checked focus with the grain magnifier, the projected image on the baseboard blooming and shrinking as I turned the knob. Timer set. Paper cut for test strips.

In the red, everything was close. Not physically—though we kept bumping elbows—but psychologically. There were no windows. No phones. No bright faces to perform for. Just our hands moving through tasks and the soft chemical bite in the back of my throat.

This is how I am, I thought as I slid the first test strip into the developer. I’m good at making a plan. I’m good at knowing what comes next. People like it when I know what comes next.

Elliot watched the paper in the tray like it was a secret surfacing.

“Timer,” I said.

He started the stopwatch on his phone but kept the screen turned down, careful. “Two minutes,” he murmured.

The image bled into being under the developer, slow and ghostly. Reeds first, then the broken line of dock, then the water’s texture like skin. It always felt like magic even when I pretended it didn’t.

While the print developed, the darkroom gave us a lull. The timer ticked. Water ran in the wash. My hands were busy, but my mind wandered to the way Elliot’s sleeves fell over his wrists, the way he’d tucked his hair behind one ear and left a small hoop earring exposed. His masculinity—if that was even the right word—wasn’t the kind that pushed. It was something softer, chosen.

“Do you like being on the other side?” I asked, surprising myself.

Elliot’s gaze didn’t leave the tray. “Of the camera?”

“Yeah.”

A beat. The paper darkened. His throat moved with a swallow.

“No,” he said. Then, after another second, “I mean. Not usually.”

I could have left it there. But the room felt like it wanted the truth, or at least something adjacent.

“Why?” I asked, and my voice came out curious instead of commanding.

He shifted his weight. In the red light, his face was all angles and shadow. “It feels like… people decide what I am too fast.” He said it like he’d rehearsed around the sentence a hundred times without ever stepping into it.

I watched him the way I watch prints—waiting for detail. “Even with a camera?” I asked.

“Especially with a camera,” he said, and a small, tight smile flashed. “The camera is honest in a way people aren’t. It catches what you didn’t mean to show.”

I lifted the test strip with tongs, let developer drip back into the tray. “But you control a lot,” I said. “You’re good at… all of this.”

He nodded once, like yes, exactly. Then his fingers worried the edge of the counter, nails short and clean. “I like controlling the image,” he said. “Because I don’t always—” He stopped.

I didn’t fill the silence. The red light made his pauses feel safer, like they could exist without being interrogated.

“You don’t always feel like you control what people see?” I offered gently.

He exhaled, a sound that seemed to fog the air despite the fan. “Yeah.”

I moved the strip to stop bath, watched it go still. My brain offered me my usual script: reassure, define, make it okay. Tell him he’s fine. Tell him people are stupid. Tell him it doesn’t matter.

But the way he’d said decide what I am too fast made my instinct feel like part of the problem.

“So,” I said instead, keeping my eyes on the tray to give him space, “what do you want them to see?”

The question hung between us with the damp chemical scent.

Elliot’s voice dropped. “I don’t know,” he admitted. Then, quieter: “Or I do, but I don’t know what it means.”

The print moved into fixer. Two minutes. The timer beeped again, soft.

He rubbed his thumb over the ink smudge like he was erasing himself. “I’ve been… thinking,” he said. “About gender. About—” He made a small motion with his hand at his own body, at his clothes, at the air around him. “About how I’m read.”

I kept my hands steady, even as something in my chest tightened and sparked at the same time. “Okay,” I said, simple.

He glanced at me then, testing. “Like, I don’t hate being called ‘he,’” he went on, careful, as if he expected the floor to shift. “But sometimes it feels like a coat that doesn’t fit right. And sometimes it fits and I like it. And sometimes I want… something else.” His mouth twisted. “I don’t have a label that doesn’t feel like I’m lying.”

“People act like you’re supposed to pick one and be done,” I said. I heard the edge of anger in my own voice and reined it in. “But you don’t have to do that.”

He watched my face, as if searching for the moment I’d get weird.

I wanted to say, I’ve got you. I wanted to step closer and wrap him in certainty. I wanted, stupidly, to be the one who could solve it.

Instead, I said, “Thank you for telling me.”

His shoulders dropped a fraction, like he’d been holding them up against an invisible hand.

Inside, my thoughts were less graceful.

So what does that make me? I thought, and immediately hated myself for making it about me. But the question was there anyway, tangled with the fact that I was still—maybe more—attracted to him right now. The softness. The honesty. The way his uncertainty didn’t look like weakness; it looked like courage.

A treacherous thought flickered: This makes him more… something. More interesting, more— My brain tried to slot him into a new category that still revolved around my desire. I caught it like a hand snatching a photo before it hit the wrong tray.

Stop. He isn’t an aesthetic. He’s a person.

Out loud, I kept my voice even. “Do you want to keep talking about it?” I asked, “or do you want to just… work for a while?”

Elliot blinked, processing the choice. He nodded slowly. “Work,” he said. “For a minute. And then… maybe talk.”

“Okay,” I said, and meant it.

We moved to the enlarger again. I lifted the negative carrier from him this time, but paused. “Can I?” I asked.

A tiny smile. “Yeah.”

Our hands met on the metal edge, accidental but real. His fingers were cold; mine were warmer from the trays. The contact was brief, but it shot through me like the click of a shutter. My body did what it always did—lean in, push, take the moment and make it something.

I didn’t.

I let my hand withdraw. I watched him, not the way I’d watched him earlier with hunger, but with attention.

Elliot didn’t pull away either. He stayed where he was, shoulders nearly touching mine as he peered up at the projected image. He adjusted the focus knob with a careful twist, then looked at me as if to ask, is this okay?

I nodded. “That’s perfect,” I whispered, and the whisper felt like a promise and a question at once.

We cut a new strip of paper. I reached under the table for the box, fingers finding the cardboard edge.

A sound broke the room—an abrupt knock, and then the door handle jiggled.

“Shit,” I breathed.

The door cracked open before I could lunge. A wedge of white hallway light sliced into the darkroom like a blade.

“Sorry!” a voice called, too loud. “Is anyone—”

I threw my body toward the door, slapped it shut. “Occupied!” I snapped, sharper than I meant to. The latch caught. The light vanished.

For a second, we stood frozen in the red, as if our eyes had to relearn the dark.

Then I looked down.

The paper box lid had been open. A stack of pristine sheets—my fault, I’d left it unlatched in the rush—sat exposed to that one unforgiving breath of white.

No sound came out of me at first. Just a pulse of heat behind my eyes. “God,” I said finally, a soft curse that felt too small.

Elliot’s face went pale under the red, or maybe that was my imagination. He stepped back like he’d been slapped. “I’m sorry,” he said immediately. “I should’ve— I shouldn’t have—”

“It’s not you,” I cut in, too fast, my old instinct to control latching onto blame like a handle. “It’s the idiot who opened the door. And me. I left the box—”

Elliot’s hands curled, then opened, then curled again, like he didn’t know where to put them. “We ruined it,” he said, and there was something deeper underneath, something that wasn’t really about paper.

The ruined materials sat there, a dumb, physical consequence. A reminder that one flash of light could erase what you’d been trying to make.

I inhaled, chemical air and all, and forced my voice to slow down.

“Okay,” I said, louder than a whisper but softer than a command. “Okay. We’re not done. We’ve got test strips. We’ve got… probably like ten sheets that were deeper in the stack and might be okay.” I peeled the top sheet away with gloved fingers. It had a faint gray cast already. Gone.

Elliot stared at it like it was proof of something.

I set the exposed sheet aside. “We can salvage,” I said. Then I looked at him, really looked. “And we don’t have to power through if you don’t want to.”

His eyes flicked up.

“Do you want to keep going?” I asked. “Or do you want to call it and leave? Either is fine.”

The choice sat between us like a clean line.

Elliot’s shoulders lifted and fell. “Keep going,” he said finally, voice steadier. “I don’t want to… I don’t want to run every time something goes wrong.”

“Then we keep going,” I said. “Together.”

We worked with what we had. I found the deeper sheets, fanned them in the red light to check for fog. Elliot re-cut test strips with the paper guillotine, precise and calm now that the worst had happened. He suggested we embrace the accident: print a frame where the river looked like it was swallowing light anyway, let the slight fog become part of the mood.

“It’ll soften the highlights,” he said. “Make it look… dreamlike. Like memory.”

“You’re an artist,” I muttered, but there was admiration in it.

His mouth curved, quick. “Don’t tell anyone. I have a reputation as a cryptid.”

I snorted, and the sound broke something in me—tension unspooling. Our laughter was quiet, respectful of the space.

While a new strip sat in developer, Elliot spoke again, softer. “When you asked what I wanted people to see,” he said, “I realized… I want them to see me as intentional. Not as a mistake.”

My chest tightened. The paper in the tray darkened, the reeds emerging again.

I set the timer, then leaned my hip against the counter, letting myself be still. “Can I tell you something?” I asked.

Elliot’s eyebrows lifted slightly. “Yeah.”

“I’m—” My tongue tripped over the beginning, annoyed at myself. I could talk my way through critiques in front of twenty people, but this felt like stepping onto an unexposed sheet. “I’m attracted to you,” I said, plain. “And I’m also… confused.”

His expression didn’t close. He didn’t flinch. He just listened.

“Not confused about you being… wrong,” I added quickly, because the fear of hurting him made my stomach drop. “Confused about me. About what I assume things mean. About how I like to—” I gestured vaguely at my whole personality. “—take charge. Like I can name things and then they behave.”

Elliot’s gaze softened. “Things don’t behave,” he said.

“No,” I agreed, a small laugh in my throat. “They really don’t.”

He looked back at the print. “I’m attracted to you too,” he said, almost grudging, like admitting it was stepping into cold water. “And it scares me, because I don’t know what part of me you’re attracted to. Or what you think it means.”

The words were careful and honest enough to sting.

I swallowed. “That’s fair,” I said. “I don’t want to decide what you are too fast.”

His eyes met mine, and in the red, the moment felt like a negative held up to light—everything reversed but still true.

“I like the way you see,” he said. “Not just… with your eyes. With how you pay attention.”

Heat moved through me. I wanted to reach for him. I kept my hands where they were, on the edge of the sink, letting the wanting exist without grabbing.

“I can pay attention slower,” I said. “If you want.”

Elliot’s breath caught, just barely. Then he nodded. “Yeah,” he whispered. “Slow is good.”

The timer beeped. We moved again, the work pulling us forward. Stop bath. Fixer. Wash. The rhythm steadied us, gave our words somewhere to land.

At last, we committed to a full sheet—the deepest one in the stack that still looked clean. Elliot chose the frame: the broken dock reaching into water, the reeds bent like they were listening. I set the exposure longer than usual, factoring in the faint fog. He adjusted the contrast filter, his fingers sure.

“Ready?” I asked.

He hovered over the timer button. “Ready.”

The enlarger clicked on, throwing the image down in silent light. For those seconds, we stood shoulder to shoulder, watching brightness hold still.

When the exposure ended, we slid the paper into developer together—his hands on the tongs, mine steadying the tray so it wouldn’t slosh. A shared task, no one leading, no one following too far.

The image emerged slowly. It wasn’t perfect. The whites were softer, the blacks less absolute, like the whole scene was breathing through gauze.

It was beautiful anyway. Maybe because of it.

Elliot leaned closer, and this time his sleeve brushed mine on purpose. He didn’t look at me; he looked at the print like it was a confession we’d made without speaking.

“Light can ruin,” he murmured.

“Yeah,” I said, watching the dock appear, the river taking shape. “And it can reveal.”

We hung the print to dry on the line, clipped
