Aya put her gloved hand to the iron and felt the cold bite through the kid.

Too quiet.

The guild’s underhold had always had a pulse: the thrum of boilers far below, the damp cough of vents, the chain-rattle of lifts, the muttered countersigns that traveled down corridors like rats. Tonight there was only the thin, antiseptic hush of a room that had been scrubbed for an execution.

Behind her, Katra shifted her weight, boots soft on the stone. Simeon—always too honest for his own comfort—swallowed loudly and then seemed to resent the sound.

Aya drew a breath that tasted of whale-oil and old masonry, and pressed.

The door yielded with a sigh, the hinges freshly greased. That alone was wrong. The guild did not waste oil on anything that didn’t kill, heat, or turn a profit.

They stepped into the underhold.

The corridor beyond was brick-arched and narrow, its gas lamps turned low. Moisture beaded on the walls in a way that suggested the sea was not merely nearby but listening. A thin fog had crept in, or been invited in—curling along the floorboards like a living thing seeking ankles.

Aya’s pulse ticked behind her mask. She told herself it was simple caution. It was not.

A distant foghorn sounded from the harbor beyond the guild walls.

Once.

Twice.

Thrice.

The third note seemed to settle in the bones of the place. Then, as if that had been the signal, the gas lamps brightened a fraction. Not enough to be kind. Enough to see.

A figure stood at the far end of the corridor where it widened into a small chamber—beneath a lintel marked with the guild’s seal: a harpoon crossing a crescent wave. A woman in a dark coat, her hair pinned severe, a familiar posture that made Aya’s stomach drop before her mind caught up.

On either side of her were men—four, maybe six, arranged not like thugs but like hired pallbearers. Their clothes were the guild’s sober working black, but their boots were too clean, their collars too stiff. Each held something that gleamed: a short-barreled pistol, a truncheon bound in brass, a length of chain weighted at the end. In their midst, a lantern with a caged blue flame threw an unnatural light across the bricks.

And in the center—

Mara Morska.

Not in chains. Not behind bars. Not a rumor in the morning papers. She was there, alive and whole, the sharp-boned face Aya had only ever glimpsed from a distance at guild gatherings now plainly visible. Her mouth held the smallest of smiles, as if she were watching children attempt a lock with a hairpin.

In her right hand was a hooked blade, its edge oily with something that caught light wrong. In her left—casually, insultingly—she held the thick key-ring of the underhold. It dangled like a watch fob.

“Do come in,” Morska said, voice soft as velvet laid over a coffin. “The door was left for you.”

Katra’s hand moved, not for a weapon, but for Aya’s sleeve—a warning touch. Simeon’s eyes had gone very wide. He looked as if he wanted to protest reality itself.

Aya forced her feet to carry her forward a step, just enough to not seem startled. The mask on her face—black leather, stitched at the cheekbones—felt suddenly flimsy.

“I was told you’d been taken,” Aya said, pitching her voice lower than her own, the guild-voice she used when she wore the mask. “The constables had you.”

Morska’s smile did not change. “Oh, they did.” She glanced to one of the men at her right. “Did they not?”

The man gave a small nod, eyes empty. “We had her in the carriage,” he said. “Then we didn’t.”

Aya’s skin tightened. The raid. The early hours. The shock. The power vacuum that had opened like a mouth—

A mouth baited.

Katra spoke before Aya could, harsh and quick. “What is this, Mara? You want to watch us kneel?”

“I have no use for kneeling,” Morska replied. “It ruins posture. I want to watch you realize what you are.”

Her gaze fixed, not on Katra, but on Aya. As if she’d known all along which of them was the hinge.

Aya’s mind ran through options with the cold speed of a ledger. Run? The corridor was a funnel. Fight? They were outnumbered and unprepared, and Morska’s men had the look of professionals who did not enjoy their work but did it cleanly. Magick—

Magick was a door, and every door cost.

She felt the immateria stir, faintly, like a draft under a threshold. The underhold was old, soaked in grief and greed, and such places made thin spots. If she reached for it, something would answer. Something hungry. Something loud.

Men trusted brass and oil and steel because those things were obedient. The immateria was not.

Morska lifted her chin. “You’ve been busy. Rising through my house like damp up a wall.”

“We kept the guild from tearing itself apart,” Katra snapped.

Simeon found his tongue at last. “We—we thought you were gone. The captains were at each other’s throats. We only—”

“—only did what you thought you could get away with,” Morska finished for him. Her tone was almost gentle. “I understand. Ambition is the only virtue I cannot pretend to despise.”

Aya made herself speak. “If you wanted us dead, you wouldn’t have oiled the hinges.”

Morska’s eyes flickered. “Clever. Keep that. You’ll need it to fill the time before the end.”

At a small gesture from Morska’s hooked blade, the men moved. Not rushing. Not dramatic. Efficient as shutters closing at dusk.

Aya’s body made to back up, but Katra’s hand was already in hers—squeezing once, hard, the way she did before a job when she wanted Aya’s attention without words.

Two of the guards approached Simeon. He raised his hands instinctively. “Wait. Wait—”

A brass truncheon pressed to his stomach, and he folded with a pained grunt. Another hand seized his collar and turned him, pinning him to the wall. No strike followed. No flourish. Just control.

Katra reached for the knife at her boot, but a chain snapped out and wrapped her wrist. The weighted end thudded against her bone. She hissed, tried to twist free.

A pistol barrel found the space beneath her jaw.

“No,” the man holding it said, bored. “Not tonight.”

Aya’s breath came shallow. She took one step, meaning—what? To stand between them? To summon? To bargain?

A hand closed on her upper arm like a vise. Another hooked fingers under the edge of her mask.

Aya jerked her head away. The leather rasped against her skin. She was suddenly aware of the softness of her own face beneath it, the vulnerability she’d always hidden—aristocratic skin, uncalloused, a traitor to the streets.

“Don’t,” she said, and hated the edge of panic in the word.

Morska’s gaze sharpened, delighted.

The guard yanked.

The mask came free with a sound like skin tearing. Cold air hit Aya’s cheeks. Gaslight painted her bare.

For half a heartbeat the corridor held its breath.

Then Katra made a sound—half curse, half laugh, as if she’d always suspected and now couldn’t decide whether to spit or applaud.

Simeon’s face went pale with a different kind of fear. Not fear of death. Fear of what Aya was.

Morska’s smile finally widened, and it was not a kind thing. “There she is,” she murmured. “I wondered when you’d stop pretending to be only a shadow.”

Aya lifted her chin as if her throat were not tightening. She forced her expression into something she had watched her father wear at dinners with men who spoke of hangings as though they were weather.

“Do you know who I am?” she asked quietly.

“I know what you are,” Morska corrected. “A well-bred girl playing at soot and knives. And something else.” Her nostrils flared slightly. “Something that stinks of the immateria.”

The word landed like a slap. Around them, one of the guards crossed himself in a quick, furtive motion. Another tightened his grip on his pistol as though it might ward off a thing that didn’t bleed.

Aya felt her stomach turn—not at their fear, but at the memory it dragged up: her mother’s hands bound, her mother’s mouth bruised from praying too loudly, the official reading out the sentence with a voice full of satisfied righteousness. Outlawed practice. Unnatural traffic. Immoral commerce.

Morska stepped closer, boots clicking softly. The blue caged flame made her eyes look like dark glass.

“I had you watched,” Morska said. “Not because I feared you. Because I like to know which of my rats has learned to read.”

Aya’s arm still ached under the guard’s grip. She did not pull away. Resistance would be theater; Morska’s men were built to end theater.

Instead Aya did the only thing she had ever been trained to do in rooms where power waited: she spoke as if she had it, too.

“You won’t kill me,” Aya said.

Katra’s eyes flicked to her, warning and bewilderment knotted together. Simeon stared at Aya as if she’d begun to levitate.

Morska paused. “And why would I not?”

“Because I’m worth more alive,” Aya said, keeping her voice steady with effort. “Not as a guildling. As a hostage.”

Morska’s gaze narrowed, amused. “A hostage to whom? Your little friends?”

“No,” Aya said. “To the people who will come looking when I do not return. The people whose names open doors you cannot pick.”

Katra gave a tiny, incredulous shake of her head. Aya felt it like betrayal and forgiveness in the same motion.

Morska considered her as one might consider a knife: weight, balance, the quality of the steel.

“You have people,” Morska said slowly. “And yet you spend your nights in my walls. That implies either great boredom or a rot in your home.”

Aya swallowed. The air tasted of metal and damp, as if the sea had put a coin on her tongue.

“I can be exchanged,” Aya pressed on. “Or ransomed. Or used. If you kill me, you get… silence. A corpse. A scandal. Investigators.”

Morska’s voice softened. “Do you imagine I fear investigators?”

“I imagine you fear losing what you’ve built,” Aya said. “You were arrested—on purpose, perhaps. To see who reached for your crown. Fine. You’ve seen. Now you need to stabilize the guild. Publicly. Quietly.”

She let the next sentence come like a blade sliding in. “A dead aristocrat in your underhold is not quiet.”

For a moment, the only sound was the faint hiss of the blue flame and the distant, settling groan of the building, as if the underhold itself resented being used for conversation instead of suffering.

Morska lifted the key-ring and let it jingle once, lightly. “You’re not wrong,” she said. “That is the hateful thing about clever girls. They force one to be practical.”

Aya’s heart hammered. Practical did not mean safe. Practical could mean cages.

Morska leaned in, close enough that Aya could smell smoke and bitter tea on her breath. “Tell me your name,” she said, “without the mask.”

Aya felt the world tilt toward a single point. In her mind, she saw her mother’s face again—eyes bright with the same cursed gift, the same curiosity, the same refusal to be small. She saw the rope.

If she lied, perhaps she bought seconds. If she told the truth, she set the final stone in place.

“Aya,” she said at last, and then, because the truth was a thing that once spoken could not be unsaid: “Aya Valebrook.”

A sound escaped Simeon—an exhale like prayer. Katra’s mouth tightened.

Morska straightened, satisfied, as if a puzzle piece had clicked home. “Valebrook,” she repeated, savoring it. “Of course. Your mother was the one they hanged for whispering to the dark between worlds.”

Aya’s nails dug into her own palms. “Don’t speak of her.”

“Oh, I will,” Morska said, pleasant as Sunday. “Because it tells me what you are willing to do, and what you are willing to become. It tells me why you came so eagerly for my scythan toy.”

She turned her head slightly. “Bind them.”

The order was met at once. Rope—tarred, rough—bit into Aya’s wrists. Another line cinched Katra’s arms behind her. Simeon was hauled forward, his spectacles askew, his mouth opening and closing like a fish hauled out of the harbor’s black water.

Aya’s mind reached, reflexively, for the immateria again—feeling the thin place pulse behind the bricks like a wound.

Something listened.

She could call. She could bargain with a thing that was not human, not lawful, not merciful. She could trade the lives of strangers later for the lives of her friends now.

She thought of her mother’s last lesson, spoken through a barred window with a voice gone hoarse: It is never free, Aya. It only pretends to be.

Morska watched her, as if reading the direction of her thoughts. “Don’t,” she said softly.

Aya stilled. “You’re afraid.”

For the first time, Morska’s smile thinned into something honest. “I’m respectful,” she corrected. “There’s a difference.”

She gestured with her hooked blade toward the deeper dark of the underhold, where the corridor fell away into steps. Somewhere below, behind spell and key, the scythan artifact waited—patient as a heart in a chest.

“Take them down,” Morska said. “Our young lady has much to tell me. And I have not yet decided what she will be worth.”

As Aya was marched forward, rope cutting her skin, she kept her face composed by force of habit. Inside, everything shook.

The underhold’s quiet had not been peace.

It had been the sound of a trap, already sprung, settling back into place.
