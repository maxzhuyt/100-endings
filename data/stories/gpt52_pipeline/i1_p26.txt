The compound had been built to look older than it was.

From orbit it had appeared as a low bruise of stone on the plain, a deliberate blemish on a landscape that had not consented. Up close, walking from the landing pad through the controlled airlock, it was all right angles and lintels, blocks cut from the local basalt and set so tightly you could not put a fingernail between them. The seams were honest; the intention was not.

Envoy Iri Halen—junior grade, first solo posting, papers still crisp with authority that wasn’t theirs—kept their hands folded, palms damp inside the regulation gloves. The air here was filtered and slightly too sweet, as if to make up for whatever had been removed. Their suit’s collar pressed at the throat with a gentle insistence.

A committee waited in the antechamber with the careful patience of people who had been briefed on how to look pleased. There was a banner of woven fiber behind them, printed with a phrase in Trade Standard and in the colony’s primary written script, both saying, in slightly different ways: *Mutual Recognition Enables Mutual Prosperity.*

Iri had read it in the packet.

“Envoy Halen,” said the senior greeter, a man whose badge named him Deputy Administrator Sero. His voice came through the compound’s translation field polished to the point of losing its grain. “Welcome to Keth. We are honored by the Assembly’s continued interest in our stability.”

*Stability,* Iri thought, and heard the word as it had been used in the dossier: a condition achieved by strict zoning, controlled reproduction rights, and “lineage-based labor allocation.” On paper it was a table with columns: Lineage A, Lineage B, Lineage C—terms that pretended to be scientific in the way a uniform pretends to be skin.

They bowed the way their training advised. A moment later, remembering something from a cultural note—*avoid exaggerated submission gestures; perceived as insult*—they corrected it, too late, the bow becoming half and then becoming nothing. Deputy Administrator Sero smiled as if he had not noticed.

“Please,” Sero said, and with that, protocol began its well-oiled march. Hands were shaken. Names were exchanged and repeated with flattering accuracy. A junior aide pressed a slate into Iri’s palm: briefing supplement, tour schedule, emergency contact protocols, a map of approved zones shaded in pale green and the rest of the settlement in gray, blank as uninhabited land.

They passed under an archway carved with glyphs. The stone was darker here, oiled perhaps, or simply handled often. The carvings were shallow, worn not by age but by fingers. Iri’s eye caught on a figure: a person bent backward, arms reaching, something like a lyre or a loop of cord in hand, a mouth open in what could be song or command. Beneath it, lines like flowing water, or like air currents, or like writing turned into ornament.

Orpheus, Iri thought automatically, and then corrected themselves: not Orpheus, because Orpheus was Earth’s story and this was Keth’s stone. But the resemblance was too deliberate to be accident. The packet had said: *Foundational glyph set derived from Old Earth myth-forms; locally conventionalized. Use as cultural bridge in dialogue.* A neat little note, as if myth were a toolkit.

The tour was curated with the confidence of someone offering you a meal they had already chewed. The administrative hall, the water processing center, the clinic. In each place, someone in a clean uniform spoke of how the colony had adapted, how lineages had specialized. “The Tesselated,” one guide said, and the translation rendered it with a capital letter, a proper noun wearing a crown, “are naturally suited to fine mechanical work. Their fingertips—” A smile. A gesture toward a workroom where people sat at benches assembling filters with heads down. “We protect them from the strain of decision-making.”

Iri’s skepticism, which had been a comforting private trait on the ship, felt here like an itch under cloth. They took notes anyway. Their job was to listen. Their job was to translate interest into policy without making enemies. Their job was to become, eventually, someone who could act without needing to be told the right posture.

The briefing packet, that night, was heavier than its mass. In their quarters—a narrow room with a stone wall left exposed as a charming accent—they laid the slate on the desk and scrolled through lineage charts, risk assessments, polite histories. The words were so tidy. The categories made such excellent sense, if one accepted their premises.

On the wall opposite the bed, a carved panel had been set like a picture. It showed the same bent figure with the loop, the same lines beneath. Someone had filled the grooves with white dust. Orpheus made luminous by limestone.

*Myth as portable governance,* Iri thought, and startled at the phrase because it was not theirs; it was from a lecture on colonial semiotics, spoken by a professor who had never left Earth. They could still hear her voice: “They take what they can carry, and they make it law.”

The next day’s official function took place in a hall meant to impress offworlders. The ceiling was higher than necessary, the stone blocks smoother, the lighting warmer. Glyphs ran along the walls in a band like a crown molding, repeating motifs: the bent singer, a woman with a sheaf of grain—Demeter, though the grain here was not wheat—an animal that could have been Raven or could have been simply a local scavenger stylized into significance. Earth myths rendered into Keth’s angular hand.

Iri was placed at the central table beside Administrator Vell, whose title, “First Voice,” suggested both democracy and ventriloquism. On Vell’s other side sat a figure introduced as Councilor Nema of Lineage C, though Nema’s clothing bore no lineage marker that Iri could see. The air was spiced with something floral that made Iri’s sinuses tighten. It was subtle enough to be deniable; it was also a reminder that their body did not belong here.

“To mutual recognition,” Administrator Vell said, lifting a cup. The liquid inside was clear, with a sheen of oil on top. Iri lifted their cup as well, careful to match the timing, the angle. Training had taught them that to toast too eagerly was rude, and to hesitate was worse.

“To mutual recognition,” Iri echoed.

A singer—one of the “Tesselated,” perhaps, if Iri believed the packet—stood at the edge of the hall and began a song in the local language. The translation field offered the words as a poem about rivers and stone and names. The melody slid under the language like a second meaning.

Councilor Nema leaned toward Iri. Their face was scarred in a pattern that might have been decorative or might have been the consequence of work. “We are fortunate,” Nema said softly, “that the Assembly sends someone so… attentive.”

The translation rendered *attentive* as *diligent.* The local word, in Iri’s ear, had a sharper sound, like a blade tested against nail.

“Thank you,” Iri said. “I’m here to understand, as well as I can.”

Nema’s eyes flicked to the glyph band on the wall. “Understanding is a kind of hunger,” they said. “Some hungers are polite.”

It could have been a compliment. It could have been a warning. The translation field chose politeness.

Plates were brought: steamed root slices, a protein paste molded into shapes resembling fish, though there were no fish on Keth. The paste smelled faintly of metal. Iri’s stomach clenched with the remembered taste of ship rations and the knowledge that refusing would become an incident in someone’s report.

They ate. They smiled when others smiled. They kept their posture within acceptable range, though their shoulders tightened with each minute. The collar pressed. The spice in the air grew heavier. Their skin itched under the fabric, a private rebellion.

At some point Administrator Vell spoke of “integration opportunities,” and Sero laughed in a way that meant: we are generous within our limits. Iri nodded at the right times. They watched who served and who was served, who spoke and who waited to be spoken for. The packet had said Lineage A held governance roles; Lineage B did logistics; Lineage C did “heavy labor and security.” Here, the security stood behind the chairs, silent, watching the room like the room was prey.

The glyphs watched too, indifferent, their meanings already decided by whoever had commissioned them.

After the function, in the corridor outside the hall, Iri paused beneath an arch carved with the bent singer. They put a gloved fingertip near the groove, not touching. The stone held a coldness that the warm air could not fully erase.

They heard their comm ping: a schedule reminder, a polite demand. *Rest period begins now. Curfew in effect. Escort available upon request.*

Iri looked down the corridor where the compound’s inner doors led to quarters and offices, and then toward a side passage where the stone blocks were less smooth. The side passage was not on the tour. It was not on the green part of the map.

They did not think, not in the words they would later use. They acted, and only afterward would they claim principle.

They walked.

At the first checkpoint a guard glanced up. The guard’s badge marked Lineage C. Their face was unreadable, or perhaps Iri simply did not know how to read it yet.

“Envoy,” the guard said. Not a question.

“I’m taking air,” Iri replied, and heard the absurdity of it—air was controlled here; taking it was not a right. They lifted their wrist and disabled the translation field’s external broadcast, leaving only their own reception. A protocol violation. A small one, the kind that seemed harmless until it became leverage.

The guard’s eyes followed the movement. For a moment Iri thought they would be stopped. Then the guard looked away, as if something behind Iri required attention. The gate clicked open.

Beyond the compound the settlement was not gray emptiness. It was sound.

Stone was still the primary material—the same basalt, the same carved lintels—but here it was patched with sheets of metal, with woven fiber, with plastic scavenged from crates. Walls leaned slightly. Doorways were hung with strips of cloth that moved in the wind like tongues. The air smelled of smoke, of cooking oil, of dust, of something sharp and green. People moved in narrow streets, stepping around drainage channels, calling to each other in voices that did not care whether they were intelligible to an envoy.

Iri’s boots crunched on stone grit. Their suit’s seal adjusted and complained. Without the compound’s polished air, their throat loosened a little. They breathed, and the breath tasted like life that had not been filtered for diplomacy.

They kept walking, letting the streets decide. Their map was useless; the approved green zones did not account for alleyways, for footpaths worn into stone by necessity. The buildings here had glyphs too, but not in official bands. They were scratched into doorframes, daubed in pigment, carved into reused blocks that might once have been part of the compound. Orpheus’s bent back appeared on the corner of a bakery, smaller, cruder, almost comic. Demeter’s grain-sheaf was cut into a water barrel, the grooves filled with blue paint that had chipped away in places.

A comm ping sounded at Iri’s wrist—insistent. They ignored it. The silence that followed was not peace; it was the awareness of being untracked. They felt, suddenly, very young. The training had not included what to do with that.

At the edge of a broader street, the market announced itself with density. Stalls clustered under awnings of patched fabric. The ground was packed dirt over stone. The air buzzed with bargaining.

A woman—Lineage B, if the packet’s physiognomic hints were to be trusted, though the hints were crude—held up a strip of woven fiber and argued with a man whose ears had been surgically altered into points, a body-mod the dossier had called “aesthetic deviation common among Lineage A artisans.” The man wore a belt heavy with tokens. He shook his head and said something that the translation field rendered as, “This is last-month weave. I won’t insult my children.”

Children, not clients. Kinship terms slid into economics like they belonged there.

At another stall, a boy sold air filters from a crate. He spoke rapidly, fingers flashing numbers. A customer—scarred, broad-shouldered—paid not with tokens but with a small vial of clear liquid. The boy held it up to the light, nodded, and handed over two filters and a third “for your mother.” The customer’s expression softened, just slightly.

A table nearby displayed seed stock in tiny sealed packets. The seller, an old person with white hair cropped close, watched buyers carefully and refused one without explanation. The refused buyer—tall, with a lineage marker stitched in red—did not argue. They simply stepped back, jaw tight, and moved on. Informal justice, enacted without gavel.

Iri drifted, listening. Their attention became appetite. Here, categories blurred. The packet’s neat columns dissolved into faces, gestures, roles. A person whose dossier lineage was “marginal” was clearly running a stall that everyone depended on: translation services, offered in exchange for food or tokens. The translator—a middle-aged person with a voice like stone rubbed smooth—sat on a low stool and mediated disputes between speakers of different dialects, their neutrality enforced by reputation alone.

Iri approached a stall where small carved stones were laid out like charms. Each was etched with a glyph: Orpheus, Demeter, Raven. They were crude, made quickly, meant to be handled. A young seller watched Iri with the wary interest reserved for outsiders with money.

“These are luck,” the seller said, and the translation field gave it as *blessing.* The seller’s eyes flicked to Iri’s suit, to the fabric that did not belong. “You want?”

Iri picked up a stone with the bent singer. The grooves were rough. It fit in their palm like a worry. “This one,” Iri said, “is—” They hesitated. “On Earth it was a story about going down to the dead to bring someone back.”

The seller blinked, the expression quick and unreadable. For a moment they looked younger, and then older. “Offworlder,” they said, not as insult, not as welcome. As diagnosis.

“I’m—yes,” Iri admitted. “Envoy.”

The seller’s mouth tightened. They reached out and took the stone back, gently but firmly. “Not for you,” they said, and turned their attention to another customer as if Iri had become invisible.

The sudden exclusion stung more than any overt hostility. It was a boundary drawn without ceremony.

Iri stepped away. Their hand felt empty, as if it had been holding not stone but permission.

At the market’s edge stood a communal cistern built from fitted basalt blocks. People queued with containers. The cistern’s front face was carved with a large glyph panel, more careful than the market charms, older perhaps, or simply made by someone with time. The bent singer dominated the center, but the details differed from the compound’s version. Here the figure’s mouth was closed. The loop in their hand was not a lyre; it was a ring, or a collar, held out as if offering it to someone outside the frame. Beneath, the flowing lines were not water; they were people, stylized bodies pressed together, a current of heads and hands.

Iri stood too close, forgetting to be discreet. The stone’s surface was worn smooth where fingers had traced the grooves.

“You read that?” a voice said beside them.

The speaker was a person about Iri’s age, perhaps a little older, with hair braided tight and eyes dark as wet stone. Their clothing was practical, stained with work. No obvious lineage marker. Their accent caught on Trade Standard like it was a borrowed tool.

“I recognize it,” Iri said. “Or I thought I did.”

The person glanced at the panel. “Offworld myth,” they said, with a tone that made it neither accusation nor admiration. “They teach you it means love goes into death and returns.”

“Something like that,” Iri admitted. They felt their cheeks warm. “It’s… about refusing to accept loss.”

The person’s mouth curved, not quite a smile. “That’s your meaning,” they said. “Here it’s about refusing to make someone else carry your loss.”

Iri swallowed. “I don’t understand.”

The person touched the stone lightly, fingers resting on the ring in the carved hand. “The singer goes down,” they said, “because the living send them. Because someone must bargain with the dead, with the cold systems, with the rules. The singer brings back what the living need—water rights, air quotas, permission to breed, permission to move.” Their voice stayed low, conversational, as if discussing weather. “And then the living look back and say, *You did it for yourself.* They put the collar on your neck and call it gratitude.”

Iri’s training offered no place to put this. They searched for a response that would not be insult.

“So the glyph,” Iri said slowly, “is a warning.”

“It’s a reminder,” the person corrected. “We mark the cistern with it so we don’t forget who pays when the council says *stability.*” They looked at Iri then, direct. “You came from the stone house. You drink sweet air. You have papers. You want to understand? Don’t start with stories. Start with who gets collared.”

Shame rose in Iri like heat, sharp and immediate. Fascination rose beside it, inseparable. They felt, in the same moment, the temptation to argue and the deeper knowledge that argument would be another way of protecting themselves.

“I didn’t know,” Iri said, and heard how thin it sounded.

“No,” the person agreed. “You didn’t.” Then, with a small kindness that felt like a knife being set down, they added, “Not your fault. Yet.”

They stepped away into the queue. Someone handed them a container, and they took it without ceremony. They were not important here. That, too, was instructive.

Iri stood for another moment, looking at the glyph until their eyes stopped seeing Orpheus and began seeing a local claim carved into stone: a policy argued through myth.

Their comm pinged again, harder. They turned away from the cistern and began walking back toward the compound, the market’s noise falling behind like a curtain.

At the compound gate the same guard stood. This time the guard did not look away.

“Envoy,” the guard said. “You are late.”
