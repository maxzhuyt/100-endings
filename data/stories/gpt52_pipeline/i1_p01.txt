I woke before the horn, because my body always woke before pain did. The straw beneath me had gone flat and sharp, and the wool blanket held last night’s sour sweat as if it meant to keep it for itself. In the half-dark I lay still and listened: the thin coughs of men who had learned to breathe around bruises, the shuffle of bare feet on packed earth, and beyond the ludus walls the first cart wheels rattling over stone like teeth chattering in the cold.

At the basin the water was already gray, a skin of oil floating where someone had washed before me. I dipped my hands and brought it to my face anyway. The iron tang from old cuts lived in the cracks of my knuckles no matter how much I scrubbed. I oiled my shoulders with a rationed smear of olive oil, rubbing at the purple bloom on my ribs, counting without meaning to: one, two, three—never four. Four was unlucky. It was stupid, but in a place where men died by the rules of other men, I kept my own small rule as if it could bargain with the gods. My left ear rang, a high thin sound that came and went, and I told myself it was only sleep leaving me.

The doors at the far end clanged and the corridor filled with the familiar smell of vinegar, sharp as a slap. A guard came through with a bucket, sloshing it along the stone where blood had dried into brown lace from yesterday’s drills. Behind him, the freedman clerk walked with his wax tablets hugged to his chest, stylus already scratching. Keys jingled at the guard’s belt, and leather straps creaked with each step, like a saddle settling on a horse.

“Line,” the guard barked, and we stood because standing was what you did when Rome asked.

The lanista did not come; he never wasted his breath on us when a messenger could do it. The clerk stopped where the light fell on his tablet and read without looking at our faces.

“Roster posted for the Kalends,” he said. The stylus ticked in the wax. “Three of you are requested for inspection by a patron. One is sold. Diodoros of Pergamon—sold to Capua. Contract sealed. He leaves at midday.”

A sound went through the room that was not a word. Diodoros did not move. He stared at the basin as if he might see his name floating there and refuse it.

The clerk went on, voice steady. “Also—grain lines riot again at the Porticus. The City Prefect wants quiet. The games must be… exemplary.” He paused, as if the word tasted better than it should. “Anyone who draws punishment before then will be fed less. Anyone who collapses in inspection will be fed less.”

Power always came as arithmetic: bodies counted, rations reduced, names pressed into wax. Outside, citizens shouted for bread. Inside, we were bread.

In the kitchen passage, I traded my place in line for a crust and a word. The kitchen slave, little Livia with her hair cut close like a boy’s, pushed a bowl of lentils toward me. The steam smelled of garlic and something old—garum that had seeped into the clay and would never leave.

“Diodoros is sold,” I said softly, so the guard at the end of the corridor would hear only the scrape of bowls.

“I heard,” she said. Her hands were red from hot water and ash. “Capua’s school takes men hard. Their doctores think scars are medals.”

“Scars are receipts,” I said, and she huffed a laugh that was almost dangerous.

A new recruit sat hunched on the bench near the door, chewing as if his jaw did not know what to do with food when no one was forcing it. His wrists still bore raw rope-burn. He looked up at me with eyes too wide for a man who had seen a slave market and lived.

“Do you get used to it?” he asked.

To what? To hunger, to orders, to the way your name became a thing others used? I watched Livia tuck a stray strand of hair behind her ear, the gesture of someone who could not afford softness. Freedom looked like her moving between pots without chains on her ankles—captivity looked like the way she flinched when a guard’s shadow crossed her.

“You get used to waking,” I said. “That’s not the same.”

Later, when the sun had climbed enough to turn the courtyard stones warm, they marched four of us through the outer gate. Not to the arena. To the medicus who served the school and the patron both, because a good investment was inspected like a horse. Our sandals slapped the wet stone of the corridor where the morning wash had run down in rivulets. Chalk dust from the training yard still clung to our calves, white on brown skin like ash.

The city hit me through the gate like breath. Incense drifted from a nearby shrine, sweet and heavy, and beneath it the fish market stank—rot and brine and guts thrown into the sun. Donkey dung steamed in the street. Someone’s perfume, sharp with myrrh, rode on the breeze and made my throat tighten with an old memory of my mother rubbing oil into my hair when I was still called by a name no one here knew.

We passed a wall where fresh notices were pasted, the glue still damp. A painted figure of the emperor smiled down, laurel on his brow, arm outstretched as if he were offering the city itself. Underneath, someone had scratched in charcoal: PANEM ET SANGUINEM—bread and blood. The letters were crude, but the meaning was clear. Across the way a grain line curled like a snake, citizens with hollow cheeks and loud voices pressed against the doors of the horrea. A soldier watched them with bored eyes, his hand resting on his sword not because he meant to use it, but because it reminded them that he could.

At the medicus’ doorway, a priest was tying ribbons to a small statue of Fortuna. The bells at the shrine nearby chimed, thin metal notes that seemed too delicate for a city built on conquest. The medicus’ room smelled of vinegar and crushed herbs, bitter and green. There were wet cloths hanging on a line, dripping steadily into a basin, and flies gathering in the corners where the light did not reach.

While we waited, I slipped my fingers beneath my tunic and found the little amulet I wore on a cord: a cold bit of bronze, a crude wheel. Fortuna, they said. A gift from a man who had died in the yard and pressed it into my palm with blood-slick fingers as he went down.

I touched it twice, then once more for the days I had stolen from death. My lips shaped words I would never speak aloud where guards listened.

“Not glory,” I whispered. “Not victory. Let me live long enough to choose.”

Choosing was a citizen’s luxury. Still, asking felt like a kind of theft, and I had grown practiced at theft.

Inside, the medicus ran his hands over my shoulders with professional indifference. His fingers found the knot near my collarbone where an old injury had never healed clean. He pressed. Pain flared bright, and with it the ringing in my ear surged until the room tilted. For a moment the vinegar smell sharpened into something metallic and I tasted copper, as if I had bitten my own tongue.

“Hold still,” he said. His breath smelled of cheap wine. He peered into my eye, lifted my lid with a thumb, as if looking for rot. “You’ve been sleeping poorly.”

“We all sleep poorly,” I said, and he gave a thin smile that meant nothing.

The door behind him opened and closed, and in the small silence that followed I heard muffled crying from the corridor—short, stifled, the sound of someone trying to make grief invisible.

Diodoros. They were taking him to be chained for the road.

I stepped out and saw him between two guards, wrists bound with leather that had been oiled to keep it from cracking. His face was dry, but his throat worked as if swallowing stones. His eyes flicked to mine, and in them was something worse than fear: the knowledge that his story was being moved like a coin from one hand to another.

“Pergamon,” I said, using the name the school used for him, the place that replaced his family. It was all I had to offer.

He tried to smile. It failed. A guard shoved him, and the leather straps creaked as he stumbled forward.

In the yard, near the gate, a nobleman’s messenger waited with a scroll case, clean sandals, and laurel tucked behind his ear as if the city crowned even its errands. He spoke to the lanista in a voice that did not lower itself, and we heard because we were meant to.

“The patron wants a display in his atrium before the Kalends,” he said. “Not a match. Just… demonstration. The ladies like to see the men oiled. The boys, too. It reminds them of virtue.”

Virtue. He said it like honey.

The lanista nodded, and his eyes slid over us as if choosing cuts of meat. “They will come,” he said.

The messenger smiled, laurel bright against his hair. “Good. The people are restless. The Prefect says the emperor’s image must be loved again. Let them chant names. Let them forget the price of bread.”

I looked at the wall where someone had scratched PANEM ET SANGUINEM, and I understood with a clarity that made my stomach go cold: I was not only a man who fought. I was a lesson. A lullaby. A warning wrapped in spectacle so the city could swallow it without choking.

They would oil me and parade me in a courtyard while citizens fought over grain. They would cheer my name as if it belonged to me.

Back in the barracks, the air was thick with sweat and old straw. Dust motes floated in a shaft of sunlight that cut across the room like a blade. Livia brought a loaf still warm from the ovens, wrapped in cloth, and broke it with her hands. The crust crackled, and the steam smelled of simple wheat and smoke, honest in a way little else was.

She sat beside me on the bench, close enough that our shoulders touched. Across the room the new recruit watched us as if trying to learn how to exist here without disappearing.

“Capua,” Livia said quietly. “Will they kill him there?”

“They’ll use him,” I said. “Like here. Just with different hands.”

I took my amulet out and set it on my palm. The bronze wheel caught the light, dull but steady. I could hear, faint as surf, a roar from somewhere in the city—men practicing chants, voices rising and falling as if rehearsing joy.

I tucked the amulet back under my tunic and tore off a piece of bread, the warmth soaking into my fingers. I held the other half out to the new recruit.

“Come,” I said. “Eat.”

I decided I would endure long enough to choose.
