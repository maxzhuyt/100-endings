The harvest moon hung low and swollen, as if it had been bled and left to clot in the sky. Its light made the thatch on the roofs look scorched even where flame hadn’t touched, made the cobbles shine like old bruises. The village should have been loud with dogs and late-season drunks, with shutters clapping in wind and gossip slipping from doorways.

Instead: doors gaped. A cradle lay overturned in a threshold. A cart’s axle had snapped and no one had bothered to right it. Chickens—if there had been chickens—had fled or been taken, leaving only a scatter of feathers stuck to something dark on a post.

Blood. Dry enough to flake under a boot, but fresh enough that the iron tang still lived in the air.

Alucard strolled down the main street as though it were his estate and the moon his lantern. His coat caught the red light and threw it back in richer crimson. A wide-brimmed hat shadowed his eyes, but the village felt the weight of his attention anyway—the way a throat feels teeth hover near it.

He paused by a chapel whose bell tower leaned a fraction to the left, as if trying to turn away. The door was ajar. An icon had been hacked in half and then burned; the face of the saint was gone, leaving only blackened gold leaf.

Something had eaten its way through faith here.

Alucard breathed in. The quiet was not peace. It was a held breath. The stones were waiting.

A ward lay over the village like a film of cold grease: not strong enough to stop him from entering, but thick enough to make the night feel… negotiated. As if something had made a bargain with the world and the world had agreed to look away for a time.

He smiled, showing nothing.

“Omen,” he murmured to the moon, and the word came out fond. “How generous.”

A seam opened at the far end of the street—not a tear the eye could see, but a sudden wrongness in the air, a pressure shift that made dust lift and hang. The scent reached him a heartbeat before the woman did: lilac and gooseberries, absurdly alive amid rot.

She appeared between one step and the next, as if the world had decided she belonged here and rewritten itself to accommodate her. Cloak fastened high, hair as dark as the chapel’s ash, posture controlled the way a blade was controlled. Violet eyes caught the moon and held it without flinching.

Yennefer of Vengerberg took in the village in a single sweep: the empty windows, the blood, the absence of bodies. Her gaze lingered on the broken icon—not reverent, not mocking, merely assessing the kind of mind that would do such a thing.

Then her eyes found Alucard.

For a fraction of a second, the ward’s pressure changed, as if it recognized a new player.

Alucard lifted two fingers to the brim of his hat. “Good evening.”

Her lip curled, small and precise. “Is it?”

“You’re here,” he said, as though that settled the matter. “So it has improved.”

She didn’t move closer; she didn’t move away. “You don’t smell like a man.”

“Do I smell like a disappointment?” he asked lightly. “That would be tragic.”

Her gaze sharpened, violet turning to a blade edge. “You smell like a mistake someone made on purpose.”

The moonlight painted her cheekbones with blood-red geometry. Her hands were bare. No rings. No ostentatious talismans. Power contained, not displayed.

Alucard’s smile deepened. “And you smell like trouble.”

“I’m looking for answers,” she said. “This village was meant to be under Temerian protection. Two weeks ago, an envoy came through. He didn’t return. Then a rumor followed him—something about a relic. Something about a moon that makes vows… binding.”

“Ah,” Alucard said, glancing up at the swollen red disc. “A romantic night, then.”

“Don’t be crude,” she said.

He tilted his head, mock-innocent. “I haven’t been. Yet.”

They regarded each other in the middle of the street, the village framing them like a stage set abandoned by its actors. Wind stirred a scrap of cloth around a well. Somewhere, something scraped—stone on stone, faint as a remembered threat.

Yennefer’s eyes flicked toward the chapel. “You’ve been inside?”

“Not yet.” He turned slightly, offering her the door as though escorting her to a banquet. “Ladies first.”

“I’m no lady,” she said, and stepped toward the chapel anyway.

Inside, the air was colder, as if the building kept night stored in its walls. Pews sat skewed, splintered. Candles had melted down to stumps, then been snapped. The altar cloth was stained in a pattern that was too deliberate to be accident.

There were symbols cut into the stone—circles and hooks, a script that made the eyes slide away when they tried to read it.

Yennefer approached, careful. Her shadow crawled across the marks and seemed to snag.

Alucard leaned against a pillar, entirely at ease in desecration. “May I ask what you are, my dear?”

She didn’t look at him. “No.”

He chuckled. “Honest. How refreshing.”

Her fingers hovered over the carved circle without touching it. “May I ask what you are?”

He sighed as though inconvenienced. “Apparently, in a world that insists on naming everything, I am always asked to explain myself.”

“And?” she pressed.

His eyes glinted under his hat. “A traveler.”

“A corpse that learned to walk.”

“An improvement on many living men,” he replied.

Yennefer’s mouth twitched with something that wasn’t quite amusement. “If you’re here to feed, you chose a poor table.”

“I didn’t come for livestock.” He let his gaze travel over her, unhurried, impolite in its calm. “I came because the air tastes like an old crime.”

Yennefer’s eyes narrowed. “You can taste magic.”

“I can taste intent,” he corrected. “Tonight reeks of it.”

The scrape came again, closer. Then a wet sound, like mud being pulled from a boot.

Yennefer’s head snapped toward the aisle. Alucard’s posture didn’t change, but the air around him tightened, as if his stillness were a coiled spring.

From the shadows behind the last pew, a hand appeared—grey, swollen, fingertips blackened. It gripped the wood and hauled something upright with a slow, stubborn insistence. A man’s body, half-rotted, eyes filmed over. His mouth hung open, and inside it something dark moved.

Behind him, another. And another. Not clumsy reanimated dead, but corpses guided by a will that knew how to use joints, how to balance, how to hunt.

A ward snapped into place with an audible click, like a lock turning. Yennefer felt it in her teeth. Alucard felt it in the way the air thickened against his skin.

The chapel doors slammed shut.

“Well,” Alucard said mildly. “That answers one question.”

Yennefer raised a hand. No words, no dramatic gesture. Just a small twist of wrist as if turning a key.

The nearest corpse froze mid-step. Its spine arched, then bowed as invisible pressure forced it to its knees. Bones creaked. Its jaw cracked as it tried to resist, and the sound was obscene.

Yennefer’s eyes didn’t soften. “Stay down.”

The corpse shuddered and obeyed.

The others surged forward, faster now, compelled by the same will. One had a knife still lodged in its ribs; it dragged the blade free with a motion that suggested memory.

Alucard moved. He didn’t lunge; he simply appeared in front of the first corpse and drove his fist through its sternum with an almost bored efficiency. Ribs snapped like dry twigs. He pulled his hand back, and the corpse collapsed, boneless.

Black threads spilled from the wound, writhing like worms exposed to light.

Alucard’s eyes sharpened. “Interesting.”

A corpse swung at him with the knife. He let it cut him. The blade sank into his shoulder. Blood darkened his coat and then, beneath the cloth, the wound began to knit, flesh pulling together as though insulted.

The corpse stared, mouth working soundlessly.

Alucard smiled at it. “That won’t do.”

He seized its wrist, twisted until bone cracked, and tore the arm free. The body toppled. The severed hand still clutched the knife, twitching.

Yennefer’s magic flared—not fire, not lightning, but control made visible. A net of pale light spun out from her fingers and snapped around the remaining dead like a noose cast wide. They jerked as if yanked by the throat.

Something in the carved circle on the floor pulsed in response.

The ward tightened. The air pressed inward, squeezing. Even Alucard’s coat seemed to cling closer.

Yennefer’s gaze flicked to the symbols. “It’s a binding circle. It’s feeding on violence.”

Alucard’s eyes tracked the black threads squirming from the corpse’s chest. “And on blood,” he said. “Always blood.”

A whisper crawled along the chapel walls—too many voices layered, speaking in a language that made the mind itch. The symbols on the floor glowed a dull red, matching the moon’s sick light through the stained glass.

Yennefer’s jaw tightened. “This isn’t a simple necromancy. It’s a rite.”

“A rite with teeth,” Alucard agreed.

The dead convulsed in her net, and the black threads in their wounds began to crawl toward the circle, drawn like iron filings to a magnet.

Yennefer looked up sharply. “If it completes—”

“—the circle closes,” Alucard finished, “and then we are both guests who cannot leave.”

He sounded almost amused. Almost.

Yennefer’s eyes narrowed, measuring. “You knew something like this could happen.”

“I hoped,” he corrected softly. “Tonight is rare.”

She felt the hunger behind his tone—not for flesh, but for the possibility of consequence. Of an end that would not be boredom.

She hated that she understood it.

The ward pressed again, a reminder.

Yennefer released the net and snapped her fingers. The corpses dropped—not free, but abruptly disconnected. For a heartbeat they lay still, puppets with cut strings.

Then the circle flared, angry at being denied.

A pulse of force shoved through the chapel, rattling wood and stone. The stained glass shivered. The corpses jerked and began to rise again, more frantic now.

Yennefer’s eyes went distant, calculating. “We need the source,” she said. “Not these—these are just fuel.”

Alucard tilted his head. “And what do you want from the source?”

Her gaze flicked to him. “You talk too much.”

“It’s a terrible habit,” he said. “I keep expecting honest answers.”

Yennefer’s mouth tightened. “There’s a relic rumored to be here. Something ancient. Something tied to fertility rites—before the Church stole and rebranded them. If the blood moon amplifies binding magic…” She stopped, as if realizing she’d given him a thread.

Alucard’s smile was slow. “Ah. So that’s your hunger.”

Her eyes flashed. “Don’t.”

He lifted both hands in theatrical surrender. “Merely observing. You want what was taken from you. You want the world to give back.”

Yennefer’s voice went cold enough to frost stone. “And you want what?”

Alucard looked up at the red moon through shattered glass. For once, his amusement thinned. “To see if anything can still bite.”

The ward creaked like a ship under strain.

Yennefer turned back to the circle. “We cooperate,” she said, each word clipped, “until we break the mechanism. Then you leave.”

“And you take your relic,” he said.

“If it exists.”

Alucard stepped closer. The air between them felt charged, not with heat but with potential harm. “Terms,” he purred. “I do adore contracts.”

Yennefer met his gaze without blinking. “No feeding on me.”

He smiled as if she’d offered him a gift. “You assume I would ask.”

“I assume nothing. I plan for everything.”

“Then plan for this,” Alucard said, and with a sudden violence that was almost polite, he seized one of the corpses by the throat and dragged it over the glowing circle.

Its rotten skin hissed as the symbols licked at it. Black threads poured out faster, feeding the pattern. The circle brightened, greedy.

Yennefer’s eyes widened in fury. “Are you insane?”

“Impatient,” he corrected. “You wanted the source. This is how we open the door.”

The circle pulsed, and the stone beneath it cracked, splitting like a mouth.

Cold air rushed up from below, carrying the stink of old earth and something sweeter—like rot dressed in perfume.

A stairway yawned where there had been solid floor.

The corpses all turned their heads toward it in unison, as if listening to a call.

Yennefer’s nostrils flared. “You just fed it.”

“I gave it a taste,” Alucard said. “Now it wants the meal.”

The ward around the chapel tightened once more—then shifted, expanding. The village’s trap was no longer just a chapel. It was a net cast wider.

Yennefer didn’t waste breath on curses. She moved, cloak sweeping, stepping toward the open stairs. “Stay close,” she said, and hated that she had to.

Alucard fell into step beside her as if they were partners in a dance. “As you command.”

They descended.

The undercroft was older than the chapel above it. The stone here was rough, damp, and carved with marks that predated any saint. The air tasted metallic, and with every step the pressure in Yennefer’s skull increased, as if the magic below was a drumbeat approaching.

The red moon’s light found them anyway, bleeding through cracks in the earth as though the world itself had veins.

At the bottom, the space opened into a cistern-like chamber. Water should have been here once. Now there was only a shallow pool of dark liquid that reflected the moon in a trembling oval.

In the center stood an altar of unpolished stone. Upon it rested an object wrapped in cloth so old it had become part of itself.

Around the altar, a circle had been etched into the floor—not the chapel’s crude imitation, but the original. The lines were inlaid with something that shimmered faintly: silver, or bone, or both.

And in the circle, suspended like a spider at the center of its web, hung a sigil made of blood that did not drip. It hovered, pulsing in time with the moon.

Yennefer’s skin prickled. Her magic responded, eager and wary.

Alucard inhaled. “There,” he whispered. “That’s the heart.”

A voice rose from the far side of the chamber—human, strained. “Don’t touch it.”

A man stepped into view, trembling, dressed in a priest’s robes stained with soot and old blood. His eyes were too bright, fevered. In his hands he held a reliquary chain and a dagger.

Behind him, half-hidden in the shadows, were two more figures—witch-hunters, crossbows raised, faces hard with fear and righteousness.

“You,” the priest spat at Alucard, seeing the coat, the posture, the wrongness. “Devil.”

Alucard’s smile returned, sharp. “I’ve been called worse by better men.”

The priest’s gaze snapped to Yennefer, then lingered on her eyes, her poise. “Sorceress. Of course.”

Yennefer’s patience thinned to a wire. “What have you done?”

The priest lifted the dagger. His hands shook. “What had to be done. The village was corrupt. The moon showed me. The relic—” His eyes flicked toward the wrapped object with desperate reverence. “It answers blood with blessing. A womb opened. A field restored. A vow made unbreakable.”

Yennefer’s throat tightened, involuntarily. The words struck too close to a wound she kept stitched shut.

Alucard noticed. Of course he did.

The priest’s voice rose. “But the blessing demands sacrifice. And tonight—tonight the blood moon makes the covenant complete. The ward will hold. The devil will be trapped. The witch will be bound. And God will—”

Yennefer’s laugh was small and cruel. “God isn’t here.”

She stepped forward, and the air around her thickened with power. The witch-hunters flinched, fingers tightening on triggers.

Alucard lifted a hand slightly, as if warning them: if you shoot, you will not like what happens.

The priest saw only enemies. He slashed the dagger across his palm and flung the blood toward the hovering sigil.

The blood didn’t splatter. It rose, drawn into the sigil like ink into paper. The lines brightened, turning vivid crimson.

The chamber shuddered. The pool rippled. The circle on the floor flared, and the silver inlay began to glow.

A pressure slammed down. Yennefer’s knees threatened to buckle. Alucard’s coat snapped as if in wind.

The ward had teeth now.

From the stairway above, the scraping sound returned—corpses descending, drawn toward the heart.

Yennefer’s voice came out tight. “The relic is powering the ward.”

“And the ward is powering the relic,” Alucard said softly, as though admiring the elegance. “A lovely loop.”

The priest thrust the dagger out, pointing at Alucard. “Blood of the damned,” he whispered, and his eyes rolled back as he began to chant.

The sigil pulsed. Threads of red light shot from it and snapped toward Alucard like leashes.

Alucard moved fast, but the magic was faster. The threads wrapped around his wrists, his throat. They burned where they touched, not like fire but like holy insistence.

His smile didn’t falter. His eyes, however, went very still.

Yennefer felt the spell’s structure—binding magic amplified by the moon, keyed to blood. It wasn’t Church magic, not truly. It was older, stolen, weaponized.

The priest had made himself the anchor.

Yennefer could cut the threads. She could also—if she dared—use them.

Alucard’s gaze flicked to her, quick as a knife. Not pleading. Testing.

Do it, it said. Or don’t.
