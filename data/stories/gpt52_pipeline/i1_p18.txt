Sisyphus had learned the exact angle where the stone stopped pretending to be helpful.

The slope there was steep enough to remind it of its duties, and shallow enough not to punish him for a moment’s inattention. He leaned in with his shoulder, palms spread, fingers finding old pits and familiar ridges. His sandals—mended twice and resoled with something that might once have been a priest’s belt—scraped for purchase.

He breathed in, pushed on the exhale, and let the rhythm do what it always did: make the act small enough to live inside.

A black shape cut across the pale sky.

It landed on the boulder with a thump that suggested either confidence or a complete misunderstanding of gravity. A crow adjusted its feet, looked down at the stone as if appraising property, and dropped a bundle of letters tied with twine onto Sisyphus’s forearm.

“Post,” said the crow.

Sisyphus didn’t stop mid-push. He finished the current stretch, nudged the boulder into the shallow notch that served as his rest spot, and let it settle with a disgruntled little grind. Only then did he sit on a flat slab of rock that warmed quickly in the sun and accept the bundle.

“Thank you,” he said.

“I’m not doing it for you,” said the crow.

“No,” said Sisyphus. “You’re doing it for the dramatic entrance.”

The crow fluffed its feathers. “Someone has to provide atmosphere. Also, you’re crushing my toes.”

Sisyphus shifted his hand politely off the crow’s perch. The bird hopped to his shoulder with the air of a clerk taking up their rightful station.

He untied the twine with the careful patience of someone whose hands had learned not to rush. The letters were of varying sizes, folded in different ways, some sealed with wax, some with string, one with what looked like bread pressed into an attempt at a stamp.

“Before we begin,” Sisyphus said, squinting at the top envelope, “what are we calling this today?”

The crow cleared its throat.

“*THE BOULDERING HEART: COUNSEL FROM THE CRAG OF CONSEQUENCE, AS DICTATED BY SISYPHUS, WHO KNOWS YOUR PAIN AND IS LEGALLY OBLIGED TO—*”

“Just—” Sisyphus held up a hand. “Just ‘Advice from Sisyphus.’”

The crow clicked its beak. “No one reads plain titles.”

“They do if they need help,” Sisyphus said, and tapped the stack. “Besides, you make up for it in your… notes.”

“My notes are essential context.”

“They’re punctuation with opinions.”

“They are editorial interventions,” said the crow loftily. “Also, we need a disclaimer.”

“No disclaimers.”

“Anonymity? At least allow pseudonyms. Some of these are—” the crow peered at the top letter, “—mortifying.”

Sisyphus slid a finger under the seal. “They wrote to a man pushing a rock up a mountain for eternity. I think they’ve accepted vulnerability.”

“I haven’t,” said the crow.

Sisyphus unfolded the first letter, smoothed it against his knee, and dictated, because dictation made it feel like work and work made it manageable.

“Dear Sisyphus,” he began.

“Honorific?” said the crow. “Perhaps ‘Most Enduring.’ Or ‘Your Rolling Eminence.’”

“Just ‘Sisyphus,’” Sisyphus said, and continued.

---

**DEAR SISYPHUS,**

**My partner never listens. Or—he listens and then he fixes. I try to tell him I’m upset about something and he starts giving me solutions and it makes me feel like I’m a problem to be solved. When I say that, he says he’s trying to help and that I’m being unfair. Then I feel guilty and stop talking.**

**How do I get him to hear me without a fight?**

**—STUCK IN THE SAME CONVERSATION**

---

The crow made a small gagging noise. “Romance,” it said, like the word had feathers stuck in it.

Sisyphus folded the letter once, not as an ending but as a way to hold it. “He probably is trying to help,” he said. “And you’re probably trying to be heard. Those don’t have to be enemies.”

“People are always making their feelings other people’s homework,” said the crow.

Sisyphus looked at the mountain for a moment as if taking its measure, then at the sky, and then back at the paper. “Tell them this,” he said, and the crow’s head tilted, ready to catch the words.

---

**DEAR STUCK,**

**Pick a time when you’re both fed and not already angry. Not during the moment. Not as you’re walking out the door. Say:**

**‘I’m not asking you to fix this. I’m asking you to be with me in it for a minute.’**

**Then give him a job that is not fixing. For example:**

**‘Can you tell me what you think I’m feeling right now?’**

**If he starts offering solutions anyway, don’t argue about whether his intentions are good. That turns it into a trial. Just repeat your request, once:**

**‘Thank you. Not yet. Listening first.’**

**If he can’t do that, set one boundary that protects you:**

**‘If we can’t have this conversation without you trying to solve me, I’m going to stop and we can try again tomorrow.’**

**And then actually stop. Not as a punishment. As practice.**

**—SISYPHUS**

---

The crow scribbled with a claw onto a slate it produced from nowhere in particular. It paused, looked up, and said, “Should we add a note that if he insists on fixing, he can fix the roof?”

Sisyphus smiled without showing teeth. “We can let the writer think that on their own.”

“Also,” the crow added, “you used ‘tomorrow.’”

“Yes.”

“You’re not supposed to have those.”

“I’ve had plenty,” Sisyphus said. “They just look the same.”

The stone behind him gave a low, ominous creak, as if resentful of being talked about.

Sisyphus patted it once, the way one might pat a stubborn mule. “All right,” he said, standing. “Hold that thought.”

The boulder chose that moment to remember its nature. It shifted a fraction, then another, and then the notch stopped being a rest spot and became a suggestion. The rumble deepened, a familiar complaint through the mountain’s bones.

Sisyphus stepped into it as if stepping into weather. Hands to stone, breath in, he leaned and began again.

The crow, affronted by the movement, lifted into the air with a sharp flap, then circled and landed on his shoulder again, indignant at having been displaced by physics.

“I will read,” it said, “because you have decided your office is mobile.”

Sisyphus pushed. The stone dragged at his sandals. He answered between breaths.

---

“Next,” said the crow, flipping the bundle with its beak. “This one has a stain that implies tears or soup.”

“Probably both,” Sisyphus said.

The crow read, and Sisyphus listened to the words as if they were footholds.

---

**DEAR SISYPHUS,**

**I can’t keep up. My days are meetings, and my nights are cleaning, and my weekends are family obligations that somehow take more energy than the work. I’m always behind. I tell myself I’ll rest when things calm down, but they never do.**

**My manager, STEVE THE IMMUTABLE, says we’re “all in the same boat,” but he doesn’t seem to be rowing. If I ask for less, I’ll look ungrateful. If I keep going, I’m going to fall over and not get up.**

**How do you do the same thing every day without disappearing?**

**—DROWNING POLITELY**

---

Sisyphus exhaled hard through his nose, a laugh that didn’t waste breath. “Steve the Immutable,” he repeated. “Ambitious name for a man who will change his mind the moment someone above him does.”

“Bosses,” said the crow, “are invented to make the gods feel better about their own management style.”

Sisyphus leaned into the stone. His shoulder burned in a familiar stripe. “Tell them to stop waiting for calm,” he said. “Calm is a visitor. Rest has to be a resident.”

The crow angled the slate closer. “Make it actionable,” it said, using the tone of someone who had just learned the word and planned to punish everyone with it.

Sisyphus nodded, because the crow was often intolerable and occasionally right. He spoke in short lines, the way you speak when your body has claimed most of your attention.

---

**DEAR DROWNING,**

**Make a list of everything you do in a week. Don’t judge it. Just write it. Work, errands, family, the things you do because no one else will.**

**Then mark three things:**

**1) One obligation you truly cannot drop this week. (Not “shouldn’t.” Cannot.)**  
**2) One non‑negotiable rest you will take even if the sky falls. (Thirty minutes counts. Put it in your day like a meeting.)**  
**3) One small joy that costs little and restores something. (A walk. A hot drink. Sitting where you can see the open air.)**

**Then choose one conversation to have. Not ten. One. With Steve, or with a housemate, or with your family. Use simple words:**

**‘I can do X and Y. I cannot do Z without breaking. Which do you want done well?’**

**Try it as an experiment for one week. Not forever. One week. Collect information. Adjust.**

**And if Steve says you’re all in the same boat, ask him—politely—who is steering.**

**—SISYPHUS**

---

The crow made a pleased sound. “You implied mutiny.”

“I implied clarity,” said Sisyphus.

“They will hear mutiny,” said the crow, approving.

The boulder shifted again, not rolling back—never that dramatic, not in the middle of his breath—but sliding just enough to steal an inch of progress and demand another. Sisyphus grunted, dug in, and continued.

“You’re bleeding,” said the crow, peering at his knuckles.

“Just the mountain taking its payment,” Sisyphus said.

“It should invoice.”

“It would,” Sisyphus said, “if it could write.”

The crow sniffed. “I can write. We could formalize this.”

“No,” said Sisyphus, and pushed.

At last he reached the rest notch again—less a destination than a negotiated pause. He set the boulder into it with the care of someone setting down an argument.

He sat. His hands were gritty with stone dust and a small, dark smear of blood. He wiped them on his trousers and reached for the next letter.

The crow was already rifling. “Ah,” it said, with relish. “A difficult person.”

“That could be any of them,” Sisyphus said.

“This one uses three underlines,” said the crow. “And the handwriting is angry.”

Sisyphus unfolded it.

---

**DEAR SISYPHUS,**

**There’s a man at my work who says whatever he wants and calls it “being honest.” He makes little comments about my clothes, my food, my competence. If I respond, he smirks and says I’m “too sensitive.” If I ignore him, he gets louder. If I go to our supervisor, she says, “That’s just how he is.”**

**Yesterday I said I didn’t appreciate his tone and he said, “Oh, here we go, the feelings police.” Everyone laughed. I smiled so I wouldn’t cry. Then I went home and couldn’t sleep because I kept imagining what I should have said.**

**What do you do when someone keeps poking you like it’s their hobby?**

**—TIRED OF BEING A TARGET**

---

The crow’s feathers rose. “Peck his eyes out,” it said, as if offering a reasonable office solution.

Sisyphus read the letter again, slower, as if giving the writer the respect of full attention. “They’re exhausted,” he said.

“They’re polite,” said the crow, disgusted. “It’s a disease.”

“It’s a survival skill,” Sisyphus corrected. Then, gently, “It can become a cage.”

He set the letter down beside him like something fragile and began to dictate, voice calm enough to be a hand on the shoulder.

---

**DEAR TIRED,**

**He is not honest. He is performing. The smirk gives it away.**

**You cannot argue someone out of enjoying your discomfort. Don’t debate whether you’re “too sensitive.” That’s his game and it has no winning.**

**Instead, name the behavior and exit. Simple, plain, repeatable:**

**‘Don’t comment on my body.’**  
**‘Don’t speak to me like that.’**  
**‘I’m not discussing this.’**

**Then: stop talking. Turn away. Go back to your work. Leave the room if you can. The point is not to convince him. The point is to remove your attention, which is what he’s feeding on.**

**If your supervisor says, “That’s just how he is,” you can say:**

**‘Then this is how I am: I won’t work while being mocked.’**

**You are not the feelings police. You’re the boundary you needed someone else to set.**

**And when you go home, if your mind replays it, remind yourself: you’re not failing because you didn’t deliver the perfect line. You’re tired because you’ve been carrying something that isn’t yours.**

**—SISYPHUS**

---

The crow stared at the slate as it copied. “The line about ‘this is how I am,’” it said slowly. “That’s… sharp.”

Sisyphus nodded once. “It needs to be.”

“We could still peck him,” the crow said, as if unwilling to abandon a hobby.

“If you must,” Sisyphus said, “peck him on company time.”

The crow let out a sound that might have been laughter or indigestion.

Behind them, the boulder made its familiar decision. Not a dramatic tumble—just a gradual betrayal, a subtle slide that said: *your pause is up.*

Sisyphus stood, brushed grit from his palms, and set himself to it again.

“While we’re here,” the crow said, taking to the air so it could keep pace and still look down its beak, “you haven’t used a single metaphor.”

“Good,” Sisyphus said, pushing.

“You know,” the crow said, “most advice-givers love a metaphor. It makes them feel wise without being specific.”

Sisyphus’s breath came in measured pulls. The stone was heavier at the start, always. “Metaphors are fine,” he said. “They just shouldn’t be used to avoid the work.”

The crow dipped forward, reading the next envelope as if it were an indictment. “This one is about purpose,” it announced. “Big feelings. Dramatic paper. Smells faintly of incense and panic.”

Sisyphus took a step, then another. The mountain’s grit bit at his sandals.

“I suppose,” he said, almost to himself, “don’t try to push today’s problem up tomorrow’s hill—”

The crow shrieked, scandalized. “HE SAID IT.”

Sisyphus tightened his grip on the stone, cheeks warming despite himself. “All right,” he said. “Yes. Fine. Once. Let it go.”

“I will not,” said the crow, delighted. “I will write it in capitals.”

“Don’t,” Sisyphus said.

“I already am,” the crow said, smug.

Sisyphus sighed and pushed until the boulder agreed to pause again. He sat, because sitting was permitted and because his legs asked for it in a language he’d learned not to ignore.

“Read it,” he said.

The crow, still riding the joy of catching him in symbolism, unfolded the next letter with unnecessary flourish.

---

**DEAR SISYPHUS,**

**I’m wasting my life. I see people younger than me doing great things. They have callings. They have paths. They wake up knowing what they are for.**

**I wake up and feel like I’m rehearsing. Like I’m always about to start, and then the day ends and I’ve done nothing that matters. I know it sounds selfish because I have food and shelter and people who care, but I can’t shake the feeling that I’m failing at being a person.**

**Everyone says “follow your purpose” like it’s a map. I don’t have a map. I have fog.**

**What do you do when your life feels pointless?**

**—AUDITIONING FOR AN EMPTY THEATRE**

---

The crow sniffed. “Imaginary audience,” it muttered. “They always pay in anxiety.”

Sisyphus rested his hands on his knees. His palms were rough, the lines filled with stone dust like old ink. “They’re not selfish,” he said. “They’re scared.”

“Scared of what?” said the crow.

“Of spending their days,” Sisyphus said, “and finding out they didn’t count.”

The crow’s slate hovered, waiting.

Sisyphus spoke carefully, not because the words were precious, but because he wanted them to be usable.

---

**DEAR AUDITIONING,**

**A purpose is not usually a map. It’s a practice. You don’t discover it once and then live correctly forever. You choose it again, in small ways, when you wake up.**

**For one week, pick one value. Not a grand destiny. A value you can touch. Examples: kindness, craft, honesty, patience, courage, learning.**

**Then do one small act each day that matches it. Small enough that you cannot fail by being human.**

**If your value is learning: read ten pages and take one note.**  
**If it is kindness: send one message that is simply true and kind.**  
**If it is craft: make one thing a little better than it was.**

**And stop auditioning your life for an imaginary audience. They never clap, because they aren’t real.**

**Start where your feet are. That is the only place you can start.**

**—SISYP
