The neon jukebox in the corner has one job and it’s failing on purpose.

A scratchy rock track—guitars like rusted wire, drums like a fist on a hollow door—clicks back to the beginning the moment it ends. The same half-second of silence, the same pop, the same opening riff. Nobody complains. Nobody looks at it too long. They drink like the loop is weather.

Spike Spiegel sits half-slouched at the bar with an empty plate in front of him and a cigarette that keeps finding his mouth as if it lives there. Ash drops into the tray without him looking. His eyes are loose-lidded, lazy in the way a cat is lazy—muscles still ready under the fur.

Revy has already claimed a booth with her back to the wall. Bottle in hand. Boots up on the opposite seat. She watches the front door, the back room curtain, the bathroom hallway, and the windows that show nothing but rain-smeared neon. When someone laughs too loud, her gaze doesn’t flicker. When the bartender bends to grab a glass, she tracks the movement like it’s a draw.

The bar is a rectangle of bad decisions: entrance on the left, a narrow strip of stools along the counter, two booths, a dartboard no one uses, a back room behind a curtain that looks like it’s been washed in nicotine. Bathroom door at the end of a short hallway. Two windows facing the street, one cracked at the corner and taped. The jukebox sits where it can see everyone.

The song ends.

Pop.

The song begins again.

Spike exhales smoke toward the ceiling fan that doesn’t spin. His chair is tilted back on two legs, balanced without effort. He looks like he could fall asleep in a firefight just to make a point.

Revy takes a pull from her bottle and doesn’t swallow right away. Her eyes are too sharp for this place. Her hands are too steady. That’s how Spike clocks her—hands, not face. The face comes later. The face is all angles and boredom and a mouth that looks like it’s had to learn to grin without meaning it.

She clocks him too. Not the suit, not the hair, not the cigarette. The weight distribution. The way he’s positioned so his right hand can go anywhere fast. The way his left eye seems to measure distance with a faint delay, like it’s not getting all the information at once.

Professionals recognize professionals the way dogs recognize storms.

The bartender slides another cheap glass down the counter. “News is on,” he says to nobody, and taps the dusty TV bolted above the shelves.

A local bulletin runs grainy footage of a man in a white suit stepping out of a limousine. The caption flashes his name: LEO FENNER. Under it: ARSON, EXTORTION, TWO COUNTS MURDER. WANTED. REWARD: 6,000,000.

A still photo replaces the footage. Fenner’s face is smooth, smug, too well-fed for a man who makes money burning other people’s lives down.

Spike’s cigarette pauses at his lips. A fraction of a beat, like even he can’t pretend not to care about that number.

In the booth, Revy’s mouth curls. Not a smile. Teeth, just enough to show them. Her eyes leave the exits long enough to pin the screen, then flick back to Spike as if the TV is merely an excuse.

Spike lifts his glass—empty—at the bartender. “Refill.”

The bartender doesn’t move. He’s watching the TV like it might bite him. “That guy’s trouble,” he mutters.

“No kidding,” Revy says, loud enough to carry. Her English is sharp-edged, vowels clipped. “Six million trouble.”

Spike turns his head without turning his body. His gaze drifts to her booth like it was always headed there. “You here for the music?”

Revy’s laugh is one bark, joyless. “Yeah. I love it when a song dies and comes back as a zombie.”

Pop.

The riff starts over.

Spike’s lips twitch. “Whatever keeps the dead moving.”

Revy swings her boots off the seat and stands. She carries her bottle like it’s a prop and her body like it’s a warning. She walks to the bar with the loose confidence of someone who’s never apologized for taking up space.

She stops one stool away from Spike. Close enough to smell his cigarette. Close enough to see he’s relaxed on purpose.

“You look like you don’t give a damn,” she says, taking his measure the way a hand checks a knife’s edge. “That an act, or you really that stupid?”

Spike exhales. Smoke drifts between them like a curtain. “Depends who’s asking.”

“I’m asking.” Her eyes flick down to his hands, then back up. “And I’m not in a patient mood.”

Spike angles his head. “Funny. You look like the kind of person who only has one mood.”

Revy leans an elbow on the bar, invading his space without permission. “You mean the mood where I put two holes in somebody’s chest?”

He meets her stare with tired amusement. “I mean the mood where you’re waiting for an excuse.”

She takes a sip from her bottle and wipes her mouth with the back of her hand, deliberate. “You got one?”

Spike shrugs, slow. “I’m just here.”

Revy’s eyes narrow, recognizing the line and hating it. “Yeah? I’m just here too.”

The bartender sets a fresh glass near Spike and tries to slide away. Spike’s hand comes up, not fast, and catches the glass. He doesn’t drink. He watches Revy over the rim.

The jukebox track ends.

Pop.

Again.

Revy’s gaze flicks to the door as it opens. A man steps inside, rain dripping off his coat. Not Fenner. Too young, too nervous. He scans the room, spots the bartender, and makes a beeline for the back curtain.

Spike sees it the way he sees everything: the slight bulge under the man’s coat at the hip, the way his shoulders are held too tight, the fear-sweat under the rain.

Revy sees it too. Her fingers flex once, like she’s checking she still has them.

The young man reaches the curtain—

—and a second man follows him in, heavier, older, eyes flat. His hand goes inside his jacket before the door closes behind him.

Spike’s chair drops to all four legs with a soft thump.

Revy’s bottle lowers.

The older man’s gaze catches on Spike’s face, slides to Revy, and hardens. Like recognition, or a plan deciding itself.

He draws.

Time clicks.

The jukebox doesn’t notice. The riff keeps playing.

A bottle shatters when a shot takes it midair—glass and whiskey explode across the bar. People scream. Chairs scrape. Someone ducks under a table like it’s salvation.

Spike moves first, not toward his gun—toward the shooter. His body unfolds from slouch to strike with terrifying economy. He steps into the line of fire where a sane man wouldn’t, because his timing is better than sane.

Revy’s guns are in her hands like they were always there. Twin pistols, black and ugly, barking in sync. Her shots are loud punctuation. The older man jerks back as wood splinters at his side; she’s not trying to kill him yet. She’s making him dance.

Spike’s foot hooks a stool and kicks it forward. The stool skids into the older man’s knees. Spike’s elbow follows, snapping into the man’s wrist. The gun clatters, lost under the music like an off-beat.

From the back room curtain, the young man—runner, muscle, whatever—stumbles out with his own gun, eyes wide. He points it toward the bartender like that’s the point of this whole mess.

Revy’s head turns, fast. One pistol swings toward him.

Spike is already there. He pivots, slips between line and target, grabs the young man’s arm, and uses it like a lever. The gun discharges into the ceiling. Dust rains down. Spike’s knee drives into the man’s stomach; the man folds.

“Don’t shoot the bartender,” Spike says, almost conversational, as if it’s rude.

Revy’s gaze flicks over Spike, assessing the move, the timing, the restraint. She hates that she respects it.

The older man lunges for the fallen gun. Revy shoots the floor in front of his hand—splinters leap up and cut his fingers. He howls.

Outside, sirens start somewhere far away and begin crawling closer.

The bartender screams something incoherent and dives behind the counter.

Spike grabs the older man by the collar and slams him into the bar hard enough to rattle bottles. “Where’s Fenner?” he asks, voice low, cigarette still stuck at the corner of his mouth like an insult.

The man spits blood. “Not—here.”

Revy steps in, pistol pressed under the man’s jaw, lifting it. Her eyes are bright with the simple pleasure of control. “Try again.”

The man’s gaze flicks to the window, to the rain-smeared street. “He already left. He was—supposed to meet—”

A shot cracks from somewhere outside. The window spiderwebs, glass hissing inward.

Spike and Revy drop at the same instant, bodies moving like they rehearsed it. Spike’s hand clamps on Revy’s wrist, not to disarm—just to pull her down behind the bar’s edge. Their shoulders collide. Hot contact. A shared breath.

Revy snarls, but she doesn’t pull away. Not yet.

Another shot. The jukebox track loops again, oblivious, making the chaos surreal, like the bar is trapped in a song it can’t escape.

Spike peeks up just enough to see through the broken window: a car idling across the street, headlights off. A muzzle flash blooms.

Revy leans, fires twice without looking, her bullets snapping through the cracked glass with confidence so arrogant it works. The car lurches; tires squeal. It peels away into rain.

“Fenner’s not coming back,” Spike says.

“No shit,” Revy spits, then grabs the older man’s collar and yanks him down. “Where’d he go?”

The man’s eyes roll. He’s half out.

Sirens are closer now. Red-blue light starts to smear across the walls.

Spike releases Revy’s wrist. “We’re done here.”

Revy’s gaze is a knife. “He’s getting away.”

Spike’s eyes slide to the door. “He already did.”

“Then move,” she snaps.

Spike vaults over the bar. He lands light, but as he cuts around a toppled chair, his foot misjudges the distance by a hair. His ankle catches. He stumbles—small, almost nothing.

Revy sees it anyway. Her eyes narrow, not with pity, but with the sudden interest of a predator sensing a limp.

Spike recovers before anyone else could notice. He doesn’t look at her. He doesn’t explain.

They hit the door together, rain slapping them in the face. The night outside is wet neon and exhaust. The song, that damned riff, follows them in memory like tinnitus.

They sprint into the alley beside the bar. The world back here is dumpsters and dripping pipes and a single flickering light that can’t decide if it’s alive.

Revy shoves Spike against the wall hard enough to make his cigarette jump. Not an attack. A stop.

“You tripped,” she says. Her voice is low, dangerous. “Don’t tell me you’re sloppy.”

Spike pushes off the wall, unbothered, like she’s just part of the weather. “I’m fine.”

Revy’s eyes rake him, looking for weakness she can exploit or avoid. “You’re not fine.”

“Whatever happens, happens,” Spike says, and it lands like a shrug and a dare.

Revy’s lips curl. “That your excuse for being half-blind?”

Spike’s gaze flicks—just a twitch—to her face. The lazy mask slips enough to show something underneath, old and tired. “Careful,” he says softly. “You might sound like you care.”

Revy steps closer, invading again, her breath smelling of cheap liquor and anger. “Don’t flatter yourself.”

A police cruiser turns the corner at the mouth of the alley. Its lights paint them in alternating colors.

Revy grabs Spike’s sleeve. “This way.”

He lets her. That’s the thing that surprises him: he lets her.

They cut deeper into the maze, through a gap in a chain-link fence, out onto a back street where a row of cheap motels crouches like guilty thoughts. A sign buzzes: VACANCY. Some letters dead, making the word look like it’s missing teeth.

Inside the motel office, the clerk barely looks up. Cash changes hands. No names. No questions.

The room is on the second floor. The hallway smells like bleach failing at its job. A door opens somewhere; laughter spills out and dies.

Revy pushes into their room first, gun up. Spike follows, scanning corners with the ease of habit.

One bed, lumpy. One chair, stained. One bathroom with a mirror that distorts like it’s embarrassed. Curtains thin enough to be a suggestion. A chain on the door that looks like it’s been ripped off and reattached out of stubbornness.

Revy claims the side of the room closest to the door. She checks the chain, checks the lock, checks the peephole. Her pistols go on the bedside table within reach, perfectly aligned.

Spike drifts to the window. He pulls the curtain aside a fraction and looks out at the rain. He stands where the glass can show him the street and also reflect the room behind him. His gun stays in his jacket, but his hands are empty in a way that still reads armed.

Silence grows teeth in the space between them.

From somewhere—another room, another floor—a thin wall carries music. The same scratchy rock track. Distant, muffled, but unmistakable. Like the universe is laughing.

Revy’s eyes flick toward the sound. “You kidding me?”

Spike’s mouth twitches. “Seems like we can’t get away from it.”

She snorts and sits on the edge of the bed without relaxing. Her knee bounces once, a contained explosion. “Fenner’s running. We lost him because you wanted to play hero in a dive bar.”

“I didn’t,” Spike says.

“You did,” she says, and jabs a finger at him like it’s a gun too. “You stopped me from shooting that kid.”

Spike leans back against the wall by the window. “He was panicking.”

Revy laughs, sharp. “So? You think panicking bullets hurt less?”

Spike’s gaze is on the rain, but his attention is on her. “You want Fenner, you need him alive. Dead men don’t pay.”

Revy’s eyes flash. “Don’t tell me how bounties work.”

“I’m not,” Spike says. “I’m telling you how this one works.”

She grabs her bottle—she must’ve kept it, because of course she did—and takes a drink. Then she wipes at a cut on her knuckle with the edge of her shirt. Blood beads, bright.

Spike’s eyes drop to it before he can stop them. A tiny tell.

Revy catches it. “What, you gonna bandage me now?”

Spike pushes off the wall and crosses the room with unhurried steps. He stops close enough that the air shifts. “Hold still,” he says.

Revy’s jaw tightens. “Or what?”

He reaches for her hand. Not her gun. Her bleeding knuckle.

For a heartbeat, she doesn’t move. Then she lets him take it, not because she trusts him—because she wants to see what he’ll do with the permission. Like a test.

Spike’s fingers are warm, surprisingly gentle. He turns her hand, inspects the cut. “It’s nothing,” he says, and his tone makes it sound like a fact he can enforce.

Revy watches his mouth as he speaks, like she’d rather watch that than his eyes. “You always touch strangers like that?”

Spike glances up. “Only the ones who look like they’ll bite.”

Her lips part, almost a smile. “Maybe I will.”

Spike releases her hand. The moment breaks, but the heat doesn’t.

Revy leans back, eyes narrowing again. “Who hired you?”

Spike walks back toward the window, giving her space like it costs him something. “A guy.”

Revy scoffs. “No kidding.”

Spike’s shoulders lift in a half-shrug. “I didn’t ask his name.”

“Liar.”

Spike’s gaze flicks toward her reflection in the glass. “Fine. I asked. He didn’t answer.”

Revy’s laugh is softer this time, almost real. It surprises her; her face hardens immediately after, like she caught herself being human. “I got a call from some suit who thinks money can fix his mess,” she says. “Fenner burned down his warehouse. Killed three people. Wanted it made right.”

Spike’s cigarette is gone—somewhere between the bar and here, it died. He seems to miss it. “Sounds personal.”

“It’s business,” Revy says too fast.

Spike turns slightly, looking at her directly now. “Everything’s business until it isn’t.”

Revy’s eyes sharpen. “You trying to psychoanalyze me?”

Spike’s expression is mild. “Just listening.”

“Don’t,” she snaps.

The distant rock track loops again through the wall, muffled pop included. A metronome for all the things they’re not saying.

Revy stands abruptly, pacing two steps and stopping. Her hand brushes the grip of one pistol, not drawing it, just reminding the room that it’s there. “We should split it,” she says. “Six million. Three each. We work together until Fenner’s down.”

Spike’s head tilts. “And then?”

“And then we go our own way,” Revy says, like she can see the end already and it’s clean. “No funny shit.”

Spike’s gaze is unreadable. “You don’t do funny.”

Revy’s mouth twists. “I do whatever works.”

Spike’s eyes drift to her pistols. “Working with you could get messy.”

Revy steps closer. “You scared?”

Spike’s face doesn’t change. “No.”

“Then say yes.”

He doesn’t answer right away. He looks at the door. The window. The mirror. The thin walls. The way the room feels like a trap with a bed in it.

Revy waits, breathing controlled, eyes fixed on him like she’s already got him in her sights.

Spike says, “Maybe.”
