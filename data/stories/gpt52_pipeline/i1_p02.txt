**21st March 1932**  
Eilean Mòr Light Station  
Off Cloch Point, Firth of Clyde

Dear Ma,

I’m here safe, and that’s the first thing. We came out from Gourock on the tender with a right load of stores, and I’ve my bed made up and the kettle on as I write. It’s a queer thing stepping off a boat onto a rock that’s meant to be your whole world, but I’m settled in well enough.

The pay is as they said—£4 a week with board, and it’s not board like your table, but there’s plenty of it: tea, flour, salted meat, oats, tinned milk, jam if you’re quick. There’s a rota with the other man, McPhee, and we’ll take turns on watch and lamp. He’s a quiet sort, older than me, and keeps his pipe clenched like it’s nailed on.

There’s repairs needing done. The rail on the lower landing’s got rust that’ll take my hands off, and the coal shed door swells shut with the damp. The lens is a marvel up close—no’ just a lamp, but a whole machine of glass and brass. I’m proud to put my hand to it, Ma. It’s honest work.

It’s blowing a bit, but nothing to fret about. The foghorn’s set to go on the quarter-hour when the haar comes in from Cloch Point. Funny thing though: last night, before we’d even lit proper, I could’ve sworn it gave a wee short call out of time—like a man clearing his throat. McPhee says the wind plays tricks round the trumpet. I suppose that’s all it is.

Tell Jeanie I got her ribbon and I’ll write her when I’ve a better head for it. Don’t be wasting stamps on me if you can’t spare them. I’ve plenty to do.

Your loving son,  
Robbie

**26th March 1932**  
5 Broomielaw Close, Glasgow

My dear Robert,

Thank God you are there and writing sensible. I read your letter twice, and I could see you with your sleeves up as if you were born with a wrench in your hand. I am pleased about the pay, but do not speak like you’ll not have me writing. I’ll write when I can, and you’ll read it when you can.

Are you eating? Proper eating, not just tea and biscuits till your stomach burns. You’ll take porridge, and if there’s meat you’ll take it, and not hand it to that McPhee out of politeness. Keep your feet dry. I’ve put two pairs of thick socks in the post with this and you’ll wear them, and if you don’t I’ll come out and put them on you myself, rock or no rock.

Your Aunt Mary has the rheumatics bad and she’s taken to her bed. The men at the yard are talking again about lay-offs and there’s a meeting at the kirk hall on Friday. Mrs Donnelly’s wee boy has the measles and you’d think it was the end of the world the way she carries on. Life is as it always is.

You say “McPhee” like I should know him. Have you spoken to anyone else? Do you see the tender often? When is your relief? And the foghorn—don’t you start with foolishness about it. The wind does make queer sounds, your father used to say so. You mind your job and you mind your sleep.

Your Ma,  
Agnes

**3rd April 1932**  
Eilean Mòr Light Station

Dear Ma,

Socks came. They’re grand. I’m wearing them the now and I’ll no’ deny it—my feet were cold before.

You asked what I do all day, so here it is, plain. In the morning: shake the damp out the blankets, scrape the stove, haul coal from the shed (if the door will open), set the kettle, check the barometer and write it in the log. After that: polish brass till I can see my face in it, wipe salt off every window, and mind the lens—clean the prisms with the soft cloth, check the clockwork and weights, and make sure the mantle’s sitting right. Then the outside: rust on the rail, tar on the steps, paint where the sea’s taken it, mend the rope on the hoist. After dinner: swab the floors, air the bedding if there’s any sun, and count stores so we’re not caught short. At night: watch, lamp, foghorn if need be, and another round in case any glass has sweated.

It sounds like I’m complaining. I’m not. It’s better to keep at it. If I sit down long my head goes thick with the sound of the sea under the stones, like something breathing.

My hands are cracked at the knuckles from salt and paraffin. McPhee says to rub them with goose grease. I’ll use soap like a Christian.

We see the tender when weather allows. They say every fortnight, but you know what the Clyde’s like. Relief is in June, if all goes to plan.

I’ll write when I can. The days run together, Ma. It’s not bad, it’s just—same.

Robbie

---

**18th April 1932**  
Eilean Mòr Light Station

Ma,

Found a thing on the lower rocks after the last blow. It was wedged in the wrack line like it had been set there. A child’s shoe. Leather, black, with a broken buckle. Full of sand, and a wee bit of weed caught in it. No foot, thank God. No body. Just the shoe.

McPhee says it’ll have come off some shore, maybe from the Point, washed out with rubbish. That’s sensible. I told myself the same. I threw it back in the sea.

But later, when I was up in the lantern room, I saw a light far out past Cloch Point—low and steady, not a ship’s mast light. It held for a minute, then it was gone. Could’ve been a boat turning. Could’ve been my eyes.

I’m only telling you because you asked me to tell you what it’s like. It’s like that: you see a thing and there’s nowhere to put it. So you put it on paper and hope it sits still.

Don’t worry. I’m fine. I’m doing my work.

Robbie

---

**21st April 1932**  
5 Broomielaw Close, Glasgow

Robert,

A child’s shoe is an awful thing to find, and you did right to put it back. There was a piece in the paper last month about a skiff off Greenock that went over with two men, and folk said all sorts, but the sea takes what it takes. Do not carry it in your head. Say a prayer and leave it with God.

As for lights—boats have lights, and men do foolish things on water. Don’t you begin inventing company for yourself out there. You mind the lamp, and you mind the foghorn, and you sleep when you can. Eat something with a bit of fat, it keeps a body warm.

I have sent you a wee book of psalms that was your Granny’s. It’s no’ for show, it’s for when your thoughts run away. And I’ve put in a tin of hand salve from the chemist, since you’ll not listen about grease.

Write again soon. Soon.

Ma

**10th June 1932**  
Eilean Mòr Light Station

Ma,

I don’t know if you got the wire. The post’s been a shambles. The tender tried and turned back, twice. We’ve enough tea and flour but the fresh is gone and the meat’s down to the salty ends. McPhee’s temper is worse for it. Mine too.

I asked for your handwriting and it sounds daft, I know. It’s not for anything wicked. It’s just—when the fog sits and there’s no horizon, you can go a bit soft in the head with the sameness. I had your last letter in my pocket and I read it till I near wore the fold through. The shapes of your words make me feel there’s a room somewhere with a fire and a chair that’s mine. If you could write out a page of anything, even the shopping, it would do.

The log is getting hard to keep straight. Not the numbers—the numbers are the numbers. It’s the nights. Sometimes I’m sure I’ve wound the clockwork and then I’m at it again, and the weights are already up. Sometimes I hear the foghorn when it’s not on. Not like a call, more like it’s inside the walls.

The lamp gives off heat even when it’s banked down. McPhee says I’m imagining it. Maybe I am. Maybe I’m just tired.

Don’t tell folk. I’m doing my job.

Robbie

---

**12th June 1932**  
5 Broomielaw Close, Glasgow

My Robbie,

I got your telegram and I could have been sick with the fright of the silence before it. Do not you do that to me again if you can help it. I know the sea does what it wants, but you must understand there is a woman on a close stair reading the same empty mat every morning.

I have written you three pages as you asked—recipes, the price of coal, what the minister said on Sunday—anything to fill a page with my hand. You’ll get it when you get it.

And I have done another thing. Don’t be angry. I went down to the harbour office at Gourock and I spoke to the man there, and I wrote to the Northern Lighthouse Board besides. I asked when your relief is due and why the tender has not made you. The clerk looked at me like I was a nuisance and I looked back till he stopped.

There was a wreck mentioned in the *Herald*—a small steamer clipped the rocks under Cloch Point in fog and got off again, thank God. I cut the piece out and I’m sending it. Not to frighten you. To show you I’m watching the same sea from here in my way.

If you need to come off, you say so. If you are ill, you say so. Pride is no’ food and it’s no’ warmth.

Your Ma,  
Agnes

**28th June 1932**  
Eilean Mòr Light Station  
(no date on the inside, just the envelope)

Ma,

I’m ashamed writing this.

Ma, I’m frightened.

It’s not ghosts. It’s not stories. It’s just me, in my own head, and the noise of water, and the light going round and round like it’s grinding time down.

There’s nights I can’t tell if it’s night until I look at the clock, and then I don’t trust the clock. I’ve sat on the stair and held the rail till my hands went numb because I was sure there were steps below me. Not on the stones—inside. I’ve called out, stupid, like a bairn.

And there’s a thing worse: sometimes, when I’m to light at the proper time, my hand stops. Not long. A minute. Two. Like if I keep it dark, whatever’s waiting will go away. Then I light it and I hate myself, because ships don’t care about my feelings and men drown if I’m a fool.

I’ve not been drinking. Don’t let that be what you think. I’ve been trying to work it out of me. I can’t.

If they send a man to relieve me, I’ll come. If they don’t, I don’t know how long I can keep this straight.

I’m sorry, Ma. I’m sorry.

Robbie

**2nd July 1932**  
Northern Lighthouse Board — Official Correspondence  
(typed, stamped)

To: Mrs Agnes McAllister, 5 Broomielaw Close, Glasgow.  
Re: R. McAllister, Assistant Keeper, Eilean Mòr Station (Cloch Point)

Madam,

We acknowledge receipt of your letter dated 12th June and note your concerns.

Owing to recent weather conditions and interruption of tender service, relief and provisioning have been delayed. Arrangements have now been made for the tender **Pharos** to proceed at the earliest safe opportunity, with a superintendent on board.

Mr R. McAllister is to be relieved and conveyed to shore pending medical review, in accordance with Board procedure.

Yours faithfully,  
J. R. Kincaid  
Clerk to the Board

**6th July 1932**  
5 Broomielaw Close, Glasgow

My son,

I am coming to Gourock on Friday and I don’t care who thinks what of it. If they won’t let me near the pier I’ll stand on the road and wait till I see your face, and that will be enough.

Listen to me. You have done nothing wicked. A man can be brave and still be driven thin by weather and loneliness. Your father would have told you the same if he’d had the words for it. He went quiet in himself after the accident at the yard and he never said “I’m frightened” to anyone, and I watched that hurt him more than any broken bone.

You lit the light. That’s what matters. You kept men from the rocks while you were shaking inside. That is not shame.

When you are home you will eat, and you will sleep in a bed that does not move, and you will see the doctor even if you dislike it. And you will let me make you tea without acting like it costs you manhood.

Hold on. Just hold on.

Ma

---

**19th July 1932**  
5 Broomielaw Close, Glasgow

Ma,

I’m in my own room again. It feels too still. The first night I woke thinking I’d missed the watch, and my heart went like a hammer till I heard the close door slam and a woman laughing downstairs. Ordinary noise. I could’ve cried at it.

They took me off on the **Pharos** in a chop that made McPhee green, and I was glad of his green face because it meant he was human and not just part of the stone. The superintendent didn’t ask me for stories. He asked me questions like a man measuring timber. How many hours sleep. How many meals. Any drink. Any thoughts of harm. I answered plain.

The doctor at Inverkip said “nerves” and “exhaustion,” and he wrote it down and that made it real in a way that helped. I’m signed off for now. No pay while I’m off, not proper, but the Board’s given a bit and I’ll manage. I’ll pay you back for any money you’ve to put out, even if it takes me a year.

I read the pages of your handwriting you sent. I’ve them folded in my jacket still. The bit where you wrote “two onions, if they’re not dear” near broke me. That’s the world, Ma. Onions and prices and folk carrying on. I’d forgot.

I keep thinking of Cloch Point when I shut my eyes—the line of it in fog, and the horn sounding, and the lens turning with that soft click like teeth. But it’s not a thing waiting for me now. It’s just a place. A hard place that did what hard places do.

Tomorrow I’ll go with you to see Aunt Mary, if she’ll have me, and I’ll sit at the foot of her bed and take her complaints like a penance. Next week I’ll go back to the yard office and ask if there’s anything I can do till the Board decides. And I’ll go back to the doctor when he tells me, even if I’d rather scrub floors.

Thank you for pushing in. I hated it, and I needed it.

Your son,  
Robbie
