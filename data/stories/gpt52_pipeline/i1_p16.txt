Lucifer woke before the screaming started.

That was how he knew something had shifted in the machinery of damnation: silence, stretched too thin, like a skin pulled over a drum. The Palace of Pandemonium had its usual smells—hot iron, old smoke, an undertow of salt from the Styx—but the soundscape was wrong. No distant chorus of bargaining, no clatter of chains being “misplaced” and found again, no demon in the hallway pretending not to cough.

Only the wet, determined grind of teeth on metal.

Gnch. Gnch. Gnch.

Lucifer lay there for a moment, staring at the ceiling fresco—an ambitious depiction of his own fall, lovingly rendered in flaking gold leaf—and listened as the chewing kept time like a clock with opinions. Then he sat up, swung his legs over the side of the bed, and felt a small crunch beneath his bare foot.

Iron filings. Again.

“Good morning,” he told the room, because there were rules. Routines. You did not let chaos think it had been invited.

He crossed the black marble to his dressing mirror. His reflection was immaculate in the way a blade was immaculate: no wrinkles, no sleep-softness, hair precisely disordered. He ran a comb—obsidian teeth set into a handle of polished bone—through it anyway. The comb clicked faintly as if impatient.

On the vanity sat his morning tray: a demitasse of blood espresso, freshly frothed, steaming. A plate of something that looked like toast if toast could swear at you. A little sugar bowl full of ashes. Beside it, a clipboard, because Hell did not run on fear alone; it ran on documentation.

He took the espresso in one hand and the clipboard in the other and walked out into the throne room in slippers that had once been an archangel’s idea of comfort.

The hellhound had its front paws braced against the Iron Throne’s left front leg and its jaws clamped around the corner like a carpenter testing wood. It chewed with focus, head rocking slightly with each bite. The throne—ancient, ceremonial, a masterpiece of molten swords and conquered crowns—did not so much as squeal. It was too proud to whine.

Lucifer cleared his throat.

The hellhound did not look up.

“Cerberus,” he said, because names mattered. “Leave it.”

Gnch.

Lucifer sipped his espresso. The foam left a red mustache that he wiped away with a thumb in one smooth motion, like a man who had never in his life been surprised by anything and certainly not by a dog eating furniture in the seat of ultimate authority.

“I am asking,” he added, “as your sovereign.”

Gnch.

He flipped the clipboard to the first page. *DAMNATION SCHEDULE, CIRCLE ALLOCATION, QUOTA ADJUSTMENTS—Q3.*

He made a neat checkmark beside *‘Sycophants—reserve spot in Ninth, promote to managerial positions in Second as punishment.’*

“Cerberus,” he said again, calmly. “If you continue to gnaw the throne, you will be exiled to—”

Gnch.

Lucifer paused. Considered his options. Exile was difficult; the paperwork alone could ruin a decade. Besides, the last time he’d threatened exile, Cerberus had looked at him with such betrayed devotion that several lower demons had filed complaints about “unprofessional vulnerability.”

He tried bribery.

He snapped his fingers, and a demon appeared at his elbow, holding a femur on a silver tray like an hors d’oeuvre at a very poor party.

Lucifer took the femur. It belonged to a politician. It still had a wedding ring on one end.

“Here,” he said, offering it.

Cerberus’s chewing did not so much as stutter.

Lucifer lowered the femur, jaw ticking once.

“Fine,” he said. “Have your breakfast where it isn’t… symbolic.”

Gnch.

The throne leg was not just scarred now. It was missing a strip, like someone had shaved it down. A small pile of filings glittered at the base, spreading across the floor like a slow, humiliating snowfall.

Lucifer, who had once declared war on Heaven over a point of principle, found himself thinking, with surprising heat: *That is my chair.*

He smoothed his robe—dark silk that drank the light—and walked toward the long table where the morning meeting waited, refusing to glance back at the gnawing sound that followed him like a metronome.

It was already full of demons, which meant it was already a headache.

They stood when he approached, not out of respect but out of instinct. They were variously horned, winged, scaled, suited, and smug. One wore a tie made of tongues; another had an entire briefcase that hissed. The last time Lucifer had attempted to enforce a dress code, Beelzebub had sent him a memo titled *RE: “NO OPEN-TOE SANDALS”* consisting entirely of laughing fly emojis.

Lucifer sat at the head of the table beneath the great stained-glass window depicting the Original Sin in tasteful reds. He placed the clipboard down with care.

“Reports,” he said.

A demon with too many eyes stood. “Overcrowding in the Third Circle, sir. We’ve got gluttons spilling into adjacent zones. They’re eating the scenery.”

Lucifer made a note. “Tell the landscapers to plant more rocks. Edible ones.”

A smaller demon—horns like staplers—rose. “Pitchfork supply chain remains disrupted. The manufacturer says the molten steel is arriving ‘late due to unforeseen angelic interference.’”

Lucifer stared.

The demon hurried on. “We can substitute with tridents, but the tridents have… morale issues. Some of the staff feel they are ‘seaside.’”

“Send a strongly worded curse,” Lucifer said. “Also, authorize overtime for the forge. I don’t care if the pitchforks are delayed; we are not switching to anything that implies leisure.”

Another demon slid a parchment across the table. “Circle Seven lava is running lukewarm again.”

Lucifer pinched the bridge of his nose. “Have we tried—”

“Yes,” the demon said quickly. “We have tried ‘adding more brimstone.’ We have tried ‘angrier brimstone.’ We have tried ‘branding the brimstone.’”

Lucifer checked the doorway without moving his head. Empty.

He returned his gaze to the table. “Fine. Bring in the consultants from Gehenna. They enjoy being unhelpful.”

A demon with an immaculate suit and a pen that dripped ink like blood cleared its throat. “We need you to sign these forms. Soul misallocation. A morally ambiguous poet has been assigned to the Fifth Circle instead of the Fourth. The Fifth has filed a complaint that he is ‘insufferable.’ The Fourth has filed a complaint that they *miss* him.”

Lucifer took the stack of forms. They were thick, heavy with red tape in the literal sense; a ribbon of cured skin bound them. He signed with a flourish that made the ink scream.

“Next,” he said.

There were disputes. A torturer accused another torturer of “stealing my best screams.” A warden insisted the chains were not “rentals,” they were “long-term leases.” A circle manager wanted a new plaque for the entrance to the Second Circle because the current one “didn’t pop.”

Lucifer listened, nodded, adjudicated. He was very good at this. He had built a kingdom on precision.

And still, between points, he found his eyes flicking toward the doorway like a man waiting for a late appointment he would never admit to having scheduled.

Gnch. Gnch. Gnch.

From across the hall, the hellhound continued its work. No one commented. No one dared. The chewing was the only honest sound in Hell.

The meeting was almost tolerable when a breeze cut through the room—cold as a knife left in snow, sharp with the smell of desert wind and old blood. The candles along the walls guttered. Several demons flinched like their skin remembered something it hated.

Lucifer’s pen paused mid-signature.

Lilith appeared in the doorway without announcement, as if Hell’s wards were merely a suggestion she found amusing. She wore black, of course, but not the administrative black of his robe. Hers was the black of night before anyone invented sleep. Her hair fell in a dark sheet over one shoulder. Her eyes, when they landed on Lucifer, did not soften; they sharpened.

“Morningstar,” she said, like a title was a weapon she could test for balance.

“First Woman,” Lucifer replied, because he had his own weapons. “You’re early. Or late. Depending on what you call time.”

Lilith stepped into the room. Demons pretended not to see her with the fervor of professionals ignoring a scandal in the break room. One demon made the mistake of glancing up; Lilith looked at it, and it reconsidered its career path.

Lucifer sat back, folding his hands. “To what do I owe this… unannounced drop-in?”

She walked to the table without asking permission, trailing silence behind her like silk. “I’m here for something of mine.”

Lucifer arched an eyebrow. “You’re going to have to be more specific. You’ve left quite a few things.”

Lilith’s mouth curved. Not a smile. A reminder. “Your copy of the prophecy.”

“One of the prophecies?” Lucifer asked mildly. “Or the one you stole from my archive and annotated with insults?”

“I didn’t steal it,” she said. “I relocated it. You have a habit of misplacing the future.”

Lucifer gestured to the demon with the forms. “We are currently misplacing the present. Sit, if you’re going to make yourself useful.”

Lilith leaned over the table, palms on the wood. The demons on either side stiffened, suddenly very interested in their nails.

“I’m not staying,” she said. “I have errands.”

“Of course,” Lucifer said. “Hell forbid you commit to anything.”

Her eyes flashed. “I committed once. It didn’t go well.”

The air between them thickened with history. The kind you didn’t put in minutes.

Lucifer reached for the prophecy ledger behind him—a heavy book bound in something that refused to be named. He slid it onto the table. “Here. Take it. Consider it my contribution to your personal mythology.”

Lilith didn’t touch it. “Also,” she said, as if remembering, “my comb.”

Lucifer’s fingers stilled on the edge of the book. “Your comb.”

“The one made of ribs,” she clarified.

“You mean the comb on my vanity,” Lucifer said, keeping his voice even, “which you left here during your last… exit.”

Lilith’s gaze flicked to his hair, as if she could see each perfectly placed strand as evidence. “You use it.”

“It’s an excellent comb,” Lucifer said. “Very sturdy. Holds up under pressure. Unlike—”

Gnch.

The chewing grew louder, as if the hellhound had decided it wanted to participate in the conversation.

Lilith’s head turned, slow. “Is that—”

“My throne,” Lucifer said, his tone a shade too light. “Yes.”

From the far end of the hall, Cerberus had not only continued gnawing; it had widened its stance, as if preparing for a victory.

Lilith’s eyes glinted. “You let it do that.”

Lucifer’s jaw clenched. “I do not *let* it.”

She straightened, stepping closer to him. “It’s chewing your authority.”

“It’s chewing metal,” Lucifer said. “It’s a dog.”

Lilith tilted her head. “Is it lonely?”

Lucifer’s hand tightened around his pen. “It’s a hellhound. It eats souls for enrichment.”

“Mm,” Lilith said. “So do you. Yet here you are, drinking espresso like it’s going to talk back.”

A demon, emboldened by stupidity, stood. “Sire, if you wish, I could—”

Cerberus suddenly abandoned the throne leg and bounded toward the meeting table with the joyous urgency of an animal that had just remembered it had legs. It skidded across the slick floor, claws sparking. Several demons yelped and leapt onto chairs.

“Cerberus,” Lucifer snapped, half-rising.

The hellhound ignored him with devotion. It shoved its head under the table, snuffling. Its tail knocked over an inkwell. Ink splattered across the misallocation forms like fresh bruises.

Lilith watched, expression unreadable. “What did it eat?”

Lucifer’s eyes narrowed. “What did it *swallow*,” he corrected, because words mattered even in chaos.

As if in answer, Cerberus emerged dragging something in its teeth: a length of iron, torn free, jagged at one end. The throne leg. Or part of it. It clanked against the floor as the hound shook its head with pride.

A demon reached out, foolishly, as if to retrieve it. Cerberus snapped—fast, not cruel, just efficient—and caught the demon’s sleeve between its teeth. The demon squealed, flailing. Cerberus shook once, as if playing tug-of-war, and the demon went limp in the way of someone who had learned a lesson without dying.

Lucifer sighed through his nose. “Put him down,” he told no one, because no one could.

Lilith’s gaze flicked to the demon on the floor, then back to Lucifer. “You run a tight ship.”

“It’s more of an organization,” Lucifer said. “Ships imply freedom.”

Cerberus trotted in a circle, dragging the throne piece like a prize. Metal scraped. Demons scattered. The chewing resumed with even greater enthusiasm, now on the *trophy*.

Gnch. Gnch. Gnch.

Lucifer looked at the chaos, then at Lilith. “You came for a prophecy and a comb,” he said, voice low. “And yet you stand here watching my dog dismantle my furniture. Remarkable timing.”

Lilith’s lips twitched. “Maybe I missed the sound.”

Lucifer’s gaze held hers a beat too long.

Then he exhaled, controlled. “Fine,” he said. “If it swallowed anything important, we’ll need to retrieve it before it… passes.”

Several demons made noises of deep professional discomfort.

Lilith lifted her chin. “You want help.”

“I want efficiency,” Lucifer said, because asking for help was too close to asking for something else.

Lilith reached down, picked up the prophecy book with one hand as if it weighed nothing, and tucked it under her arm. “Then let’s be efficient.”

They left the meeting in disarray. Lucifer didn’t dismiss the demons; they knew how to disperse when they were no longer useful. He could hear them already arguing over who would file the incident report.

In the corridor, heat rose in waves, the architecture shifting subtly as Hell adjusted around their movement. Walls sweated tar. Torches burned in sullen colors.

Lucifer walked beside Lilith with the pace of someone trying not to match her stride. Cerberus padded ahead, tail held high, oblivious to the symbolism it was actively consuming.

“You’ve been busy,” Lilith observed, glancing at the passing doorways: torture pits on the left, archive stacks on the right, a demon union bulletin board plastered with flyers like *STOP UNPAID WAILING*.

Lucifer’s eyes followed her gaze. “It’s called governance.”

“It’s called distraction,” Lilith said.

He ignored that. “We’re going to the forge. If the throne leg is compromised, I want it replaced before the next… procession.”

Lilith laughed once. It sounded like a blade being tested. “You care what they think.”

“I care what *I* think,” Lucifer corrected. “Also, if the throne collapses under someone’s weight, they’ll file a complaint. And I’ll have to read it.”

Cerberus stopped at a junction and sniffed, then veered toward the torture pits.

“No,” Lucifer said. “Not today.”

Cerberus wagged harder.

Lilith leaned down slightly, her voice softening in the way of someone who knew exactly how to be heard. “Come,” she said, and clicked her tongue once.

Cerberus paused, then—betraying every principle of loyalty—trotted back to her side.

Lucifer’s eyes narrowed. “Of course you can do that.”

Lilith straightened, not looking at him. “It’s not magic,” she said. “It’s attention.”

The forge was a cathedral of heat. Infernal blacksmiths—massive, soot-streaked creatures with arms like anvils—hammered at glowing iron. The air rang with impact. Chains hung like curtains. Somewhere, something screamed, but it was a functional scream, the kind that came with productive labor.

A foreman approached, wiping its brow with a rag that caught fire and then apologized. “Your Majesty. We weren’t expecting—”

“Yes,” Lucifer said. “No one ever is.”

He gestured at the chewed throne leg piece Cerberus still held, now dented and wet. “Repair. Replace. Reinforce. Whatever you do, make it… dignified.”

The foreman eyed the metal. “That’s been—”

“Eaten,” Lucifer supplied.

The foreman looked past Lucifer to Lilith, then back, as if checking whether this was some sort of test. “We can replace the entire support structure,” it said carefully. “But it will take time. And—”

“And?” Lucifer asked.

“And the forge union has filed a grievance,” the foreman added quickly, as if ripping off a bandage. “They want hazard pay for ‘working on objects that have been personally violated by domesticated beasts.’”

Lilith’s eyebrows rose. “That’s a category now.”

“It’s a modern Hell,” Lucifer said, deadpan. “We innovate.”

He reached into his robe and produced a stamp—heavy, iron, embossed with his sigil. He pressed it onto a waiting form without reading it. The parchment smoked.

“Hazard pay granted,” he said. “In exchange, I want it done by tonight.”

The foreman blinked. “Tonight?”

Lucifer smiled. It was not kind. “Do you want to see what the dog does if it gets bored?”

Cerberus, as if on cue, dropped the chewed metal and started gnawing on a nearby anvil.

The foreman swallowed. “Tonight,” it agreed.

As the blacksmiths began to haul away the throne leg piece, Lilith stepped closer to Lucifer, lowering her voice so the forge noise swallowed it.

“You’ve gotten… good at this,” she said.

“I was always good at this,” Lucifer replied automatically.

Lilith’s gaze slid over his face, lingering a fraction too long. “No,” she said.
