The boardwalk gave under her heels with a tired, varnished creak, and the evening air had that Coney Island trick of tasting like both salt and sugar. She came to a stop as if she’d reached the edge of something marked on a map—though there wasn’t any mark, only the shooting gallery with its bright teeth of targets and its row of guns laid out like promises.

The sodium lamps clicked alive overhead and, a heartbeat later, began their little failures. A stutter of light. A steadier glow. Then another flicker that made the painted ducks seem to bob in place and the faces in the crowd flash from pretty to tired to sharp as hatpins. Under the unsteady amber, men’s collars and women’s lipstick appeared, vanished, appeared again. The sign above the booth—SHOOT TO WIN—went briefly half-blind.

She kept one hand on the lacquered rail, feeling the tack of damp wood under her glove. Her skin, bare at the wrist, held the day’s warmth, and the night laid its coolness over it as if testing where she would yield.

She could go on. She could turn back. The very fact she was stopped proved she was not doing either.

His locks.

The phrase came uninvited, like a scrap from a novel she should have been too sensible to finish. His locks—thick, black, and careless, as if he had no reason to keep himself in order. She shut down the rest of the thought before it could climb into words, as if words would make it real.

I don’t know his name, she told herself, with the briskness of a woman straightening a hem. I don’t know anything at all.

And yet: Why did I come back?

At the gallery, a carny in a rolled-up shirt and a vest too tight leaned on his elbows and talked up the line. “Five shots, folks. Five shots, and she’s yours.” He thumbed a brass cartridge as if it were a coin. The prizes hung behind him in gaudy ranks: plush rabbits with glassy eyes, cheap silver trinkets that caught the sodium flicker and pretended it was moonlight, a locket on a ribbon—its clasp shaped like a tiny key.

Gun oil cut through the sweetness of fried dough. Powder-smoke lingered in the wood grain and in the air like a memory that refused to admit it was a memory. A couple stood shoulder to shoulder near the end of the booth, their voices kept low but their anger bright—something about money, something about who’d promised what. A man laughed too loudly, as if laughter were how you paid for permission to be here.

This place ran on bargains. Not all of them were honest.

She told herself she had only come to see. To satisfy curiosity. To prove to herself she could look and not be pulled. And under that, the smaller, meaner truth she wouldn’t phrase: to feel, for one minute, like she was the one who chose the rules.

The light flickered, and she felt him before she saw him—the sudden awareness of being watched with attention rather than accident. She turned slightly, and there he was beside the crowd as if he’d stepped out of it.

Obsidian curls, yes. Under the lamp’s unsteady glow, they took on different textures—soft as velvet one moment, sharp as wire the next. His eyes, too, changed with the stutter: kind for a breath, then unreadable, then severe enough to make her straighten as if she’d been addressed by a teacher.

He was close enough that he didn’t need to raise his voice.

“I wondered if you’d come back,” he said.

The sentence was polite; the knowing in it was not. It slid under her skin like a finger under a glove.

She kept her face composed. “You’re very sure of yourself.”

“I’m observant.” His gaze moved, not grazing her body in any crude way, but taking inventory all the same: her hat pinned exactly, her gloves, her mouth held as if it were a secret. “You stopped in this same place last night.”

“I stop where the crowd stops.”

He smiled as if she’d made a joke. “No. The crowd moves. You stop.”

The lamp buzzed, flickered. His expression cut, softened, cut again. It was like trying to read a sign while someone kept turning the lights off.

“I suppose you keep a ledger,” she said. “Of women who pause.”

“Only the interesting ones.”

She let the word interesting hang between them. A woman could take that as compliment or warning, depending on what she needed.

“What’s your name?” she asked, as if names were simple things.

He waited long enough that she heard a shot crack and a tin target jump, and the pause felt deliberate.

“A name is a kind of claim,” he said.

“That’s a very fine sentence,” she replied. “It doesn’t answer me.”

His eyes narrowed with amusement. “You read.”

“I’m literate,” she said, and regretted the defensiveness the moment it left her mouth.

“You came alone,” he said, not quite a question.

“Did I?” She turned her head slightly, as if checking for a companion, as if she might produce one from the crowd out of spite. “That seems an awfully personal thing to ask someone you haven’t even named.”

He leaned a fraction closer. “I already told you. A name is a claim.”

“You’ll have to manage without one,” she said, and heard her own voice for what it was: calm, controlled, and just a shade too steady.

The carny called, “Step right up, miss. Let him win you something.”

She didn’t look away from the man beside her. “Do you do what strangers suggest?”

“Sometimes,” he said. “When it makes things clearer.”

“And do you like clarity?”

His mouth tilted. “I like the moment before it.”

The flicker made his face briefly gentle. It made her chest tighten. Then the light steadied and he was merely a handsome man on a boardwalk, which was safer, and also—somehow—less bearable.

He nodded toward the guns laid out, their barrels catching the sodium light. “Do you shoot?”

“That depends,” she said, and wished she hadn’t.

“On what?”

“On whether I intend to hit anything.”

He laughed, quiet. “That’s honest.”

She moved closer to the booth as if she’d decided. The carny’s attention snapped onto her like a hook. “Five shots, miss. Pick your target.”

The man beside her—no name, no permission—said, “Let me.”

“No,” she answered, too quick. The word came out like a hand up.

His brows rose. A correction hovered on his tongue; she could see it in the angle of his jaw.

“You’ll hurt your wrist,” he said instead, gently, and that gentleness felt like the correction anyway.

“My wrist is not yours to worry over,” she said, and then, because she wouldn’t be made childish, she added, “Thank you.”

He watched her with an expression that, in the flicker, looked almost like approval.

She reached for one of the gallery rifles. The wood was smooth, worn by other hands. It was heavier than it looked. The metal was cool through her glove. Gun oil rose sharp and familiar, and she didn’t like that it was familiar.

The carny slid a cartridge in with a practiced click. “Nice and easy, miss. Squeeze, don’t yank.”

The man came close enough that she could feel the heat of him along her shoulder. He didn’t touch her, not yet, but his presence adjusted the air.

“Your stance,” he murmured near her ear. “A little more—there.”

She could have told him to move. She could have stepped away. Instead she shifted her feet because his voice made it seem like the most natural thing in the world to obey.

His locks. The thought returned, thick and insistent. She imagined putting her fingers in them—an image so improper her cheeks warmed under her powder. The forbidden part of the thought surged right behind it, the reason she’d come back, the reason she’d stopped.

Because I want—

A shout of laughter from behind them broke it, and the sodium lamp stuttered hard, turning the whole booth into a series of jump-cuts: a grin, a muzzle, her own gloved hands, his profile sharp as a coin.

She set her cheek to the rifle stock. The wood smelled faintly of sweat and carnival smoke. She aimed at a row of small metal stars that spun when hit. The target looked easy until she noticed the slight tilt, the way the mechanism favored the house.

He spoke again, low. “Make a bargain with me.”

She didn’t take her eye from the sights. “You’re in love with bargains.”

“Aren’t you?”

“I’m careful.”

“Careful is another word for afraid,” he said, and there it was—the gentle correction, finally sharp enough to draw blood.

She lowered the rifle just an inch, enough to look at him from the corner of her eye. “Don’t tell me what my words mean.”

His smile returned, slow. “All right.”

The carny leaned in, sensing sport beyond the targets. “You two making it interesting?”

The man ignored him. “If you hit the middle star,” he said, “I’ll give you my name.”

“And if I miss?” she asked, because she could hear the trap in the question and wanted to name it first.

“Then you walk with me,” he said. “To the pier. Ten minutes. No more.”

Ten minutes was nothing. Ten minutes was also exactly how long it took to make a decision you couldn’t undo.

She could have refused. She could have said, I don’t bargain with strangers. But the lamps flickered, and in the dark between the bright moments she felt reckless as a girl in a dim hallway.

“You’ll give your name either way,” she said.

His eyes widened slightly, pleased. “You’re bold.”

“I’m not,” she said. “I’m practical. If I’m walking with you, I want to know what to call you while I’m regretting it.”

He laughed under his breath. “Fair.”

“And,” she added, because she could feel him steering, “if I hit it, you walk with me. The same ten minutes. You don’t get to collect me like a prize.”

The carny made a little sound of appreciation, as if she’d put on a show. “Hear that, mister? She’s got teeth.”

The man considered her, and the lamp turned him from charming to severe and back again. “All right,” he said. “If you hit it, I walk with you. If you miss, you walk with me. Ten minutes. And I give you my name either way.”

“Now,” she said, lifting the rifle again, “we’re even.”

“Not even,” he murmured. “Only agreed.”

Behind them, a woman’s voice cut through, sharp as a pin. “Is that who I think it is?”

Another voice—a man’s, older, with a caution in it—answered softly, “Careful, miss.”

The words landed on her back like a hand. She didn’t turn. Turning would make it real. Turning would give the crowd a claim on her.

The carny said, too bright, “Ignore ’em. Folks like to talk.”

She took a breath. Her body knew what to do; it had known before she allowed herself to admit it. Heat gathered behind her ears. Her hands steadied on the stock, on the trigger. She felt the man’s gaze like a weight, like a dare, like a promise.

Don’t yank, she told herself. Squeeze.

She fired.

The crack split the air, swallowed immediately by the carnival’s noise. The star jumped—almost. It spun a fraction, as if it had considered obeying her, then settled back with a stubborn little shiver.

A miss by a breath.

The carny made an exaggerated sigh. “Ohhh, so close. House wins again.”

Her mouth went dry. She’d wanted to hit it, yes. She’d also—some part of her, dishonest and thrilled—had wanted to miss.

The man’s expression, in the flicker, looked like relief. Or hunger. Or both. “One shot,” he said softly. “So near.”

She lowered the rifle and set it back into the rack with more care than necessary, as if carefulness could erase what she’d done.

“A deal’s a deal,” she said, because if she didn’t speak first, she might not speak at all.

The carny reached up and plucked the little locket from its ribbon as if pity were part of his patter. “Here, miss. For style.” He pressed it into her gloved palm. The clasp—a tiny key—bit gently into the leather.

She stared at it. Locks and keys. Containment dressed up as trinket. She curled her fingers around it, feeling the ridiculous weight of meaning.

The man stepped closer. “My name,” he said. “You earned it, even if you missed.”

“Did I,” she replied, and hated that her voice shook just slightly.

His gaze flicked to the crowd, to the watcher’s place, to the suggestion of reputation hovering like smoke. Then back to her. “Call me Louis,” he said.

It sounded like it might be true. It sounded like it might be the kind of lie that was almost honest.

“Louis,” she repeated, tasting it. “All right.”

He offered his arm—not gallantly, not theatrically, simply as if the world expected it and he meant to meet that expectation on his own terms. She hesitated long enough to feel the sodium light stutter, turning his offered arm into a sequence: inviting, insisting, withdrawing.

She did not take it.

“I said I’d walk,” she said. “I didn’t say you could parade me.”

A flicker of something crossed his face—annoyance, admiration, both. “Then walk,” he said. “Beside me.”

She adjusted her grip on the locket, as if it were proof of something. “Ten minutes,” she reminded him.

“Ten,” he agreed. “To the pier. Where it’s quieter.”

Quiet sounded like danger.

They started through the crowd, matching pace. The boardwalk boards complained under their feet, and the carnival swelled around them: laughter, music, the smell of hot grease and ocean rot together. Behind them, the shooting gallery kept cracking and clinking, small violent sounds dressed up as play.

As they left the booth, the sodium lamps above the walkway stuttered hard. For an instant the faces around them went wrong—features rearranged by shadow, mouths too wide, eyes too empty—then the light steadied and everyone was ordinary again, just people looking for something to spend.

Beyond the rides, the ocean lay black and flat as a sheet of ink.

His locks, she thought again, and this time she didn’t push it away.

She didn’t turn back.
