The lodge took them back the way it always did, like a mouth remembering the shape of your name.

JT shouldered the last of the gear through the mudroom. Wet wool and cold metal and that old lodge smell—cedar boards steeped in kerosene, mouse droppings ground into the corners, the faint sweet rot of last year’s firewood. Dad’s hand went to the generator cord without looking. One pull, then the second, and the engine caught with its familiar cough. The lightbulb over the table blinked once, steadied.

Mike set three rifles on the rack and laughed like he’d said something funny in the truck. “Damn near thought you were gonna cry back there,” he told JT, and didn’t look at him when he said it.

JT wiped his boots on the mat that had always been there—brown rubber, cracked in the same place. Except the crack wasn’t where he remembered. It ran new, diagonal, like a cut. He told himself he’d never paid attention to it before.

Dad stacked the ammo boxes with the labels out, neat as a store shelf. His nails were too clean for the day they’d had. JT watched his fingers, expecting to see soil under them, or blood, or whatever the clearing had put on them. Nothing. Dad’s hands looked like they had that morning when he’d poured coffee.

Outside, wind worried at the spruce. Inside, the lodge swallowed sound. The generator should have made the thin panes tremble. JT stood in the kitchen and listened hard for it—hard enough that the muscles in his jaw ached. The engine was running. He could see the cord vibrating. But the noise came through like it was a memory of noise.

“Get the meat hung,” Dad said, calm. “Mike, you take the quarters. JT—water. We’ll eat.”

JT went for the bucket. The enamel pail was on the same nail by the back door, handle worn shiny. A spiderweb clung to the rim. That was normal. The wrong part was the wet ring on the floor beneath it, as if it had just been set down dripping.

He told himself Mike had grabbed it earlier. He told himself he was tired. He told himself not to be the one who made trouble.

*

They ate at the table where the varnish had bubbled in a long-ago fire. Dad’s stew was thick, more potato than meat, the same cheap pepper taste. The radio sat on the shelf above the sink, turned down low to static and the occasional weather man’s voice.

Mike talked with his mouth full. “First caribou of the season,” he said, raising his tin cup in a toast that didn’t quite reach his eyes. “Good omen.”

Dad tipped his cup, too, and smiled like he’d practiced it. “Good meat,” he said. “Clean kill.”

JT watched the spoon in Dad’s hand. The knuckles were pale from how tight he held it. His smile stayed on like a mask even when he looked down into the bowl.

“Clean,” Mike echoed, and his eyes went somewhere past JT’s shoulder, like there was a window there instead of the dark wall. He blinked slow, as if waking.

JT’s tongue felt thick. The clearing sat in the back of his throat like a clot. He saw it when he closed his eyes—Dad standing with his rifle lowered, palm half-raised like he was blessing something in the brush, Mike’s lips moving without sound, and the caribou’s calm eyes taking them in as if it had always known what would happen.

He cleared his throat. “Back there—” he started, and both of them stilled, just for a beat. Not in a startled way. In a listening way. Like dogs lifting their heads.

Dad’s spoon scraped the bowl. “Back where?”

JT felt his own face heat. The silence pressed the words flat. “The… the shot,” he said. “It was—”

Mike’s laugh came quick, loud, overbright. “He’s spooked,” he told Dad. “City nerves. That clearing looked funny with the light. He thinks the woods are haunted.”

Dad’s eyes met JT’s. Bright, almost wet. He gave a small nod as if agreeing with something JT hadn’t said. “Long day,” he said. “We’ll turn in early. Tomorrow we’ll find another. Plenty of animals out there.”

The way he said *out there* made JT’s stomach roll. Out there was supposed to mean away. Separate. Not close enough to hear through the walls.

The weather man crackled on the radio. “—pressure dropping overnight. Expect thick fog in the lower—” then static swallowed the rest.

Mike pushed his empty bowl away. “We might cut up to the old cabin,” he said, casual. “If the fog comes in thick, could use a roof. And you know those ridges—caribou like to skirt along that line.”

Dad didn’t look up. “That place?” he said, like he was talking about a pothole. “No reason to go that far.”

“It’s not that far,” Mike said. “Half a day, if you take the creek.”

JT’s spoon paused halfway to his mouth. “What cabin?”

Mike grinned. “You never heard? Old trapper built it way back. Folks say you don’t stay the night. Folks say there’s something living in it.”

Dad’s spoon clinked too hard against his bowl. “Folks say a lot,” he muttered.

Mike leaned in, lowering his voice like a storyteller. “My uncle said he went in there once and found a table set like someone was about to eat. No footprints. No fire. Just… set.”

JT waited for the punchline. Mike didn’t give one.

Dad pushed back from the table. “Enough,” he said, and the word came down heavy, the way a rifle butt does when you set it on a floor. He stood, began rinsing dishes with brisk, ordinary motions. Water ran. The generator’s hum stayed wrong in JT’s ears—present, distant.

The silence at the table wasn’t empty. It was a contract.

JT chewed stew he couldn’t taste and didn’t mention the clearing again.

*

Later, when Dad and Mike were in the main room cleaning their rifles, JT went out to the pump with the bucket. The night had thickened. Fog pooled low between the trees like milk. The lodge’s porch light made it glow.

He stepped off the porch and the boards creaked behind him as if someone else had put their weight there a half-second after he did. He stopped, turned.

The porch was empty. The door’s window reflected his own face, pale oval, eyes too wide.

“Wind,” he whispered, though the air was still.

He crossed to the pump, boots sinking in the wet moss. The woods around the lodge looked the way they always had—spruce trunks, black and straight; dead branches like ribs. But the space between them seemed… shallower. As if the tree line had moved closer while he wasn’t watching.

He worked the pump handle. Water gushed into the bucket, cold and metallic. He listened for the generator, for Dad’s voice, for Mike’s laughter. Sound went only a few feet and died.

A hoof-step clicked somewhere behind him.

JT froze with the pump handle down. He waited for a second step. Another click came, closer, then nothing. The fog made the trees vague, their edges softened. He tried to place the sound in his mind—deer, caribou, moose—but it didn’t fit. Hooves on wood, not on moss. Like antlers tapping the side of a porch. Like bone on plank.

He lifted the bucket and forced himself to walk back, counting his steps to keep himself anchored. Twelve to the porch. Fifteen when you went slow. He took one, two, three—then his foot hit the first porch step on *eight*.

He stopped again, heart kicking hard.

The porch had not moved. He had not walked faster. The distance had folded.

JT looked down at the boards. The grain ran like little rivers under the light. In the dust near the door, there was a print.

Not boot.

A clean, two-toed impression, deep enough to show the split, as if something heavy had stepped there and held its weight, considering.

JT set the bucket down without meaning to. Water sloshed onto the boards and ran around the hoofprint, not into it. The print stayed dry, sharp-edged.

“JT?” Dad’s voice came through the door, too normal. “You get the water?”

JT wiped his wet hands on his pants and scooped the bucket up so fast his wrists stung. “Yeah,” he said. “Yeah. Just—pump’s stiff.”

Dad opened the door. Light spilled. For a second, JT thought Dad’s eyes caught the print and changed—something like relief, something like recognition—but Dad’s gaze slid over the boards as if they were blank. He took the bucket, nodded. “Good,” he said, and shut the door.

JT stood on the porch in the cold air, staring at the dry hoofprint until the fog swallowed the edges of the light.

*

In the morning the fog hadn’t lifted. It had climbed.

It threaded through the trees at head height, thick enough that the world ended twenty yards out. The lodge felt like an island in it. The generator chugged. Coffee boiled. Dad and Mike moved through their routines with practiced ease—roll sleeping bags, check knives, load packs. Normal, normal, normal.

They went out anyway.

They followed the creek because Dad said it would keep them from getting turned around. The water was black under the fog, moving without sound. JT walked behind them with his rifle slung and his throat tight.

They found sign sooner than they should have. Tracks in soft mud along the bank—caribou, big, clean, and fresh. Too fresh. The edges of the impressions still crumbled as if the hoof had lifted moments ago.

Mike crouched, touched the mud. “They’re close,” he said, and his voice carried oddly, like he was speaking into a pillow.

Dad’s face settled into its hunting look. Focused. Calm. “Stay behind,” he told JT. “Watch your lane.”

They moved up the bank, easing between spruce. The fog made everything close, intimate, like the woods had leaned in. JT’s breath sounded loud in his own ears, and then it didn’t—like someone had turned down the volume on him.

Ahead, a shape resolved between trunks.

Caribou.

It stood broadside on a small rise, dark against the fog, antlers branching like a crown. Its chest rose and fell slow. It watched them without turning its head away.

Dad raised his rifle. Mike did too. JT brought his up, sighting along the barrel.

The animal didn’t bolt.

It didn’t even stiffen.

It took one step forward, down the rise, toward them, careful as a man approaching a handshake. Another step. It stopped again, square in the little clearing between the trees, presenting its shoulder like a target in a range.

JT’s mouth went dry. Disease, he thought. Rut. Salt lick. Something. Anything that made sense.

The caribou breathed out, and the exhale came as a faint, human-sounding sigh. JT felt it in his own chest, like the air had been borrowed.

Dad’s trigger finger stopped short of pressure. His rifle lowered an inch. His left hand drifted up, palm forward, the way it had in the clearing.

“Dad,” JT hissed, but Dad didn’t hear him. Or pretended not to.

Mike’s rifle sagged, too. His lips moved, shaping a word JT couldn’t catch through the fog. Thank you, maybe. Or *please*. His eyes were glassy, fixed on the animal as if it were a person he recognized.

JT watched them, the three of them—two men and a caribou—locked in a tableau that made no place for him.

The caribou stepped again, closer.

Then, behind it, through the fog, a straight line appeared where no straight line should be.

A wall.

Planks, grey and vertical. A sharp corner. A roofline like a dark hat pulled low.

JT blinked hard. There hadn’t been any structure here. He’d hunted these woods since he could hold a .22. There was no cabin on this ridge.

The caribou drifted sideways, and the cabin stood fully revealed as if the fog had been a curtain pulled back on cue.

Mike let out a soft laugh that was almost a sob. “There it is,” he said, and the way he said it was not surprise.

Dad’s hand dropped. His shoulders loosened. “We’re here,” he murmured, not to JT, not to Mike. To the air.

JT’s scalp prickled. “You knew,” he said, but his voice sounded far away.

They walked toward it because there was nowhere else to walk. The woods funneled them in, trunks angling like crowd barriers.

The cabin sat in a little pocket of cleared ground. It was too intact—no sagging roof, no busted windows. The door hung straight on its hinges. The logs were weathered but not rotted. The place looked maintained in the way abandoned things never are.

Beside the door, nailed to a post, hung a strip of oily canvas folded neatly in thirds, like a flag put away with care.

Dad stopped short of it. His breath came shallow. His eyes brightened again, that wet shine. “Don’t touch anything,” he said, voice hoarse.

Mike stepped past him anyway, hand outstretched like he couldn’t help himself. He didn’t touch the canvas. He just hovered there, fingers trembling.

JT glanced back to the trees.

The caribou was gone.

No crash of brush. No fading hoofbeats. It was simply not in the fog anymore, as if it had stepped behind the cabin and vanished into a space that wasn’t space.

Mike tried the door. It opened inward without a creak.

Inside, the cabin was dim but not dark. Light filtered through the dirty panes, grey and even. The air smelled of old smoke and something sweeter, like tallow.

A table sat in the center of the room.

On it lay a single rifle cartridge, brass polished to a dull shine, placed perfectly in the middle. Next to it, a tin cup turned upside down. A folded scrap of paper, clean-edged, as if it had been torn recently.

JT’s throat tightened. “We didn’t—”

Dad stepped in and the floorboards didn’t squeal. He moved like he’d been here before. Like he was walking through a church.

Mike followed, eyes fixed on the table. “That’s mine,” he whispered.

JT looked at the cartridge. The headstamp was familiar. He checked his pocket without thinking. Felt his own ammo box through the fabric. Full.

The cartridge on the table was not one of his. He could see the small nick on the rim, the way Mike’s reloads always had—Mike’s careless press, Dad had scolded him once. JT had watched Mike do it at this very lodge.

JT reached for the scrap of paper, then stopped. His hand hovered in the air. Something in him refused to break the arrangement.

A sound came from above them.

A footstep.

Slow. Deliberate. On floorboards that shouldn’t exist. The cabin was one story. JT had stood outside and seen the roofline. There was no loft, no second floor, no attic with space to walk.

The step came again, directly overhead, and dust sifted down in a thin line between two rafters.

Mike’s face lifted, eyes wide, pupils huge. He smiled, and it was the wrong kind of smile—soft, grateful. “It’s up,” he breathed.

Dad raised his rifle, not aiming at the ceiling, not aiming at anything. He held it low, like a man holding a candle.

He spoke into the room, quiet but clear. “We brought what you asked,” he said.

JT’s stomach dropped. “What did you bring,” he said, and his voice sounded like someone else’s.

Dad didn’t look at him. His gaze stayed fixed on the empty air above the table. “Hush,” he murmured, the way he’d spoken to JT when he was little and crying in the dark.

Another sound: a scrape, from the inside of the door behind them.

JT snapped around. The door they’d entered by shuddered once, as if something on the other side had leaned against it from within the room. The latch lifted a fraction of an inch, then settled again. The movement was small, precise, like a finger testing a lock.

Mike exhaled a laugh. “It knows,” he said. “It knows we came.”

JT backed away from the door until his shoulder hit the wall. The logs were cold against his jacket. He tasted pennies, metallic fear.

Above, the invisible footsteps moved toward the center of the room, stopping directly over the cartridge.

The air thickened, pressing against JT’s ears. The generator’s hum from the lodge—miles away—seemed suddenly close, then vanished entirely. The world narrowed to the cabin and the three of them inside it and whatever stood just out of sight.

JT saw, in his mind, the clearing again. Dad’s palm raised. Mike’s mouth shaping words. The caribou waiting.

Dad’s finger tightened on his rifle grip, knuckles whitening. His mouth moved. “Thank you,” he whispered, and the words were full of relief, like a prayer answered.

JT couldn’t stand it. He lifted his rifle, aimed at the ceiling where the footfalls had been.

“JT,” Mike said, sharp, as if waking, and for a second his eyes met JT’s with something like warning. Then his gaze slid away again, pulled upward like a magnet.

JT’s hands shook. He could feel the stock against his cheek. “Stop,” he said, but he didn’t know who he was talking to.

The ceiling was just planks and soot-stained beams. Nothing to shoot. Nothing to prove.

And yet the pressure above him felt like a body.

JT squeezed the trigger.

The rifle fired.

The sound did not echo.

No crack bounced off logs. No sharp report punched the air. The shot went off like it had been swallowed by thick cloth, a dull *thump* that JT felt more in his teeth than his ears.

There was no recoil.

The barrel didn’t kick. The stock didn’t slam his shoulder. It was like pulling a trigger in a dream.

For one suspended second, nothing happened.

Then something wet hit JT’s face.

Warm droplets spattered his cheek and lips. He flinched, wiped at it, and his fingers came away slick and dark.

He looked up.
