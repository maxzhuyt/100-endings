I woke before the river bells, mouth thick as felt and sour at the back of my tongue. The air in the room lay stale—old limewash, wet rope, and the sweet rot that climbed up from the Pearl when the tide turned. Somewhere below, a man hawked and spat. Somewhere farther, a sampan pole knocked a hull in a steady, patient tap.

My hand was already in my coat pocket.

The jade figurine sat there like a cold tooth. A small thing, no bigger than my thumb joint, carved into a squat little beast—lion, dog, whatever the craftsman had meant. My nail worried the ridge of its back until the skin went tender.

I forced myself up. The boards complained. The rented room in the foreign factories had a narrow bed, a washstand with a cracked bowl, and a single chair that always looked as if it had given up. The mirror above the stand had a flea-bite in the corner, and in that bite I saw a fragment of my own eye—red-rimmed, impatient.

I stripped and washed. The water was cold enough to bite the wrists. My stomach lurched as if it resented the idea of emptiness. I breathed through my nose and tasted metal anyway.

Uniform next. It took longer than it ought to have, because the fingers would not quite obey. Shirt, stock, then the blue coat with its brass—buttons I had polished last night by lamplight like a man who still believed in rules. I shaved until my jaw ached, not because I needed to but because it was a task with an edge, something the hand could do cleanly.

Orders lay folded on the little table, weighed down by a pewter spoon. The paper looked severe and certain in the grey morning. I did not touch it. I looked at it just long enough to feel the heat of shame rise in my face, then I turned away as if the thing had no claim on me.

A knock came, quick and officious.

“Merrick? Lieutenant Merrick, sir?”

I slid the jade deeper into my pocket, set my shoulders, and opened the door.

A clerk stood there with a ledger under his arm and ink on his fingertips. Young, pale from too many hours inside, hair oiled flat. He glanced past me as if expecting to see the whole of His Majesty’s Navy laid out in my room.

“Sir,” he said, and tried to keep his eyes on my collar. “Mr. Deighton asked if you’d confirmed the—”

“Not now,” I cut in, smiling as if I had all the time in the world. My lips felt dry; I kept them moving so he wouldn’t notice. “Tell Deighton I’ll attend at the godown before the second bell. There’s… a matter of correspondence.”

He blinked. “The manifest review is at ten, sir. Captain Hargreaves—”

“I’m aware of the hour.” My thumb scraped the jade hard enough to sting. “And I am aware of Captain Hargreaves. Do you want to be the one who tells him I was delayed because you kept me in a doorway with questions?”

“No, sir.”

“Then run along.”

He hesitated, shifting the ledger. “There’s also the… the Chinese comprador from the *Mercury* waiting downstairs. He said you promised—”

“Did I?” I kept my tone light, amused. My stomach twisted, a sharp little hook. “Very well. Send him up. Five minutes.”

“Yes, sir.”

He went. I closed the door gently and leaned my forehead against it for a breath, letting the wood take a bit of my weight. The sweat at my lower back felt cold already, as if the skin could not decide whether to burn or shiver.

Five minutes. Ten o’clock. Second bell. The day had its hooks; it would not stop to be kind.

When the knock came again, it was softer.

I opened the door on a Chinese man in a short jacket of dark cloth, hair in a queue, face composed in a way I envied. He held his hands tucked into his sleeves, eyes politely lowered, and yet I felt him looking at everything.

“Lieutenant,” he said in careful English. “Good morning. I am Lin.”

“Lin,” I returned, because that was the name he used with us. “You wanted… what was it? A signature?”

He smiled in a small way that did not touch his eyes. “Yes. For the storage fee. The captain say you can sign. The tide change, boats go. Need quick.”

He offered a paper and a brush pen already wet, as if time were a thing to be managed by preparation. My hand trembled when I took it. I hid the tremor by placing the paper flat on the table and leaning over it, as though my signature required thought.

“What hong is holding it?” I asked.

Lin’s gaze flicked up. “Sun Lee hong.”

Of course. The name sat in my gut like a stone. I signed anyway—my name crisp, practiced, the flourish at the end as clean as it had ever been. A man could ruin himself with such tidy strokes.

Lin took the paper and bowed slightly. “You go to godown later?”

“Before the second bell,” I lied.

He did not contradict me. “The foreign merchants say there is… more watch today. The officials. People nervous.”

“People are always nervous,” I said. My mouth tasted like old pennies. “Tell your captain not to fret.”

Lin’s smile widened by a fraction. “I tell. Thank you, Lieutenant.”

He left without hurry. I watched the door after him, listening to his steps fade on the stairs, and tried not to think of which storage fees I had just approved and why the paper had been so ready.

Outside, Canton woke like a machine taking up its rhythm. I could hear the factories’ courtyard filling—voices in English, Scots, a barked order, a laugh. From beyond the walls, the city proper breathed in another language entirely: vendors calling, wood striking wood, a distant gong, the chatter of boatmen.

I put on my hat, drew my coat close, and walked down into the passage that smelled of damp stone and boiled tea. A runner in a blue vest nodded at my uniform and stepped aside as if I were a law of nature.

At the gate, the guard—Company man, not Navy—touched his cap. “Morning, Lieutenant.”

“Morning.”

“How’s the river?”

“Still wet,” I said, and he chuckled, satisfied.

The sun had burned through the early mist enough to make the river glare like pewter. The foreign factories lined the waterfront with their orderly facades, flags hanging limp in the heavy air. Beyond their walls, Canton rose in layers—tile roofs, carved eaves, narrow lanes that vanished between buildings like cuts in paper.

I should have turned right toward the godowns, toward the neat stacks of tea and silk and the men who would ask for my presence as if it mattered.

Instead I turned left, toward the press of boats and the smell that grew stronger with each step: fish, tar, human waste, incense, cooking oil. The uniform moved among the Chinese crowd like a knife through cloth. Men watched and did not watch. Women with baskets angled their bodies away, eyes down. A child stared until his mother yanked him close.

The jade warmed in my pocket, or perhaps my hand warmed it. I could not tell anymore where my body ended and the need began.

At the edge of the foreign quarter the street tightened, the buildings leaning closer, the light narrowing. Signs hung with black characters on white boards, paint flaking at the edges. A man passed with a pole across his shoulders and baskets swinging, the bamboo flexing with each step. He smelled of sweat and river water. His basket brushed my sleeve; I felt the damp through the wool.

“Lieutenant.”

The voice came from a doorway that was not quite a doorway—more a shadow between two shops. A man stepped out, half in light, half in shade. He wore European trousers with a Chinese jacket, a compromise stitched together. His hair was cut short in the foreign style, but he kept a queue braided thin as if he could not bring himself to sever it fully.

“Chao,” I said, as if we were merely acquaintances who met by chance in daylight.

“Mr. Merrick,” he returned, because he liked to remind me I was a man before I was a rank. His English was fluent and dry. “You walk far from your bells.”

“They ring whether I hear them or not.”

He watched my face. His eyes were quick, like a clerk’s, and missed nothing. “You look unwell.”

“Canton doesn’t suit me,” I said.

“Canton suits everyone,” he replied. “Only question is what it takes.”

The lane behind him smelled of incense and something sour, like old smoke trapped in cloth. A lacquered door further in bore a chipped red paint that had once been bright. A paper charm was pasted above it, curling at the corners.

I swallowed. My throat clicked. “Is he in?”

Chao’s mouth lifted in a humourless smile. “He is always in. Come.”

The lane swallowed us. The noise of the riverfront dulled, as if a hand had been laid over it. Here the sounds were closer: a cough behind a screen, the murmur of a woman’s voice, the scrape of a chair on tile. A brazier hissed somewhere, and the scent of charcoal mixed with the cloying sweetness of burned resin.

My skin prickled with sweat though the air was cooler in the shade. My gut cramped once, hard, then eased into a constant low threat. I kept my hand in my pocket because if I took it out it might shake in view.

We stopped at the lacquered door. Chao did not knock. He spoke a phrase in Cantonese, low and quick. There was a pause long enough for my heart to begin to race, and then the door opened a finger’s width.

A face appeared, young, eyes assessing. The door widened. We slipped inside.

The room beyond was dim, lit by a single oil lamp that made the shadows heavy. A mat lay on the floor, and a low table held a teapot and cups. The smell here was stronger—sweet smoke embedded in wood, in cloth, in hair. It clung to the back of my throat like syrup.

Behind the table sat a man in a long robe of dark silk. His hair was grey at the temples, pulled back neat. His hands were clean, nails trimmed and pale, as if he did nothing more strenuous than turn pages.

He looked up, and his eyes were mild as water.

“Lieutenant Merrick,” he said in English with a careful accent. “You come early.”

“Do I?” I forced myself to step forward, to bow my head just enough to be polite without being submissive. “Your man said you were available.”

“My man says many things.” He gestured to the mat opposite. “Sit.”

I sat. The movement made my stomach roll. The jade pressed into my palm like a reminder to keep my fingers closed.

Chao remained standing near the door, neither guard nor guest, his posture relaxed in a way that still suggested readiness.

The dealer poured tea. The stream made a small sound, intimate. He slid a cup toward me.

I lifted it. The smell was sharp, bitter, too clean. I sipped because refusing would be a kind of weakness. The tea hit my empty stomach like a slap.

“You want the usual?” the dealer asked.

“I want what works,” I said. My voice sounded steadier than I felt.

He nodded as if this were a reasonable preference. “Quality costs. You know.”

“I know.”

He waited. Silence in that room had weight. I could hear my own pulse in my ears. The jade was slick now; my thumb had rubbed a groove in the skin beside the nail.

“I don’t have enough coin,” I said, because the lie would have been worse and he would have known. “Not today.”

The dealer’s expression did not change. “Not today,” he repeated softly, as if tasting the phrase. “But you want today.”

“Yes.”

He set his cup down precisely. “Then you pay another way.”

Chao’s eyes slid to me, then away, as if he did not want to be seen witnessing my fall. My shame rose hot and useless.

“What do you want?” I asked.

The dealer’s fingernail tapped the table once. “A small help. One paper. One name.”

“I don’t sign manifests for you,” I said, though I had signed a storage fee an hour ago without asking too many questions.

He smiled slightly. “Not for me. For river.”

I held his gaze. The lamplight made his pupils glossy. “Speak plainly.”

He lifted a hand, palm up, as if offering something harmless. “There is a crate in a godown near Honam. Not mine. Not yours. The clerk will say it is tea. It is not tea. It needs to be… moved. Before dusk.”

My stomach tightened. “You want me to move contraband through Company eyes.”

He tilted his head. “You are Navy. Not Company. You have different eyes.”

“And different consequences,” I said.

Chao cleared his throat softly. “Mr. Merrick,” he murmured, “it is only one crate.”

The dealer’s voice remained mild. “One crate, one pass. You have friends at the water gate. You make them look other way. Then you have your medicine. And tomorrow, perhaps, you have coin again.”

Tomorrow. The word felt like a threat.

“I can’t be seen,” I said, and hated how small it sounded.

The dealer’s gaze dropped to my pocket, to the slight bulge where the jade sat. I felt suddenly naked. “You will not be seen,” he said. “You will be… useful.”

I drew a breath. The air smelled of sweet smoke and clean tea and my own fear.

“What do I get?” I asked.

His smile deepened by a fraction. “Relief.”

“And the crate?”

He shrugged, a graceful foreign gesture on his Chinese frame. “It goes where it goes. It is not your concern.”

It would be my concern the moment something went wrong. It would be my concern in the shape of a rope, a court-martial, a bullet. But my hands were trembling now, a fine vibration I could not stop, and my mouth watered unpleasantly.

I leaned forward, forcing my voice to remain level. “I’ll do it,” I said. “But I want the better quality. Not the scrap you gave me last week.”

Chao’s eyebrows lifted, surprised by my audacity or my desperation.

The dealer studied me. Then he nodded. “You bargain like a man who still thinks he has choices.”

“I have some,” I said, though it sounded like bravado even to me.

He reached beside him and took up a small wrapped parcel—oil paper tied with string. He placed it on the table but did not push it toward me.

“After,” he said. “You bring the crate to the small pier by the broken willow. You know it. Then you come back. Then you take.”

My jaw clenched. I tasted metal again. The jade dug into my palm. “Credit,” I said. “Half now.”

The dealer’s eyes sharpened for the first time. Not anger—calculation. “Half now,” he repeated, and looked at Chao.

Chao’s lips pressed together. “He is shaking,” Chao said quietly, as if that were a fact about weather.

The dealer sighed as if indulging a child. He untied the string, opened the parcel, and pinched off a small portion with a tool—a neat brown-black bead. He wrapped it separately and slid it across the table with one finger.

“Enough for you to walk,” he said. “Not enough for you to sleep.”

My hand closed over it too quickly. I hated myself for the speed. “Fine,” I said.

The dealer retied the parcel. “Before dusk,” he reminded me, voice soft as silk.

I stood. My knees felt hollow. Chao moved with me, guiding without touching. At the door, the young man who had admitted us watched my uniform with an unreadable expression, as if he had seen too many uniforms come through for comfort.

Outside, the lane’s light seemed harsh after the dim room. The world pressed in close—the smell of frying dough, the stink of drains, the thick human air. My pulse hammered, but underneath it was a thin thread of relief: I had something. Not enough. But something.

Chao walked beside me, his hands still tucked in his sleeves.

“You know where the godown is,” he said.

“I know,” I answered. “How do I get the crate out?”

“You go as officer,” Chao said, as if it were simple. “You ask to inspect. You say there is rumour of theft. The clerk will be afraid. He will show you everything.”

“And the crate?”

Chao’s glance slid to me. “It will have a mark. Three small scratches on the corner. Like this.” He mimed it with his thumb against his sleeve. “You tell porter take it to your boat.”

“My boat?” I snapped.

Chao’s smile appeared again, brief. “Not your boat. A boat. You hire. You pay.”

“I don’t have coin,” I said.

Chao’s eyes flicked to my pocket again, to the weight of the jade. “You have something,” he said mildly. “Everyone has something.”

The river bells rang a little later—one, then another—marking the hour. The sound moved through the city like a command. Somewhere, men would be gathering for inspections. Somewhere, my name might be spoken.

I walked faster, toward Honam, toward the godown district where the warehouses squatted like fat beasts along the water. The streets widened, filled with carts, coolies, bales. The air here was thick with tea dust and river mud. Men shouted in Cantonese, in pidgin English, in Portuguese. A boatman cursed as he hauled a rope.

At the godown gate, a Company guard eyed my uniform and straightened.

“Lieutenant,” he said, wary respect. “Didn’t expect—”

“Neither did I,” I replied, and made my voice brisk. “There’s talk of pilferage. Captain Hargreaves doesn’t like talk. He likes answers.”

The guard swallowed. “Yes, sir.”

“Fetch the clerk,” I said. “Now.”

He ran. My heart pounded. My hand found the jade and clenched it until the carved edges bit. The withdrawal made every sound sharp, every smell too strong. Sweat ran under my collar. I kept my face composed because faces were what men read in Canton.
