The studio was already awake when I pushed the door in with my shoulder.

Light spilled off the sprung floor like it had been waxed for a photoshoot. A full mirrored wall ran the length of the north side, seam down the center like a spine. Ballet barres hugged the mirror, brushed steel and pale wood. A stack of speakers sat to the front-left, a black camera tripod planted near the front-right like an extra person waiting to be told where to stand. Two long folding tables along the west wall held paper cups and water jugs, towels folded into tidy rectangles.

Above the mirror, dead center, a digital wall clock glowed white numbers against black. It wasn’t telling the time of day. It was counting down.

I slipped to the back-left corner—stage left, if the mirror was the audience—and set my bag down small, like it might apologize for being there. My shoes were clean but tired at the edges, soles gone soft from too many sidewalks. My shirt was a thrift-store black that had been washed into gray. Around me, trainees drifted in wearing matching sets, seamless tops, socks that still looked new. They moved in clean lines even when they weren’t trying: calves flexing as they pointed and rolled, fingers splayed in mirrored symmetry, hair slicked and fixed.

No one took the back-left unless they meant to disappear.

I faced the mirror and found myself twice: the person standing there and the person trying not to take up space. The clock’s numbers fell one by one like a slow drip. I watched them without meaning to, feeling the rules of the room settle onto my shoulders before anyone spoke them out loud.

Tape X’s dotted the floor in a grid—thin white crosses spaced evenly, four rows and four columns. A map disguised as decoration. I chose the X in Row 3, Column 1, two meters from the mirror and one meter in from the west wall, far enough back that I could hide behind a shoulder if I needed to.

Row 2, Column 1 was directly in front of me, occupied by a girl with a razor-sharp bob and a white jacket zipped to the throat. She checked her spacing by glancing at the mirror’s seam, then at the X, then at her own toes. To my right, Row 3, Column 2, a tall guy with broad shoulders stepped onto his tape like he was clicking into a socket. He nodded once, not to me exactly, but to the fact of a neighbor.

The door was on the east wall, near the back. Every time it opened, a thin line of hallway light cut across the floor and then vanished as the door shut. The instructor’s table was near the front-right corner, just below the clock’s right edge, set with a tablet, a notebook, and a small silver clicker like a remote control for people.

The grid filled in. Sixteen bodies, each one a number that could be moved.

The studio went quiet before the instructor even arrived. That was how you could tell they were famous: the air preemptively arranged itself.

Sharp shoes on wood. Not sneakers—dress shoes, hard soles. The sound cut straight through the room’s hum and landed in my ribs. The instructor walked in from the east door without looking around, as if the space belonged to them by default. They crossed to dead center front and stopped under the clock. From where I stood, I had to look past their shoulders to see the numbers draining.

They held a phone in one hand, thumb hovering over a metronome app, and the silver clicker in the other. No wasted motion. They didn’t smile. They didn’t need to.

“Marks,” they said. One word, flat. The room snapped into place: chins up, shoulders down, feet aligned. Even my neighbor to the right shifted half an inch without lifting his gaze, like correction was a reflex.

The instructor lifted the phone, tapped, and a thin clicking beat began. They watched the mirror the way a surgeon watches a monitor.

“Silence.” Their voice wasn’t loud, just sealed. “Posture.”

We stood in parallel, feet hip-width, knees straight without locking. Then turned out. Then parallel again. The instructor paced the front line, eyes scanning levels: shoulders, ribs, pelvis, knees, ankles. They called out corrections like coordinates.

“Ribcage—down.” A girl in Row 1 exhaled like she’d been punched and adjusted.

“Neck—longer.” A boy’s chin lifted a fraction.

They clapped once. A sharp report.

“Five-six-seven-eight.”

We began the warm-up on their count. No music yet. Just metronome and breathing. Arms up in second, down, roll the shoulders back without lifting them, shift weight cleanly, isolate chest forward and back, side to side, nothing extra. The mirror multiplied our compliance. When one person’s elbow floated too high, three others dropped theirs as if guilt was contagious.

I knew discipline. I’d drilled footwork until my toes burned. But this was different. This wasn’t practice; this was inspection.

The instructor’s gaze flicked over me and didn’t pause, and I told myself that meant I was safe. Then the girl in front of me glanced back through the mirror, saw my feet a hair off the tape, and slid her own heel a centimeter to the left, creating a line I couldn’t ignore.

Without turning, she whispered, “On the X.”

Not unkind. Not kind. Just fact.

I nudged my left shoe onto the tape. The X kissed the sole.

“Counts first,” the instructor said, tapping the clicker against their palm as if punctuating each syllable. “Texture later—if you earn it.”

The metronome stopped. Music blasted in, crisp and bright, engineered to be counted. The instructor faced the mirror and demonstrated the phrase like they were drawing it in the air.

“Watch. No noise.”

Count 1–2: step-touch right, left, sharp, elbows locked at ninety degrees, forearms slicing forward like blades.

3&4: pivot to a downstage-right diagonal—toward the mirror’s right side—two chest pops, isolated, hands framing the cheekbones, fingers tight.

5–6: travel forward two small steps on the balls of the feet, knees lifting to identical height, no bounce.

7–8: snap into a picture pose—weight centered, head angled precisely, jaw still.

It was clean. It was undeniable. It made my own body feel loud.

“Row by row,” the instructor said. “Back row first.”

That meant us. Row 4 then Row 3. My back-left corner suddenly wasn’t a hiding place; it was first in line.

“Five-six-seven-eight.”

I stepped-touch right, left. My elbows tried to lock and my shoulders tried to ride up with the effort. On 3&4 I pivoted to the diagonal and hit the first chest pop clean, the second one softer, because my body wanted to breathe through it. My hands rose to my cheekbones, but my wrists loosened. On 5–6 I traveled forward and my foot rolled through the step like I was gliding, like the floor was a friend.

On 7–8 I snapped—or tried to. But I landed half a shoe-length ahead of my tape X, my right elbow flared wider than the others, and my head hit the angle late, a fraction of a beat that felt microscopic until I saw it in the mirror.

In unison, a fraction is a fracture.

The instructor’s hand cut down.

The music stopped mid-count. Silence fell so hard I could hear the speakers’ faint electrical hiss.

They didn’t look angry. That would have been easier. They looked like they’d spotted a stain.

They walked straight to Row 3, Column 1—my square—without checking a map, without doubt. Their shoes clicked a metronome of their own. They stopped close enough that I could smell their perfume or cologne, something clean and expensive, like hotel soap.

Their voice was still even. “You.”

I froze, then bowed my head slightly because I’d seen others do it, because I’d learned honorifics from subtitles and panic, because the room had a language I only half spoke.

They placed two fingers on my right shoulder and pressed down, not hard, just absolute. My shoulder dropped. They hooked a finger at my hip and rotated it a degree so my pelvis squared to the mirror. They nudged my left foot back onto the tape X, heel aligned to the cross’s center, then adjusted my right foot’s turnout by a hair.

Their hands were precise, not gentle.

“Again. Alone,” they said.

The rest of the trainees stayed on their marks, statues in branded fabric, eyes forward. No one moved. But in the mirror, sixteen sets of eyes were on me without turning their heads.

The clock glowed above the instructor’s head. The numbers fell. The room had a timer and I was burning it.

Heat rose under my thrift-store tee. I could feel the sweat at my spine, the cheap cotton darkening where it touched my skin.

“It’s the floor,” I said before I could stop myself. My voice sounded too big in the quiet. “It’s—slick.”

The instructor tilted their head a millimeter. “The floor is the same for everyone.”

“I’m on count,” I insisted, hating the way my mouth kept talking, the way ego scrambled for cover. “I’m just—adding—”

“Adding is not asked,” they said, and tapped the clicker once, like a judge stamping a document. “Five-six-seven-eight.”

I did it. Step-touch. Pivot. Chest pops. Travel. Picture.

My body tried to put a little pocket into the step-touch, a softness between counts. I killed it. My arms trembled with the effort of being sharp. On the picture, my head hit on time. My elbow stayed in. My feet landed on tape.

It felt like swallowing something too big.

“Again,” the instructor said, and I went again. My breath caught. My brain shouted counts at my muscles like they were a foreign language.

They didn’t say good. They didn’t say bad. They just watched the mirror and let the clock keep eating time.

Then they turned away from me as if I’d been filed.

“Formation change,” they said.

The instructor pointed at the grid like moving chess pieces. “Front line, step forward. Row two—reassign.”

My stomach sank. I’d been hoping to be sent farther back, punished into invisibility. Instead, the instructor’s finger landed on me.

“You. Row 2, Column 2.”

Not the front, but close enough that the mirror’s seam couldn’t hide me. I stepped forward and right, crossing behind the girl with the bob, careful not to clip her elbow. My new mark was in the second row, second column—near center-left. To my left was Row 2, Column 1, the bob. To my right was Row 2, Column 3, a dancer with long limbs and a calm face, the kind of calm that meant she never missed.

The instructor placed me next to the strongest trainee in the room—Row 2, Column 3—as if my body needed a reference point like a ruler.

“Watch your spacing,” the bob whispered without moving her lips.

We ran the phrase again in formation.

Then the instructor extended it.

“Now sixteen,” they said. “Travel.”

Counts 1–8: the same phrase, but the travel on 5–6 carried us diagonally toward center, converging like lines drawn to a point.

On count 5, everyone dropped into a low squat—level change synchronized, thighs parallel, heels grounded.

On 6–7, a turn to face away from the mirror, backs to our own reflections, as if denying ourselves proof.

On 8 and into the next eight-count, backward travel returning to the original grid, steps measured so the tape X’s caught us like magnets.

More bodies. More geometry. Less air.

“Five-six-seven-eight.”

We moved.

Diagonal travel: my shoulder nearly brushed the calm dancer’s arm on my right; I could feel her heat without contact. The bob on my left was a blade cutting through space. On the low squat, my knees wanted to wobble. Everyone else dropped like a machine. Facing away from the mirror, I lost my anchor—no seam, no reflected line—and for a split second my body reached for something familiar: groove, the way you ride the beat when no one is watching.

My heel landed a half-inch too wide. The calm dancer adjusted, barely visible, shifting her foot to avoid collision. In the mirror, even though my back was to it, I saw the ripple: one tiny correction radiating through a line like a crack in ice.

The instructor’s clicker snapped.

“No music,” they said.

The speakers went dead. The room emptied of everything but breath and rubber squeaks and the instructor’s voice.

“Counts only.”

“Five-six-seven-eight.”

We did it in silence. Step-touch right, left—sneakers whispering. Pivot. Chest pop. Chest pop. Travel. Picture. Diagonal. Drop. Turn. Backward travel.

Without music, the counts were naked. There was nowhere to hide inside a bass line, nowhere to tuck a mistake into a snare.

My body tried to behave. I locked my elbows. I kept my wrists hard. I measured my travel: two small steps, not two-and-a-glide.

Then came the transition: a pas de bourrée-like step—back, side, front—into a sharp hit.

My footwork knew it. My brain counted it.

But my arm betrayed me.

On the step, my shoulder wanted to roll. A wave slipped from my wrist up through my elbow before I could stop it, a smooth ripple that stole time like a thief. It was a half-beat. A breath. A blink.

I arrived late to the picture.

Not late like falling behind in traffic. Late like a door slamming and your fingers still in the frame.

The calm dancer’s eyes flicked toward me in the mirror for the first time, quick as a warning light. She adjusted her spacing again, tiny, instinctive, to keep our shoulders from colliding.

The instructor’s hand went up.

“Stop.”

We froze mid-breath. My thighs burned from the level change, sweat prickling under my shirt. The clock’s numbers continued to fall, indifferent.

The instructor walked to Row 2, Column 2—my new square. Their shoes clicked on the wood like punctuation.

“Two counts,” they said. “Only two. Again.”

They demonstrated: pas de bourrée step—back, side, front—then a hit, knife-sharp, energy contained in the same pathway, same endpoint.

“Five-six,” they counted.

I did it. My arm stayed stiff like a plank.

“Again.”

“Five-six.”

Again. My shoulder twitched.

“Again.”

“Five-six.”

The room watched without moving. Sixteen people holding still while I lived inside two counts. My breath got loud. I tried to make it small, but it kept coming.

“Again.”

On the fifth repetition, the wave tried to rise like nausea. I felt it in my forearm first, the old comfort of letting the beat travel through me. I crushed it down, turned it into a hard line. The hit landed on time.

The instructor didn’t nod. They just stared at the mirror as if confirming a measurement.

Then they leaned in, close enough that only I and maybe the bob and the calm dancer could hear.

Their voice lowered, not soft, just private. “You have attack.”

My chest tightened with something that wasn’t relief yet.

“You hear music,” they continued. “That is rare.”

I swallowed. The room was too bright. The mirror held my face in a way I couldn’t control.

“But,” the instructor said, and the word was a blade sliding out of a sheath, “it is useless if you cannot repeat it on command.”

They lifted a hand and mimicked my wave, the one that had stolen time. In their body it looked beautiful—of course it did. Then they showed how it would be here: the same energy, but standardized, forced into a strict pathway, wave flattened into a line that hit the endpoint every time.

“Same attack,” they said. “Same pathway. Same endpoint. Every time.”

Their eyes met mine in the mirror. Not unkind. Not cruel. Clinical.

A thought flashed through me, sharp and unwanted: If I can do it their way, doors open. If I keep my way, I might go home with nothing but a story about almost.

The horror wasn’t that they wanted to break me. The horror was that they could make it sound like help.

They stepped back to center front, under the clock again, reclaiming their place beneath time.

“One full-out run before break,” they said. “No mistakes.”

We returned to starting positions—back to the grid, back to the tape X’s like returning to assigned seats. My feet found Row 2, Column 2. Facing the mirror, I could see my neighbors perfectly: bob on my left, calm dancer on my right, front line ahead, the instructor in the middle like a needle pointing north.

The clock glared. The numbers read 12:14.

My lungs drew in air that tasted like dust and perfume and effort. My muscles hummed with fatigue, the kind that makes you honest.

The instructor lifted their hand.

“Five,” they counted.

My shoulders settled down.

“Six.”

My fingers tightened.

“Seven.”

My knees softened, ready.

“Eight.”

The first eight-count began.

Step-touch right, left. Sharp. Elbows locked. Forearms slicing. No bounce.

Pivot. Chest pop. Chest pop. Hands at cheekbones, fingers tight.

Travel forward two steps, knees lifted identical height.

Count 7–8: picture.

This was the fork. The moment was small enough to fit inside a breath, but it was everything.

I landed count 8 squared with the line, head angle exact, elbow in, feet centered on the tape. Clean. Replicable. The wave stayed caged inside my arm like an animal that had learned the size of its enclosure.

For a fraction of a second, the formation in the mirror looked perfect.

The instructor’s eyes flicked, almost imperceptibly, and their chin dipped—one millimeter. Not approval. Confirmation.

“Again,” they said, cold and calm, as if the first run had never happened. “Twelve more times.”

The clicker snapped. The metronome app ticked. Sneakers squeaked as we reset on our X’s.

“Five-six-seven—”
