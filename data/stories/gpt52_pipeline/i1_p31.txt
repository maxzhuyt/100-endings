The car didn’t even stop all the way. It rolled up to the curb like it was allergic to stillness, the tires whispering over wet-looking asphalt that wasn’t wet, just dark from old rain and older spills.

“Here,” the driver said, eyes forward, hand already on the gearshift. The AC in the front fought the New Orleans heat and lost in slow motion. “Downtown. This is as far as I’m going.”

My backpack was on my lap. The zipper teeth had chewed a little crescent into my thumb earlier and it still stung. My phone was a brick in my pocket—screen black, battery icon in my head like a joke. I had a hundred percent courage when I got in the car in Tennessee, and somewhere around the Mississippi line it started dropping in increments I could feel.

“I—” My voice cracked. It always did when I tried to make it sound like it belonged to me.

The driver finally looked at me. Not mean, exactly. More like tired, like I was a stain he’d been hoping would wipe off.

“Don’t make it my problem,” he said. “You got out here. That’s what you wanted.”

The words landed with the soft humiliation of a door shutting. I shoved the backpack strap over my shoulder with hands that wouldn’t stop shaking. My nails were ragged. I could see myself in the tinted window, a blur: too-small T-shirt, hair smashed from sleeping wrong, face caught between boy and not-boy and the constant fear of being called the wrong thing out loud.

I stepped out.

Heat hit like a palm to the chest. Noise hit like a body.

The car rolled away before I could change my mind. Exhaust, then nothing but the city.

New Orleans did not pause for me. It did not open its arms. It swallowed. There were people everywhere: tourists in bright shirts and beads like they’d been born with them, locals moving like they’d memorized the sidewalk, vendors with carts that smoked and hissed, a man on a corner yelling about salvation, a cop’s uniform blue in the periphery like a bruise.

And under all of it—threaded through it—music. A saxophone, somewhere, playing something that sounded both lazy and urgent, like it didn’t care if anyone listened and also like it needed someone to.

My mouth tasted like pennies. I licked my lips and felt them crack.

Don’t go back, I told myself. The only plan. The only commandment.

I started walking because standing still made me feel like a target with a blinking light.

The first block was a blur of smells: frying something sweet, old beer, river damp, perfume too expensive and sweat too honest. My backpack pulled on my shoulder like a reminder: you brought what you could carry and it wasn’t enough.

I tried to look like I belonged. I tried to make my walk loose. I tried to keep my shoulders from hunching like I expected a hand to grab my collar and jerk me backward into “home.”

Rule one formed without permission: Don’t stop.

Rule two: Don’t stare.

Rule three: Don’t look lost.

The problem was I was lost in every possible sense.

My phone stayed dead. I pressed the side button anyway, like I could bully it into life. Nothing. I could almost hear my mom’s voice in my head—Taylor, why can’t you just—except she didn’t use Taylor anymore once she decided it was just “a phase” and started calling me by the name on my birth certificate with a sharp sweetness that meant punishment.

My fingers tightened around the backpack strap until my knuckles went white. My hands were shaking. If anyone looked close, they’d see it. If anyone looked close, they’d see everything.

A convenience store sign glowed a block ahead. My throat felt like sandpaper. Water. I needed water. I patted my pocket. A few wrinkled bills. Not much. Enough for one bottle, maybe, if the city wasn’t as expensive as it looked.

Inside, it was cooler. A clerk behind bulletproof glass watched me like I’d already stolen something. I walked to the fridge, grabbed the cheapest water I could find, and held it in my hand like it was a life raft.

My brain did quick math. If I bought this, I’d have less later. If I didn’t, I’d be stupid now.

Rule four: Don’t be thirsty on purpose.

At the counter, the clerk’s eyes flicked over me—hair, chest, hips, the way my T-shirt hung. His mouth twitched like he’d decided what I was and didn’t approve of it.

“Sir,” he said, then corrected himself too fast, “ma’am—”

My stomach dropped through my feet.

I forced my voice lower. “Just—just this.”

He rang it up. I handed over cash with fingers that didn’t want to cooperate. My voice wanted to disappear. I wanted to disappear.

Outside again, the heat slapped me for leaving the cooler air. I unscrewed the cap and drank too fast. Water spilled down my chin. I wiped it with the back of my hand and kept moving.

The saxophone came and went as I walked—sometimes louder, sometimes swallowed by traffic, by laughter, by a bus sighing to a stop. It tugged at me like a hook in soft tissue. I hated that it did. I loved that it did.

Rule five: Follow sound only if you want trouble.

My feet ignored me.

The street opened up into something like a corridor of people. There were balconies above, ironwork like black lace, and the smell of something spicy and alive. I drifted with the crowd until I saw the source of the music.

A man stood near a corner, saxophone gleaming dull gold, hat on the ground for tips. His cheeks puffed, his eyes closed like he was somewhere else. The song was slow and bruised. It made my ribs ache. It made my throat tighten in a way that wasn’t about thirst.

I stopped.

Rule one screamed. Don’t stop. Don’t stop.

But my body did it anyway, like the music was a hand on my shoulder. The crowd flowed around me. My breathing synced to the saxophone without asking. For a few seconds, I wasn’t a runaway with a dead phone and a backpack full of nothing. I was just a person listening. A person allowed to stand still.

It almost undid me. Grief came up like bile—Tennessee, the kitchen table, the way my dad’s face went hard when I said the word trans out loud, like I’d brought a virus into the house. The way my mom’s eyes went wet, then cold. The way my name became a weapon again.

If I cry right here I’m done, I told myself. If I cry, someone will see. Someone will decide something about me.

The saxophone bent a note, made it waver like it was about to fall apart and then held it anyway. My eyes stung.

“First time you hearin’ him?” a voice said near my elbow.

I flinched so hard my shoulders jumped. The water bottle crinkled in my grip.

A kid—teen, maybe—leaned against the wall like it belonged to them. Slim, maybe my age or a little older, skin the color of café au lait, hair in short twists under a cap. Their eyes were sharp, amused without being kind. They wore shorts despite the heat like everyone else, but there was something in the way they stood that wasn’t tourist, wasn’t lost. It was practiced.

They looked me up and down without making it obvious, which somehow made it worse.

“You ain’t from here,” they said. Not a question.

My tongue stuck. “I—” I could hear my own cracked voice again. Hate it. Hate it. “No.”

They nodded like they’d checked a box. “You got that look. Like you just got dropped off and somebody told you good luck.”

My face burned. Humiliation is a fast heat.

“I’m fine,” I said, automatically, because that’s what you say when you’re not.

The kid snorted. “Sure. Fine with the dead phone and the ‘please don’t talk to me’ shoulders.”

My hand went to my pocket without thinking, checking the phone like I’d left it somewhere obvious. They’d clocked it anyway. Clocked me.

“I didn’t—” I started.

They held up one hand. “Relax. I’m not a cop. Cops don’t dress like this.” They flicked their fingers down their own outfit like it was evidence. “Name’s Jax.”

“Taylor,” I said, then immediately felt my stomach tighten like I’d handed them a key. My name was the only thing I’d kept that felt like mine.

Jax tilted their head. “Taylor, huh.”

The saxophone slid into a brighter riff. The musician didn’t look at either of us.

Jax jerked their chin toward a patch of shade under an awning. “You gonna pass out standin’ in the sun like that. Come on.”

Rule six tried to form: Don’t follow strangers.

But my throat was still tight and my phone was dead and the city was huge. And Jax was the first person who’d spoken to me like I was a person instead of a problem.

I followed.

Under the awning, the air was still hot, but at least it wasn’t directly trying to cook me. Jax squatted, back against the wall, like they’d done this exact movement a thousand times. They pulled a half-crushed bottle of water from a tote bag and took a sip, then offered it to me without ceremony.

I hesitated.

Jax’s eyebrow lifted. “You don’t gotta drink it. I ain’t tryna poison you. That costs extra.”

A laugh escaped me before I could stop it. It sounded wrong in my mouth, like a noise borrowed from someone braver.

I took the bottle and drank a careful mouthful. The water was warm but real. I handed it back.

“So,” Jax said, like we were just two kids hanging out, like I wasn’t a raw nerve, “rule one: don’t stand still where folks can decide you look like money or trouble.”

“I was listening,” I said, defensive, then softer, “to the sax.”

Jax’s gaze flicked toward the musician. “Yeah, he’ll get you. He do that.” Their voice changed just a fraction, something like respect, something like longing. Then it snapped back. “Rule two: don’t go into stores like you got nothin’ to lose. They smell that. They watch you harder.”

“I paid,” I muttered.

“I know. I saw.” Jax tapped their temple. “Rule three: if you need to charge that dead brick, you don’t do it at the tourist spots unless you want somebody askin’ you why you ain’t got a mama.”

My chest tightened. The word mama hit like a thumb on a bruise.

“You got somewhere to go?” Jax asked, casual, but their eyes were not.

“No,” I said, and it came out too honest, too quick.

Jax nodded again, like another checkbox. “Figured. You look… young.”

“I’m sixteen,” I said before I could stop myself. My mouth kept betraying me.

Jax whistled low. “Okay. Okay.” They leaned closer, dropping their voice. “Listen. You don’t tell that to everybody. You tell that to the wrong person, they start thinkin’ they can own you.”

The words made my skin crawl because they sounded like truth.

“Why are you—” I started, then stopped. Why are you helping me? felt like a trap question. People didn’t help for free. Not in Tennessee. Not anywhere.

Jax smiled without showing teeth. “Why? ’Cause I’m bored. ’Cause I remember my first day. Pick one.”

“What’s the price?” I asked. My voice came out flatter than I meant.

Jax laughed softly. “There it is.” They dug into their tote and pulled out a small portable charger—one of those little bricks with a cable. They waved it like a magician. “I got juice. You want it, you walk with me for a bit. You don’t wander off alone. That’s it.”

“That’s it?” I didn’t believe them. My brain kept adding: until you owe me, until you’re trapped, until you can’t say no.

Jax’s smile faded just enough. “Also,” they said, and now it was blunt, “if you got cash, you buy us somethin’ to eat later. I ain’t your charity. You ain’t mine.”

Relief and suspicion braided together in my chest.

“Okay,” I said, because hunger made decisions easier. “Okay.”

Jax held out the charger. “Gimme your phone.”

I fished it out. The screen was a dark mirror that showed my face for half a second—my eyes too big, my jaw too soft, the faint shadow I wanted to exist and didn’t.

Jax plugged it in. A tiny light blinked on the charger. My phone stayed black for a beat, then the screen flickered weakly, like a heart restarting. Battery: one percent.

I exhaled like I’d been holding my breath since Tennessee.

“See?” Jax said. “Logistics. You live by ’em.”

My phone buzzed weakly with old notifications trying to catch up. No service bars. Of course.

Jax kept talking, like they were giving a tour. “Bathroom situation: you go in places where you look like you belong. Coffee shops. Big hotels if you act like you know where you goin’. You don’t ask permission. You just do it.”

My stomach dropped.

Bathrooms. The word was a trapdoor. In Tennessee it had been a battlefield. Here it was still a battlefield, just with different uniforms.

Jax glanced at me, too quick. “You… good?”

I swallowed. My tongue felt thick. Say it. Don’t say it. If I say it, they can use it. If I don’t, I’m alone in it.

“I,” I began, then my voice went small despite me. “People—get it wrong.”

Jax’s eyes narrowed, not mean, just focusing. “Yeah,” they said slowly. “They do.”

The saxophone outside hit a sharp high note, then slid down like a sigh.

I almost said I’m trans right there. The word hovered behind my teeth, daring me. But I pictured my dad’s face, the way the word turned him into stone. I pictured the driver’s last line: Don’t make it my problem.

So I swallowed it. The truth went down like something too big.

Jax didn’t push. They just nodded like they’d filed it away. “Then you keep your head up,” they said. “And you don’t argue with strangers. You let ’em be wrong and you keep movin’.”

It was advice that tasted like defeat. It was also survival.

We started walking together, Jax half a step ahead like they were used to being followed. The charger cable dangled between us like a leash I’d agreed to.

They pointed things out without pointing. “That corner? Don’t. That alley? Hell no. That man with the white shoes? He ain’t lookin’ for conversation. He lookin’ for somebody to buy.”

My skin prickled. “Buy what?”

Jax’s glance cut sideways, quick as a knife. “You don’t need that lesson today.”

I wanted to ask how long they’d been here. I wanted to ask where their parents were. I wanted to ask if they were okay. All the questions felt like stepping onto thin ice.

We passed a group of cops standing near a patrol car, laughing like the heat didn’t bother them. My spine went rigid.

Jax’s hand flicked out, caught my elbow—not hard, just a correction. “Don’t run,” they murmured. “Just walk.”

My heart started banging anyway, stupid and loud. In my head, Tennessee flashed like a strobe: my dad’s hand slamming the table, the sound so sharp I jumped; the way my mom said, If you leave, don’t come back, like she was doing me a favor; the way I flinched before anyone even moved.

This is what home taught me: flinch first.

We walked past the cops like we were made of air. One of them looked at us, eyes sliding over. My throat closed. I expected a shout. A hand. A question.

Nothing.

We turned a corner. The noise of the main street dulled, replaced by quieter traffic and the hum of people existing without performing for tourists. Jax led me under another awning, this one attached to a cheap diner with a flickering sign. The kind of place that served coffee that tasted like it had opinions.

Inside, the air was cold enough to make goosebumps rise on my arms. Jax slid into a booth like they owned it. Vinyl stuck slightly to the backs of my thighs when I sat.

A waitress came by, bored eyes sweeping us. “Y’all want somethin’?”

Jax flashed a quick smile. “Just water for now.”

The waitress left without caring.

Jax leaned forward, elbows on the table, voice lower. “Listen, Taylor.”

My stomach tightened again. When someone says your name like that, it means a turn.

“What?” I asked.

Jax’s gaze flicked to the window, then back. “You got anybody gonna be lookin’ for you?”

The question had teeth.

“No,” I lied, then felt my face betray me with heat. The truth was complicated: someone might look, but not with love. With anger. With control.

Jax nodded like they understood more than my words. “Okay,” they said. “Me, I got somebody I don’t want findin’ me.”

“Who?” The question slipped out.

Jax’s jaw tightened. For half a second, their fingers tapped the table too fast. A tell. Fear. Or something like it.

“Nobody,” they said too quickly, then corrected, “Just—someone. Don’t matter.”

“That’s not an answer,” I said before I could stop myself.

Jax’s eyes sharpened. Then they smiled, but it didn’t reach their eyes. “You smart. That’s good. Also dangerous.”

They leaned back, gaze drifting to the diner’s muted TV, then back to me. “My line is I don’t go near Canal after dark,” they said, voice casual again. “And I don’t call nobody. Ever. You understand?”

The specificity made my skin tighten. Canal after dark. Don’t call anybody. Ever. Like rules carved into bone.

“Okay,” I said, because what else could I say?
