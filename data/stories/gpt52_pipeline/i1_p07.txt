Den Den Town always felt like it was talking over itself.

A shop on the corner leaked 8-bit chimes through its open door. Somewhere deeper in the alley of signage and narrow aisles, a clerk kept calling out, “Irasshaimase,” like a metronome. The air smelled faintly like warm plastic and fried something from a stall that didn’t belong between stacked CRTs.

Mina moved carefully anyway, headphones on, hands in her sleeves like she was trying to take up less space. The headphones were too big—thick padded cups, a headband that slid if she looked down too fast—but she liked the way they made the district quieter, like she could choose what got in.

She stopped at a crate labeled MIDI / USED and flipped through tangled lives: a blue USB cable with a cracked sheath, a stiff patch cord with a price tag that looked handwritten in apology. A tiny mixer—four channels, one knob missing—sat on a shelf like it was ashamed. Next to it, a cassette multitrack with a yellowed “AS-IS” sticker stared back, heavy and stubborn.

“Hmm,” she murmured, not really to anyone.

On the wall behind the counter, a rack of bargain pedals glinted under fluorescent lights: a chorus unit with a dented corner, a distortion with the paint rubbed off where someone’s foot had lived. In another bin, anime OST CDs in plastic sleeves—some sun-faded, some pristine—waited like time capsules.

Mina touched the edge of one sleeve and pulled her hand back, like the decision itself might make noise.

She checked a price tag on a small desktop synth module—compact, knobs tight, the kind you could tuck beside a laptop and pretend you were the sort of person who took up space with sound. She read the tiny numbers twice, just to be sure.

Too much.

She exhaled through her nose, a quiet private disappointment. It was fine. She wasn’t here to buy big things. She was here to—she didn’t even know how to say it without making it embarrassing.

A sharp guitar note cut through her chosen quiet anyway.

It wasn’t the kind of playing you could ignore—the line was clean and bright, the vibrato confident, the melody so recognizable it pulled a memory up by the collar. A classic anime opening theme, the one with the impossible friendship montage, the one Mina had looped until her mother threatened to confiscate her phone.

Her fingers froze on a synth knob. Her breath caught, small and stupid.

She turned. One headphone cup slipped off her ear.

“—ah.”

The sound hit her full on now, unfiltered: overdriven but not messy, the tone shaped like someone who knew exactly where the chorus should bloom. Mina pushed the headphone back up, but the headband slid against her hair and she had to hold it in place as she stepped out of the shop and into the flow of people.

Coins clinked somewhere. The arcade ahead hiccuped with machine jingles and the slap of buttons. A couple of boys in school uniforms leaned against the entrance, watching something with the reverence usually reserved for fireworks.

Mina followed the guitar like it was a string tied around her wrist.

The busker stood just outside the arcade entrance where the foot traffic narrowed into a natural little stage. Slightly older than Mina—maybe early twenties—feet planted, shoulders loose, as if Den Den Town itself was her living room. She played a sticker-covered electric guitar: bright decals of little mascots and band logos, one corner dominated by a shimmering holographic star. A worn strap crossed her chest, softened by use. From the headstock dangled a tiny pick charm, swinging with each movement like punctuation.

Her fingers flew. She hit the song’s peak—those climbing notes that always made Mina’s chest tighten—and she leaned into it, smiling like she was sharing a secret.

A small crowd had formed without trying. Someone murmured, “Sugoi,” like they didn’t mean to say it out loud. A girl in a cardigan bounced on her heels, filming. A man in a suit paused mid-stride, watched for four beats, then decided he could spare more.

When the busker landed the final chord, she held it just long enough for it to sparkle, then muted the strings with the side of her hand. She gave a quick little bow, grin easy.

“Arigatō,” she said, voice warm. She winked at the cardigan girl, and the girl giggled like she’d been personally chosen.

Mina stood at the edge, not part of the semicircle, trying to look like she’d just happened to stop. Her heartbeat felt too loud inside the headphone cups.

The busker’s eyes swept the crowd as it broke apart, people returning to their missions. Her gaze landed on Mina and stayed there with a casual certainty that made Mina’s stomach drop.

“You like that one?” the busker called, tipping her chin toward Mina’s headphones.

Mina’s mouth did the thing it always did—opened a fraction too late. “I—um. Yes.” She bowed automatically, too deep for the situation. “It was… very good.”

The busker laughed, not unkind. “Very good? That’s like a teacher grade.”

Mina’s face warmed. “Sorry. I mean—” She lifted a hand to adjust the headphones because she didn’t know what else to do, and of course the headband slid forward, one cup slipping down her ear. She fumbled, fingers catching her hair, the plastic creaking.

The busker stepped closer, still holding her guitar, and tilted her head like she was watching a cat try to fit into a box.

“Your headphones are trying to escape,” she said.

“They’re—” Mina tried to fix them quickly and made it worse. The cup dangled half off. “They’re too big. I’m sorry.”

“Don’t apologize to me.” The busker’s smile softened. “Here, if you push the band back like this—” She stopped herself, hand half-raised, then pulled it back politely. “Or, uh, unless you don’t want a stranger touching your head.”

Mina’s ears got hotter. “No. It’s okay. I can.” She forced the headphones into place with two hands, pressing down until they sat where they were supposed to.

The busker watched her succeed like it was an achievement worth witnessing. “There you go. Victory.”

Mina let out a tiny breath she hadn’t noticed she was holding.

The busker angled her guitar slightly, as if making room for conversation. “That opening theme, right? Everyone knows the chorus, but the solo line is the fun part. You looked like you recognized it immediately.”

“I did,” Mina said. Then, because she couldn’t help it, she added, quieter, “Your tone is… really close to the original arrangement.”

“Oh?” The busker’s eyebrows lifted. “Okay, music nerd. Tell me more.”

Mina’s throat tightened in the familiar way—being invited to speak felt like stepping onto a stage with no instrument—but the words were already lining up in her head, impatient.

“I mean,” she began, too fast, then corrected herself. “In the TV version, the guitar sits on top of a pretty bright synth pad. Like… a saw wave with a fast attack but—there’s a filter sweep in the pre-chorus that makes it feel like it opens. Your overdrive is… not too saturated, so it doesn’t mask the melody.”

The busker blinked once, then grinned wider. “You’re serious.”

Mina nodded, heart pounding for a different reason now. “Also, the rhythm guitar in the background is doing palm-mutes on the off-beats, and you’re hinting at that with the way you mute between notes. It makes it—” She swallowed. “It makes it bounce.”

The busker let out a delighted sound. “That’s exactly what I’m trying to do! Most people just go, ‘Cool song.’ You’re out here analyzing filter sweeps.”

Mina’s fingers curled around the edge of her sleeve. “Sorry. I—when I like something, I… notice.”

“Don’t apologize,” the busker said again, like it mattered. “That’s awesome. Do you play?”

Mina hesitated, eyes flicking to the guitar, then to the pedals on the ground—small board, neatly organized, a tuner with scuffed edges like it had lived through a lot of streets. “Not guitar.”

“Okay. What do you play?”

Mina’s voice got steadier as the subject became safer. “Synth. Mostly. And… I mess with MIDI. I make little arrangements. Not—nothing serious.”

The busker leaned in a little, as if the arcade noise behind them required it, but Mina felt like it was more attention than necessity. “Serious is overrated. Do you do original stuff or covers?”

“Covers,” Mina admitted. “And… sometimes I change things. Like if the original has strings, I’ll rebuild it with a different texture. Or—if the chord voicing is muddy, I’ll—” She stopped herself, realizing she was talking a lot.

The busker didn’t look bored. If anything, she looked energized. “Wait, so you’d actually know what chords that song uses?”

Mina nodded, almost embarrassed by the fact that she did. “The chorus is—” She hummed the shape under her breath, then said, “It’s not complicated, but the way the bass moves makes it feel like it is.”

The busker laughed again, delighted. “You’re dangerous. If I get something wrong, you’ll hear it.”

“I wouldn’t—” Mina started, then took a breath and tried again. “I mean, I would. But I wouldn’t say it like a… like a correction. I just—like talking about it.”

“Same,” the busker said, and there was a beat of quiet where Mina realized that was true. Same, but opposite. The busker had put her love on the street, amplified. Mina had kept hers in her headphones, in her room, in folders named “WIP FINAL2.”

A coin clinked into the open guitar case. The busker glanced down and nodded at the passerby automatically. “Thanks!”

The crowd had thinned to nothing now, the momentary concert dissolved. The arcade’s neon flickered in the corner of Mina’s vision, reflecting off the guitar’s glossy stickers. Someone inside shouted in triumph at a game. Den Den Town kept moving around them, indifferent but loud.

The busker reached down and adjusted a cable, coiling it with practiced efficiency. She checked a small setlist taped to the side of her amp, the edges curling. “I’ve got, like, two more songs I can do before my fingers start protesting.”

Mina shifted her weight, unsure if that was her cue to leave. She didn’t want to be the kind of person who lingered and took up someone’s time. She was already taking up—something.

The busker looked up. “What brings you here? Like… Den Den Town, specifically. Shopping? Escaping? Sacred pilgrimage?”

Mina almost smiled at the word choice. Almost. “Shopping,” she said, then added, because honesty had started to feel less dangerous, “And… I guess… looking.”

“For what?”

Mina glanced toward the shop she’d left, toward the bins of cables and the patient used gear. She chose a small truth. “Something I can afford.”

The busker made a sympathetic face. “Relatable.”

“And,” Mina said, surprising herself, “I like… being around sound stuff. Even if I don’t buy anything.”

The busker’s eyes softened, like she understood the feeling of orbiting what you wanted. “Yeah. Sometimes you just want to be near your people, even if you don’t talk to them.”

Mina’s headphones slid a millimeter as if trying to comment. She nudged them back gently, less frantic than before.

The busker rested her hand on the guitar body, thumb tapping a silent rhythm. “You said you do synth stuff. Like, on a laptop? Or hardware?”

“Both,” Mina said. “Mostly laptop. But I like hardware. It feels…” She searched. “It feels like you can touch the sound.”

The busker nodded like that made perfect sense. “I get it. That’s why I can’t quit the guitar even though my back would love it.”

Mina let out a small laugh—barely audible, but real.

The busker’s grin returned, quick and bright, like she’d won a point. “Okay,” she said, as if deciding something. “I’m starving.”

Mina blinked. “Oh.”

“There’s a kaitenzushi place like two minutes from here,” the busker continued, waving her hand in a direction that implied she’d mapped the area by appetite. “The salmon there? It melts. Also the little fried eggplant. Also—honestly, everything. Want to go?”

The question landed in Mina’s chest with more weight than it should have. Want to go. A doorway opening like it was nothing.

Mina’s instinct rose up fast: No, thank you. I shouldn’t. I don’t know you. I’ll bother you. I have to—nothing, actually, she had nothing to do, which somehow made it harder.

She nodded too quickly, and the headphones slid again, the headband slipping forward like a pratfall timed by the universe.

Mina’s hands flew up. “Ah—sorry—”

The busker laughed, gentle. “They’re really committed to the bit.”

Mina managed to get them back in place. “I—um. Kaitenzushi…” She hesitated, words stuttering at the edge. “You mean—together?”

“Yeah,” the busker said, simple. Then she added, lighter, giving Mina an exit like she’d promised without saying it: “But only if you want. No pressure. I can also just go inhale fish alone like a gremlin.”

Mina’s shoulders loosened a fraction at the permission. She looked at the busker—at the easy stance, the stickered guitar, the pick charm still swinging slightly like the last note hadn’t fully stopped.

“I want,” Mina said, then corrected herself because she was still her. “Yes. If it’s okay.”

The busker’s grin flashed like arcade lights. “It’s okay.”

She moved fast, all efficiency now—unplugging, coiling cables, folding a small stand, snapping the latches on her case. Mina watched, impressed by how quickly a public performance could become a portable life.

The busker slung the guitar over her shoulder, case in one hand. “I’m Rika,” she said, as if they’d been introduced ages ago and were just finally doing the formal part.

“Mina,” Mina replied. She bowed again, smaller this time, more normal.

Rika started walking into the flow of Den Den Town, and Mina fell into step beside her without thinking too hard about where to put her hands.

As they passed the arcade entrance, the neon washed over them in shifting colors. The street sounds blended—shop jingles, distant game music, a clerk’s “Irasshaimase”—and Mina realized she could hear it all even through her headphones, because she’d turned her volume down without meaning to.

Rika glanced sideways at her. “So, Mina the synth wizard. What’s your go-to? Like, what do you make when you’re not trying to impress random street guitarists?”

Mina swallowed, then answered quicker than she would have ten minutes ago. “Stuff that sounds like summer nights. Even if it’s not summer.”

Rika made a thoughtful noise. “Okay. That’s a vibe. I respect it.”

Mina’s headphones shifted again, and she adjusted them with one hand, calm, almost fond. Ahead, a conveyor-belt sushi sign glowed with a cartoon tuna, beckoning like a promise.

Rika pointed. “See? Told you. Two minutes. Prepare to have your life changed by salmon.”

Mina surprised herself by replying without rehearsing it first. “If it’s not life-changing, I’m going to give you a teacher grade.”

Rika laughed loud enough that a couple of passersby glanced over, smiling at nothing they could name. “Ouch. Harsh.”

They kept walking, side by side, swallowed into the bright, noisy comfort of Den Den Town.
