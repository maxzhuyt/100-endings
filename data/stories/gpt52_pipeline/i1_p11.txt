Ms. Kline did the pairing the way she did everything: like she was cutting a deck of cards she’d already decided the order of.

“Okay. For the enzyme lab, you’re in pairs. I’m projecting the list—”

The projector coughed to life, that high rubbery *squeee* as the image snapped into focus. The sound went straight into my molars. I watched the names line up in two neat columns, black on white, like the universe trying to be helpful.

I found mine fast—muscle memory from years of scanning for my own name in places I hoped it wouldn’t be.

And there it was, right next to his.

My name + Noah Hernandez.

Noah, who sat two rows over and one seat ahead, who laughed like he meant it, who had the kind of handwriting that looked like it belonged to someone who would never lose their keys, who had once held the door for me and said, “Go ahead,” like it was nothing and I had thought about it for three days anyway.

My stomach did the thing where it drops but also tightens, like it’s trying to become a different organ. My hands were suddenly aware of being hands.

“Go sit with your partner,” Ms. Kline said, already clicking to the next slide. *Squeee.*

Chairs scraped. Bags unzipped. The room rearranged itself with the loud, casual confidence of people who know how to be people in public. I stayed seated for half a beat too long—just long enough to look like I had a reason, just long enough to look like I might be waiting for someone to come to me.

Don’t do that. Don’t be weird.

I stood too fast. My chair legs squealed against the floor in a shorter, meaner imitation of the projector. Heat climbed my neck. I pretended to be adjusting my backpack strap, like anyone would look at me and think, *Ah yes, backpack strap issues. Normal.*

Noah turned, saw me, and smiled in a way that was simple enough to kill me.

“Hey,” he said.

The word was nothing. A pebble. A single syllable.

My brain treated it like a meteor.

Hey could mean: hey, friend; hey, partner; hey, I’m happy it’s you; hey, I’d say this to anyone; hey, I’m being polite because I have to sit with you now for fifty minutes.

I walked over and sat in the empty seat next to him. My body decided to become a plank: upright, stiff, overbalanced. My mouth was already dry, like it had been warned there would be a test.

“Hi,” I said. The smallest sound my throat could produce while still technically counting as a greeting.

He pulled his Chromebook closer, clearing space between us. The desk was narrow; our elbows were going to have to negotiate like diplomats. He slid a folder toward me without looking like he was making a big deal out of it.

“We’re supposed to do catalase, right?” he asked, tapping the worksheet.

I nodded too quickly. My hair fell into my face. I tucked it behind my ear with my left hand, which felt like a decision with consequences.

Ms. Kline’s slide advanced. *Squeee.* The sound cut the air cleanly, like a paper cutter.

Okay. Talk. Say something functional. Say something that suggests you are a competent lab partner and not—whatever your body is doing.

“So…” I began, because my mouth is always ready to make the worst possible first choice. I could still steer it. I could.

Option A: *So, have you done this lab before?* Safe. Professional. Slightly boring, but boring is survivable.

Option B: *So, do you want to do the hypothesis or should I?* Cooperative. Shows initiative. Doesn’t reveal personality. Good.

Option C: *So, I’m glad I got paired with you.* Absolutely not. Red alert. That is how you end up as a story someone tells later. That is how you become an anecdote.

Option D: *So, catalase is basically the enzyme that breaks down hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen, and the bubbles are oxygen, and—* No. That’s a lecture. That’s you trying to prove you belong here by talking too much.

Option E: *So, um, sorry, I—* Why would you apologize. For existing? For having vocal cords?

The word “So…” hung there like a coat someone left on the floor.

Noah looked at me, waiting politely, pencil poised. His patience had weight. It made me feel like I owed him a sentence.

In my head, I delivered Option A smoothly.

*So, have you done this lab before?*

In my head, he answered, *Yeah, last year in honors.* Maybe he smiled. Maybe he said, *Not really, but I think it’s easy.*

In my head, I laughed at the right volume. In my head, I was a person whose lungs understood timing.

In real life, my tongue chose a smaller path.

“So… do you—” I started again, then realized I was about to say *do you* with nothing after it, which would make me sound like I had never formed a question in my life. “Do you want to… start with the… hypothesis?” I finished, like I was offering him a fragile object.

He nodded, easy.

“Sure,” he said. “We can do that. We need like… an if-then statement, right?”

Relief hit me so hard it made me lightheaded. He hadn’t flinched. He hadn’t looked confused. He hadn’t said, *What?* which is the word my brain always expects, like the universe has a script for my humiliation ready to go.

“Yeah,” I said. One word. Minimal. Reliable. Boring.

He bent over the worksheet, writing. His pencil made soft, consistent sounds. Mine sat in my hand like a tool I didn’t know how to operate.

I could hear everything.

The air conditioner’s low hiss. The clicking of keys from the table behind us. Someone whispering in the back, the whisper like static. The rustle of notebook pages. Noah’s sleeve brushing paper.

My own breathing, too loud, too fast. I tried to slow it down, but then it became a manual process, like walking down stairs while thinking about how you walk down stairs.

Don’t be weird.

Where do my hands go? Right now they are… holding a pencil in a way that suggests I am about to write. But I am not writing. So I look like I’m pretending to participate. Put the pencil down. No, then you look like you’re not helping. Start writing something. But what? He’s writing the hypothesis. You can write the same thing? That’s copying. But it’s a shared worksheet. It’s literally a partnership. Why do partnerships feel like auditions.

I shifted my elbow. The desk creaked, microscopic but in my ears it was a siren. Noah didn’t react. Normal people don’t react to desks.

I forced my eyes onto the worksheet. The words on the page were familiar. I’d done this kind of lab before. I knew how to do this.

My brain, however, had selected a different assignment: monitor your face for signs of strangeness.

Am I frowning? Am I smiling? Smiling would be weird because nothing is funny. Not smiling could make me look cold. A neutral expression is safest. But is my neutral expression actually neutral or does it look like I’m judging him. Stop thinking about your face. That will make your face do something unnatural. Don’t do that.

* Squeee.*

The projector advanced. Ms. Kline was explaining procedure steps. Everyone’s attention flicked forward like a school of fish turning at once. For half a second, I was allowed to look at the screen, to be a student again instead of a bundle of possible impressions.

Step one: label test tubes. Step two: add liver. Step three: add hydrogen peroxide. Observe reaction.

Simple. Observable. Facts.

My shoulders lowered a millimeter.

Then my eyes slid back toward Noah, because of course they did, because I was sitting next to the reason my brain had turned into a courtroom.

He had written: *If catalase is present in the liver, then adding hydrogen peroxide will produce oxygen bubbles.*

He paused and looked at me.

“Does that sound right?” he asked.

It was a door. It was him handing me the handle.

In my head, my authentic thought formed quickly, cleanly: *That’s perfect. Also, the way you write hypotheses makes me feel like you could solve any problem, including me.*

Not that second part. Obviously not that second part. But the first part, yes. I could say yes, and maybe add something useful. Maybe be someone who contributes and also has a personality.

I could say: “Yeah, and we can mention that more catalase would mean more bubbles, so… like, reaction rate.”

That’s true. That’s smart. That’s normal-smart, not desperate-smart.

I imagined saying it and him nodding, impressed—just slightly. I imagined him saying, “Oh yeah, that’s a good point.”

I imagined his eyes doing that thing where they crinkle at the corners when he smiles.

Then my brain supplied an alternate cut: I say it and it comes out too fast, like I memorized it. Or I say “reaction rate” and I pronounce it weirdly. Or I add “like” in the wrong place and sound like I’m trying to be casual about science, which is embarrassing. Or I say “more bubbles” and he thinks I’m stating the obvious. Or he says, “Yeah, obviously,” not to be mean, just because it is obvious, and I shrink into a molecule.

Time stretched. His question was still hanging between us. His eyebrows lifted a little, not impatient, just… waiting.

I answered too quickly to match the amount of thought I’d spent.

“Yeah—yeah, that’s good,” I said, and then, because silence felt like failure, I added, “Um. Looks right.”

Not the useful add-on. Not the thing that would have made me feel like I existed as more than a yes/no dispenser.

His mouth tipped in a small smile anyway. He didn’t seem to notice the mismatch between his real invitation and my safe little response.

“Cool,” he said, and went back to writing.

My chest did a weird double beat, like my heart had tripped on a step.

Did I sound bored? Did I sound like I didn’t care? Did I sound like I thought he needed my approval? *Looks right*—why did I say that. That’s what you say when you’re checking someone’s math homework, not when you’re—when you’re—

Don’t be weird.

I stared at the worksheet so hard my eyes started to blur. I wrote my name at the top, carefully, to prove to myself I could still do a basic human action.

Noah flipped the page of his notes. The paper made a soft *thup.*

I adjusted my grip on my pencil. It slipped.

It didn’t fall all the way. It just clattered against the desk once, a sharp little *tick* that cut through the quiet in a way that felt illegal. My hand jerked to catch it, and in the motion my elbow nudged my water bottle. The bottle wobbled, wobble-wobble, threatening drama.

I froze, as if stillness could prevent physics.

The bottle settled without spilling. Disaster averted. Nothing happened.

But my brain stamped the moment in red ink anyway: *She can’t even hold a pencil like a person.*

Across the room, someone laughed at something their partner said. The laugh bounced off the walls. I was sure—sure—that laugh was about me, even though no one had looked. Even though nobody cares. Even though it’s not about you. It’s never about you. That’s the whole point: you’re not special enough for people to be tracking your pencil noises.

My cheeks were hot. I pretended to be reading the procedure again, as if I had dropped my pencil on purpose, as part of the reading process.

Ms. Kline clicked. *Squeee.*

The projector squeak hit like a gavel.

Case closed: awkward.

Except nothing was closed, because my mind reopened it instantly, cross-examining every microsecond.

Why did your hand jerk like that? Why did you freeze? Normal people would just pick it up. You made it a thing by reacting like it was a thing. He saw. He definitely saw. Even if he didn’t react, that doesn’t mean he didn’t see. In fact, not reacting could mean he’s being polite because he noticed and felt bad.

Noah didn’t look up. He was highlighting something in his notes with a steady hand, the kind of hand that would never betray him by dropping a pencil.

I had to fix it. I had to balance the universe. If I left the pencil incident unaddressed, it would sit between us like a wet sock.

So I overcorrected, like my brain loves to do: if you can’t be effortless, be useful. If you can’t be charming, be competent. If you can’t be calm, be extra.

“I can do the data table,” I said suddenly, voice too loud compared to the quiet of his highlighting. “Like—set it up now. If you want.”

The words came out in a rush, like I was throwing them before I could change my mind. I was already reaching for the worksheet, already drawing boxes, already trying to prove I belonged at the desk.

Noah blinked, then looked at what I was doing.

“Oh, yeah, that’d be great,” he said, easy again. “Thanks.”

Thanks. Normal word. Warm enough. My brain immediately translated it into: you’re doing too much but I appreciate it because it benefits me.

I kept drawing lines, too many lines, making the table overly neat, like neatness could redeem me.

I started narrating what I was doing because silence felt dangerous now.

“So we’ll have like… trial one, trial two,” I said. “And then… bubble height maybe? Or like… time to stop bubbling.” I laughed, a little hiccup of a laugh that arrived half a second late, as if my body had to process whether what I’d said counted as funny.

Noah nodded. “Yeah. Bubble height could work.”

His neutrality was kind. It was also, in my head, a blank screen my anxiety could project anything onto.

He’s bored. He wishes he got paired with someone else. He’s only being nice because that’s who he is. He’s counting the minutes until he can go back to talking to his friends who know how to be normal.

I heard myself in my own head shift, like my narration stepped back from me, as if creating distance could make it hurt less.

She is the kind of girl who offers to do the data table like it’s a peace treaty. She is the kind of girl who makes a simple partnership feel like a performance review.

You’re doing it again, I told myself. You’re making it weird by trying not to make it weird.

* Squeee.*

Slide change. Reset button pressed, except my brain didn’t reset; it just took the squeak as permission to start a new loop.

Noah turned his Chromebook slightly toward me.

“Do you want to type, or should I?” he asked.

An actual practical question. A normal collaboration moment. My brain wanted to answer: *It doesn’t matter. You can type. I can type. We can both type. We can trade off. This is not a referendum on your worth.*

What came out was overly polite.

“You can,” I said quickly. “I mean—unless you want me to. I can. Either is fine.”

Which is the verbal equivalent of handing someone a menu and then apologizing for the existence of choices.

He smiled again, that same simple smile, like my words had not just fallen down the stairs in front of him.

“I can do it,” he said. “Just tell me what you’re thinking.”

Tell me what you’re thinking.

It should have been easy. It should have been the dream. An invitation. Permission.

My mind immediately drafted the line I wanted to say—not about catalase. About him.

Not *I like you.* Not the big confession. Something smaller. A bridge you could plausibly walk back from if needed.

*We should work together again sometime.*  
*You’re really good at explaining things.*  
*Do you want to compare notes later?*  
*Do you want my number—for the project?*  
Or, the most dangerous because it sounded the most casual: *Are you going to the game Friday?*

I held one in my mind like a fragile glass: *We should work together again.*

Not a confession. Just a suggestion. Just a sentence people say. People say it all the time, probably. People who aren’t me.

My brain immediately generated the montage, the branching futures like tabs opening too fast.

Best case: I say it lightly, he grins, he says, “Yeah, definitely,” and then later it turns into sitting together on purpose, and maybe it becomes a pattern, and maybe patterns become something else.

Awkward case: I say it, he pauses, he says, “Oh, yeah, sure,” in a tone that means he is being polite, and then next time he finds a way to be paired with someone else, and I spend the rest of the semester decoding the politeness.

Worst case: I say it, he laughs—not meanly, just surprised—and says, “Uh, yeah, I guess,” and then he tells someone, or someone overhears, and it becomes a thing, and I become the girl who said that like she was asking for marriage over catalase.

Even worse case (because my brain is creative): I say it, he says, “Oh, I actually have a girlfriend,” even if he doesn’t, even if I didn’t ask that, because the universe loves misunderstanding.

All of these felt equally likely. My mind, brilliant and unhelpful, insisted on running each one to the end, insisting on teaching me the lesson before I made the mistake.

Safety won by default, as it always did.

Instead of the line, I pointed at the worksheet.

“We should include,” I said, “that the independent variable is… the presence of catalase, and the dependent is like… amount of oxygen produced.” My voice steadied as soon as I talked about something measurable.

Noah started typing, fingers quick. “Okay. Presence of catalase. Amount of oxygen.” He glanced at me. “That’s good.”

That’s good.

Two words that should have been enough to quiet the courtroom in my head. Instead, my brain took them as evidence I had barely met the minimum.

He’s saying it’s good because it is objectively correct, not because he likes you. Don’t confuse correctness with connection.
