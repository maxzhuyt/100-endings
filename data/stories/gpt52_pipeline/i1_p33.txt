The Outpost always sounds like it’s breathing.

Air recyclers in the ceiling hum on a low note, like a big animal sleeping. The hanging lights sway in the station’s lazy spin, a fraction behind the rhythm of boots and magnetic soles. Someone’s laughter hiccups and the whole room answers it—glasses on metal, a chair leg scraping, the soft thud of a dart hitting the corkboard we pretend isn’t a navigation panel from three owners ago.

I wipe the counter anyway. It doesn’t need it. It never needs it. It’s the motion that tells my hands what to do when my head wants to wander.

“Shift change?” Juno asks, already perched on her usual stool like she’s part of the furniture. Her courier jacket is half-zipped, pockets bulging, hair pinned up with a data stylus.

“Shift change,” I confirm. “Your ship didn’t crash. I’m guessing you’re disappointed.”

She snorts. “Only in the romantic sense. Any mail for me?”

“Only the kind you don’t want me reading,” I say, and slide her a cup of chicory brew. Not coffee—coffee is for planets that pretend they aren’t dying—but it’s hot and bitter and smells like mornings I don’t have.

Across the bar, Thatch drags himself in from the airlock corridor, boots still dusted with regolith from the mining tunnels. He gives the room a long scan, like he’s counting heads and checking for new problems.

“Thatch,” I call. “You’ve got that face again.”

He points at my counter with one thick finger. “You’re behind it. That’s the face.”

“Rude.”

“Accurate.”

I toss him a rag. He catches it one-handed like it’s a familiar insult. “Wipe your own boots before you smear the place.”

He wipes at his boots with exaggerated care. “I’m a guest.”

“You’re a regular,” I correct. “Guests pay full price.”

“Then what am I paying with?” he asks, and taps his temple.

“Regret,” I say. “Same as always.”

He laughs, and it loosens something in my chest. That’s what this place does. It takes the tight parts of you and makes them forget they’re clenched.

At the far end, Old Miri is already in her corner booth with her card deck spread out and her little repair drones buzzing around her ankles like bored pets.

“Evening, Nets,” she calls without looking up. “Your lights are flickering again.”

“They’re not flickering,” I say. “They’re swaying romantically. It’s ambiance.”

“They’re flickering,” she repeats, and one of her drones bumps a table leg, offended on her behalf.

Juno leans toward me and lowers her voice. “Why do they call you Nets, anyway?”

Because I catch things. Because I used to set them.

Because it’s easier than letting anyone say the name I wore before.

I tilt my head. “Because I’m good at untangling drunks and bad decisions.”

Thatch barks out a laugh. “That’s not why.”

“That’s the only reason you’re allowed to know,” I tell him.

He raises his hands in surrender. “Fine. Nets. Untangler of idiots. Give me something with actual alcohol.”

“Actual alcohol costs actual credits,” I say, and reach for the tap line. The Outpost’s house brew is a fizzy algae lager that tastes like someone described citrus to a machine and then apologized.

My fingers check the coupling automatically. The line hums under my touch. No leaks. No foam storm. Tonight, at least, the universe is behaving.

I pour Thatch a pint.

It comes out a little heavy. My wrist hesitates. The foam rises too high. I glance down, annoyed at myself, and top it off anyway like I meant to.

Thatch watches my hands. “You’re generous today.”

“I’m efficient,” I say. “If I give you enough, you’ll stop talking sooner.”

“Lie,” he says. “You’re trying to buy my silence.”

Juno lifts her chicory cup like it’s a toast. “To Nets, who bribes miners with beer.”

“Miners are cheap,” I shoot back. “And loud.”

Miri shuffles her deck with a click-click that’s almost soothing. “That one’s not cheap,” she calls, nodding toward Juno. “She just pretends to be.”

Juno clutches her chest. “My reputation!”

“Your reputation is ‘steals socks off clotheslines,’” Thatch says.

“I don’t steal,” Juno replies. “I redistribute.”

I take a stim-mint from the jar under the counter—just one, just enough to keep the edge off the tired—and palm it without thinking. Habit. A vice that looks like breath freshening if you squint.

Behind me, the message terminal pings once. A forwarded packet from some miner who can’t spell his own name. I flick it to “later” with my knuckle. Message forwarding isn’t illegal. It’s just… flexible. Out here, discretion is currency, and I’m the cashier.

Thatch leans in. “Any new faces docked today?”

“Just the usual,” I say. “Three haulers. A maintenance skiff. That tourist couple who thinks vacuum exposure is romantic.”

Juno groans. “They asked me where the ‘real Outpost’ is. Like this is a theme bar on a cruise liner.”

“It is,” I say. “The theme is ‘don’t die.’”

The room warms around us. Voices overlap. Someone starts up the old song about a comet and a broken heart; someone else throws a peanut at them to shut them up. I slide snacks in little bowls—salted fungus crisps, roasted seed pods—because full mouths mean fewer fights.

Then—

A sharp crack splits the air.

Glass on metal, then a tinkling scatter across the floor.

Every conversation stutters. Just for a heartbeat. Like the Outpost flinches.

Thatch twists in his stool. “Again?”

A voice from the far side calls, “Sorry! Sorry—my elbow did a thing!”

Juno’s laugh is too bright. “Your elbow has committed more crimes than I have.”

“It’s not a big deal,” I say automatically, like I’m smoothing a wrinkle. My voice stays even.

My body doesn’t.

My shoulders go tight. My stomach drops the way it does when a hatch seal alarms. I’m already scanning—where, who, what broke, was it an accident, is it—

The broken glass is just a tumbler near the dartboard. A kid in a maintenance jumpsuit is crouched, gathering shards with a napkin like that’ll stop anyone’s boot from finding them.

“Careful,” I call. “Don’t bleed on my floor. It’ll start thinking it’s important.”

A ripple of laughter. The room resumes breathing.

I let my hands keep moving. Wipe. Pour. Slide. Smile. The rhythm is a net. It catches the parts of me that want to bolt.

The airlock cycles.

It’s a soft sound—pressure equalizing, seals shifting—but it’s different from the chatter. It’s the Outpost clearing its throat.

I look up.

The stranger comes in like they’ve been running through vacuum and only just remembered there’s air.

They’re tall, hunched a little, their hair in thick tentacled dreads that cling together like they’ve been wet recently. Their coat is patched, but the patches are too neat—like they were done by a professional who didn’t want to leave a trace. Their gloves are mismatched. Their hands tremble as they peel one off, and I catch a glimpse of a wrist band where the tag’s been cut away, the skin beneath raw.

They smell faintly of ozone and cold metal. Like a discharged capacitor.

They don’t scan the room the way tourists do, curious. They scan it like a map for exits.

They slide onto the stool right in front of me, close enough that the gravity of them presses against the counter.

“Vega whisky,” they say. Their voice is scraped thin. “Neat. Better make that a double.”

Thatch whistles softly. Juno straightens, eyes narrowing in interest.

I keep my face neutral. “Vega?”

“Do I sound like I’m joking?”

“We don’t stock much Vega,” I say, reaching for the top shelf anyway. “And we definitely don’t stock enough to waste it on someone who’s about to spit it back up.”

Their gaze flicks to my hands, to the bottle, to my knuckles. Not my face. Like faces lie and hands don’t.

“I won’t waste it,” they say. “I’ll pay.”

“Credits spend,” I allow. “But ‘Vega’ out here usually means either you’ve got money you shouldn’t—”

“Or I’ve got problems I didn’t order,” they cut in.

I pause half a second, bottle hovering. “You sure you want neat?”

Their mouth twitches. Not quite a smile. “You sure you want me to answer that?”

“Fair,” I say. I pour.

The whisky hits the glass like amber light. The smell rises—smoke and dried fruit and the kind of oak you only get from places with real trees. It doesn’t belong in the Outpost. It’s too clean. Too expensive.

They take the glass with their bare hand. Their fingers are stained with dried ice dust, the kind that clings when you’ve been in a hurry near a cryo line. There’s a faint scorch mark across the back of their glove, like something tried to bite them.

They drink half the double in one swallow.

Juno leans toward Thatch and murmurs, “That’s either courage or stupidity.”

Thatch murmurs back, “Same fuel.”

The stranger sets the glass down with controlled care. Their eyes lift and, for the first time, meet mine.

“Who runs the Outpost?” they ask.

“Gravity,” I say. “And the ductwork. Depends on the day.”

“I’m serious.”

“So am I,” I reply pleasantly. “You want ownership info, there’s a registry on Station Six.”

“I don’t have time for Station Six.” Their fingers tap the rim of the glass. Tap-tap. “Security cams. You have them?”

“Some.” I wipe a spot that isn’t dirty. “Mostly they watch the snack bowls so people feel guilty.”

Their gaze flicks up toward the ceiling corners. “Are they recorded?”

“Depends how much you pay,” I say, and flash my bartender smile like it’s a closed door with a nice welcome sign.

They exhale through their nose. “Docking logs.”

“Public terminal has arrivals,” I say. “Departures, too, if you like disappointment.”

They lean closer. Their voice drops. “You keep discretion, Nets?”

The way they say it—like they’ve already decided I do—pricks at my skin.

Behind me, Miri’s drones buzz, one of them making an irritated chirp like it senses the shift.

“I keep drinks cold,” I say lightly. “I keep fights from spilling. Discretion is a… premium service.”

The stranger’s eyes flick to the message terminal behind my shoulder, then back to me. “I’m not here to cause trouble.”

“Everyone who says that is,” I answer. My tone stays warm. My insides don’t.

They swallow. “I’m hiding.”

That lands between us with the weight of a dropped tool in a quiet corridor.

Juno’s stool squeaks as she shifts, pretending she isn’t listening. Thatch pretends even harder.

“Hiding from who?” I ask, because that’s what you ask when someone drops a truth on your counter like a tip.

The stranger’s jaw tightens. “Does it matter?”

“Yes,” I say. “It matters a lot if the ‘who’ comes through my airlock with a warrant and a bad attitude.”

They stare into their whisky like it’ll answer. “They scan.”

“We all scan,” I say. “That’s how we don’t accidentally import plague fungus.”

“Not that kind,” they snap, then catch themselves, lowering their voice again. “Not hygiene. Identification. Pattern. History.”

My hands still.

Pattern. History.

A phrase I haven’t heard in—no. Not here. Not in this room. Not with these people.

I make myself breathe. “We get the occasional sweep,” I say. “Usually for contraband. Usually announced.”

“They won’t announce,” the stranger says. “They’ll just—” Their fingers curl, mimicking a wave passing over a surface. “Sweep the room.”

My tongue finds the edge of my stim-mint like it’s a lifeline.

Thatch clears his throat loudly, trying to shove the tension back into the floor. “Hey, Nets,” he says, too casual. “You still doing those… favors? Messages, packages? For a fee?”

I look at him. He’s watching me, eyes sharp under the miner grime. He knows. Not specifics. But he knows I have a side channel. He also knows I’m careful.

“Thatch,” I warn, smiling like I’m amused.

“What?” he says. “Just asking. I got a cousin on Ceres who—”

“Your cousin can learn to spell,” I say, and slap a bowl of seed pods in front of him. “Eat. Quietly.”

He grins, but his gaze flicks to the stranger and back to me, concern tucked under the humor.

The stranger’s attention snaps to Thatch. “You forward messages off-station?”

“That depends,” I say.

“On what?”

“On whether I like you,” I reply.

Their mouth tightens. “I can pay.”

“Everyone can pay,” I say. “Not everyone should.”

They lean in again, and I catch a faint metallic scent beneath the ozone—blood, or something like it, thin and sharp. Their tremor is worse now. Not withdrawal. Adrenaline.

“You don’t understand,” they say. “If they find me here, they won’t just take me. They’ll… clean up.”

“Clean up,” I repeat softly.

“They’ll make examples,” the stranger says. “They’ll ask questions. They’ll break things that don’t belong to them.”

I glance around the room—the dartboard, the patched seats, Miri’s drones, Juno’s mismatched gloves, Thatch’s battered boots. My counter. My lights that sway “romantically.” This little pocket of warmth welded onto the edge of nowhere.

I keep my voice even. “Who are ‘they’?”

The stranger’s eyes lock onto mine with sudden precision. “You already know the shape of them.”

The mint goes bitter on my tongue.

Because I do.

Not the exact face, not the uniform, not the badge. But the shape: people who call it procedure when it’s really hunting. People who scan for patterns, for histories, for the names you buried.

I force a laugh that comes out almost right. “I know a lot of shapes,” I say. “Try me.”

They reach into their coat, slow enough not to spook the room, but fast enough that my nerves sing.

Thatch’s hand drops casually to his belt. Juno’s fingers curl around her cup. Miri’s drones rise a few centimeters, buzzing like angry bees.

The stranger pulls out something small and flat and slides it across the counter.

A credential wafer. Matte black. No station crest. Just a thin line of silver code that makes my eyes ache when I try to focus on it.

They don’t smile. “I know what you used to be called,” they say quietly.

My heart does a hard, stupid thing, like it’s trying to punch its way out of my ribs and run without me.

Juno’s voice cuts in, forced-bright. “Hey! Nets! You okay?”

I blink. My hands are gripping the edge of the counter hard enough that my knuckles have gone pale.

“I’m fine,” I say, too quick.

Thatch’s gaze is on my face now, not the stranger. “You don’t look fine.”

“I said I’m fine,” I repeat, and my smile is a practiced lie I can wear for hours. Years. Lifetimes. “Just—mint went down wrong.”

The stranger’s eyes don’t leave mine. “You can help me,” they say. “Or you can pretend you never saw me.”

“That’s a generous set of options,” I say, keeping my voice light. My pulse is a drum in my ears. “What exactly do you want?”

They tilt their head toward the airlock corridor like they can hear something the rest of us can’t. “A place to sit for ten minutes without being scanned.”

“We’re a bar,” I say. “Sitting is our business model.”

“Don’t be clever,” they whisper. The tremor is back. “They’re close.”

The Outpost’s door chime rings—cheerful, stupid, programmed by someone who thought cheer could fend off vacuum.

A docking klaxon sounds once, short and official, and the room quiets on instinct.

Miri’s head snaps up. “That’s not a maintenance skiff,” she mutters.

Juno slides off her stool, trying to make it look like she’s just stretching. “Nets,” she says softly, “who’s docking?”

I look past the stranger, past the warm bodies and the swaying lights, to the little arrivals screen mounted near the airlock.

A new ship ID scrolls across in crisp text.

UNREGISTERED SCAN AUTHORITY.

Thatch swallows. “That… sounds like trouble.”

The stranger’s voice is barely audible. “Don’t let them sweep me.”

The airlock begins to cycle again.

Someone at the door says, loud enough for the room to hear, “Station security. Open up.”

My mouth opens on a reflex I don’t control, and the name almost comes out—an old one, buried deep.

Instead I say, too fast, “We’re open.”

The stranger’s fingers close around their empty glass like it’s a weapon or a prayer. “Nets,” they breathe, “choose.”

The seal thumps as the outer door unlocks.
