Midas called it a kiosk because calling it a “home” felt like tempting whatever was left of the old world into remembering it had opinions.

It was, in fairness, a kiosk. Or what remained of one: a concrete rectangle with a roll-down shutter permanently stuck half-open, the roof sagging like an exhausted eyelid, and a faded advertisement still insisting—cheerfully, inaccurately—that *Fresh Sandwiches Are Made Daily*. Midas had never seen a sandwich here. He had seen two moths fight over a receipt.

Inside, the comforts were of the sort you learned to love because the alternatives had teeth.

There was a kettle that still boiled if you plugged it into a small solar panel, then into a car inverter, then into an extension cord that had once powered something optimistic like Christmas lights. It took forty minutes and made a noise like a disappointed goose. The water came out tasting faintly of history and, on one memorable occasion, pennies.

There was a “security system,” which consisted of a tin can on a string, strung across the entrance at ankle height. If you tripped it, the can fell onto a stack of bottle caps in a bucket and made a sound that could wake the dead or at least alert them to the fact that you were clumsy.

And there was Chewy.

Chewy was, according to the sun-bleached label on the back of his neck, a *Koala Companion: Animatronic Friend! New! Hug! Talk! Laugh!*.

He had button eyes that conveyed judgement in the same way a tax form does: without emotion, and yet personally. His fur was once grey. Now it was the colour of a dish rag that had survived several arguments. When he moved, his servos whirred with the strained determination of a man trying to lift a sofa alone. His voicebox worked about seventy percent of the time, which meant he spoke like a wise prophet whose visions were interrupted by advertisements.

Chewy sat on the counter that used to display sandwiches, his little plastic paws folded. His battery warning LED blinked an unhurried red, like a distant lighthouse advising ships to sink elsewhere.

Midas spread their supplies out on a plastic tray as if presenting quarterly figures.

“Right,” he said, with the solemnity of someone about to announce a budget cut that would definitely involve cannibalism. “Morning Council.”

Chewy’s head turned in three precise increments. His internal speaker crackled. “HELLO FRIEND! I’M CHEWY!”

“Yes,” Midas said. “We’ve established the minutes. Attendance: me, you, and the lingering smell of old mayonnaise. Agenda: today’s mission. Objective: water filter cartridges.”

Chewy blinked. The blink took two seconds, as though he had to file paperwork for each eyelid.

Midas tapped the tray. It held: a roll of duct tape (half a roll, if you didn’t count the parts where it had fused into itself), a screwdriver with no handle, three bandages that had been used once and *rested*, a tin of peaches from a year that felt like a rumour, and a coil of copper wire he’d found and kept because the world now ran on the principle that anything shiny would be important later.

He had also written *THE PLAN* on a scrap of cardboard in block capitals. Underneath, in smaller letters, he’d added *Try not to die.*

“Step one,” he said, reading as if it were a contract. “We go to the old sports store. Step two, we find the camping aisle. Step three, we acquire replacement cartridges for the filter. Step four, we return home base. Step five, we—”

Chewy interrupted with a burst of sound: a high, tinny laugh, the sort you heard in toy aisles when parents were having second thoughts.

Midas stared at him. “Was that… appropriate commentary?”

Chewy’s laugh cut off mid-cackle, as if someone had unplugged joy. “I LOVE YOU!”

“That’s nicer,” Midas conceded. “But also not commentary. Any objections?”

Chewy’s LED blinked again. His head tilted. “LOW BATTERY. PLEASE ENJOY DEMO MODE.”

“Demo mode,” Midas repeated, tasting the words like a bad canned soup. “We are, in a sense, all in demo mode now.”

Chewy made a small whirr that might have been agreement or a motor trying to remember what it used to do.

Midas rolled up their cardboard plan, tucked it into his jacket like a warrant, and stood.

“Council adjourned,” he said. “Let’s go shopping.”

Outside, the street was a museum of mistakes.

Cars sat where they’d been abandoned, some with doors open as if the occupants had stepped out to ask for directions and never returned. The pavement was cracked into puzzles. Grass grew in the seams with the smug persistence of life that hadn’t read the news.

The air had that particular post-collapse clarity: no exhaust, no perfume, just dust and wind and the faint, medicinal tang of ozone that drifted in from the south like a thought you couldn’t quite place.

Midas pulled the shutter up a little more and stepped over the tin-can string without tripping it, because he was not an amateur. Chewy toddled after him on stiff legs, each step accompanied by the small complaint of his joints.

They had not made it ten metres before the omen arrived, punctual as a bill.

There was new graffiti on the nearest wall—fresh black paint against the old flaking posters. A symbol had been sprayed in a quick, confident hand: three parallel lines through a circle, like a simplified target with an attitude problem.

Under it, in block letters:

**THE WOODS REMEMBER.**

Midas paused. “Huh.”

Chewy’s head snapped toward it with a speed that suggested something inside him had just been prodded. His speaker hissed. “WARNING! CHOKING HAZARD.”

“Noted,” Midas said. He leaned closer, reading. “The woods remember. Like… trees? Like… a metaphor?”

Chewy’s button eyes stared at the symbol. His LED blinked twice, then held steady, as if listening to something. “DO NOT PLACE NEAR WATER.”

“That’s good advice generally,” Midas said. He straightened up. “Anyway. Graffiti. People get bored. Nothing to do with us.”

They walked on. Behind them, the symbol sat on the wall and did what all good warnings do: waited patiently to be ignored.

The sports store was three blocks away, which in the old world would have been a mild inconvenience and in the new world was an expedition requiring planning, rationing, and a willingness to die for something as romantic as a plastic cylinder full of charcoal.

The storefront sign had fallen, leaving only the metal frame: *SP—RT—*.

Inside, the air smelled of rubber, dust, and ambition. Shelves had been stripped by earlier scavengers, but the deeper aisles were always a gamble. People were lazy. People were also dead. Both traits were useful.

Midas moved like a man browsing sales. He held a battered shopping basket because it made him feel civilized and because it could also be swung at knees.

“Inventory categories,” he murmured, as if narrating a documentary. “Useful. Possibly useful. Will definitely cause tetanus. Shiny enough to start a war. Cursed.”

Chewy shuffled beside him, his eyes scanning. His internal flashlight—one of the few upgrades Midas had managed, by duct-taping a penlight to Chewy’s forehead—cast a narrow cone of light like a judgemental spotlight.

They passed a rack of hiking boots that had been chewed by rodents or philosophy. Midas poked one with his screwdriver. “Shoe soles,” he said wistfully. “Ah, the lost art of not feeling every pebble personally.”

Chewy’s speaker popped. “NICE SHOES!”

“Not to you,” Midas said. “You don’t have feet. You have… ambitions.”

He found a shelf of camping stoves. Most were gone. One remained, dented and rusted in the shape of regret.

“Cursed,” he decided promptly.

Chewy laughed again, soft and wrong. “HAHAHA!”

Midas glanced at him. “Do you have a setting for *sympathy*?”

Chewy’s laugh slid into static. Then, in a bright, cheerful voice: “CALL NOW AND RECEIVE A SECOND ONE FREE!”

“That’s not how anything works anymore,” Midas said. “Except misery.”

They reached what had once been the water purification section. The shelf label still read *FILTERS & FLASKS*. The shelf itself was bare except for a few torn cardboard hangers and, miraculously, a small locked cabinet underneath.

Midas crouched. “There,” he whispered, reverent. “The mythic object.”

Chewy leaned in. His forehead light illuminated the lock like a spotlight on a suspect.

Midas examined it. A cheap padlock. In the old world, it was there to stop teenagers from stealing iodine tablets. In the new world, it was guarding the Holy Grail.

“I can pick that,” Midas said, because he was the kind of man who said things into existence and then had to deal with the consequences.

Chewy’s paw rose. Before Midas could object, Chewy’s claw—plastic, blunt, designed for hugging—slid into the lock’s hasp with surprising precision. His servos whirred in a different pitch, like a safe cracking itself.

There was a click.

Midas blinked. “Chewy,” he said slowly. “Where did you learn that?”

Chewy’s speaker emitted a jingle, faint and warped by time. “AT PARTICIPATING STORES ONLY.”

Midas opened the cabinet.

Inside were three water filter cartridges, still in packaging. The packaging was sun-faded, but the word *REPLACEMENT* was legible, and that was the closest thing to a love letter he’d received in months.

He lifted one like an offering. “We’re going to live,” he said.

Chewy’s LED blinked. “PLEASE INSERT COIN.”

“I will insert—” Midas stopped himself. The world was hard enough without starting arguments with koalas.

He put the cartridges in the basket. He added, because it seemed wise, a packet of iodine tablets with an expiration date that had become purely decorative. He also found a roll of athletic tape and, in the corner, a small metal pot that wasn’t obviously haunted by rust.

“Useful,” he declared. “Possibly useful. Might be cursed, but in a manageable way.”

Chewy’s speaker crackled. “I AM PROUD OF YOU!”

Midas felt a warm, foolish surge of affection. He was about to say something sentimental when they turned down the next aisle and found the sheltered stairwell that led to the staff area.

On the wall, beneath a half-torn poster of a man smiling while holding a football he would never get back, was more graffiti.

This one was older. The paint had dried and cracked. It looked less like boredom and more like someone writing while their hands shook.

The same symbol: three lines through a circle.

And beneath it:

**KNOCK THREE TIMES ON THE DEAD TREE THAT STILL HAS A NUMBER.**

An arrow pointed to the left, toward the back exit. Underneath, in smaller handwriting:

**DON’T TRUST THE SPEAKER.**

Midas read it twice, because he liked the idea of destiny being patient enough to leave notes.

Chewy’s head jerked again. His servos whined. His speaker said, in a voice that was suddenly lower, distorted: “EMPLOYEE ACCESS ONLY.”

Midas looked at him. “You recognize it.”

Chewy’s LED went from blinking to a steady, anxious red. “PLEASE… PLEASE…”

“Please what?”

Chewy’s voicebox squealed. Then, very clearly, as if it had been waiting years to say it: “KNOCK. KNOCK. KNOCK.”

Midas felt the hairs on his arms rise. “All right,” he said, because when the universe handed you a riddle in spray paint you either solved it or you died of curiosity. “We’ll… note it.”

He pulled out his cardboard plan and, on the back, copied the symbol in clumsy lines. Under it, he wrote the sentence.

Chewy watched, unblinking.

From somewhere deeper in the building came a soft *beep*.

Midas froze. “Did you hear that?”

Chewy’s speaker chirped. “YOU ARE NOW ENTERING A SECURE ZONE.”

“Ah,” Midas said. “No.”

Another beep. And then, as if the store had been waiting for years for someone to finally trigger the correct sequence of wrongness, the overhead speakers crackled.

A voice came out, cheerful and dead.

“Thank you,” it said, “for visiting SportsMart. For your safety, please remain calm. Security breach detected.”

Midas stared upward. “We are not a breach,” he said, as if the speaker might appreciate clarification.

Chewy’s LED pulsed like a heartbeat.

The speakers continued, warming up with the enthusiasm of a system glad to have a purpose again. “All exits have been secured. Assistance will arrive shortly. Please enjoy our selection of—”

The lights flickered. Somewhere, a metal shutter began to roll down with slow, grinding certainty.

Midas grabbed the basket. “Chewy,” he hissed. “Did you—”

Chewy’s servos whirred, panicked. “I’M A LITTLE TEAPOT!”

“Not now!”

A motion sensor in the corner blinked to life, its tiny red eye opening like a demon with a municipal job.

Midas ran.

They sprinted down aisles of empty racks. The basket banged against Midas’s leg, the cartridges inside clacking like bones. The speaker continued to narrate their doom with customer-service cadence.

“Please do not run,” it said. “Running increases the risk of injury. Injuries may void your warranty.”

A shutter slammed down over the front entrance. Another began to descend over the back.

Midas veered toward the staff stairwell, the one with the graffiti. The arrow pointed left. He went right, because he refused to be controlled by vandalism.

The right corridor ended in a locked door. Of course it did.

Chewy’s flashlight beam jittered. His speaker said, brightly: “UH-OH!”

Midas spun back. The back shutter was almost closed.

“Left!” he shouted, to no one in particular, and threw himself toward the stairwell.

They made it under the descending metal by the grace of desperation and a low ceiling. The shutter scraped Midas’s backpack, tearing off a strip of fabric like a receipt for his stupidity.

They stumbled into the stairwell. It was dim, smelling of old sweat and newer mould. The graffiti stared at them.

Behind them, the speaker’s voice filtered through the metal. “Thank you for your cooperation,” it said. “Have a productive day.”

Midas panted. “We’re fine,” he said, lying. “We’re fine. We just… took the express exit.”

Chewy’s laugh burst out again, too loud in the enclosed space.

“Stop laughing!” Midas snapped.

Chewy’s laugh dissolved into static, then: “THERE THERE!”

Midas took the stairs two at a time and pushed out through a door that should have been locked but wasn’t, because the world enjoyed inconsistency.

They emerged into the alley behind the store.

And there, leaning against a dumpster like people who had been expecting them, were survivors.

There were five of them, which was enough to be a problem and not enough to be an army. They wore mismatched gear: bike helmets, leather jackets, and one man had draped himself in a sash made of lanyards. Around their necks hung plastic membership cards on strings, laminated like holy relics.

The woman in front smiled with the careful politeness of someone about to ask you to empty your pockets.

“Good morning,” she said. “Welcome to the district of the Loyal Rewards Program.”

Midas adjusted his grip on the basket. “We’re just passing through.”

“Of course,” she said. “We all are, in the end. May I see your card?”

“What card?”

“Our card,” she said, as if he were being deliberately obtuse. “All scavenging within this commercial zone requires a valid membership. It’s for safety.”

One of the men behind her held up a traffic cone, cradling it like a baby. A strip of cloth was tied around it like a ceremonial ribbon.

“That’s… for safety?” Midas asked.

The woman nodded. “Sacred Cone ensures orderly commerce. Without orderly commerce, there is chaos.”

Midas glanced around at the cracked alley, the weeds, the distant skeletal buildings. “Yes,” he said carefully. “We’d hate for chaos to get in.”

Chewy’s speaker chirped. “WELCOME VALUED CUSTOMER!”

The group’s attention snapped to Chewy.

The woman’s eyes narrowed. “Is that… a Speaker?”

Midas’s stomach dropped. He remembered the graffiti’s warning. *Don’t trust the speaker.*

Chewy’s LED blinked. “PLEASE STATE YOUR REQUEST.”

The woman stepped closer, her smile tightening. “Where did you find that unit?”

“He found me,” Midas said quickly. “Rescue situation. Very touching. Lots of tears.”

Chewy laughed, as if on cue, and then said: “LIMITED TIME OFFER!”

The woman’s politeness sharpened into a blade. “Speaker units are property of the Program. They issue announcements. They keep order. They are… important.”

Midas tried diplomacy, because he had a sincere belief that words could solve problems if arranged correctly.

“We don’t want trouble,” he said. “We’re just looking for water filter cartridges.”

The woman’s smile became almost pitying. “Oh. Water filtration is Tier Two. You are not authorized for Tier Two.”

Midas, sincerely confused, said the exact wrong thing.

“I don’t think authorization means anything anymore,” he said.

The alley went quiet in the way a room goes quiet when someone insults the host’s mother.

The man with the cone hugged it closer.

The woman’s voice remained polite. “We prefer to avoid violence,” she said. “It complicates the paperwork. Hand over the basket. And the Speaker. And we will issue you a receipt.”

“A receipt,” Midas repeated.

“Yes,” she said. “It will entitle you to—”

Chewy’s speaker cut in, bright as a sunrise. “NO REFUNDS!”

Midas made a decision.

It wasn’t a good decision. It was, however, a decision.

He grabbed Chewy under one arm, basket under the other, and ran.

Behind him, the woman called, still politely: “Sir! Sir! You are in violation! Please stop running! Sir!”
