I remember the back door giving a small, stubborn click when I pulled it to, the sound too sharp in a house that had been holding its breath all evening.

Upstairs, my parents’ bedroom was dark, but dark didn’t mean asleep. In Kiruna—outside Kiruna, really, in the little village where my mother had grown up and where she had decided we needed “a few weeks of quiet”—silence had different rules. In Stockholm, night was a covering. Here it was a surface you could scratch.

I stood on the step and let the cold take its measurements. It wasn’t dramatic, not the way people describe the north when they want to impress someone. It was simply bigger than I was used to, as if the air had more space inside it and that space was filled with small, active sounds: the thin tick of spruce needles in wind, something moving under crusted snow, the distant creak of a porch swing that nobody sat on.

My watch face glowed a sickly green when I tilted my wrist. 23:41. I kept checking it the way you touch your pocket for keys. It didn’t protect me, but it gave my hands something to do.

The yard was a pale rectangle between the house and the treeline, the snow tamped down where my father had shoveled earlier. Beyond that the fields ran out into darkness, and the darkness wasn’t empty. It had edges, the way a room has corners. I stepped off the shoveled path and my boot broke through a crust with a crack that felt like a mistake.

“Aren’t you going to change your mind?” a voice said softly from the side of the house.

Astrid came out from behind the woodpile as if she’d been waiting there the whole time. Of course she had. She was wearing her father’s old parka that swallowed her thin shoulders, the hood up, blond hair escaping in dry strands. A backpack hung from one shoulder. She looked, in the low light, like any girl from my class going somewhere her parents didn’t know about. The only difference was the way she watched me—steady, impatient, like she’d already moved on to the next part.

“I’m here,” I whispered, and hated how much that sounded like agreeing to something.

Astrid lifted the backpack strap with a small shrug, the weight settling. “Good. Then listen.”

She spoke as if we were about to sneak into the swimming pool in town, not walk out across someone’s fields to sit on a burial mound at midnight. She unzipped the bag just enough to show me, by the light of a cheap flashlight, the contents arranged like she’d packed for a school trip.

A thermos. A packet of chewing gum. Cigarettes with a Swedish warning label I’d seen in my cousin’s drawer. A cassette player with headphones wound around it, the kind everyone had in the mid-nineties, scuffed and held together with tape. Then the other things: a little paper salt carton folded flat, a length of thread wound neatly around cardboard, an iron nail as long as my index finger, a heel of rye bread in foil, a small knife with a dull wooden handle, and something wrapped in a cloth that looked like a handkerchief.

“Why the nail?” I asked, because it was easier than asking why any of it.

Astrid’s mouth made a line that could have been a smile. “Iron is iron. My mormor says it’s rude to go empty-handed.”

“This is your grandmother’s idea?” I asked, though I already knew it wasn’t. My mother had mentioned Astrid’s grandmother in that careful tone adults used when they were trying not to call someone superstitious.

Astrid shut the zipper most of the way, keeping the flashlight beam inside. “She didn’t tell me to do it. She told me how. There’s a difference.”

She started walking without waiting for me. I followed because the alternative was to stand there alone, the back door behind me like a judge’s face.

We cut along the edge of the yard, past the shed, through a gap in the fence where the wood had warped. The snow here was deeper, uneven. Our breath made quick clouds that were gone before I could watch them disperse.

Astrid kept her voice low and practical.

“First rule,” she said. “You don’t bring anything shiny you can’t leave. Rings, earrings, watches—take them off. They catch.” She glanced back at my wrist.

I pulled the watch strap loose with stiff fingers. The cold made the buckle clumsy. The skin beneath it was pale and damp. I held the watch in my hand, suddenly stupidly attached to it.

“Put it in your pocket,” Astrid said, “and don’t touch it again.”

I did, and my pocket felt heavier, as if it had swallowed time.

“Second,” she continued. “You don’t say names. Not yours, not anyone’s. If you hear your name, you don’t answer. If you think you hear your name, you don’t answer. If you’re not sure, you don’t answer.”

“What if it’s you?” I said.

Astrid made a sound that might have been a laugh if it had any warmth. “Then I shouldn’t say it, should I?”

We walked between the fields and the first line of trees. The snow here had a crust that held for a step and then broke for the next, so my weight was always being corrected. The forest smelled of sap and something metallic. I could hear, far away, a motor on the main road—one car, a long steady sound that faded.

“Third rule,” Astrid said. “No looking behind you once we start. You can look now. You can look all you want now. But when we sit, you look at what’s in front of you or you close your eyes. You don’t turn your head. You don’t twist to see. If you feel something behind you, you let it be behind you.”

A cold pinch ran up my spine. I wanted, instinctively, to test the rule by turning, to see the field and the house and the smudge of our own footprints. I forced myself not to, which made the urge worse.

Astrid kept going, her words gathering.

“No talking during. If you need to cough, you do it into your sleeve, silently. If you need to scratch, you don’t. If you see lights, you keep your eyes down. If you hear someone ask you a question, you don’t answer. Not ‘yes,’ not ‘no.’ Nothing. If something offers you something, you don’t take it. If you feel like you should stand, you don’t stand.”

“How long do we sit?” My voice sounded too loud, swallowed by my scarf.

“Until it’s done,” Astrid said.

“And how do you know when it’s done?”

Astrid’s hooded head turned slightly toward me. The flashlight beam swung over the snow, over a low stump, over a hare track, and then away again. “You’ll know,” she said, and for the first time her voice wasn’t teasing. It was almost flat.

I could have turned back then. I could have said this was ridiculous, we’d get caught, my mother would kill me, I had a train back to Stockholm in two weeks and didn’t need whatever this was. But we were already far enough that the house lights were gone behind the trees, and the idea of walking back alone, breaking our own trail, felt worse than going forward with someone who knew where she was stepping.

The burial mound wasn’t impressive. Not at first. There was no dramatic hump rising out of the snow like a sleeping animal. In the dark, it was only a slight swell in the ground, the kind you might think was a drift. The trees opened into a small clearing where the snow was thinner, grass pushing through in stiff, yellow blades.

Astrid stopped and pointed her flashlight down.

There, under the grass, was a ring of stones. Not a perfect circle, not the way you’d draw it. Stones half swallowed by earth, the tops showing like knuckles. In the center, the ground dipped, a shallow depression. I could see, at the edge of it, something dark and cylindrical—an old beer can crushed flat, the aluminum crinkled into a flower.

On a larger rock just outside the ring someone had scratched initials with a knife. The letters were shallow and hesitant. Nearby, a burned match lay black against the snow.

“It’s just a hill,” I said, but I didn’t mean it.

“It’s not,” Astrid replied, and stepped closer without hesitation, as if there was a path you could see if you knew it. She crouched and ran her gloved hand over one of the stones. “Here.”

She aimed the flashlight at a slightly flatter stone inside the ring, positioned like a seat facing toward the treeline. “Mormor called it stolen,” Astrid said. “Like it was taken out of something bigger.”

“Who stole it?”

Astrid shrugged. “People. The dead. I don’t know. Sit there.”

My mouth went dry. I looked at the crushed can, at the initials, and the effect was worse than if the place had been untouched. It meant other kids had been here and had still left. It meant this was a place you could treat like any other—until you couldn’t.

Astrid set her backpack down and unzipped it fully. The flashlight lay on the ground, beam angled up, making the grass around us glow and our shadows stretch long and wrong across the snow.

She took out the thread first. It was a thin white cotton, the kind my mother kept in a sewing tin. Astrid tied one end around her finger, not tight, then stood and walked slowly around the depression, laying the thread on the ground in a circle, careful not to tangle it.

“This is just so you know where you are,” she said, as if anticipating my skepticism. “So you don’t… drift.”

Drift. The word sat in my head like something heavy.

She placed the iron nail at the point closest to the trees, pressing it into the ground until it stood upright in the frozen earth. Then she sprinkled salt in a line along the thread, not continuously, but in small, deliberate piles, like commas.

“Hold this,” she said, and handed me the bread.

It was cold and dense, smelling faintly of caraway. I took it with both hands as if it might crumble, though it wouldn’t. It felt more real than anything else in the backpack.

“Don’t eat it,” Astrid added. “Not yet. Just hold it in your mouth when I tell you.”

“What’s the knife for?” I asked, nodding at it on the ground.

Astrid’s gloved fingers paused over the cloth-wrapped bundle. “For cutting thread,” she said lightly. Then, as if correcting herself: “For cutting things that shouldn’t be tied.”

She unwrapped the cloth. Inside was a small object—at first I thought it was a stone, but it was smoother, darker, and when she turned it in the flashlight beam it caught no light at all. Something like bone? Something like wood? It was carved into a simple shape with no obvious meaning. Astrid held it for a second, then placed it near the nail.

I wanted to ask where she’d gotten it. I didn’t.

“Take off your watch,” she said again.

“It’s in my pocket.”

Astrid looked at my pocket, then at my face. “Fine. Don’t touch it. Sit.”

I lowered myself onto the flat stone. It was colder than the air, cold that went straight through denim. Astrid adjusted my feet with her boot, nudging them together, toes pointed forward.

“Hands under your thighs,” she instructed, and I obeyed, folding my gloved hands beneath me so my wrists were trapped. “Back straight. Don’t lean. You’re not watching TV.”

I sat up, my spine suddenly aware of itself. The bread waited in my hands, which were now pinned.

Astrid sat opposite me, just outside the thread circle, knees drawn up, her own hands tucked under her legs. She set the flashlight between us but angled it away, so it lit only the ground, leaving our faces in shadow.

“Okay,” she whispered. “Now.”

“What—” I began.

Astrid lifted a finger, not dramatic, just a clear instruction.

We waited.

The first minutes were nothing but my body complaining: the cold at the back of my thighs, the way my scarf scratched my chin, saliva gathering because I didn’t know what to do with my mouth. I listened for something to happen and in doing so made the night louder. A branch snapped somewhere in the trees. Not close. A rabbit, I told myself. Or snow settling.

My pocket pressed against my hip where the watch was, and I could feel its shape too clearly, as if it were a foreign object.

Somewhere far off, a dog barked once and then stopped.

My eyes adjusted. The darkness in front of me became less like a wall and more like depth. I could see the line of trees, their trunks a darker dark against the sky. The sky itself wasn’t black but a low bruised blue, thinly illuminated by snow.

Astrid didn’t move. Her stillness made mine feel like a performance.

Then, clean and distant, a single church bell rang.

Not a tolling. Not a sequence that could be explained by someone practicing or a service beginning. One chime, clear as a coin dropped on stone, and then nothing.

My stomach lurched as if the sound had been under my ribs. The bell came from the direction of the village church, which I’d seen earlier that day, white and plain, locked and dark. There was no midnight mass in my mother’s village. There were no bells for no reason.

Astrid’s shoulders twitched. Just once. A small involuntary lift, like she’d been touched.

Then she inhaled, slow. “Good,” she whispered, too quickly. Too much like she was trying to make it ordinary.

“Was that supposed to—” I started.

Astrid’s head turned a fraction toward me, and even without seeing her eyes I felt the warning. I shut my mouth. The bread in my hands suddenly smelled stronger, earthy and sour.

We sat.

Time stretched, then broke into pieces. Every sensation arrived, stayed too long, and then was replaced by another. The cold crept under my coat at my lower back. My toes went numb. My breath sounded loud inside my scarf, a wet rasp.

The forest reorganized itself by sound. Something moved to the right—small, quick. A heavier shift to the left that could have been a branch lowering under snow. The wind rose briefly, a thin hiss through grass, and then stopped too abruptly, like a record player needle lifted.

Astrid leaned forward slightly and whispered without moving her mouth much. “Don’t move.”

“I wasn’t,” I whispered back, but even that felt like moving.

Astrid’s gloved hand lifted a few centimeters, palm down, as if pressing air flat. “No talking,” she mouthed.

I swallowed. The act felt huge.

Minutes or hours later—my sense of it is useless now—I felt an itch start at the side of my nose. At first it was nothing, a tickle. Then it became a demand. My eyes watered. The itch spread, as if the skin itself were waking up and noticing the cold.

I held still, hands trapped under my thighs, and tried to think of anything else. The bell sound replayed in my head, each ring with its clean decay.

Astrid shifted her weight just enough to make the grass crackle beneath her. “It’s working,” she breathed, so quietly I almost didn’t hear it.

“What is?” I mouthed.

Astrid’s face was a shadow, but her voice had that casual authority again, the tone she used in school when she borrowed someone’s pen without asking. “You can ask,” she whispered. “In your head. Not out loud. You can ask for… things.”

I stared at the dark in front of me. “Things,” I mouthed.

Astrid’s whisper came like a secret shared under a blanket. “You can find out what people really think. What they’ve done. You can make someone stop. You can make someone… want to.”

My throat tightened. In Stockholm, gossip was a sport and cruelty was a language you learned to speak with the right accent. Here, in this village where everyone knew everyone’s grandmother, my humiliation felt more exposed, like skin in cold air. I thought of my new school, of the girl who’d asked where I was from and then laughed softly when I said it, as if Stockholm was a costume I’d borrowed. I thought of the teacher who’d called me “city girl” in front of the class when I didn’t know the dialect word for something simple. I thought, shamefully, of the boy who’d looked at Astrid and then looked through me.

I pictured the girl’s face. I pictured the teacher’s hands on the desk. I pictured the boy’s mouth, how it curved when he smiled at someone else.

In my head, I formed a question. Small. Petty. It felt like a pebble I could spit into a well.

What does she say about me when I’m not there?

The moment I thought it, my skin prickled. Not with fear exactly. With the sensation of being heard.

Astrid whispered, barely audible. “Don’t be nice,” she said, as if she could smell my hesitation. “They aren’t.”

Something moved in the grass just beyond the thread circle. A soft, deliberate parting, like fingers combing through hair. My eyes snapped down to the illuminated patch near the nail. Nothing was there. Only grass blades trembling as if from a draft.

Then, from somewhere ahead, not from the trees exactly but from the space between them, I heard a sound that might have been wind.

Except it had shape.

It rose and fell in two syllables that matched the length of my name.

Lin-ne-a.

My lungs locked. My whole body reacted before my mind could decide what I’d heard. Tears sprang hot in my eyes and immediately cooled on my lashes. Nausea rolled up from my stomach like something surfacing.

I did not answer. I did not move.

Beside me, Astrid’s breath hitched once.

“Did you hear—” she began, and stopped, the words cut off as if she’d bitten them.

I stared straight ahead, because the rule was no looking behind, but the urge to turn was so strong it felt like a hand at my chin, guiding my face. The darkness behind me became a physical idea. I could feel it like heat.

Again: the sound. Not louder. Closer in the way a thought is close.

Lin-ne-a.
