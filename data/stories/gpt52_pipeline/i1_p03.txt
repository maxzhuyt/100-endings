The bell above the door gave a frantic little yelp as he shouldered into the bookshop, dragging a gust of rain and the sharp, metallic smell of wet slate with him.

Outside, on the narrow street, a voice called—too bright, too certain. “Oi! Rhys! Over here!”

He slammed the door, pressed his back to the glass for half a second as if he could hold the whole world out with his spine, then turned the lock with a decisive click that sounded like a punchline.

He flashed a grin at the empty shop as though the shelves might applaud. “Right,” he said to no one in particular, breathless, laughing in the wrong place. “Brilliant. Perfect. Lovely little—”

“Bore da,” someone said, flat as a ruler.

He jolted. Behind the counter—chipped pine, a scatter of receipts, a mug with *DARLLENWCH* on it—a person looked up from a ledger without haste. Their hair was pinned back; their cardigan was the color of oatmeal; their expression was the sort that didn’t shift for storms or famous men.

They took him in: dripping coat, too-expensive boots, the damp curl escaping his hat, the way his eyes kept flicking to the front window like it might suddenly sprout cameras.

“Shop’s open,” they said, “so I’m assuming you’re either buying a book or you’re lost.”

He lifted his hands in surrender, still smiling, still trying to make the air friendly enough to breathe. “Buying. Absolutely buying. I’m a great buyer. I buy. Books.” His gaze darted to their face, seeking the smallest lift of mouth—approval like a handhold.

Nothing.

They stood, walked around the counter, and paused at the puddle he’d made on the worn rug. “Take your feet off my mat,” they said. “Or at least stop drowning it.”

He looked down, as if surprised by the existence of water. “Oh—God, sorry.” He shuffled two inches and attempted a charming wince. “This is not how I normally enter places. I usually—” he gestured vaguely, “use doors with a bit less… siege.”

“I can see you’ve had practice entering places,” they said, deadpan. “But not with manners.”

He blinked, then laughed too loud, then checked their eyes again. “I deserve that,” he said quickly. “I’m Rhys.”

“I know.” They crossed to the front and, without asking his permission, pulled the little lace curtain across the lower half of the door window. Then they flipped the sign to *CAU / CLOSED* as if it were Tuesday and not a small-town street suddenly full of hungry attention. “And that,” they added, “is why you’re not staying there.”

He tried for charm attempt number one, the automatic one. “Thank you. You’re very kind. I’ll make it worth your while.”

Their eyebrows rose in a way that suggested they had seen tourists attempt to tip their way into decency and had not been moved. “You’ll buy a book. That’s how you’ll make it worth my while.”

He swallowed, smile still on. “Of course. What do you recommend? Something… Welsh. Something that says, ‘I’m not a menace who tramples rugs.’”

They walked toward him, not close enough to touch, but close enough that he could smell tea on their breath. “Take your coat off,” they said. “You’re dripping on the first editions.”

“Right. Coat.” He tugged it off, fumbled it, nearly dropped it. His hands weren’t steady. He was trying to keep his face bright while his body betrayed him.

They took the coat without ceremony and hung it on a hook behind the counter as if he were any damp customer. “Name?” they asked.

He frowned. “I just told you.”

“Your other one,” they said. “The one people use when they’re not shouting at you through glass.”

He hesitated, the grin faltering for the first time like a lightbulb flickering. “Rhys is… fine,” he said, too quick.

They held his gaze a beat longer than polite. Something in his eyes tightened—annoyance, embarrassment, hurt. It passed so fast it might have been imagined, except their mouth softened by a fraction, as if they’d seen the bruise under the makeup.

“Fine,” they said. “Rhys. Go stand away from the window before someone takes your photo through my poetry shelf.”

He brightened at the smallest shift—relief flooding him so quickly it was almost undignified. “Thank you,” he said, and tried charm attempt number two: self-deprecation. “I’m not usually this… skittish. I’m normally very brave. Heroic, even. There was a horse once—”

“Mm.” They started toward the back, gesturing with a blunt finger. “Come on, then. If you’re buying, you’re browsing. Down here. Away from the front.”

He followed, drawn into the shop’s depths. The floorboards complained softly. The air changed: paper and dust and old glue, a faint sweetness of dried lavender tucked into a jar by the till. A low lamp burned beside an ancient armchair that looked as if it had held a hundred rainy afternoons. A shelf labeled *BARDDAETH / POETRY* ran along one wall; another bore a hand-lettered sign: *MAPIAU / MAPS*.

“I like this place,” he said, and it came out less like a line and more like a discovery. His fingers hovered over a row of spines as though he didn’t trust himself not to smudge them. “It’s… proper. Not one of those shops that sells candles shaped like books.”

“It’s a shop that sells books,” they said. They reached past him to straighten a display of used paperbacks, their knuckles brushing his sleeve—accidental, brisk, but it sent heat up his arm as if he’d been touched on purpose.

He glanced at them, then away, then back again. Always checking. “Do you own it?”

“I do.” They slid a book into place. “And I don’t do signed photos.”

“That’s fair,” he said immediately, then tried charm attempt number three: flattery with an edge of sincerity. “You’re… very good at being unimpressed.”

They snorted. It was quick—one sound that could have been nothing, except his whole face changed as if the sun had come out. His shoulders dropped a notch.

He latched onto it with careful joy. “There it is,” he said softly, as if to himself.

Their hands stilled over a stack. “There what is?”

“Your laugh,” he said, then, realizing he’d said too much, added, “It’s—rare. Like a—limited edition.”

They looked down at their hands, as if busying themselves with order. Their fingers were ink-stained at the tips. “You’re a menace,” they muttered, but their voice had lengthened, the syllables less clipped.

He watched their hands as if they were a story. “What’s your name?” he asked, and for once it didn’t sound like a tactic.

They hesitated. The pause was small, but it was real.

“Eira,” they said at last. “It’s on the sign above the door if you ever looked up.”

“I did look up,” he said quickly, then caught himself—caught the way he always scrambled to be liked, even now, even with one person and a poetry shelf and rain ticking the windows. He let out a breath. “I’m sorry. I’m—”

Eira tipped their head. “What?”

He stared at the spines until the titles blurred. “I’m exhausting,” he said, and he tried to laugh, but it didn’t quite take. “Isn’t it? The constant… patter.”

Eira’s eyes narrowed, not in anger but in focus, like a reader leaning into a difficult line. “Is it patter,” they asked, “or are you afraid of silence?”

The question landed like a thumb on a bruise.

Rhys’s smile returned out of reflex, too fast. “Silence is fine. I love silence. I meditate. I—” He stopped. His gaze flicked to Eira’s face, and for once he didn’t find a reaction he could control. He found only attention.

His shoulders rose, then fell. “I did an audition once,” he said, and the words came as if he were trying them for size. “Years ago. Small thing. Theatre. I’d been telling everyone—my mates, my mum—that this one would be it. I walked into the room and…” He made a helpless little gesture. “Nothing. My mouth went dry. My brain did that thing where it empties itself, like someone pulled a plug. And the casting director just… watched me. Quiet. No expression. Like waiting for a kettle that wasn’t going to boil.”

Eira didn’t interrupt. They leaned a hip against the shelf, arms loose, giving him space without stepping back.

“I started making jokes,” he continued, voice low. “Terrible jokes. Anything to fill the gap. He didn’t laugh. Nobody did. And I remember thinking—this is it. This is what it feels like when people decide you’re not worth the air.”

His throat bobbed. He swallowed. “Afterwards, I went into the loo and practiced smiling in the mirror until my face hurt, because I couldn’t bear the thought of walking out and looking like I’d failed.”

Eira’s fingers tightened on the edge of a book, then relaxed. Their eyes—grey-green, steady—held his. “Who made you think you had to earn being looked at?” they asked quietly.

He blinked, startled by the gentleness more than the question. “My dad,” he said, and the admission was so simple it almost sounded like a child. “He… only noticed me when I was performing. Funny. Loud. Useful.”

Eira’s jaw shifted as if they were biting back something sharp. When they spoke, their voice was softer than before, but still blunt. “That’s a rotten way to raise someone.”

Rhys let out a shaky breath that might have been a laugh. “Tell him that.”

A sudden rap on the front door cracked through the shop.

Rhys flinched so hard his hand hit the shelf, rattling a row of slim volumes. He swore under his breath, then overcorrected into brightness like flipping a switch. “Probably just—someone wanting to buy a candle shaped like a book,” he said too fast.

Another knock. A muffled voice: “Hello? We saw someone come in—”

Rhys’s eyes went wide. The practiced grin was back, fixed and frantic. “Right,” he whispered, already edging toward the aisle as if escape might be found between *MAPIAU* and *BARDDAETH*. “I’ll just—slip out the back. Is there a back? There’s always a back in—”

“There isn’t,” Eira said.

Rhys’s breath hitched. He looked like a man about to bolt through a wall.

Eira moved before they could think—before the unimpressed façade could reassert itself. They reached for his wrist and tugged him, firm and practical. “This way,” they said. “And if you knock anything over, you’re putting it back in order.”

His wrist in their hand went still, not because he’d calmed but because something in him had been startled into quiet: the feeling of being handled without spectacle.

Eira led him between two shelves to a narrow corridor that smelled of cardboard and printer ink. At the end was a door half-hidden by a hanging curtain of old Ordnance Survey maps, their green lines and faded grids fluttering when Eira pushed through.

“Stock room,” Eira said, slipping inside. “Mind the box. Don’t stand on the—honestly, Rhys, you’re like a wet deer.”

“I’m a very dignified wet deer,” he whispered, trying to make it a joke and failing. The room was small, stacked with cartons and a folding table. Close enough that he could feel Eira’s warmth when they turned to pull the curtain back into place.

Outside, the doorbell jangled again as someone tried the handle. The *CAU* sign held. A camera flash strobed through the thin gap at the edge of the curtain like lightning.

Rhys sucked in a breath. “I’m sorry,” he whispered. “I shouldn’t have dragged this to your—your—” He glanced around, absurdly, as if the boxes might judge him. “Your sanctuary.”

Eira’s hand hovered near the curtain, steady. “You didn’t drag it,” they said. “They did.”

He looked at them—really looked. In the dim stock-room light, their unimpressed armor looked less like disdain and more like discipline. Like someone who knew how to keep their life their own.

“You’re doing this,” he said, voice rough, “because you’re kind.”

Eira’s eyes flicked to his face, then away, as if kindness were an accusation. “I’m doing it because you look like you’re about to be sick,” they said. “And because it’s raining, and you’ve already flooded my rug.”

His mouth twitched. “That rug—iconic. I’ll have to write it a thank-you note.”

“Don’t,” Eira said. Then, after a beat: “Unless you buy it a book.”

The knocking outside faded into mutters. Footsteps receded, a car door slammed, an engine started and moved off. The street quieted until only rain remained, patient and insistent.

Rhys exhaled slowly, like letting go of a part he’d been holding too tight. “Eira,” he said, testing the name as carefully as he’d tested every joke earlier, but this time it wasn’t for approval. It was for closeness. “You didn’t have to.”

“I know,” Eira said.

In the small space, their words seemed to take up physical room. Rhys’s hands were empty, open at his sides, as if he didn’t know what to do without a script.

“I do want to buy a book,” he said finally. “A real one. From you. Not—” He waved vaguely toward the door and the world beyond it. “Not as a prop.”

Eira’s expression changed—a tiny thaw around the eyes. “What do you like?” they asked. “Don’t say yourself.”

He made a quiet sound that might have been laughter. “I like… things that tell the truth without shouting,” he said. “Is that a genre?”

Eira considered him for a moment, then reached past a box and pulled a slim hardback from a carton, as if they’d known exactly where it was. The cover was simple, the title stamped in Welsh and English: *The Mabinogion / Y Mabinogi*. They held it out.

“Old stories,” Eira said. “Heroes, curses, people turning into animals. Love that doesn’t behave.”

Rhys took it as if it were fragile. His fingers brushed theirs, and this time neither of them moved away too fast. He looked down at the book, then up. “You’re recommending me trouble,” he said softly.

“I’m recommending you something that lasts,” Eira replied.

A pause, full and unhurried.

Rhys swallowed. “Will you—” He stopped, grimaced, tried again without the shine. “Would you let me come back? Another day. When it’s not… this.”

Eira’s gaze flicked toward the curtain, toward the rain and the street and the pressure of eyes. Then back to him.

“If you come back,” they said, “you come as a customer. You pay. You wipe your feet. And you don’t expect me to clap.”

He nodded, solemn as a vow. “Deal.”

Eira reached into their pocket, found a pencil, and opened the book to the inside cover. In neat handwriting, they wrote something and tore a thin strip of paper from a pad on the table. They tucked it inside the book like a secret.

Rhys glanced down. On the paper: **“TUESDAY. AFTER LUNCH. IF IT’S RAINING, COME ANYWAY.”**

He looked up, caught. “That’s—”

“Practical,” Eira said, voice dry, but their ears had gone pink.

Rhys’s smile this time was small enough to be real. “Diolch,” he said, the Welsh careful on his tongue. “I’ll try not to be a wet deer.”

Eira’s mouth tilted. “Good,” they said. “Because I’m not hiding you in my maps twice.”

Rhys held the book to his chest for a second, like proof. “I’ll come back,” he said.

Eira lifted the curtain and let the stock room’s dimness give way to the shop’s warm lamplight. “Then come back,” they replied, as if it were nothing more than a line on a receipt. “And shut the door properly next time. The bell complains.”
