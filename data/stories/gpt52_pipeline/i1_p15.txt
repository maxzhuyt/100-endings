The clinic door sticks for a second before it gives, the hinge sighing like it’s annoyed I’ve come back. The front desk is to my left—two chairs, a low table with old magazines and a bottle of hand sanitizer that smells like hospital oranges. Fluorescent light makes everything look clean in the way a gym bathroom looks clean: bleached, thin.

“Name?” the receptionist asks without looking up.

I say it. The syllables feel like I’m signing something I don’t agree to.

She taps, prints, slides a clipboard across. “Have a seat. He’ll be with you.”

Mandatory orders. As if my body is a workplace hazard to be corrected. I sign where the line tells me to sign and sit with my back too straight, because slouching tugs the front of my hip and reminds me exactly what’s wrong.

My left hip doesn’t let me forget. It isn’t a dramatic pain most of the time; it’s worse than that. It’s a tight, bright pinch deep in the crease when I lift my knee or try to stride out, like someone hooked a finger under a tendon and is pulling. If I move wrong, it turns sharp enough to make my vision narrow. If I move right, it’s a constant warning.

A door on the right side of the waiting area opens. “You can come back.”

The hallway smells like disinfectant and exercise bands—rubber warmed by hands. He leads me to the second room on the left. I clock it automatically, the way I used to clock a new gym space: exits, obstacles, what I could use, what could use me.

Door behind me. Treatment table centered with its long side parallel to the far wall, paper roll mounted at the head. A rolling stool tucked near the foot. A sink and soap dispenser on the right wall, paper towels stacked neatly. On the left wall, above where my eyes would land if I lay down, a musculature diagram mounted in a thin metal frame—red and blue ink, flayed bodies posed like they’re demonstrating without complaint.

An arena. Small. Bright. No place to hide.

“Go ahead and have a seat on the table,” he says, professional tone, a hand indicating the near edge.

I take one step toward it and my hip catches. Not enough for anyone else to see, but I feel the tiny hitch, the way my pelvis wants to tip to spare the front. I hate that he might notice. I hate more that he will, because that’s why I’m here.

I sit on the edge, paper crinkling under me. My hands grip the table’s vinyl as if it’s going to move.

He closes the door with his foot, quiet. Then he turns fully toward me.

It’s him.

For a half second my brain tries to place him in the only context it wants: the old weight room, chalk dust in the air, metal plates clanging, his laugh sharp and quick when someone missed a rep. Back then he wore cut-off tees and lived in a state of constant exertion, forearms veined, neck corded, eyes always measuring.

Now he’s in a gray polo with a clinic logo and black pants. Hair shorter. Clean. Calm. A laminated badge clipped at his hip. The same shoulders, though—wide enough to crowd a doorway—and the same hands, long-fingered, capable. He looks at me like a person, not a competitor.

“Hey,” he says, and then corrects himself smoothly. “Good morning. I’m Mark. I’ll be your physiotherapist today.”

My name comes next, said with that practiced clarity clinicians use. It still hits like a punch. He knows exactly who I am, but he’s giving us a script we can both hold onto.

I force my face into neutral. “Yeah. Hi.”

His eyes flick down—quick, automatic—to my hip and then back to my face. Not predatory, not lingering. Assessment.

“Before we get started,” he says, “I need to confirm: you’re here for left anterior hip pain—suspected hip flexor strain versus tendinopathy, per the referral. Is that right?”

I nod.

“Any changes since your last appointment? Any numbness, tingling, bowel or bladder changes?”

“No.”

“Okay.” He holds a clipboard against his forearm. The motion makes the muscles in his forearm shift under skin, a memory of grip strength and ropes and bars. My stomach does something stupid—an involuntary, low heat like my body is confusing proximity with permission.

I clamp down on it immediately, like slamming a lid on a boiling pot. Not here. Not now. Not with him.

“What’s your pain right now, zero to ten?” he asks.

“Three.”

“And at its worst this week?”

“Seven. Maybe eight if I try to sprint.”

He doesn’t react to the number. Professionals never do; they just file it.

“Describe it for me. Sharp, dull, burning?”

“Pinch. Deep.” I gesture to the front crease where my thigh meets my pelvis. “Sometimes sharp if I lift my knee fast.”

“Anything that makes it better?”

“Not moving.”

A hint of something crosses his face—humor maybe, or empathy—but it’s gone as soon as it appears. “Okay. Today I want to check your movement, see what reproduces it, and then we’ll do some manual work and start loading it in a controlled way. I’ll explain what I’m doing as we go. If anything feels too much or wrong, tell me. Stop means stop, and we adjust.”

His voice is steady. The words are simple. The authority in them is absolute.

“Fine,” I say, and hate the way it sounds like surrender.

He sets the clipboard on the counter by the sink, then steps back. “Can you stand up for me? Just in front of the table.”

I slide off the table carefully, leading with my right leg so the left doesn’t have to lift. I stand with the table behind me, the diagram in my peripheral vision on the left wall.

He positions himself a few feet away, not in my space. “Just walk to the door and back.”

I do. Two steps toward the door, turn, two steps back. The hip pinches on the turn. I keep my face blank.

“Okay,” he says. “Stand with your feet hip-width. Weight even.”

I try. My body wants to shift off the left. He sees it anyway.

“Good. Now—bring your left knee up like you’re marching.”

The command is absurdly simple. I lift the knee a few inches and the pinch lights up, sharp and immediate.

“There,” I say before I can stop myself. My voice gives me away.

“Okay. Put it down.” He watches me set it down. “That’s at about… thirty degrees of hip flexion. Where do you feel it?”

“Right there.” I tap the crease again.

He nods. “Have a seat on the edge of the table again.”

I sit. The paper crackles under me like a reprimand.

He rolls the stool closer but doesn’t sit yet. “I’m going to palpate around the front of your hip and the hip flexor tendon. I’ll keep it clinical and you let me know if anything feels uncomfortable beyond what’s expected.”

“Sure,” I say, and my throat tightens on the word.

He washes his hands at the sink—water, soap, rinse, paper towel—every motion precise. The ritual creates distance for a second. Then he comes back, stool rolling softly.

“Can you lie back for me? Knees bent, feet on the table.”

I lie supine, head near the paper roll, the diagram now directly above and slightly to my left. The flayed figure’s iliopsoas is highlighted like a road on a map. I don’t look at it. I do anyway.

My knees are up. Feet flat. The position pulls on the front of my left hip. I keep my breath shallow so my abdomen doesn’t move too much.

He sits on the stool on my left side, close enough that his knee almost touches the table. “I’m going to start just outside your hip bone.” He places his fingertips on the bony ridge of my pelvis—ASIS, I think automatically, because once I knew these landmarks for lifts and cues and mobility drills. He presses lightly, then a little deeper, moving a centimeter at a time.

His touch is not rough. It’s worse because it’s exact.

“There?” he asks, as his fingers dip into the soft space just inside the bone.

A shock of tenderness radiates inward, sharp, and my left thigh wants to jerk away. I stop it by force.

“Yeah,” I say, clipped.

“Okay. That’s consistent.” He moves lower, along the tendon line. “Any numbness or tingling?”

“No.”

He slides his hand away, then places his palm flat on my pelvis. “Next I want to check your range of motion. I’ll support your leg. Tell me the moment you feel the pinch—not after.”

His hand goes under my left ankle, the other supporting under my knee. The contact is functional, cradling my limb like it’s a lever. He lifts, bringing my hip into flexion slowly.

My stomach tightens. My skin remembers old spotter hands, old closeness. I hate myself for noticing the warmth of his fingers through my leggings.

“Now?” he asks at maybe twenty degrees.

“Not yet.”

He lifts a little more. His other hand shifts to the front of my pelvis, firm, preventing it from tilting. Stabilize the pelvis, move the femur. Basic.

At thirty degrees the pinch appears, deep and bright.

“Now,” I say, and my voice catches on the word.

He stops immediately. Holds. “Okay. Rate that—zero to ten.”

“Five.”

“Is it sharp, like a catch, or more like a stretch?”

“Catch.”

He lowers my leg slightly until the catch eases. “Better?”

“Yeah. Three.”

“Good. I’m going to rotate your hip a bit. Same rules.”

He moves my knee outward, external rotation, while his stabilizing hand keeps my pelvis from rolling. The pressure shifts the sensation: less pinch, more tension along the inner thigh.

“That feel okay?” he asks.

“Fine.”

He nods, then brings my knee inward, internal rotation. The pinch spikes again, sharper this time.

“Sharp,” I say immediately. “That’s sharp.”

He stops. “Okay. Thank you.” He changes the angle—reduces flexion slightly, keeps rotation smaller. “How about there?”

The sharpness dulls into a deep ache. “That’s tolerable.”

“Good. That’s what I want—tolerable, controlled. Not stabbing.” His tone is even, but there’s a thread of care in it that makes me angry. I don’t want care. Care makes this real.

He sets my leg down gently. “Now I’m going to do a Thomas test position to see hip flexor length and if it reproduces your symptoms. I’ll guide you into it. You tell me if you’re comfortable.”

I swallow. “Okay.”

He moves to stand near my left hip. “Scoot down so your hips are closer to the edge of the table.”

I shift, the paper tearing slightly under my back. The edge of the table is now just under my pelvis. My head is farther from the diagram; my eyes still catch it, that red iliopsoas running like a rope from spine to femur.

“Bring your right knee to your chest and hold it,” he instructs.

I do, clasping my hands around my right shin. My left leg hangs off the table, gravity pulling. Immediately the front of my left hip tightens, and the pinch flares.

He places one hand on my left thigh, just above the knee, and the other on my pelvis. “I’m going to apply gentle downward pressure to let the hip extend. This should feel like a stretch in the front of the hip, maybe discomfort, but not sharp joint pain. Tell me what you feel.”

His hand on my pelvis is anchoring me. The other presses my thigh down a few centimeters. The stretch blooms along the front of my hip—iliopsoas, rectus femoris—deep and insistent.

My toes flex involuntarily. My left quadriceps tremors. My breath stalls, not from pain alone, but from the fact that his body is close enough that I can hear the soft sound of his inhale, feel heat radiating from him.

“Pain scale?” he asks.

“Six,” I manage. “Stretchy. And… a pinch.”

“Where’s the pinch?” His voice is lower, not intimate—focused.

“Deep. Same spot.”

He eases off a fraction. The pinch fades, leaving a heavy pull.

“Now?” he asks.

“Four. Stretch.”

“Good.” He holds it there, steady. “Stay breathing. In through your nose, out through your mouth. Don’t fight it with your ribs.”

I want to tell him to stop talking to me like I’m someone who needs coaching. I want to tell him I used to coach myself through pain that mattered.

Instead I breathe because my body listens to him.

“Tell me if you want me to back off,” he says quietly.

“A little more,” I hear myself say, and immediately hate the words. Like I’m asking for something.

He increases pressure by a hair. The stretch intensifies, deep ache spreading down the front of my thigh. My hands tighten on my right shin. My abdomen hardens to protect. My left thigh trembles harder.

“Okay—there,” I say quickly. “That’s… close.”

He stops, holds. “What’s your number?”

“Seven,” I admit.

“Sharp?”

“No. Just… intense.”

“Okay.” He maintains the position for a few seconds, then oscillates—tiny movements, easing and returning, like testing a locked door without breaking it.

My pulse is loud in my ears. The room is too bright. The paper under me crackles with every involuntary shift.

Then his thumb adjusts on my pelvis, sliding half an inch to prevent me from anteriorly tilting. The contact is so precise it feels like he knows where my body is lying to him.

My breath changes. I feel it change. So does he.

He pauses—just a fraction too long. His hand still on my pelvis, fingers splayed. The heat of his palm seeps through fabric into bone.

“I’m going to move my hand a bit more medial,” he says immediately, neutralizing the moment with language. “Is that okay?”

“Yes,” I say too fast, too sharp, as if speed equals control.

He shifts. Pressure becomes purely mechanical again. My resentment snaps back into place like a brace.

He releases slowly, letting my left leg rise slightly as the stretch lets go. The sudden absence makes my hip feel exposed, raw.

“Sit up for me,” he says.

I let go of my right knee, bring both legs back onto the table with care, and push myself upright the same way I always do now—hands behind me, weight on my arms so my hip doesn’t have to do the work. I see him notice. I pretend not to.

He stands and gestures to the diagram on the wall. “Can I show you something?”

I follow his hand. He steps to the left wall, pointing with a capped pen. The drawn figure’s pelvis is tilted, muscles layered like geography.

“Here,” he says, tapping a long red muscle that disappears into the pelvic bowl. “Iliopsoas. It’s a hip flexor, but it also affects how your pelvis sits. When it’s irritated or guarding, it can feel like a deep pinch right where you described.”

His finger moves to another highlighted band. “And here—rectus femoris, part of your quadriceps. That one crosses both hip and knee, so when you’re trying to protect the hip, you end up changing your gait and overloading other areas.”

I stare at the diagram and feel myself flatten into it. Not an athlete. Not a person. A set of attachments and levers, labeled in Latin, offered up for correction.

I used to study these charts to get stronger. Now it’s a map of what I’ve lost.

“Okay,” I say, because what else is there.

He turns back to me. “Let’s do some active work. That’s where you start taking control back.”

Control. He says it like it’s available.

He grabs a small loop band from a drawer and a folded towel. “We’ll start with isometrics for hip flexion—pain-modulating and tendon-friendly. Then we’ll do a glute med exercise to help your pelvis stop cheating.”

He kneels on the floor beside the table, movement fluid. “Lie back again. Knees bent, feet flat. I’m going to place this band over your feet and cue you to drive your left knee up into my hand without actually moving.”

I lie down. The diagram is back in the corner of my eye like an accusation.

He loops the band around my forefeet and anchors it by holding the other end. “Okay. I’m going to put my hand just above your left knee. You’re going to gently push up into my hand—about thirty percent effort. We’re not trying to win. We’re trying to load without provoking. Ready?”

His palm settles above my knee, firm. He’s close enough that his forearm brushes the side of my thigh. My body registers it with an unwanted jolt.

“Ready,” I say.

“Push.” His voice is calm. “Hold. Two… three… four… five. And relax.”

I push. My hip flexors engage, a controlled burn in the front. The pinch threatens, but it stays muted at this low load. My thigh trembles on the fourth second.

“Good,” he says. “That tremor is normal. Your system’s been guarding. Again—push. Hold.”

We repeat. On the third rep, I push a little harder, spitefully, like if I can out-effort the problem it will back down. The pinch flashes sharp.

“Ah,” I hiss.

He lifts his hand away immediately. “Okay. What did you feel?”

“Sharp. Same spot.”

“Okay. So we found your threshold.” No judgment. Just data. “Let’s back the intensity down. This is not about proving anything.”

My jaw clenches. “I’m aware.”

He doesn’t respond to the tone. He just resets, professional. “Go at twenty percent. Think ‘gentle.’ Ready?”

I hate that I obey. I push gently. The burn stays, the pinch doesn’t. My body is learning whether I like it or not.

“Perfect,” he says. “Now we’re going to work glute med. Turn onto your right side, facing me. Bend your knees slightly.”
