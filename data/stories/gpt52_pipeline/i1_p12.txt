My phone is at seven percent, which is a number that means nothing and everything.

Seven percent is: if I open the camera it might die. Seven percent is: if I plug it in, it will decide it doesn’t like my cord today and take that personally. Seven percent is: I can still get a text, but I can’t count on it. Seven percent is a relationship.

I press the side button to wake the screen like I’m checking for a pulse. The red sliver at the top looks embarrassed for me.

I tip my tote bag onto the bed and dig for the charger. Receipts, a gum wrapper, the pen I stole from a bank, a hair tie that isn’t mine. No charger. Of course. The one item that keeps me connected and I have everything except the thing. I go to the wall outlet by the nightstand and stare at the cord that reaches it in theory, in the way some people say they’re “free Thursday” and you know what that means. The cord is frayed where it meets the plug. I jiggle it. The lightning bolt appears, then disappears. I hold it at an exact angle with two fingers as if I can convince it to care.

The charger works when it wants to. It’s been distant.

I take my hand away. The bolt vanishes. I think: don’t start. Then, immediately: but also start, because if you don’t start you’ll miss something, and if you miss something you’ll deserve whatever happens.

In the bathroom, the mirror gives me my face without the courtesy of an explanation. I turn the shower on too hot, then too cold, then stand in the middle and let the water hammer my shoulders like a verdict.

They’re late.

Not to the café—yet. Late as in the text from twenty minutes ago: Running a little late.

No emoji. No sorry. No “can’t wait.” Just that flat sentence, like a closed door with a note taped to it.

I tell myself: it’s neutral. People are allowed to be late. People have jobs and traffic and lives. People don’t have to decorate every logistical update with a heart.

Then I tell myself: if they wanted me, I wouldn’t have to do this math.

I rinse conditioner out of my hair and think about my rule, the one I never wrote down but obey like a religion: silence is judgment. If they don’t say anything, they’re thinking something. If they’re thinking something, it’s bad. If it’s bad, it’s about me.

I wrap my hair in a towel and open my closet. Black dress. Jeans. The shirt that’s soft enough to pretend I’m effortless. The other shirt that looks like I tried. Trying is the worst crime. Not trying is also a crime. I lift one blouse off the hanger and put it against my chest.

If I wear this, I’m auditioning.

If I don’t wear this, I’m letting myself go.

If I choose wrong, it will show in the way they look at me when they arrive, the microsecond before they decide.

I set the blouse back. I pull it off again. I put it back. I stand there until the air in the closet feels stale, like a room someone has been crying in.

My phone buzzes from the bed.

I lunge like the floor might fall away if I don’t catch it.

It’s the same text thread, the same name, the same small circle photo of them squinting into sunlight. The new message is not new; it’s the old one, because my phone is dying and the vibration is a ghost.

Running a little late.

I scroll up anyway. I don’t mean to. I’m just checking something. I’m just—because the brain is a machine for locating danger and calling it love.

Yesterday: “Can’t tonight, sorry. Raincheck?”

The day before: “Busy day. Talk later.”

A week ago: paragraphs. A meme. “Miss you.”

I reread “Busy day. Talk later.” like it’s a riddle with one correct answer and if I solve it I can survive.

Busy day means: you’re not the priority.

Talk later means: not now, not you.

I hear myself and try to interrupt it. They’re busy. They’re allowed. I have been busy. I have sent “talk later” a thousand times and meant it. I have meant it.

But I also know the way my own messages change when I am slipping away. The shortening. The removal of softness. The lack of punctuation as a form of anesthesia.

I open the text box and type: Are we okay?

It looks naked on the screen. Too small for what it’s asking. The question contains a whole childhood, a whole tender animal pressing its nose to a closed fist.

I hold my thumb over send.

If I send it, I am needy.

If I don’t send it, I am pretending I am not needy, which is also needy.

Battery: 6%.

I backspace. I type: Hey! Just checking in—still on for 7?

That’s normal. That’s casual. That’s a person with a life. I delete it because the exclamation point feels like begging.

I type: Everything good?

Delete.

I type nothing. I stare at the blinking cursor like it’s waiting for me to incriminate myself.

Battery: 5%.

I drop the phone onto the duvet as if it’s hot. My hands are damp from shower water; my palms leave faint prints on the screen. I rub them on my jeans like I can wipe the panic off.

Mascara. I sit on the edge of the bed with the little wand poised, breathing through my nose. The first swipe trembles. I blink too hard and smear the black. Of course. Of course I ruin things at the last moment.

I lean close to the mirror and try to fix it, but fixing means making it worse.

And then the rehearsal starts, because it always starts when there’s a small mistake and no one to tell me it doesn’t matter.

Okay. Scene.

You’re at the café. They arrive. They sit down. They look at you like—

Like this:

Them: “Hey.”

Me: “Hey.”

Them (pause, eyes flicker): “You look… nice.”

Me: “Thanks.”

Them: “So… I’ve been thinking.”

My stomach drops as if an elevator cable snapped.

Me: “About what?”

Them: “About us. About—” a little laugh, like they’re trying to be kind. “You’re a lot lately.”

Me: “I’m a lot?”

Them: “You’re just… intense. It’s like every time I don’t text back right away you assume I hate you.”

Me: “I don’t assume you hate me. I just—”

Them: “You do.”

Me: “I just—care.”

Them: “It’s exhausting.”

Me: “Okay. Okay, I can fix it. I can be chill. I can—tell me what you need. Tell me what you want.”

Them: “I want space.”

Me: “Space.”

Them: “Yeah.”

Me: “So you want me to disappear.”

Them: “Don’t make it dramatic.”

Me: “I’m not making it dramatic, I’m just—this is what it feels like—”

Them: “See? This.”

Me: “Fine. Fine. Go. You can go.”

Them: “Are you serious?”

Me: “Yes. No. I don’t know. I’m sorry. I’m sorry. I’m sorry.”

Stage direction: My throat closes. My heart bangs like it’s trying to get out. My hands go numb.

Second-person intrusion, like a voice from the balcony: You’re going to ruin it. You’re already ruining it. Look at you, mascara on your face, begging.

I wipe under my eye too hard and the skin stings. I look like someone who cries in public. I look like someone who gets left.

I stand up, put the mascara down carefully like it might detonate, and go back to the bedroom to plug the phone in again.

The cord gives me the lightning bolt for two seconds and then takes it away. I hold the connector in place with my thumb until my nail turns white.

Battery: 5%. Charging: yes—no—yes—no.

“Please,” I whisper, not sure if I mean the phone or the person.

The phone buzzes.

This time it’s real.

A new message.

My whole body floods with relief before I even read it, like my nervous system has been waiting with a bucket at the door.

I swipe it open.

“Sorry, got held up at work. I’m excited to see you. Want me to grab you tea on my way?”

There it is. Excited. Tea. Care. A small act offered. A normal person message. A kind message.

My chest loosens so abruptly it hurts, like a cramped muscle finally releasing. I can almost feel my face soften.

And then, immediately, the other part of me sits up, clears its throat, and says: forensic time.

No exclamation point after excited. Period at the end of the second sentence? There isn’t one. The question mark is there, but question marks can be obligations. Want me to grab you tea—like they’re already halfway out the door. Like they’re buying goodwill. Like they’re covering.

And “sorry” is one word. Not “I’m so sorry.” Not “I hate that I’m late.” Just sorry, like a coin tossed at my feet.

Battery: 4%.

I type: Tea would be amazing, thank you :)

I put the smiley face in like a peace offering, like a small soft animal showing its belly. Then I hate myself for it and consider deleting it.

If I remove it, I’m cold.

If I keep it, I’m asking for something.

I send it. The bubble whooshes away. My heart follows it like a dog chasing a car.

I put my phone face-down, like that changes anything.

All right. Plan.

I won’t be needy tonight.

I won’t mention the message gaps. I won’t mention the way they used to send me three texts in a row and now send one. I won’t mention that when I wake up I check if they’ve liked my story like it’s a heartbeat. I won’t mention the fear that lives behind my ribs like a tenant who refuses to move out.

I’ll be funny. Detached. I’ll be the kind of person who has options.

I pick jeans instead of the dress. Jeans say: I didn’t orchestrate this. I throw on the soft shirt because softness is my one honest trait, and then I try to hide it with a jacket.

In the mirror I practice my face: uninterested, amused, fine.

Fine says: I could leave first.

Fine says: if you don’t want me, I will act like I never wanted you.

Inside, something curls up and watches me with wet eyes.

“You’re not going to embarrass us,” I tell it. Like it’s a dog. Like it’s a child. Like it’s me.

Keys. Wallet. Lip balm. I pat my bag, making sure I have the basics for survival: ID, money, phone that is dying.

I turn Low Power Mode on.

The screen dims like a room at the end of a party.

Battery: 3%.

Outside, the air is sharp. It smells like wet pavement and someone’s cigarette. The world looks too bright for how small my life feels.

I start walking toward the café, my steps too fast. My phone is in my hand because I’m afraid to put it away. If I put it away, I might miss something. If I miss something, it will mean—

It will mean they tried and I failed to receive it.

I open the map even though I know the route. The blue dot moves like it’s trying. The phone hesitates, loading, and my throat tightens.

Battery: 2%.

I should turn the phone off to save it. I should. That’s what normal people would do: preserve the tool.

But turning it off feels like closing my eyes in the dark. Like choosing not to know. Like letting them disappear on purpose.

I keep it on. I keep it bright enough to see. I keep it alive by refusing to let it rest, like a person in a relationship who texts too much because they’re afraid silence will kill it.

At the corner, my phone buzzes again. A jolt through my palm.

It’s them: “Here in 5. I’m sorry again.”

My breath catches. Here in 5 means they are coming. They are coming. The simplest fact becomes a miracle.

Battery: 1%.

I type: No worries! See you soon

I hesitate before sending because “No worries” is a lie. Because there are worries. There are entire ecosystems of worries.

I send it anyway, because the lie is part of the armor.

The café is warm and loud. The smell of coffee hits me like memory. I take a table near the window and sit with my hands wrapped around nothing. The chair feels too big. The room feels like a stage.

I watch the door like it’s going to announce my sentence.

When they walk in, my body recognizes them before my mind does. My skin prickles. My chest goes tight and loose at the same time.

They see me and their face opens. A real smile. Their eyes crinkle. Their shoulders drop as if they’ve been carrying something and setting it down.

“Hey,” they say, and it sounds like a hand reaching out.

“Hey,” I say, and I try to sound like a person who hasn’t been practicing for trial all day.

They come closer and touch my arm, just above the elbow, a brief warmth that makes my whole nervous system flare. “I’m sorry,” they say. “Work was a mess. Are you okay? You look—” They tilt their head, concerned, gentle. “You okay?”

A normal question. A caring question. Evidence.

“Yeah,” I say too quickly. “Totally.”

They slide into the chair across from me. Their knee bumps mine under the table and they don’t pull away. “I got you tea,” they say, and lift a paper cup like it’s a gift. “It’s hot. I hope that’s okay.”

The softness of it almost breaks me. I can’t decide whether to laugh or cry. I take the cup and my fingers brush theirs.

“Thank you,” I manage. “That’s—really sweet.”

They watch my face as if they’re trying to read it, not to accuse but to understand. “I’ve been… kind of out of it,” they admit, lowering their voice. “Not with you. Just—everything. My mom’s been calling nonstop. And my boss—” They roll their eyes. “I’ve been fried. But I’ve missed you.”

Missed you. Two words that should be enough.

Inside my head, the other track runs anyway, like a subtitle I can’t turn off.

Missed you could mean: I missed the idea of you. Missed you could mean: I missed having someone to text when I’m bored. Missed you could mean: I missed you the way you miss a habit, not a person.

They reach across the table and tap my hand with two fingers. “I’m here,” they say. “Okay?”

Okay. The simplest offering. Presence.

I nod. I take a sip of tea and it burns my tongue, which feels like proof I’m alive.

We talk. We order coffee. We make fun of the chalkboard menu spelling. They tell me a story about a coworker who tried to microwave a metal spoon and set off the alarm, and I laugh—a real laugh, surprised to hear it.

For a stretch of minutes, I am just there. A person in a café with someone who likes them. The world narrows into warmth and caffeine and the shape of their hands as they talk.

Then their phone lights up on the table.

They glance down.

It’s quick. It’s nothing. It’s a reflex. A normal thing.

My stomach drops anyway. My brain zooms in like a camera: the angle of their eyes, the micropause in their smile.

They’re bored.

They’re relieved to have an excuse.

They’re texting someone else.

They’re checking the time to see when they can leave.

“I’m doing it again,” I think, like a small clear bell in the middle of noise.

Okay. Grounding. What is actually happening?

They glanced at a screen. People glance at screens. They didn’t flinch. They didn’t withdraw. Their foot is still touching mine.

Breathe in. Four counts. Hold. Out.

I repeat in my mind: They said they missed you. They brought tea. They touched your hand.

For a moment, the spiral slows. I can feel the café again. The sound of the espresso machine. The warmth of the cup. Their face.

Then they say, lightly, “Sorry—I should probably answer that later,” and their tone is casual, an attempt to reassure, but my brain catches on the words should probably like a hook.

Should probably = they want to, they shouldn’t, they will anyway.

Later = not now, not you.

My throat tightens. The rehearsal script rustles to life behind my eyes, pages flipping.

Don’t ask for reassurance, the tactical part of me says. Don’t. If you ask, you’ll make it real. If you ask, they’ll see the need and decide it’s too much.

Ask right now, the starving part says. Ask or you’ll die. Ask or you’ll spend the next week rereading every message until you’re hollow.

I smile too wide. “Yeah,” I say, bright. “Of course.”

My phone is a black brick in my bag. I haven’t checked it since I sat down. I can feel it dying like an animal in the dark.

They’re talking about their mom again, about how tired they are, and I want to be kind. I want to be someone they can lean on. I want to be the calm harbor.

Instead, resentment starts forming like ice along the edges.

You’ve been tired before and still texted people you cared about.

You’ve been busy before and still found time for others.

Maybe I’m not in the category of people you make time for.

The armor part of me stands up straight. Fine, it says. Don’t. Don’t make time. I won’t ask. I won’t reach. I will let you have your distance and I will pretend it doesn’t hurt. I will leave first if I have to.

And then, like a leak, one of the rehearsed lines rises to my mouth.

“So,” I say, aiming for casual, aiming for teasing, aiming for anything that isn’t a plea. “You’ve been kind of… distant.”

Their face shifts, not away from me but toward me, attentive. “Have I?” they ask softly. No defensiveness. Just a question.
