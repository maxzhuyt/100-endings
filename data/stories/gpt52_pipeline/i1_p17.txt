The Clerk of Somnolence—thin as a bookmark, robed in mothsilk—struck the lectern with a gavel that sounded like a snapped spine.

“All rise for His August Office, Dream of the Endless,” the Clerk intoned, though most beings in the gallery didn’t so much rise as *unfurl*.

The vaulted ceiling of the Court of the Dreaming held its own weather: drifting scraps of lullabies, the soft grit of sand that never quite fell, chandeliers made of frozen yawn. Somewhere in that height, a sound like distant mirth—Mora’s faint cackle—bounced at exactly the wrong moments, as if the architecture itself were in on it.

The Clerk consulted a docket that looked suspiciously like a dried tongue.

“Matter before the Court: *First Love v. Mora*, re: *Max’s Recurring Wildflower Field Dream*—File Thirteen Thousand Eight Hundred Seventy-Two, subcategory Adolescence, note: ‘pre-first kiss latency,’” the Clerk read. “Allegations include: unlawful incursion, breath-theft, unauthorized paralysis feature, and—” the Clerk squinted, as if even reading it caused a blush— “improper use of the phrase *a girl’s gotta eat* in a protected romantic sequence.”

A ripple of stifled laughter moved through the benches: a couple of nightmares with bureaucrat pins, a half-dozen wistful fantasies with ink-stained hands, and one very old fairy tale pretending it wasn’t listening.

At the bench, Dream sat as if he had always been there and always would be. His robe drank light; his eyes held the absence of morning. Nothing about him suggested irritation, and yet irritation was the real air in the room, felt like pressure behind the teeth.

He glanced down—slowly—at the Clerk.

“It is *dreamscape*,” Dream said, voice quiet enough to make it an absolute. “Not *sequence*.”

The Clerk bowed. “Yes, my lord. Protected romantic dreamscape.”

Dream’s fingers—pale, long, unhurried—tapped once on the arm of his chair. It was the only thunder.

“This matter is already in progress,” Dream said, as though announcing a distasteful fact about the universe. “Proceed. Briefly.”

At the petitioner’s table stood First Love.

He looked as if someone had attempted to distill sunset into a person and then been embarrassed by the result. Rosiness clung to him. His hair fell as if styled by hopeful trembling hands. His collar was unfastened, not in dishevelment but in the way a boy looks in a mirror before he knows the rules.

He cleared his throat. His eyes flicked, once, to the gallery—then away.

“My lord,” First Love began, voice earnest enough to be almost indecent in this room, “this dream—this dreamscape—was mine in the way a song is someone’s before it becomes everybody’s. I built it to recur because recurrence is how the human mind learns to want without understanding it.”

Across the aisle, at the respondent’s table, Mora lounged.

She wore a shape that suggested a woman only because it was convenient for the imagination. The rest of her was suggestion: too-long limbs folded with proprietary ease, hair that looked like smoke caught between teeth, eyes like wet stones left in a dark bowl. Her smile was small and delighted and wholly, bureaucratically unapologetic.

She gave a soft cackle that rang up into the ceiling and came back twice, as if amended.

Dream did not look at her. He looked at First Love with the bored patience of someone forced to watch mortals discover friction.

“You will submit an exhibit,” Dream said.

First Love swallowed. “Yes, my lord.”

He raised a hand, palm outward. The air over the center of the court trembled. A circle of sand—Dream’s sand, obedient—lifted and spun, becoming a lens.

“Exhibit A,” the Clerk announced.

The lens filled with scent first: green, sun-warmed, the honest sweetness of crushed stems. Then the court saw it.

A field of wildflowers, impossibly dense, each petal too bright to be real and therefore perfect for a mind that had not yet been disappointed. The sky above it was a high, clean blue that hadn’t learned to threaten. Warm air moved like a hand over skin. Bees hung, lazy as punctuation.

And there—Max.

Not quite a man, not quite a boy. All awkward angles and sudden tenderness, the way adolescence is a creature that keeps bumping into itself. He stood in the wildflowers as if he’d been placed there and was afraid to move in case it broke.

Opposite him stood a girl with the kind of face that a teenager can carry like an injury for years. Her smile, indeed, had been vetted: it landed at a precise angle between invitation and mystery, enough to make him brave and not enough to make him safe.

They leaned toward each other, slowly, as if both were pretending the other wasn’t watching. The air grew louder with their breath. The almost-kiss hovered, an exquisite stutter.

In the court, even the nightmares stilled. A clerk with wings of parchment blinked too fast.

First Love spoke over the image, voice softer now, as if talking might bruise it. “It recurs because it never resolves. Each time it returns, he believes—truly believes—that this time, the kiss will happen. That belief is… formative.”

Dream’s gaze stayed on the lens. His expression did not change. And yet, in the stillness, the tiniest tightening at the corner of his mouth suggested he recognized—if not valued—the craft.

Then Mora’s cackle slipped in like a knife slid into butter.

“Mm,” she murmured, audible despite not being amplified. “So sweet. So *sustainable*.”

First Love’s fingers curled. “My lord, this is what it was until the respondent—”

Dream’s tap cut him off. “I understand. Submit your second exhibit.”

Mora’s smile widened, pleased to be invited to misbehave with a stamp of approval.

She rose with a slow grace that made the air feel touched. “With gratitude to the Court,” she said, tone the same one might use while thanking someone for passing the salt.

She extended a hand. Her nails were dark and glossy, like beetle backs.

“Exhibit B,” the Clerk said, and flinched as Mora’s cackle ricocheted again, this time in a higher register that made a sleeping feeling prickle along the floor.

The lens shimmered.

The wildflower field returned—same sun, same bees—but the colors seemed fractionally wrong now, as if someone had breathed on a painting while it was still wet. Max stood again. The girl smiled again. The almost-kiss approached.

Then, as if on an edit point, as if someone had cut the film and spliced in something else:

The girl moved first. She stepped closer. She reached up, playful, bold. She pushed Max backward into the flowers, laughing. She swung a leg over him and straddled him with teasing confidence, and Max’s face went blank with astonished terror and yearning.

And then her smile *stretched*.

The skin of her face rippled as if something beneath it had become impatient. Her eyes darkened, widening too far. Her mouth opened, not in laughter, but in hunger. The girl’s shape sagged off itself like a mask sliding away.

Mora was suddenly there in the dreamscape, sitting astride Max’s chest, heavy in the way a nightmare is heavy—density without mass, inevitability without weight. Her hair fell around his face like a net. Her hands pinned his shoulders with lazy strength.

Max tried to move.

He didn’t.

The court watched his eyes dart, panic starting as a small animal in his throat. His breath hitched. His fingers trembled against the flowers, which now looked too much like staring eyes.

Mora lowered herself until her mouth hovered just above his.

She did not bite.

She *fed*.

She drew his breath out in slow, savoring pulls, as if tasting the heat of it. Each inhale he attempted came late, thin, defeated. Each exhale became hers.

In the court, someone—a bailiff made of stitched-together lullabies—made an involuntary sound of disgust.

Mora’s cackle, soft and delighted, threaded through Max’s inability to scream.

First Love’s face had gone pale-pink, then white, then something like frost.

Dream watched without blinking. When the exhibit ended, the wildflower field collapsing into sand and scent and the lingering memory of weight on a chest, Dream said only, “The paralysis feature was not filed.”

Mora inclined her head. “An improvement,” she said. “It adds… realism.”

“It adds liability,” the Clerk muttered, unable to stop himself.

Dream’s gaze flicked to the Clerk—brief, cool—until the Clerk remembered he was paper and shut up.

Dream turned back to Mora. “Respondent. Your defense.”

Mora’s hands folded. She looked very much like someone about to explain something to a child who’d asked a foolish question.

“I did not steal,” she said. “I foraged.”

First Love made a small, strangled noise, as if the word *foraged* had been pressed into his skin.

Mora continued, unhurried. “Recurring dreamscapes are ecosystems. He returns. The scene repeats. The emotions ripen. It is… renewable.” She glanced at Dream, eyes gleaming. “Your realm is full of such cycles, my lord. You know this.”

Dream said nothing.

Mora smiled as if permission had been granted anyway. “Breath is plentiful in a teenage boy,” she said. “The body panics, yes, but the mind—” her gaze slid briefly to where the exhibit had been— “the mind is enthralled. Fear is a flavor. Desire is a flavor. Put them together and—”

She spread her hands, as if displaying a perfectly reasonable meal.

“A girl’s gotta eat too, you know?” she added, brightly, as if that were the end of law.

For a beat, the court held its breath. A nightmare in the back adjusted its tie.

Dream’s fingers tapped once.

“A thesis is not a statute,” Dream said. “Petitioner, cross-examine.”

First Love stepped forward. He looked as if the act of standing near Mora made his own warmth wilt.

“Mora,” he said, voice trembling with something like outrage and something like shame, “did you have consent?”

Mora blinked, slowly, as if the word were quaint. “From whom?”

“From Max,” First Love said, and the name sounded suddenly too intimate in the mouth of an entity.

Mora’s lips parted in a small laugh. “He comes back,” she said. “Night after night. He returns to the field. He lies down in it, practically. What is that if not consent?”

“It is recurrence,” First Love snapped. “It is a mind stuck on a hinge. He returns because he cannot resolve it. You exploit that.”

Mora’s eyes narrowed, amused. “Do I?” She leaned forward. “Or do I give him resolution? He wakes. His heart races. His body remembers. He learns that longing has teeth.”

First Love’s cheeks flushed. “He wakes gasping.”

“He wakes *alive*,” Mora said. “Breathing again. Isn’t that the purpose of breath? To be taken and returned?”

First Love’s hand clenched around nothing. “You sit on his chest.”

“I sit where I am invited,” Mora replied, and her cackle tickled the edges of the court like mold.

First Love’s voice rose. “Invited by what? By his terror? His—his—” He faltered, the embarrassment hitting him like a slap. He glanced at Dream, pleading for the dignity of not having to name the bodily truth of adolescence in a court full of myths.

Dream’s expression remained flat. “Say it,” Dream said.

First Love swallowed hard. “His arousal,” he forced out. “His confusion. His—his lack of understanding. You take advantage.”

Mora’s smile sharpened. “And you,” she said, turning her head slightly, eyes on First Love now like needles, “do you not take advantage of that same confusion? Did you have his consent when you engineered ‘formative romantic experiences’?”

The phrase sounded worse spoken aloud, like a marketing slogan nailed to a heart.

First Love stiffened. “I—”

Mora lifted a finger. “You chose the girl’s smile. You chose the warmth of air. You chose the distance between their mouths. You chose the *never*.” Her voice grew almost tender. “You curated longing like a garden. You planted it in a boy and called it art.”

First Love’s mouth opened, then closed. The court murmured—interested now, the way a crowd becomes interested when a fight turns from spectacle to personal.

Dream’s eyes shifted, faintly, as if a pebble had finally hit his shoe.

Mora’s gaze returned to Dream. “So tell me, my lord,” she said, “who owns what in a sleeping mind? The dreamer? The artisan? The predator? The Court?”

The Clerk of Somnolence cleared his throat with the desperation of someone trying to keep a meeting on agenda. “Objection—metaphysical property law exceeds the scope of—”

Dream’s hand lifted, and the objection died in the air like a moth slapped mid-flight.

Dream spoke, still quiet, but the silence around his words was total. “The scope is whatever I am forced to endure,” he said. “Continue.”

First Love looked at Mora, then at Dream. “He is a person,” First Love said, voice quieter now, the sincerity breaking through the procedural posture. “Not a territory.”

Mora leaned back, as if settling into comfort. “People are territories,” she said, matter-of-fact. “That is the first truth of dreaming. They are the only territories that grow while you take from them.”

Dream’s tap sounded again, a measured impatience. “Enough philosophy from litigants who confuse appetite with argument,” he said. “Call your witness.”

The Clerk blinked. “Witness?”

First Love hesitated. “My lord, I—”

Dream’s gaze pinned him. “You will not speak for the dreamer as if he were a parcel of land with no voice,” Dream said, and for the first time something like contempt showed—brief, sharp, and directed at both tables. “Summon him.”

A rustle went through the court. The sand on the floor shifted, gathering into a shape.

It formed a boy in a hoodie, seated suddenly in the witness stand as if he’d been dropped there between one blink and the next. His hair stuck up at the back. His eyes were wide in that particular way that said he had no idea he was asleep and yet everything here felt more real than his bedroom.

Max looked around. “What—” he began, and then stopped when he saw Dream. Something in him recognized authority without knowing why. His posture collapsed into a nervous hunch.

The Clerk leaned forward, voice gentle with the practiced kindness of someone who has watched many mortals be confused. “State your name for the record.”

Max swallowed. “Max,” he said. “I guess.”

“Max-as-dreamt,” the Clerk amended, scribbling.

Mora made an appreciative noise, as if admiring a dish being brought to table.

First Love approached the stand cautiously, like someone approaching a wild animal that might bolt. “Max,” he said, and the name sounded different now—less like a file label, more like a plea. “Can you describe the wildflower dream? The way it used to be?”

Max’s face reddened instantly. His eyes darted to Mora, then away. “Uh,” he said. “It’s—” He swallowed again. “It’s like… I’m in this field. And it’s warm. And she’s there. And I know her. I don’t know her. But I—” He squeezed his eyes shut in misery. “I want to kiss her. And it never—like, it never happens. But it’s nice. It’s… nice.”

First Love’s shoulders eased, as if hearing the word *nice* was oxygen.

“And now?” First Love asked, voice tight again.

Max’s eyes opened. He stared at the floor. “Now it’s—” His throat bobbed. “Now it’s scary.”

Mora’s cackle, very quiet, slipped under his words like a finger under a collar.

“She—” Max began, then stopped, mortified. He looked up at Dream, as if asking if he was allowed to be embarrassed in a cosmic courtroom.

Dream’s expression did not soften. “Speak,” he said.

Max swallowed. “She climbs on me,” he said, voice barely audible. “And I can’t move. And I’m—” He squeezed his eyes shut again, face burning. “I’m kind of… into it? But then it’s not her. It’s—” He opened his eyes and looked, unwillingly, at Mora.

Mora smiled at him with intimate recognition, as if they shared a secret.

“It’s you,” Max whispered.

Mora inclined her head, gracious.

Max’s hands clenched on the arms of the stand. “And I can’t breathe,” he said, and now the fear made him speak faster. “Like, I *can*, but I can’t. And I try to yell and nothing comes out. And I wake up and my heart is—” He pressed a hand to his chest. “It’s going crazy. And I’m sweaty. And sometimes I… sometimes I don’t want to go back to sleep. But then the next night I do anyway because I keep thinking maybe it won’t happen.”

First Love looked like he might dissolve into petals.

Mora looked delighted, like someone hearing good reviews.

Dream’s irritation sharpened, a subtle change in pressure that made the chandeliers of yawns tremble. His fingers stopped tapping. When he spoke, the room leaned toward him the way tide leans to moon.

“Do you want her there?” Dream asked Max.

Max froze. The question was too clean, too sharp, the kind that makes a muddle of feelings look childish.

“I don’t know,” Max said, helplessly. “No. Yes. Not like that. I just—” He shook his head, angry at himself. “I don’t know what I want.”

Dream watched him for a long moment. In that gaze was the sense of vast things forced to consider small ones because small ones were, annoyingly, the point.

Dream’s eyes shifted to First Love. “You claim authorship,” Dream said.

First Love nodded quickly. “I crafted it, my lord. I shaped it. I tended it.”

Dream’s gaze shifted to Mora. “You claim right of access,” Dream said.
