They came in twos and threes, never in a line, never with lamps. The moon was enough to find the break in the outer wall where the old temple’s gate had been, enough to pick their way through waist-high nettles and fallen roof tiles without flashing a light that might carry across the fields.

Inside, the nave was a long wound of stone. Pillars stood like snapped teeth, their tops lost in darkness where the roof had once arched. A bell lay on its side near the far end, half-buried in rubble, its bronze skin green with age. Side alcoves—shrines emptied long ago—held shadowed shapes that might have been statues if you didn’t look too hard.

They spoke as if they were meeting for tea.

“Wind’s turned,” someone murmured, rubbing his hands. “You feel it in your joints.”

“Road was mud for a league,” another answered, as if that were the purpose of the night.

Coins clicked softly between palms. Folded slips of paper passed from sleeve to sleeve. A man with a knife-scar down his cheek crouched and drew a circle with chalk on the broken tile floor, his hand practiced, the line confident even where the stone was cracked. He widened it with a second pass, then tapped the edge with his knuckles as if checking the tautness of a drum.

Near the circle’s right side, three paces away, someone planted a single torch into a seam between stones at the base of a collapsed pillar. The flame caught, flared, and settled. Its light made a hard-edged island on the floor; beyond, faces were only halves—cheeks and eyes lit, mouths swallowed by darkness. The torch’s shadow of the pillar fell across the chalk ring in a thick bar, and men shifted their feet as if that shadow were another boundary.

In the torchlight you could see what the night pretended not to be: a bucket of water set just outside the circle; rags folded and stacked on a flat stone; strips of cloth; a chipped roof tile kicked aside that would bite an ankle if someone stepped wrong. Someone had swept the center clean, but grit still glittered where the broken tiles met.

“Don’t crowd the line,” an older woman hissed, though she moved closer herself, taking a place at the edge of light. “If he steps out, it’s on you.”

A laugh—low, contained. “He won’t step out.”

The first fighter arrived from the left entrance gap, with three men in darker cloth at his back and a silence in front of him that was not respect so much as expectation. He was not armored, not in any uniform, but his posture made clothes seem incidental: spine straight, chin level, hands loose at his sides as if already holding a hilt. His hair was tied cleanly; his sash was white, but the cloth was old and mended.

They tightened that sash for him anyway, hands quick and careful, like attendants at a shrine.

“Straight through,” one whispered at his ear. “No games.”

“End it,” another said, and the word carried a class of its own: assumption, entitlement, the certainty that decisive things belonged to them.

He didn’t answer. His eyes held the torch’s flame without blinking, and in their reflection there was something too still to be calm.

The second fighter came from the right, where the nave’s side alcoves opened like mouths. His people were louder, not out of courage but out of hunger. They clapped him on the back, rolled his shoulders, slapped his forearms as if testing meat.

“Loose,” a woman told him, tugging his sleeve up. She wrapped his wrists in cloth, tight enough to support, not tight enough to bind. “Let him spend himself.”

“Make him miss,” said a young man with a bruised jaw, grinning too wide. “Humble the noble.”

The Piguaquan fighter breathed out through his nose, long and slow, and his shoulders sank a fraction as if his bones had been oiled. His hair was tied too, but not so neat; a few strands had already escaped and caught the torchlight like wire.

Between the two groups, names were spoken like sparks.

“You shouldn’t be here,” someone muttered from the Jigen side, not loud enough to challenge, loud enough to be heard. “Masters forbade it. After last spring.”

“After your boy broke Elder Wen’s tooth in the market?” came the answer, sharp and amused. “After you bowed and called it an accident?”

A ripple ran through the crowd. Not laughter—something meaner.

The Jigen-ryū fighter’s jaw tightened. In the torchlight the tendon along his neck stood out.

“It was a cheap win,” a voice from the Pigua side continued, eager now. “Your man struck when the elder turned away. Then you all pretended you hadn’t seen. That’s the debt.”

The Pigua fighter’s eyes flicked toward that speaker—warning, restraint. But the words had already landed where they were meant to.

Someone on the Jigen side stepped forward half a pace, then stopped as if a leash tightened.

No one mentioned what would happen if the masters learned of this. They didn’t need to. The secrecy itself was the pressure in the air, the knowledge that every witness was also guilty.

A neutral figure—older, with a cloak that had been expensive once—moved into the torchlight. He carried no weapon, only a short stick of chalk and a small knife for cutting seals on wager slips. His face was calm in the way of men who have watched too many fights to be moved by the first blood.

“Circle is circle,” he said, and his voice was brisk, almost bored. “No blades. Sleeves rolled, belts checked.”

Hands patted clothing, quick and performative. A few men held up palms to show emptiness. The facilitator’s knife flashed once, slicing a knot on a pouch to prove nothing hidden, then was tucked away.

“No interference,” he continued. “No pulling men back. If your fighter goes down, you do not touch him until I say.”

Coins clinked. Someone swallowed loud enough to be heard.

“Victory,” the facilitator said, and here his eyes went from one fighter to the other, “by submission, or unconsciousness. Blood is not victory unless it buys those.”

The crowd repeated it under their breath, like prayer. Submission. Unconsciousness. No leaving.

The torch hissed, a drop of resin popping; for an instant the flame leaned, and the shadow of the pillar slid across the chalk.

“Opposite ends,” the facilitator said. “When I step back, begin.”

The Jigen fighter took his place at the left edge of the circle, the broken tile ridge behind him an arm’s length away. The Pigua fighter stood at the right edge, closer to the torch side, the collapsed pillar fragment beyond his shoulder like a low wall.

They faced each other, and for a breath the ruined temple held its own silence.

The facilitator stepped back out of the circle. The torch’s light made a hard line along the Jigen fighter’s cheekbone; the rest of his face was shadow, unreadable.

The Jigen fighter moved first. Not a probe. Not a test. His back foot drove, his hips snapped, and he exploded forward in a straight line meant to collapse distance before thought could form. His lead hand cut in like a spear, his rear shoulder loading behind it—everything committed, everything aimed at ending.

The Pigua fighter yielded as if the floor had rolled under him. He slid back and to his own left, pivoting off the line of attack, letting the rush pass where his face had been. In the torch’s moving shadows, his shoulder twitched—a feint or a flinch, the crowd couldn’t tell—and then his arm came around in a wide, whipping arc.

The strike didn’t travel straight. It came from his back, from the turn of his waist, a hammering forearm that sought the side of the Jigen fighter’s neck as the rush carried him past.

The Jigen fighter’s guard rose on instinct. The forearm glanced off his raised elbow with a dull smack that echoed in the nave. The sound was close enough to make men blink.

He pivoted on the ball of his foot, trying to square again, and the Pigua fighter’s second arm followed—backfist, wide and snapping toward the jaw.

This time it grazed. Knuckles caught cheek. A thin line of red appeared where skin split, startling in the torchlight, too bright, too alive. The crowd sucked in a single breath, as if one throat.

The Jigen fighter didn’t touch the blood. He drove forward again, cutting the distance he’d lost, feet slapping stone. His fist shot toward ribs.

The Pigua fighter turned with it, folding his torso, letting the line skim, and the two were suddenly close enough that their breath mixed. The Pigua fighter’s elbow came up, short now, meant to jam and bruise.

The Jigen fighter shoved, a body-check that used his forward force like a battering ram. The Pigua fighter stumbled half a step but caught himself at the edge of the chalk line, toes curling on grit.

“Ring!” someone shouted, panicked, as if the chalk itself were law.

The crowd surged in, a half-step each, tightening the circle of bodies around the circle of chalk. The air thickened with sweat and excitement. Coins flashed in torchlight as more money was thrown down on the flat stone that served as table. Someone called, “Finish him!” from the left. Someone else, from the right, sang out, “Make him chase!”

In that noise, the fighters’ timing changed. A shout masked a breath. A stomp sounded like a step.

The Jigen fighter pressed, irritated now by emptiness—by the way his decisive entries found only air and glancing contact. His eyes narrowed, and he did what his training had always rewarded: he doubled down.

He burst in with a sequence, not a single strike but a chain—lead hand to blind, rear hand to crush, step to close, shoulder behind it. The crowd saw commitment, saw righteousness in forward motion.

The Pigua fighter watched it all the way in. His feet did not retreat in a straight line. He angled. He stepped to the outside right of the Jigen fighter’s advance, his hip turning like a hinge, and as the Jigen fighter’s weight poured forward, the Pigua fighter’s arm whipped across the opening the rush created.

It landed on ribs with a sound like wet wood struck. The Jigen fighter’s breath punched out of him, involuntary. His forward foot hit grit where broken tile had been ground to sand. His heel slid a fraction.

The Pigua fighter took that fraction and made it a fall. His leg reaped—not a formal throw, just a brutal bump behind the ankle as the Jigen fighter’s center of gravity tipped. The Jigen fighter tried to recover, but his back foot found the ridge of a cracked tile and snagged.

He went down hard on his side. Stone met bone. The impact echoed off the pillars.

A roar rose—triumph from one side, outrage from the other—and in that roar was the ugliness of people who had needed one man to be humbled so they could breathe.

The Jigen fighter rolled immediately, not to escape but to stand. His face was set, but the breath he drew came in shallow pieces, ribs protesting. When he rose, his left arm hung a fraction lower than before, protective without meaning to be.

The Pigua fighter’s shoulders remained loose, his hands swinging with that long, whipping readiness. But he was smiling now, and the smile was a crack. Pride had gotten in.

He circled, keeping the torch to his side, letting its light make a strobing band across his opponent’s eyes as he moved through shadow and back again.

“Chase,” someone from his faction hissed. “Make him—”

The Jigen fighter did not chase. He adjusted.

He set his feet as if the chalk circle were the only ground that existed. He stopped trying to follow the arc and instead cut it, stepping to where the Pigua fighter would have to be if he kept circling. His shoulders lowered, not looser, but more aligned—doctrine refined into geometry.

The Pigua fighter began another whip, turning his hips for a wide strike meant to punish forward motion. In that rotation, for a heartbeat, his weight shifted to one foot and the other lifted—just enough.

The torch flame leaned as if blown by an unseen breath, and the pillar’s shadow slid. In that moving darkness the Jigen fighter made a micro-step, almost invisible, closing the gap at the exact moment the Pigua fighter’s hip opened.

His fist drove straight, not aimed at the head where a man can roll with it, but into the body—solar plexus, a brutal line that landed as the Pigua fighter’s lungs were mid-breath.

The Pigua fighter’s rotation broke. His arms flared wide, whip turned to spasm. He staggered backward, and his heel hit something that wasn’t flat stone.

A loose block—part of a fallen beam’s support—rolled under his foot. For an instant he windmilled, trying to regain balance, and his back bumped the pillar fragment near the torch. The flame sputtered with the sudden rush of air, flaring bright, then dim, then bright again, making the scene jump like a series of still images.

The Jigen fighter was on him. Not subtle. Correct. He drove with shoulder and forearm, pinning the Pigua fighter into that broken stone, using the obstacle like a wall. Dust puffed from the pillar fragment, dry as ash, and it hung in the torchlight, making both fighters cough as they breathed it in.

The crowd pressed closer still, their bodies a second wall behind the chalk. Someone’s sleeve brushed the circle line, smearing chalk with sweat.

“Out!” a voice cried, alarmed, as the Pigua fighter’s foot skidded near the boundary.

He tried to turn away, to regain the circle that had saved him earlier, but the rubble underfoot stole clean pivots. His ankle rolled on grit. His ribs—hit hard—didn’t let him draw air enough to float.

The Jigen fighter’s hand caught the back of his neck, not in a hold, just to anchor. The other fist rose, straight and compact.

For a heartbeat, the Pigua fighter’s eyes met his. Not hatred. Recognition.

This had never been sport.

The Pigua fighter brought his forearm up, trying to wedge space, to make a hinge again, to find the whip. He was a fraction too slow. His muscles were loose by doctrine, but looseness couldn’t answer collapse.

The Jigen fighter drove a short strike—forearm or fist, it hardly mattered—into the side of the head where the jaw meets the skull. The sound was sickeningly solid. The Pigua fighter’s legs buckled.

He slid down the pillar fragment, leaving a smear of sweat and dust, and his head turned wrong as it met the edge of broken tile. Not a dramatic crack, just a dull contact that stopped motion too abruptly.

For a breath he lay still. Then his body twitched once, like a puppet cut loose.

The roar died as if someone had thrown a blanket over it.

In the torchlight, blood surfaced at the corner of the fallen man’s mouth and ran, slow at first, then faster when he exhaled. It wasn’t symbolic. It was wet and dark and real, and it pooled in a shallow depression between tiles.

The Jigen fighter stood over him, chest heaving. His own split cheek bled in a thin line, but he didn’t feel it. His eyes were fixed on the blood at the other man’s mouth as if trying to understand what it meant in a temple with no gods left to watch.

“Yield,” someone whispered, too late, voice cracking.

The facilitator stepped forward, his calm finally gone from his face. He knelt, fingers at the Pigua fighter’s throat, then jerked his hand back as if the skin itself accused him. He looked up at the Jigen fighter, then at the crowd, and for once had no words that sounded like procedure.

The Jigen fighter’s mouth opened, and no sound came. His hands flexed, empty. He looked like a man waiting for a command that would make this make sense.

No one gave it.

The crowd began to move—not as one, but as many individuals suddenly remembering they had names and homes and masters who asked questions. Coins were snatched off the stone in desperate handfuls. Wager slips disappeared into sleeves as if they could be un-written.

“It was fair,” someone insisted loudly, already backing away. “Rules were spoken. Circle is circle.”

“His own fault,” another said, voice high. “He slipped.”

“Masters can’t know,” someone else hissed, and that fear finally outweighed pride.

A man grabbed the Jigen fighter by the elbow, tugging him toward the entrance gap. “Come. Now. Before—”

The Jigen fighter resisted for half a second, eyes still on the body, then let himself be pulled, feet dragging through chalk dust and blood mist.

On the other side, the Pigua fighter’s people hesitated, staring at their fallen weapon as if unsure whether touching him would make them complicit in a new way. At last two of them crouched, hands trembling, and tried to lift him. His head lolled. Blood threaded down his chin onto their wrists.

The torch flared again, then guttered low as if exhausted by watching. Someone—no one could say who—kicked the bucket of water toward it. The water splashed, darkening stone, and the flame hissed down to a smoking stub.

In the dim that followed, the chalk circle was still visible, but smeared. A rag lay half in the ring, half out, soaked through and heavy. The broken tile that had caught a heel gleamed wet where blood had found its crack.

Feet hurried away through nettles and shattered stone. The temple took back its silence, but not its innocence. In the dark, one faint sound remained: a wet, uneven breath, and then—perhaps—no breath at all.
