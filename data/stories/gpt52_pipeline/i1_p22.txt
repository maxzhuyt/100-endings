The overhead light in the corridor flickers like it’s remembering how to be alive.

Not a steady stutter—irregular. Two quick blinks, then a long, humming burn that makes the stains on the floor look almost warm, then darkness again. Each time it comes back, Jolyne’s shadow jumps to a new angle like it’s trying to get away from her.

A guard walks a half-step behind her, keys clacking with the same bored rhythm as every other night. Except his gaze is wrong. Too smooth. He looks at her the way a camera looks: not cruel, not kind—just recording.

Jolyne keeps her hands at her sides and her face blank, and she counts anyway. Cameras in the corners—three in this stretch, and the third one is new. A strip of paper talismans is pasted along a doorframe, inked with tight warding marks that look like they came from a priest who hates his own prayers. The air has that faint, clean sting of disinfectant trying to cover something older.

This prison has always been a cage. Tonight it feels like a mouth.

The light flickers again and the guard’s footsteps don’t change, but the door ahead unlocks before his key reaches it. A neat click. A polite sound.

Jolyne stops without meaning to. The guard doesn’t bump her; he stops too, perfectly, like he was always going to.

Her skin prickles. “You didn’t—”

“I didn’t,” he says, and his voice is empty of curiosity. His eyes don’t even focus on the lock.

The light blinks twice, fast. Somewhere down the hall, an intercom hisses and dies without broadcasting anything. Jolyne tastes metal at the back of her tongue.

Someone is adjusting the world around her.

“Move,” the guard says, not impatient. Just prompted.

Jolyne moves, but she threads a thought through her fear like a needle: if the doors open themselves, then somebody’s already inside the system. Somebody who doesn’t need keys.

The flicker becomes a metronome. She matches her breathing to it and hates herself for it.

At the next intersection, the corridor widens into a dead zone between blocks. The light overhead is dimmer here, as if the bulb is tired of being watched. The cameras track her, each with a tiny red dot that seems to pulse in time with the flicker.

Then—where there should be only concrete, doors, and the odor of captive bodies—there is a woman standing calmly in the middle of the hall.

No guard at her shoulder. No badge. No restraints. She looks like someone who has never had to ask permission to exist.

Her hair is a soft red that catches the sick light and makes it look like she’s holding a small ember above her shoulders. Her suit is neat, her posture gentle. Her smile is practiced warmth, poured carefully into the shape of kindness.

This place doesn’t allow sweetness. Anything that looks like it is either bait or poison.

The guard beside Jolyne straightens as if a string has pulled him upright.

The woman tilts her head. “Jolyne Cujoh?”

Jolyne keeps her mouth shut for a beat too long. She watches the woman’s eyes: gold-brown, calm, intent in a way that feels like pressure on the throat.

“Who the hell are you?” Jolyne asks.

The woman’s smile doesn’t change. “Makima.”

Just a name, offered like a handshake. Like it’s enough to put order into the air.

Jolyne shifts her weight, testing the floor with the ball of her foot. The light flickers and Makima’s shadow barely moves.

Makima’s gaze slides over Jolyne—not her face, but the subtle tension in her shoulders, the way her fingers curl as if they remember a thread. “I’ve heard you’ve had… difficulties here.”

“Yeah? You here to make it worse?”

“I would like to help,” Makima says, voice mild. “This facility isn’t designed for someone like you. You could be transferred. You could have better food. A private cell. Safer conditions.”

Jolyne lets out a short laugh. It comes out sharp. “In prison, nothing’s free.”

Makima’s expression softens as if she’s amused by a child’s cynicism. “Of course not. But sometimes a person receives what they need.”

Jolyne hates the way the words wrap around her like cotton, trying to muffle her instincts. She hates the way the guard beside her is holding his breath like he’s waiting for Makima’s permission to exhale.

The overhead light blinks. Once. Twice. Long burn.

Jolyne feels it then—the wrongness isn’t only the building. It’s the space around this woman, as if the air has been assigned to her.

Makima takes a step closer, not invading, just reducing the distance in a way that forces Jolyne to either back away or accept it. Jolyne doesn’t move.

Makima’s eyes flick briefly to Jolyne’s wrists. “You’re injured?”

“Fine.”

“I’m curious,” Makima says, and the word is too gentle for what it does. “They say you can unravel yourself. That you can become string.”

Jolyne’s stomach tightens. There it is. Not concern, not charity. A hand reaching for the mechanism inside her.

“What they say,” Jolyne answers, “is none of your business.”

Makima nods as if she’s heard a reasonable boundary and filed it away. “Stone Free,” she murmurs, tasting the name. “How far does it extend from you? If you unravel too much, do you risk losing consciousness?”

Jolyne’s temper jumps, a spark looking for oxygen. She forces it down, forces her voice steady. “You ask a lot of questions for somebody who just walked into a restricted corridor.”

“I have permission,” Makima says.

From who? Jolyne almost asks, but she knows the trap. The answer will be either a lie too big to challenge or a truth too ugly to swallow.

She decides to do what she always does when someone tries to label her: she moves without showing she’s moved.

Stone Free is quiet. A thin filament slips from beneath her cuff, so fine it could be lint. She drags it along the seam of her sleeve, lets it drop to the floor where it can’t be seen in the dim. It snakes toward the wall, searching for vibration, for air currents, for the honest language of physical space.

Makima’s shoes make no sound on the concrete.

That’s either impossible or it’s practiced. Jolyne doesn’t know which is worse.

The filament touches the base of Makima’s pant leg and quivers. There’s a feeling there, like pressure without heat. Like the world leaning.

Makima’s eyes lift—directly to Jolyne’s face, not to the string. Her smile stays soft. “You’re very smart.”

Jolyne’s throat tightens. She tugs the filament back, not fast enough to look guilty, just enough to keep it from being caught.

The light flickers. In the brief dark, the corridor sounds change. Not louder—just… arranged. The guard’s breathing stops.

When the light returns, the guard’s mouth is open mid-word. His eyes are wide, wet. His hand hangs in the air as if he’s about to point. He doesn’t move. Not even a tremor.

Jolyne turns her head slowly. “Hey.” She snaps her fingers in front of his face. Nothing.

Makima watches like she’s observing a procedure. “He’s being quiet,” she says. “So we can talk.”

Jolyne’s heart kicks hard against her ribs. “What did you do to him?”

Makima’s tone stays polite. “It’s temporary.”

The guard’s keys slip from his frozen hand, but they don’t hit the floor. They drift, almost lazily, and settle into Makima’s palm as if she’s always owned them.

Jolyne’s skin goes cold. For one stupid beat she wonders if the prison itself is enchanted, if the wards and talismans have a mind and Makima is simply the latest visitor.

Then Makima’s fingers close around the keys and Jolyne knows. The prison isn’t doing this.

Makima is.

Jolyne breathes through her nose, slow. Panic will make her stupid. She cannot afford stupid.

Stone Free unspools again, but this time it isn’t a single filament. It’s a plan.

She lets threads seep into the vent grating near her ankle, into the seam beneath the door to the linen closet, up the wall toward the flickering fixture. Each strand is a nerve, a listening line. She feels air currents. She feels the faint vibration of distant footsteps. She feels the light’s ballast hum, stuttering on the edge of failure.

Each flicker becomes a ping inside her fingers. Dark—light—dark—light. She times her micro-movements to it, so if a camera tries to catch her hand twitching, it catches only shadow.

Makima watches with the patience of someone reading a report. “You’re making yourself into an instrument.”

Jolyne keeps her eyes hard. “Don’t flatter yourself.”

“I’m not,” Makima says. “I’m admiring you.”

The words should be pleasant. In her mouth they’re a collar.

Makima steps in, close enough that Jolyne can smell something faint and clean—soap, maybe. Something domestic. It makes Jolyne want to spit.

“I can protect you,” Makima says softly. “This is a dangerous place. You’re brave, but bravery doesn’t keep you alive by itself.”

“I’ve been keeping myself alive my whole damn life,” Jolyne snaps.

Makima’s smile narrows, almost fond. “Then you understand. The world is full of people who don’t know what they are. They need a hand. A leash. A purpose.”

Jolyne’s disgust rises hot and immediate. “Lady, this isn’t Disney. I’m not your pet.”

The politeness in the air cracks. Not in Makima’s expression—she stays serene—but in the pressure around her, the way the corridor seems to listen harder.

Makima’s head tilts a fraction. “Pet,” she repeats, as if considering the word. “No. Not that.”

The flickering light dies for a full second, a deeper blackout than before. Jolyne’s strings feel it—the ballast hiccups, a tiny seizure in the metal.

In the dark, Makima’s voice is calm. “An asset.”

When the light returns, Makima lifts her hand slightly—an almost casual gesture.

Every camera in this stretch swivels at once, all red dots aligning on Jolyne like a constellation deciding to become a target.

An alarm doesn’t sound. That would be too honest. Instead, a soft chime pings somewhere, and the lock mechanisms down the corridor begin to click in sequence like polite applause.

Makima looks at Jolyne as if offering a compromise. “Show me Stone Free.”

Jolyne doesn’t answer. She acts.

Stone Free snaps outward in a burst—white string whipping into a net that flashes in the flicker-light. She throws it low and tight, looping around Makima’s ankles, up her calves, binding her knees. Another coil slices the air and wraps Makima’s wrists, cinching them to her sides. A third thread darts up and hooks the light fixture itself, anchoring the whole restraint to the ceiling like a spider web reinforced with intent.

It’s clean. Efficient. Not lethal.

Makima’s smile doesn’t falter. For a heartbeat, she is genuinely restrained, her body held in place by Jolyne’s will made physical.

Jolyne steps in close, breath harsh. “You’re not the only one who can rewrite the room.”

Makima’s eyes are steady. “Very good.”

Then Makima’s gaze shifts—past Jolyne—to the frozen guard.

The guard inhales, suddenly, like someone released from underwater. He coughs once, convulsing. His eyes focus. His mouth shapes a scream that doesn’t come out.

Instead, his hands move to his own throat.

Jolyne’s strings feel the motion before her eyes do—the vibration of frantic fingers, the scraping of nails against skin. The guard begins to choke himself, not with madness, but with obedience.

“Stop!” Jolyne jerks her hand up, instinctively pulling her net tighter around Makima as if pressure can force a command back into the other woman’s mouth. “Stop it!”

Makima’s head remains upright despite the bindings. Her voice is unstrained. “You want to restrain me without consequences.”

The guard’s face turns red, then purple. His feet kick, boots thudding dull on concrete. The overhead light flickers and each blink is like a shutter closing on his suffering.

Jolyne’s throat closes. Anger surges, useless and hot. She can attack Makima. She can crush her windpipe, snap her neck, tear her apart with Stone Free—

—and the guard will still die because Jolyne hesitated, because Makima made him a lever and pulled.

Jolyne’s loyalty flares in the most inconvenient way possible. She can’t watch someone pay for her defiance. She won’t.

Stone Free splits. Part of the string keeps Makima bound—thin, tense, ready to tighten further. The rest whips to the guard, wrapping his wrists, yanking his hands away from his throat. It’s like trying to pull a man out of a current.

The guard’s hands fight the string. Not his strength—something behind his eyes.

Makima watches, pleased in a quiet way. “You’re kind.”

“Shut up,” Jolyne hisses. Sweat beads at her hairline. The light flickers. Her strings tremble with the effort of holding two opposing forces.

Then Makima’s body shifts.

Not against the bindings—through them, as if the concept of restraint has been politely declined. A wet sound, almost small. Jolyne’s net bites into flesh that seems to give way, then… reforms.

For an instant Jolyne sees something wrong: Makima’s skin dimples beneath the string, then smooths as if the damage never happened. As if the cost has been sent elsewhere.

A faint thump echoes from a distant cellblock—too far to identify, too soft to be coincidence.

Jolyne’s stomach drops. She doesn’t need an explanation spelled out. She’s seen enough systems to recognize a contract when it’s working.

Makima doesn’t have to win by force. She wins by making the price unacceptable.

Jolyne’s mind races, assembling the horrible logic: hurt Makima, and someone else bleeds for it. Kill Makima, and someone else dies. Escalate, and the prison becomes a sacrificial machine with people as currency.

Makima’s smile is unchanged, which makes it worse. “You understand.”

Jolyne swallows bile. Her fingers twitch, wanting to strangle the calm out of Makima’s face, wanting to break something that can’t be broken. But her strings on the guard feel his pulse hammering back to normal, feel him gulp air in ragged sobs.

Makima speaks gently, like offering a hand to someone standing on a ledge. “Cooperate with me, and you can leave this facility. I can make that happen. You can be safe. Others can be safe.”

Jolyne’s laugh comes out hoarse. “And the leash comes with it.”

Makima’s eyes soften, almost sympathetic. “Everyone has one. Most people just don’t see it.”

The cameras’ red dots stay trained on Jolyne. The locks down the corridor finish clicking. Somewhere, more footsteps begin to converge—not hurried, not panicked. Coordinated.

Makima turns her head slightly, as if listening to a schedule. “If you resist, I will still get what I want. But you will watch people suffer because you wanted to prove something.”

Jolyne’s jaw clenches until it hurts. The moral vice closes around her ribs. Take the deal and be owned. Refuse and become the reason someone else dies. It’s a rigged game designed by someone who thinks kindness is just another tool.

The overhead light flickers again—two quick blinks.

Jolyne’s strings are already threaded through the light fixture. She feels the ballast shiver. She feels the thin, brittle wire that feeds it. She feels the exact point where a single tug will make it fail.

Makima’s gaze stays on her, patient.

Jolyne makes her choice in the space between blinks.

Stone Free snaps upward, not at Makima—at the light. The string bites the wire. The fixture sparks once and the corridor plunges into darkness so complete it swallows the red dots of the cameras.

In the black, Jolyne moves by touch. Her string network is her eyes now—filaments in vents, under doors, around ankles and metal, sensing vibration, mapping bodies. She feels Makima’s position as a dense absence, a pressure point that doesn’t breathe.

She doesn’t run for freedom. There is no freedom here. She runs for something narrower: agency.

Her string darts out, fast, through the crack beneath the linen closet door where she’d already threaded it. It finds a bundle of uniforms, a stack of sheets. She wraps them tight, a cocoon forming in seconds. She stuffs it with enough mass, enough shape, to be mistaken for a body in the dark. She yanks it into the corridor and sends it stumbling forward on string-pulled momentum like a puppet.

At the same time, she slips herself backward, flattening against the wall, strings hugging her close, minimizing her presence. She presses another filament to the intercom panel she’d sensed earlier—feels the dormant current, the line that leads elsewhere.

In the dark, she whispers into the grill, not for Makima. For whoever might still be themselves on the other end. “Listen. Don’t—don’t touch her. Don’t hurt her. She—she makes other people pay.”

Her voice shakes with anger, but the message goes out as a thread, thin and urgent.

The lights stay dead. Footsteps rush into the corridor—guards, maybe more. Their boots drum on concrete, loud in the blackout.

Someone shouts, confused. Someone grabs the moving cocoon and goes down, tangled.

Makima’s voice cuts through it all, unraised. “Jolyne.”

It’s not a call. It’s a claim.

Jolyne’s strings tighten around her own ribs, reminding her she’s still made of something she can control. She keeps her breath shallow, listening through her filaments as bodies collide, as orders are given, as the prison tries to correct itself.

A lighter click sounds near her—small, intimate.

A door unlocking.

Not the guard’s keys this time. Something else. Permission granted
