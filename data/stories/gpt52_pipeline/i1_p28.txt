Eli slid into the bus seat like he owned it, even though his backpack straps were twisted and his ears still burned from Mom yelling, “Hurry!” at the door.

He didn’t care. Not today.

He tugged his sleeve back and revealed the watch.

It wasn’t like the chunky plastic kid watches at the mall. This one was smooth and black, with a glass face that looked deeper than it should. When the bus hit a pothole, it vibrated once—hard—like a pulse against his wrist.

“Is that the new BlinkBand?” Tyler Crane leaned over, eyes wide.

Eli grinned. “Found it,” he lied, tapping the screen. Menus flicked by too fast to read. The second hand wasn’t a hand at all—just a tiny white slash that jittered like it couldn’t decide where “now” was.

Outside, near the school’s bike racks, a stray cat sat on a concrete parking stop. Gray, scrawny, one ear nicked. Its yellow eyes tracked Eli’s wrist, not his face, as if the watch was the only thing that mattered.

Eli tapped again.

For half a heartbeat, the screen flashed a single word in blocky letters:

REWIND?

Then it vanished, as if it had never been there at all.

---

Cafeteria milk cartons always exploded for other kids. Not Eli.

Until today.

He swung his tray around the corner, saw Maya and Jonah waving him over, and his elbow clipped the milk. It toppled, hit the table, and white cold spilled everywhere—over Maya’s sketchbook. Her eyes went huge.

“Oh my—Eli!”

The cafeteria got quiet in that way that means everyone is pretending not to watch while watching.

Eli’s face went hot. His thumb found the watch without thinking. He pressed the side button.

The world hiccuped.

His ears popped. The fluorescent lights stuttered. For a second, his stomach felt like it dropped through the floor. Sounds warped like someone spun a record backward.

Then—snap—he was turning the corner again. The tray was still level. The milk was still standing.

Maya and Jonah waved.

This time Eli held the tray like a waiter. He took the corner wide. He sat down without a spill.

“Nice,” Jonah said, impressed.

Eli laughed like it had always been easy.

Under the table, a shadow moved. The stray cat was there—how did it even get inside?—peering between chair legs. It stared straight at Eli’s wrist, unblinking, as if it remembered the milk on the floor that now didn’t exist.

Then it darted away, tail stiff, like it had counted something and didn’t like the number.

---

By third period, Eli couldn’t stop.

Mrs. Donnelly asked, “Eli, what’s the capital of Vermont?”

His brain blanked—then he pressed the button. Ear pop. Flicker. Reset. Now he listened to her ask Jonah instead, heard “Montpelier,” pressed again, and answered like a genius.

In gym, he missed the dodgeball on purpose just to feel it—press, rewind, catch—press, rewind, throw it perfectly so Tyler yelped and everyone cheered.

He rewound a laugh when it came out snorty. He rewound a stumble at the water fountain. He rewound when he said “you too” after the lunch lady told him to enjoy his meal.

Each reset came with a tiny tax. A faint pressure behind his eyes. A soreness in his knees like he’d run stairs. Once, when he rewound too fast, his voice cracked on a single word—“sure”—and the sound was deeper than it should’ve been, like a man clearing his throat through him.

Tyler squinted at him in science. “Dude… are you okay? You’re acting, like, weird confident.”

“I’m fine,” Eli said, too quickly.

At the end of the hall, near the trophy case, the stray cat sat perfectly still. It watched him pass. When Eli rewound near it—just to re-do a lame joke—it didn’t flinch at the flicker like everyone else.

It only turned its head, tracking him, patient as a clock.

---

After gym, Eli ducked into the boys’ bathroom, more to look at the watch than anything.

The mirrors were spotted and the lights made everyone look sickly, but Eli still froze.

His face looked… off.

Not “bad hair day” off. Not “I need sleep” off.

There was a shadow above his upper lip. Not dirt. Tiny dark hairs, like the beginning of fuzz. His jawline seemed sharper, like someone had tugged it downward. When he swallowed, his throat moved in a way he’d only ever noticed on his dad.

He leaned closer. Pores. A small angry bump on his chin that hadn’t been there this morning.

“Okay. Allergies,” he whispered. “Or… gym sweat.”

He rolled his shoulders and felt his shirt pull tighter across his back. The sleeves, which usually covered his wrists, sat a little higher.

His shoes pinched.

A seventh grader pushed past him, stopped, and stared. “Uh… this is the middle school bathroom.”

“I know,” Eli snapped.

The kid backed out like Eli was some random high schooler who’d wandered in by mistake.

Eli splashed water on his face, but the reflection didn’t look more familiar.

In the mirror, behind him in the doorway, the stray cat sat like it belonged there. It blinked slowly, once, like: There you are again.

---

By last period, the world had started speaking to Eli like he was someone else.

Mr. Patel, the assistant principal, stopped him in the hall. “Can I help you? Visitors need to check in.”

“I’m a student,” Eli said, voice coming out too low on the last word. He cleared his throat, embarrassed.

Mr. Patel’s eyes went straight to Eli’s wrist. “Where did you get that watch?”

“It’s mine,” Eli said, and immediately felt stupid. Like a little kid saying “because I said so.”

In art class, Maya didn’t scoot over to make room like she always did. She hugged her sketchbook protectively.

“You didn’t sit with us at lunch,” she said, hurt in her voice. “You just… walked away. Twice.”

Eli’s mouth opened. He didn’t have the right explanation. How do you say, I erased it? How do you say, I did it over and it didn’t count?

From the classroom doorway, the stray cat slipped in and sat squarely in Eli’s path, blocking the aisle between desks. It didn’t hiss. It didn’t move.

It just looked up at him, steady as a warning sign.

Eli stepped around it, heart thumping too hard for a normal school day.

---

In the last five minutes before the bell, Eli couldn’t stand it anymore. He stared at the watch face under his desk.

The second-slash jittered. The watch vibrated softly, like it was breathing.

Eli pressed and held the side button.

The screen brightened. A clean interface slid into view, nothing like a normal smartwatch menu.

TIME BORROWED: 00:06:12  
AGE SHIFT: +00:06:12  
BATTERY: ⌛

Eli’s skin went cold.

“No,” he mouthed.

He tapped “REWIND” again, just to test. Just to see if it was lying.

The world lurched. Ear pop. Flicker.

And then pain bloomed in his knees—sharp, adult pain. His fingers looked… longer. Veins stood out on the backs of his hands like blue threads under the skin. When he swallowed, his throat clicked.

The watch updated:

TIME BORROWED: 00:07:04  
AGE SHIFT: +00:07:04

Eli squeezed his wrist until it hurt. “It’s minutes. It’s only minutes,” he told himself. But the mirror memory—stubble—said otherwise. Minutes didn’t do that.

Across the room, the stray cat’s ears flattened. It stared at the watch like it hated it.

Like it had seen this game before.

---

The bell rang. Everyone surged into the hallway. Eli kept his sleeve pulled down, heart hammering.

He was halfway to his locker when he saw Maya ahead, walking too close to Tyler Crane. Tyler’s hand hovered by her sketchbook like he was going to snatch it. Maya’s shoulders were tight, like she was trying not to cry.

Eli’s stomach twisted. He marched forward. “Tyler. Back off.”

Tyler turned, surprised. Then his grin got mean. “Or what? You gonna tell on me, Grandpa?”

He reached and yanked the sketchbook. Pages fluttered. Maya gasped, grabbing for it.

Eli saw it—one page tearing.

He slammed the watch button. Not a tap. A hard press. Again. Again—because the first rewind didn’t feel far enough, didn’t fix the rip.

The hallway stretched like taffy. The lights flickered like lightning. Eli’s ears screamed with pressure.

Snap.

He was standing at his locker, hand on the combination dial.

But the hallway was wrong.

The posters on the wall weren’t about the spring dance. They were about a “Safety Assembly” in bold red letters. The trophy case held different trophies, like history had been rearranged.

And Maya—Maya wasn’t down the hall at all.

Jonah walked past Eli without looking up.

“Jonah!” Eli grabbed his sleeve. “Where’s Maya?”

Jonah jerked away like Eli was a stranger. “Who?”

Eli’s mouth went dry. “Maya. Maya Rivera. She sits—she sits with us.”

Jonah frowned, confused, and kept walking.

Eli stumbled backward, dizzy. His locker door slammed shut, not by his hand.

At the end of the hallway, the stray cat sat in the exact same place as before, unmoved by the new posters, the new noise, the new world.

It looked at Eli like it had been waiting for him to finally mess it all up.

---

Eli followed the cat without deciding to.

It padded through a side door that should’ve been locked and into the space behind the gym—dumpsters, cracked asphalt, the smell of old lunches. The cat stopped beside a patch of weeds and stared at a piece of metal half-buried in dirt.

A watch.

Not Eli’s watch. An older one, scratched and dull. The band was torn like teeth had worried at it.

Eli crouched and dug it out. The back was engraved, the letters worn but readable:

PROPERTY OF D. HASKELL

Eli’s chest tightened. He knew that name. There was a missing student poster in the main office that everyone pretended not to look at. A kid who’d “moved away” years ago. A rumor that he’d disappeared right after getting in trouble for stealing.

The stray cat circled Eli once, slow. Its eyes went from Eli’s face to Eli’s wrist and back, like it was comparing versions.

Eli’s reflection warped in the watch’s scratched metal. For an instant, he didn’t see a seventh grader at all. He saw someone older—someone with tired eyes.

The cat sat down, tail wrapped neatly around its paws.

Judge. Witness. Hall monitor of time.

Eli’s throat tightened. “What did you do to him?” he whispered, and the cat only blinked.

Like: You did it.

---

Eli ran back inside, mind spinning, ignoring the stares people gave him—stares that slid off him like he didn’t belong in this building.

He found a bathroom, locked himself in a stall, and yanked his sleeve up.

The watch screen lit on its own.

REWIND TO RESTORE?  
COST: ???

His hands shook so hard the letters wobbled.

If he didn’t fix it, Maya was gone—erased like the spilled milk. Jonah didn’t remember her. The posters were different. The world had changed around him like a dream deciding on a new plot.

But if he rewound again—farther—how much would it steal? Minutes? Hours? Years?

His knees ached as if answering.

Eli stared at his knuckles. They looked too big. He thought of being older, cooler, taken seriously. He’d wanted it so badly.

Now the idea felt like falling.

The stall door rattled. Someone outside said, “Sir? This bathroom is for students.”

Sir.

Eli pressed his forehead against the cold metal wall. The watch vibrated, that heartbeat-thump against his wrist, impatient.

Through the crack beneath the stall, a gray paw appeared. The stray cat had followed him in. It slid its paw farther, touching the toe of Eli’s shoe like a tap.

Then it reached higher and rested its paw on Eli’s wrist—right on the watch face.

Not gentle. Not cute.

Blocking him.

Or holding him down.

Eli swallowed. “If I do this… I can get her back.”

The cat’s claws pricked through his sleeve—just enough to sting.

Eli couldn’t tell if it was warning him or daring him.

The screen waited, bright and blank as an open mouth.

He hit REWIND.

---

The bathroom lights went out.

The world flipped inside his head. His ears roared. His stomach dropped so hard he thought he might throw up his spine. For a moment there was nothing—no sound, no smell, no sense of where his arms were—just the awful feeling of being pulled backward through himself.

Then—snap.

Noise returned. Hallway noise. Lockers. Voices.

Eli blinked and realized he was sitting in a desk.

A desk that barely fit his legs.

His knees jammed against the underside. His thighs pressed the chair like it was too small. His hands rested on the desktop—and they were not kid hands anymore. The knuckles were pronounced. The fingers were long, with faint dark hair on the back.

He looked down at his shirt. It was stretched across his shoulders. The sleeves stopped halfway up his forearms.

“Eli?” Mrs. Donnelly’s voice cut through the room. Sharp. Suspicious. “Are you new? You can’t just sit in here.”

Eli’s heart slammed against his ribs. He turned toward the window, desperate for a reflection.

The glass showed him in a warped rectangle: a face older than seventh grade. Older than eighth. His jaw was broader. His eyes looked like they’d been awake for years. Above his lip was no longer fuzz—it was real stubble.

And on his wrist, the watch glowed.

TIME BORROWED: 03:12:46  
AGE SHIFT: +03:12:46

Eli’s mouth opened, but his voice came out as a stranger’s. “I—”

Outside the classroom door, in the sliver of hallway visible through the window, the stray cat sat perfectly still, staring in.

Watching.

Counting.
