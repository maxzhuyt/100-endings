There’s something enticing about solitude, especially for an introvert like myself. But solitude in our time seems difficult to find and hold on to.
        A person could be forgiven for thinking that the nation of Allustria would be ideal for those needing solitude. It tends toward the assertively mountainous, making overland travel difficult if not impossible for those without the means, and most of the higher peaks have year-round snow. But dirigibles have become commonplace in the past decade, allowing people to travel farther than ever at a fraction of the cost. And Allustria, once derided as a place very few would seek out despite stunning vistas, suddenly became a desirable destination for travelers seeking novel experiences. 
        Travel opens the world, but it also makes finding solitude more difficult.
        But after some extensive research, and a certain amount of bribery to parties who knew people who knew people, I’d leased a cabin well away from the main travel routes. It had been intended as a noble’s hunting lodge. Kirenna, the woman who owned the cabin, told me that when I met with her about the lease. 
        Admittedly, it was possibly too large to properly be considered a cabin. It had two stories, with the lower space devoted to a living area, small library, bathing room, and kitchen, while the upper space was more of a loft where one could sleep and benefit from the rising heat.
        It was also fully furnished: seating enough for six people, or eight if you were very comfortable with each other. There was a kitchen with a larder, a cold closet, and a stove that was powered by crystals of solidified mana, a dining area with hutches for plates and utensils, a bathtub and indoor toilet, and a luxuriously sized bed on which three people could sleep comfortably. 
        The man at least had excellent taste in his materials, though I’m sure nearly all the wood and most of the stone were imported from Solust. The furniture frames were all polished, reddish-blonde wood, the cushions were stuffed with down and covered with a soft, sturdy linen, and blankets sewn of rabbit fur were draped across nearly every seating surface. Meanwhile, the kitchen and bathing room used a pale, speckled granite that added to the lightness of the interior, making the space seem even bigger than it was.
        Less than a month after it was completed, however, the common folk overthrew the nobility of Solust. The man didn’t even have time to flee that country for his hunting lodge before he was imprisoned, then executed. His loss was my gain, and I’d never had much use for nobility, even in my home nation of Lanivet.
        The former noble’s taste in books was questionable. When I first arrived, there were three books in total. Two of them were the same guide to animals of the region, one heavily marked with notes on possible hunting techniques and the other pristine. The third was, shall we say, an indiscreet romance featuring a noble wooing his way through a bevy of common but beautiful women. I sent all three back with Kirenna the first time she delivered supplies, and she laughed, saying they’d make good kindling.
        Once a week, Kirenna would stop by with mostly foodstuffs and other non-durable goods. I’d make her lunch, then give her whatever writing I’d completed over the course of the week for the next mail carriage. In exchange, I got to hear about her town of Marden, the goings-on, her wife and husband and the general store they ran, as well as hilarious tales of travelers stunned by the sophistication of what they’d thought was a provincial backwater. After I discovered she had a special fondness for frostberry tarts, I started making those for her in appreciation for all the time she took from her life to help me maintain my solitude. 
        When I first arrived, I’d intended to spend most of my time writing. Between my academic work on the language of the ancients who once walked this world but disappeared some time ago, and my slowly growing career writing tales where damsels turned the tables on their captors and became heroes, I anticipated being able to write to my heart’s content without interruption.
        After all, I had enough magic to keep the rooms lit, the water tank from freezing over, and myself warm when I ventured outside into the valley I currently called home. The great mages called such magic petty, but it was more than enough for a simple life. Their spells worked on a much grander scale: balancing weather, banishing malevolent entities, enabling communication between the leaders of nations, and the like. My gifts were sufficient to sustain myself in the solitude I’d chosen. 
        My first month at the cabin was something of a revelation; I had desired solitude without remembering the cost in effort needed for the tasks of basic living. The amenities included by the now-dead noble eased my circumstances somewhat, but I still needed to launder my clothing, clean up after cooking, and gather supplies from the surrounding valley to supplement what I’d brought with me. I’ll admit, I had a great deal to learn, but at least it was the middle of spring when I arrived. Kirenna gave me a map she’d made of the valley, citing locations and resources that she thought would be helpful, and provided a few pointers for someone more accustomed to a city than the mountains of Allustria.
        As the months wore on, I grew more and more comfortable with my surroundings. Kirenna expressed a bit of surprise that I’d thrown myself into the more practical aspects of my existence; over lunch once, she confessed that her first impression of me was that I was a spoiled academic with more money than sense.
        I laughed and confided my history to her. My mother had wanted her daughter to have opportunities in the world that weren’t limited by her appearance. Growing up, I had learned practical skills more suited to an urban life, but I’d also studied more scholarly pursuits. The universities of Lanivet were remarkably accepting, and offered scholarships to those who could demonstrate an aptitude for a given area of study. I passed the exams, and spent the next decade and more in the halls of a university that did its best to provide for the students’ every need. So while I was now learning entirely new things, I at least had a history of a more practical sort.
        But with the end of the short, mild summer, a new complication arose. Allustria’s sudden popularity as a travel destination meant that a tourist had discovered skiing. While the people of Allustria used skis to more easily traverse the terrain, the tourist told all of his friends about what he perceived as something more recreational. 
        Allustria’s economy was booming thanks to the influx of visitors, so of course the king asked the weather mages to ensure there would be more snow than usual over the winter. Kirenna warned me that while my solitude was unlikely to be broken, she couldn’t guarantee that her supply trips would continue with any regularity due to the planned increase of snowy weather.
        “I appreciate your concern,” I told her, “but I’ve been stockpiling as much as I can, and I did read that noble’s annotated hunting book. I’m not an avid fan of hunting, but I can at least set snares and plan for the leaner months.”
        She gave me a look that I recognized as exasperated concern, but once I showed her the improvements I’d made to the larder, as well as the small greenhouse I’d built, she conceded that I might not starve in between visits. Touched by her concern, I made sure to send her home with enough frostberry tarts for her and her partners.
        When the first snowstorm hit, I spent the first few days focused entirely on practical matters. It took some time to adjust to moving in snowshoes, and it helped that I knew the terrain well enough to not get lost. I also kept the path from my door to the stables clear for Kirenna, though I did not love shoveling snow over and over again. It was a pleasant surprise to discover that the architect of the cabin had set up a means to use snowmelt to refill the water tank, but I probably should have cleaned the mechanisms before the snow started falling.
        When Kirenna finally showed up a week and a half after her last delivery, she was pleasantly surprised to see me doing as well as I was. Sheepishly, I admitted that I’d found the practical work a refreshing change of pace, and hadn’t really written anything new to send back with her.
        “I’m sure your editors will understand that your characters will just have to stay in perilous circumstances for a bit longer,” Kirenna teased.
        “But not too much longer,” I grinned, “since it’s the sales of those stories that help me pay for this.”
        “I never asked, does writing pay all that well?”
        Searing some venison in a pan on the stove, I shook my head. “It pays enough that most of those funds go into your pocket for my supplies. I admit, when I first got the scholarship to the university, I took the money and used it to gamble. After three days, I walked away with triple the money I’d been awarded for that first year, then I invested in my mother’s business. That investment is what’s paid for the rest.”
        Kirenna laughed. “You are far too interesting to be hiding away here!”
        I grinned as I transferred the cooked meat to plates. “And yet, I am far more comfortable away from people. When you grow up in a city, there’s always a crowd around you. I found fairly quickly that having a few people around me is fine, but once that number goes past five, I suddenly want to find a quiet room away from them.”
        “This must suit you quite well, then,” she mused, tilting her head at me like a bird.
        “It does, but I do get a bit lonely from time to time. Your visits do a great deal to alleviate that, but I’d not pull you away from your wife and husband for longer than they might be comfortable with.”
        She snorted. “They’ve been nudging me to ask you whether you’d be interested in me.”
        I barely managed to set my fork down instead of dropping it. “That seems, I don’t know, a bit odd?”
        “What? That my marriage to my partners might be comfortable enough that they’d tease me about my interest in you? And encourage me to pursue you, should you be interested?”
        “I’m familiar with open relationships,” I began slowly, “and Lanivet has a history of people living in healthy triads, especially among the common classes. But the three of you seemed utterly content with each other when I first arrived in Marden.”
        “And we still are,” Kirenna countered instantly, “which doesn’t preclude any of us expressing interest in another person. You’re thoughtful and kind, which are not the same thing, you’re not afraid of hard work, and by all the gods can you cook.” She held up a bit of the venison and shook her head. “What you’ve done here with the local herbs, it’s to die for.”
        “Ah,” I teased, “it’s all about my cooking.” 
        “No, truly, Romaya, it’s not.” She rolled her eyes fondly at me. “Marden is an isolated town in a nation that’s never been all that popular. You’ve become part of the town, even here in this cabin. And while the people of Marden treat you like a bit of a celebrity, we understand how much you treasure your solitude. Nobody is going to tell the tourists about you.” She had more of the venison while I sat in stunned silence, then continued, “My partners and I have been keeping your privacy as best we can, but word has gotten around.”
        I blinked. “Celebrity?”
        “The noble built this cabin four summers ago, and it’s been empty until you came. You leased the place for a whole year and discussed possibly staying longer. And you get and send things regularly through the mail. There was bound to be curiosity about you.” Kirenna grinned. “My partners and I do our best to keep the gossip to a minimum.”
        I looked down at my plate, and started eating again. I hadn’t really considered the impact I’d have on Marden, I’d just been looking for somewhere I could live away from people. 
        “Thank you,” I murmured after finishing my food.
        “For what?”
        “For all your help, and your company. I’ve looked forward to your visits and spending time with you. Even if it’s only been a few hours each week.”
        “Do I detect a note of interest beyond that?” she said with a wink.
        “I think so? Like I said, it’s only been a few hours each week. I’m sure we’ve been on our best behavior around each other.”
        Suddenly, Kirenna stood, strode to the front door, and gestured out at the heavy snowfall that must have started while we were eating. “Oh no,” she said, mock dramatically, “it’s snowing. Whatever shall I do. I’m sure the snow is too heavy for me to return to my partners.”
        I started laughing. “Did you plan this?”
        “My timing? Of course! If you weren’t at all interested, I’d have kept you company for the next couple of days and returned home with no hard feelings. At least now we have the time to figure things out.”
        I grinned. Solitude had been enticing when I moved here, but I was open to change and company as well.
