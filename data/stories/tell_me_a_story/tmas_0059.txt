The Métro has always been my favorite means of transport in Paris. It used to take me twenty minutes to reach my classes at the Sorbonne from my quaint student housing. While I was usually tired in the early mornings, I would look forward to the crowded yet entertaining ride that awaited me around dusk.
        Being a frequent Métro rider, I started recognizing some of the many faces that joined me on my commute each day and, on occasion, would attempt to make conversation with them in my subpar French. Though I wasn’t sure if I always fit in as an American, Paris felt like a city for everyone: the lonesome, the well-connected, and those content to be alone. I felt myself to be the latter, though I delighted in people-watching.
        I made a friend on the Métro who felt the same. In fact, he might have enjoyed it more than me; I learned he had a habit of drawing the citizens of Paris, particularly its Métro-goers. One fateful day, this included me.
        He was one of the many daily riders I shared a commute with. This man appeared to be in his sixties. I recognized him by the small tufts of white hair around his head and the wild eyebrows that framed his watchful eyes. I noticed that he’d often look around at fellow passengers, but I found myself deeply confused when his gaze was fixed on me. His glimpses continued, intermittently looking down at his lap, then back up at me. 
        Was there something on my face? Did I look like someone he knew? My growing distress must’ve been apparent, as moments later he rose from his seat to come and sit beside me.
        “I’m sorry if I’ve disturbed you,” he started with a nervous smile, “I was drawing you for practice in my sketchbook. I draw strangers all the time, and usually they don’t notice. You’re very attentive.”
        I felt relieved, but was still confused. As far as I knew, I’d never been drawn before. “May I see it?”
        With a nod, the stranger flicked quickly through the thick pages of his sketchbook. I didn’t expect him to show me some of the best drawings I’d ever seen; it was obvious that he was hugely talented and well-trained. Some of the elaborate drawings were done in charcoal, some in pen, and others were lightened by bright and intricate watercolors. Scenes from all around Paris flew past as he leafed through, and I spotted a bustling Champs-Elysee, a golden Seine, and the Eiffel Tower while it sparkled at dusk.
        Most of the sketches were people, expertly illustrated in all positions, sizes, and expressions. Many were fellow Métro users, no doubt unaware that their image was being masterfully recorded during their commute. I was nothing less than entranced as he carefully thumbed through the pages. “Wait!” I interrupted, jutting my hand onto the page of a scene I recognized. It was partly unfinished, but I knew exactly what the sketch portrayed.
        “The Pont Neuf! That’s…” I kept my fingers on the page, slowly smiling at my pleasant surprise.                         “You drew that from my spot. That’s my favorite place in the entire city.”
        “Really?” He asked, and I nodded, thinking of all the times I’d returned to the same familiar set of steps.
         “Yes, I stop on those stairs and just stare at the bridge. Especially at night, it’s just so beautiful. I go there when I’m feeling sad or disconnected from everything. It reminds me just how lucky I am to be here in Paris.”
        We briefly pivoted to small talk. I confirmed my obvious American-ness, sharing short anecdotes from my experience growing up along the California coast. He was born in Arles, the southern French city that Vincent van Gogh once derived a wealth of inspiration from. Growing up between Arles and New York, his Southern accent was only noticeable in every other vowel, and his English was excellent.
        “Do you think growing up with Van Gogh’s influence made you want to be an artist?” I inquired. The man wore a peaceful smile, staring into the darkness of the window in front of us.
        “I think he helped me appreciate what I see every day. There is beauty in every landscape, every person, and every detail, no matter how often you see them. And what better way to capture that beauty than by copying it down?”
        He turned the page to reveal the quick pencil sketch of my image. With a quiet gasp, I gaped at the page, studying his study of me. My legs were crossed as they still were, my left side was cast in partial shadow, and I was staring forward with a pensive, almost longing look. The drawing was partially unfinished, yet still magnificently done. 
        “My goodness, you’re such an incredible artist!” I insisted. Looking back up at him, he seemed pleased with his work. While it was somewhat odd to be drawn by a stranger, I found his eccentricity endearing. So many people filled his book that it made me curious what motivated him to record someone’s image at random.
        “If I may ask, why did you choose me today?”
        The artist smiled kindly while looking down at his sketch. “Well, you looked sort of… sad. In a pensive way. Like you’re missing something.”
        He turned to look at me. “Do you think that’s true?”
        I thought for a moment, then shook my head. “I’m not, as far as I know. But maybe it means that whatever I’ve lost is gone for good!”
        We shared a laugh, but his chuckling petered into what sounded more like groans.
        “I’ve been quite forgetful as of late. I worry it’s because of my age. Perhaps I’m only imagining that you are struggling, too.”
        I wasn’t sure what to say. Right when I was about to inquire further, he slowly stood. “This is my stop, but thank you for both the conversation and the practice.”
        I mirrored his smile, almost sad that he was leaving so soon. “Thank you, too! I’ll see you around. What was your name?"
        The man cleared his throat before answering me, and the train slowly reached the stop. “I am Camille. And you?”
        “Claudia,” I answered, and he gave me a wave before stepping off the train with his sketchbook in hand. 
        The sound of whispers caught my attention, and I turned to my right, where two middle-aged women sat. They grinned at me with raised eyebrows.
        “That’s Camille Bonnaire,” One of them relayed in a hushed tone. Intrigued, I leaned closer toward them. “What, that old man? Is he famous?” I questioned, and by their excited faces, it seemed that he was.
        “He’s one of the finest painters working in France today," the other one observed with their hand on their chest. “I’ve loved his work since I was very young. How exciting to finally see him in person!” 
        I felt honored to see him almost weekly on the Métro. I continued asking questions; it turned out that he’d regularly hosted exhibitions in local museums, lectured for Sorbonne art classes, and was known on an international scale. 
        As they exited the train, I could barely contain my own joy. Not even ten o’clock on a Tuesday, and I’d already been sketched by one of the best artists in the country!
        Only a week later, I spotted Camille waiting for the Métro as I returned from my last class. This time, however, he didn't appear to be in good spirits. His expression was melancholic, and he stared into the dark tunnel with what looked like tears in his eyes.
        “Camille!” I called, slowly approaching him. The man turned and looked at me as if he’d never seen me before. After a moment, he squinted.
        “Oh, the girl from the train! What was your name again?” 
        I was happy he recognized me at all. “Claudia. It’s good to see you!”
        He smiled weakly, then appeared suddenly downcast, turning away. Though I didn’t want to pry, I felt compelled to help him. 
        “Are you alright, Camille?”
        With a forlorn sigh, he turned back to me. “That sketchbook has gone missing—the one I had drawn you in. It’s accompanied me for nearly ten years, and now it’s gone. I’m… You don’t want to hear this.”
        I stepped closer, nodding. “No, I do! I’m so sorry. Where do you think it could be?”
        Seemingly growing tearful, he briefly turned away from me again, shaking his head in resignation and touching his face. “I have no idea, I must have misplaced it. I’ve retraced my steps over and over again and searched everywhere that came to mind. I know I was working on a drawing recently—much too recently to forget. I’ve never misplaced it before. My memory is beginning to fail me.”
        I had no clue what to say in response to his woes. This time was even worse. The drawings he’d shown me from the book must’ve taken him hours to perfect, and it’d have taken years to accumulate the mass of art that he’d stored in it. I was filled with sympathy.
        “It has to be somewhere nearby, you’ll find it.” I told him, though I had no basis for my insistence. I just hated to give up hope. Camille solemnly shook his head.
        “I like to think it will return to me, but it’s been a few days now. I am losing hope.” 
        The train arrived, and I walked beside him towards the opening doors. “I’ll keep an eye out for it. Maybe it’s somewhere on the Métro!”
        He shrugged. “Maybe so. Someone could have found it and taken it for themselves. I certainly would, if I’d discovered such a book.”
        I wanted to laugh at his suggestion, but it didn’t feel right under the circumstances.
        “Hey, don’t worry, Camille. I'm sure it's out here somewhere. I’ll keep my eyes peeled.”
        The artist let out a small laugh—a kind yet disbelieving chuckle. “Well, I appreciate your kindness, Claudia.”
        I returned to my neighborhood during the golden hour following our exchange. Walking home, I did as I promised. I scanned the ground, the park benches, and the café tables as I passed, feeling slightly ridiculous as I did so. I didn’t know where Camille frequented, what area he lived in, or where he'd chosen to draw the last few days.
        I continued to reflect on our first conversation. He said he’d been ‘working’ on a drawing, I recalled.                 Where could he have been drawing? If it was on the Métro, as I’d speculated, he might be correct in his suspicions – at least, it’d be especially difficult to find. It was already a needle in a haystack. Where could it possibly be?
        Suddenly, I had a flicker of hope. Could he have returned to finish working on an older drawing? I stopped in my tracks as I weighed the possibility. 
        Whenever I felt lost or hopeless, I’d go back to my favorite spot in all of Paris. His sketch of my favorite view appeared incomplete; could he have returned to work on it?
        I quickly ran back to the Métro, hopeful that my hunch was right. Perhaps I inspired him to revisit the drawing after describing my love for the location. While this mission might have been in vain, I’d at least get to see my favorite sight again. It had been some time since I’d last stopped by, and especially with the clear skies that fateful evening, I knew I’d be pleased with the view.
        Reaching my stop, I ran up the stairs and scanned the streets around me as I walked. I saw no fallen books or loose papers, and anticipation surged through me as I reached my destination.
        The Pont Neuf was gorgeous as usual, but for the first time, I stood on the adjacent stairs feeling disheartened. I scanned along the stairs, the stone railing, and out along the promenade. I saw no books. 
        I stood solemnly until I heard the sound of flapping paper in the breeze. I turned to my right and noticed a weathered book lying flat below the railing, thick and well-used. I beamed as I snatched it from the ground; it was slightly damaged but still in fair condition. I leafed through the pages to be certain and flipped to the page that showed the view before me. 
        The bridge’s shape was once outlined in pencil marks, now made permanent in exacting black ink.         It was filled out beautifully, and I raised it up to the equally enchanting skyline. 
        After a moment, I shut the book and held it close as I walked back up the steps, eager to return the precious sketchbook to its rightful owner. I assumed that I’d see him again that week during my commute, but it turned out that I didn’t have to wait.
        I saw Camille approach the stairs with a downcast expression, his eyes scanning back and forth along the ground as he walked. Finally, he spotted me further down the steps.
        We locked eyes, and I watched him recognize me. As I held up the sketchbook once more, his confused expression melted into pure gratitude, and we shared wide smiles beside the sunlit Seine.
