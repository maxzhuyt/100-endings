The last battle of the last war fought between the kingdoms of Galata and Mosfeld took place outside my town of Stevinsal. Their armies, worn to the bone, gathered on the plains where we used to tend our crops and our animals. Our three seers warned us well ahead of time where these two forces would meet, so we abandoned our town and took only what we could carry from there. But I still felt a deep-seated anger for these kingdoms, these warriors, and their sovereigns. Our town had suffered during the war as each side fought the other for territory.
	I watched from the woods, though I was probably closer than my elders would have liked. The generals directed their troops into battle from anti-grav sleds that hadn’t been recharged in weeks, while soldiers used their energy pistols as clubs and resorted to more “primitive” weapons like swords. 
	Both nations fought for revenge. At least a hundred years worth of slights, territory grabs, resource hoarding, and generally poor leadership led each kingdom again to the plains of Aliva, outside our town.
	The battle lasted two days before the generals realized that there would be no winner aside from the god of the dead and his carrion creatures. At least those creatures fed well. On the third day, messengers rode out from both sides, and we watched as people met carefully now, weapons discarded in favor of carts bearing what little food each side had left.
	My parent was one of our three seers, and all three told our people that we should continue to wait. The armies could still turn on us if they saw us; shapechangers were rarely accepted in those kingdoms, accused of acting as spies or worse when they were discovered. None of us were terribly interested in dealing with the soldiers in any case; they’d raided our storehouses to feed themselves, an understandable response, but with winter coming soon, it would force us to rely on hunting and gathering to sustain ourselves.
	Seven days after the messengers rode out, the sovereigns of both kingdoms came to my town with very little ceremony and small retinues. Queen Alis of Mosfeld looked as though she hadn’t slept well in weeks, while King Varkan of Galata appeared nearly as emaciated as his troops. They spent several days in negotiations, but we had no idea what they were discussing. Several of my people tried to sneak into the camp they’d set up in our town, but the soldiers were still alert for unfamiliar faces. 
	As curious as I was, instead of legs and arms, I chose a shape with paws. It is an old, old saying that a cat may look at a king, and so I did. I wanted to see the king and the queen who’d destroyed their lands and made my people flee their homes.
	What I saw instead were two sovereigns, desperately tired, who assured each other that their advisors, who were responsible for the ongoing tensions between the kingdoms, had been executed. Both of them were apparently new to their rule, and had relied on their advisors more than they should have. 
	They also provided evidence that the merchants who’d profited from the constant aggression had had their warehouses emptied, their accounts drained, and that money given to the people. And finally, that the agitators among the populace who’d encouraged war were all imprisoned and would be tried as war criminals.
	“I’m still not sure whether our kingdom will survive this,” Queen Alis admitted on the third day during a pause in their negotiations while their exhausted guards stood outside the room. “The warmongers on my side drove this conflict for so long that our fields may not produce enough food.”
	King Varkan stared down at his hands. “We could share our harvest and seedstock. It would mean a lean winter for Galata, but I think my people could live with that. I know my father couldn’t, but he’d been raised with stories of the glory of war, and wouldn’t listen to me when I told him the cost.”
	“My mother and father fought in the battle of Alstand, died there, and left me with a kingdom full of regrets. I still have subjects who think the war is justified over a slight from a hundred years ago, but they’ve never seen a battle or counted the cost in lives.” She paused, glancing around the room in fatigued curiosity. “I wonder where the people are. Presumably they saw the armies coming and fled. It seems… unkind to use this hall without acknowledging the cost to others.”
	Varkan nodded. “The cost to the people who lived here and the people of our countries as a start, as well as the cost to those who’ve been wronged by our prejudices, whether human, shapechanger, elf, or the other peoples who’ve likely avoided our kingdoms altogether.”
	I blinked in shock. Was this king truly discussing apologizing to my people?
	“Far too many to apologize to, and without anything but words, I do not know if they would take us seriously.” She paused again. “Speaking of words, you have my condolences on the passing of your father, if that’s at all appropriate.”
	Varkan spat, an action that almost startled me into falling off the ledge I’d chosen to lay on so that I might watch the two sovereigns. “The man was a delusional child, playing with toy soldiers on maps even into his sixth decade, encouraged by advisors and merchants who lost loved ones to the conflict or never felt the touch of famine.” Trembling, the man pushed himself out of his chair. “I accept your condolences in the spirit of good faith on your part, but we are better off with him dead. Had I any choice in the matter before his death last month, I would have sent negotiators to you to end this miserable conflict.”
	Alis nodded, on the verge of tears. “I wanted to reach out to you, but my chief advisor continually undermined me to the court. He told them a woman was unsuited to lead a war, on account of my gentle nature, and spread other, more harmful ideas. I do not like murder, but executing him in front of my court was a painful necessity. He always declared that steel was stronger than flesh, so I proved him right with my sword. I hope that the god of death punishes him and all the other warmongers we’ve had to deal with.”
	I crossed my paws as I considered the situation below me. These two were nothing like the people I had imagined leading a fruitless war. It sounded like Varkan’s father and Alis’ advisor were among the worst offenders, but they were dead and beyond vengeance.
	Varkan sat back in his chair, the brief moment of strength now exhausted. “I’m sure the patriots of my kingdom would rise up if they could, but even they have begun to see the cost of a war that has lasted almost a hundred years.”
	Alis laughed bitterly, and said, “Perhaps we should take all of our patriots, arm them, and make them fight each other. Allow them their bloodlust and mindless anger, while the rest of us try peace for a change.”
	“If there were a feasible way to make that happen, I would,” Varkan sighed. Tears started running down his face. “I am so very tired, Queen Alis. Tired of the hate, tired of the idea that somehow war is good for a kingdom, tired of how little we’ve actually accomplished in the past hundred years that would have made our kingdoms better and not worse.”
	“I regret that I was not strong enough to stop this war last year after my parents’ deaths,” Alis admitted, the tears finally running down her face.
	I watched as they wept unashamedly. Late last night, my parent begged me to wait and listen today, having had a vision of the future, and now I began to understand why. Two people, their sovereignty put aside for the moment, being truly vulnerable in this meeting. I was no seer to perceive the pathways of the future, but this seemed a pivotal moment. But whether to intervene or continue to watch?
	Varkan stared at the table between them for a long moment, then looked up at his counterpart. “It seems a foolish notion, but.” He stopped, and shook his head. “No. Perhaps too foolish by far, especially with the decades of fighting.”
	“We sit here across from each other, both of us vulnerable,” Alis said slowly. “Perhaps if you said what this notion is, together we could determine if it truly is foolish.”
	This felt like the moment my parent had wanted me to wait for. If Varkan revealed his notion, then perhaps these kingdoms could put war aside for good. Depending on his idea and her reaction, of course. But if he did not speak, or she refused, well, war might reignite here in this room.
	After a span of time that felt too long, Varkan whispered, “A marriage of alliance. Necessity. Put an end to war between our kingdoms forever.”
	I watched as Alis blushed to the roots of her golden hair. “There are… practicalities to consider.”
	“An heir, when you prefer the company of women, and I have no interest in intimate relations with anyone? The aftermath of surviving the winter and next few years, when people begin to see past survival and return to loyalties now put aside? A name for a united kingdom?” Varkan exhaled, defeat already written on his face as he raked thin fingers through his reddish-brown hair. “See? As I said, a foolish notion.”
	Alis frowned, not in anger, but in thought. “Not foolish, for I had a similar idea and similar concerns.” Silence fell in the room as the two sovereigns struggled with this new wrinkle.
	I realized that how I thought of these two had changed; I thought they were the bitterest of enemies before they ever came to negotiate peace, but now I saw they were people on the verge of honest friendship. I suspected that this was the moment my parent had hoped for, and where I could finally intervene. So as a cat, I leapt down onto the table between them, purring. I suspected that Varkan would be more open to petting me, but I headbutted both their hands for attention.
	Alis chuckled weakly. “I don’t think I’ve ever seen a cat quite this golden. But, as with all cats, demanding and attention-seeking.” She carefully scratched my head between my ears, and I closed my eyes in delight.
	Varkan moved to pet me as well, showing a familiarity with cats that I appreciated, as he slowly and softly ran his hand along my back. “My father preferred dogs, and while I do like them, I far prefer cats.”
	Alis murmured agreement, and for a time, I let them pet me, bonding over something as simple as paying attention to what they saw as an animal. But then I saw my chance and shifted position such that their hands touched. Seemingly an accident, of course, but if there was no need for my vengeance, then there was a need for me to promote peace.
	For a moment, the two looked at their hands touching, then Alis tentatively grasped Varkan’s hand gently. I moved to the end of the table and perched there, waiting for what came next for these two.
	Varkan managed a smile. “This… feels like a start, at least.” He carefully strengthened his grasp on her hand while still being gentle. “And we have this cat to thank for bridging the gap.”
	I couldn’t help myself. “You’re welcome.”
	The shock on their faces was perhaps more of a delight than it should have been as I slowly shifted back into a human form. Well, human enough aside from my ears and tail. But as I watched them carefully, both watched me cautiously but with more curiosity than fear.
	Alis recovered first. “How may we address you?”
	I smiled at them. “I’m no monarch, though one of my parents is enough of a leader to where you could maybe call me an heir? But Isha is fine. Welcome to our town of Stevinsal and to our Hall of Governance.”
	Varkan carefully extended his other hand in an offer of greeting. “Whatever your provenance, Isha, I am grateful you were here to help us.”
	“As am I,” Alis agreed as she also extended her hand. “I’m sure you heard everything we said, but I would still apologize to you and your people. I’m sure the words themselves are cold comfort without action, though.” Varkan nodded firmly in agreement.
	I took both of their hands. “Warmer comfort than you might think. My people will be pleased to know you are both sincere in your desire for peace, and we’re not unaware of the toll of the war on your kingdoms.” I smiled lopsidedly. “And I’ll say this for myself. I’m very glad to meet two people who are happier to make peace than war.”
	I watched as both sovereigns looked at me with appraisal, perhaps more.
	“There is much work to be done,” Varkan noted.
	“But it’s work worth doing,” Alis answered.
