Miguel, my travel agent, looked at me with a bit of disbelief, gesturing to the holos of fantastic tropical beaches, gambling palaces in domed cities beneath glimmering blue oceans, and resorts filled with laughing tourists.
	“You want to go where, Willow?” he asked. “I have totally affordable packages to the beaches of Eta Cassiopeiae Prime, or the resorts on Kappai Ceti.”
	I smiled at him. “They’re fine and all, but the terraforming on 20 Leonis Minoris did something they haven’t managed with any other planet: redwood forests.” Here on Earth, the redwood forests that remained on the Pacific Northwestern coast of the North American continent were off limits to all visitors. The restoration project had been going on for over a century, but the clear-cut logging of the twentieth century and climate change into the twenty-first made the entire region vulnerable enough that only ecologists and conservation personnel were allowed in. And I was neither.
	“It’s boring,” he argued. 
	“What, just because the beaches aren’t white sand? Or because there isn’t extensive gambling? I’ve told you before, gambling really isn’t my thing.”
	He sighed. “It’s also more expensive right now than the other packages. I know, I know, you have the budget for it, but there hasn’t been much development precisely because the redwoods took off there. There’s only four major cities, and at the last count, twenty minor cities across the entire planet.”
	“Honestly, Miguel? That’s why I want to go. I need a break from the urban sprawl here on Earth. And what isn’t packed cityscape is either designated as a vacation destination, or you  have to be specifically assigned to it. At least on Sequoia, I have a chance to wander through the redwood forests.” 
	In typical fashion, as soon as redwoods ended up dominating the terraforming process, some marketing hack decided to name the planet after the genus of the tree, presumably anticipating yet another tourist destination. However, the Ruling Council of Colonies strictly limited development and tourism on Sequoia for the first hundred years, and that restriction had only been lifted a year ago. 
	Miguel nodded, conceding the point. “The government of Sequoia did set up some preserves specifically for tourists. Did you have any particular one in mind?”
	“No, but I trust you to pick one, given the parameters I set.”
	“Okay, Willow,” Miguel sighed, “I’ll pull something together, and by next week you should be on a different planet for your vacation.”
	I grinned at him, shook his hand, and left the agency to head back to my apartment. After ten years of working with only on-planet vacations, I figured I’d earned the right to treat myself. And from the catalog of immersive sensory experience recordings, or ISERs, the redwoods of Sequoia seemed to be what I most wanted.
	My shuttle touched down at the Mendocino Spaceport on Sequoia five days after I’d asked Miguel to schedule the trip. He’d given me my itinerary the next morning, I’d boarded the shuttle up from Earth that evening, and it took three days for the jumpship to get me to Sequoia. The only other passengers were xenobotanists who wanted to study the development of the redwoods on Sequoia, so I spent most of the trip replaying my favorite ISER, “Muir Woods Trail at Dawn.”
	The shuttle pilot informed us it was late afternoon, Sequoia time, with light breezes and minimal cloud cover at the moment, and that our local contacts would be waiting for us inside the spaceport terminal. The xenobotanists, all aflutter, made a dash for the exit, while I took a couple of deep breaths. Sequoia wasn’t going anywhere in the few minutes it would take them to disembark, and for some reason, I found myself a bit nervous. Probably because this was my first time ever off-Earth.
	But I wasn’t going to be able to see the redwoods if I didn’t get off the shuttle, so I took a deep breath, claimed my carry-on luggage, and made my way to the exit tunnel. Even though the atmosphere was nearly Earth-identical, once I exited the airlock, it just seemed fresher. 
	Arden, my guide, agreed. “I know you’ve got regulations on Earth for pollution, and the governments have all put effort into cleaning up their messes, but I noticed a difference the one time I went to see the original redwoods.” They brushed a hand through their lavender hair and laughed. “There’s a fraction of a percent more oxygen in the air, but personally, I think it’s just that most of the surface is covered in forests.”
	“Where do I get to stay tonight? My travel agent has a list of things I can do, but there’s no real itinerary, which I’m honestly fine with.”
	“Mx. Davis, I’m your host for the trip,” Arden smiled. “Sure, the government has nature preserves specifically for people like yourself, but there really isn’t infrastructure for a burgeoning tourist trade yet. So the government reviews the profiles of people who’re coming to the planet, then they ask a pool of residents for someone to volunteer to host visitors. I took a look at your profile, and suspected we might get along.”
	Curiously, as we walked through the fairly small terminal to their hovercraft, I asked, “What made you suspect that?”
	“We both have a tendency to be more comfortable with smaller numbers of people, what they used to call introverts back in the early twenty-first century and we have similar dietary needs. And though you’ve never actually experienced them, you seem to love the redwoods as much as I do. I get reimbursed for my time and whatever supplies I need to provide, so this is a bit of a mini-vacation for me, too.”
	Once inside their hovercraft, they suggested a range of restaurants they liked, and we ended up chatting for a couple of hours over dinner at a seafood place that I fell in love with. They told me stories of living on Sequoia, interspersed with suggestions for locations I might want to visit and places to avoid. In exchange, I told them about my job and my life back on Earth – the kind of things two people might discuss when getting to know each other.
	And in the morning, I woke up to a thick blanket of fog outside the window of my guest bedroom. I’m not sure how long I spent in the double bed, staring out the window in wonder. Even after over a hundred years of work on the environment, naturally occurring fog was a rarity on Earth.
	Arden finally gently knocked on the door frame, announcing with an amused tone in their voice, “Hey Willow, breakfast is ready. And if you think this is amazing, just wait until I get you into the redwoods proper.” Grinning, I nodded. 
	Once we’d had breakfast, Arden asked me whether I was interested in just being around the trees or hiking. 
	“What’s the difference?” I asked curiously.
	“Well, there’s a fantastic picnic area, if you just want to sit and enjoy the ambiance. It’s one of my favorite spots when I’m not up for hiking. On the other hand, that picnic area does function as the start and end point of a very accessible hiking trail, and the tourism board has a bunch of mobility assistance devices available for people who want to use the trail but aren’t able to walk it.”
	I considered my options. “I don’t want to get overwhelmed, so maybe let’s start at the picnic area, and then I can see what I want from there?”
	Arden nodded. “That sounds like a plan. We’ll pick up food and supplies on the way, and not to worry. The fog around here clears for a few hours in the afternoon, but that’s about it. Something about the terraforming process, the terrain, and the proximity to the ocean guarantees it. That’s probably why the redwoods expanded here so well.”
	By the time we got to the picnic area, the fog had lightened a bit, but not dissipated. There were a few people already there, but one of them headed off to the hiking trail right after we started setting up, and the other two eventually headed back to their hovercraft and left. 
	I’m not sure how long I spent in that grove, experiencing sensations that were somehow more real than any ISER I’d tried. The cool, damp air against my skin tingled; logically I knew it was just tiny droplets of water suspended in air, but being there added all of my lived experience to the mix. The fog drifted slowly through the trees, swirling around the trunks and hiding, then revealing the upper reaches where thicker branches had developed. I noticed a smell that mixed moist earth, a mild spiciness, and faint sweetness. And when Arden said I could touch the trunks, I spent probably a silly amount of time just touching the surfaces of the trees, feeling the rough, almost cloth-like texture of the bark.
	Arden was sitting at one of the tables when I’d finally had my fill for the moment. They’d set up a tablet, along with our picnic in stasis boxes, and gave me a happy little grin when I turned back to them. 
	“Yeah, I do that about once a month. Just come here and get lost in the sensations. Even though I live here, it’s surprisingly refreshing.”
	I nodded, not yet ready to speak. It was everything that I’d wanted from my vacation, and I’d barely spent half a day awake here. 
	“I’d recommend you have a snack now,” they said kindly, “since this isn’t an environment you’re used to, and the gravity is a bit stronger than Earth’s. You’ve got, what, three weeks here?” At my nod, they continued, “So you’ve got some time for exploring what you want. I’ll recommend that you get some direct, lived experiences around here for the first couple of weeks. We’ve got some ISER equipment available too, high-end quality stuff, headsets you won’t even notice after a couple of minutes, and you’ll probably want to record your favorite experiences for later. But save that for your last week, I think?”
	I sat down across from them, gently running my fingertips across the table that was clearly made from the local redwood, feeling the polished grain that was so different from the rough bark, and sighed. “Part of me wants to move here, but I’m pretty sure my job won’t agree to a nearly fifty light-year lag in my work.”
	Arden smiled. “We have plenty of opportunities here. We need a wide range of skills since the planet’s opening up, and clearly you’re as sensation-driven as anyone I’ve met. I have no idea if you’ve ever made an ISER of your own, but given how you were behaving, I suspect you’d be very good at it.”
	I blinked a bit, stunned. “I’m an ISER designer back home, mostly focusing on script and staging, then editing the final product. I don’t think I’ve ever thought about being the one in the recording.”
	“All I’m saying is, if your company isn’t tapping into that side of you, it’s their loss.”
	I looked up into the canopy. The fog had thinned a bit, but it wasn’t gone, leaving the tops of the trees still covered by a cool gray blanket. “I think… give me a week of this. Let me see if I can live here and not get tired, bored, or overwhelmed. Then I can decide.”
	Chuckling, Arden popped one of the smaller stasis boxes, pulled out a granola bar wrapped in rice paper, and passed it to me. “Take your time. Sequoia isn’t going anywhere.”
