When I was first imprisoned in the bottle, four thousand years ago, he who created my prison allowed me to use magic mirrors to see the outside. I doubt that in his wildest dreams he ever imagined I would learn how to manipulate those mirrors and to see much further. Likely, he believed my limitations would drive me to yearn for release and long to serve someone with my magic. I used them to learn about the world around me.
        My prison always alerted me when a possible owner came near. Most mortals could not perceive my bottle, being unable to conceive of anything outside their own sadly limited world. Others could see the bottle, but they would see only something made of carnival glass. Only a very few had the potential to see more, and even then picking up my prison wasn’t enough. To free me, they would have to rub the bottle. 
        The newest potential owner came into the thrift store where my bottle sat with other glass decor, placed by a man who volunteered out of the goodness of his heart. I had hoped he might see the bottle for what it was, yet it was not to be; his partner and his collection of stuffed animals were the only magic he’d ever needed in his life.
        But then a woman picked up my bottle as if drawn to it, even under the harsh fluorescent lights of the chain thrift store. From reading the small rectangle of her identification as she presented it to the cashier, I learned her name was Lydia. It was likely something in her yearned for a more magical life. 
        The car ride was instructive. She muttered about “wasting money when rent was due” and lamenting that she’d have to buy ramen for the next week. Not once did she direct resentment at the bottle in the bag in her passenger seat, held in place by the seat belt, blue-green and swirled like an ocean entwined with kelp. She took great care bringing my prison into her apartment, nestled in bubble wrap and tissue. 
        My prison is indestructible by mortal hands, but her care was a balm to my existence.
        The bottle was given pride of place on the coffee table in the middle of the room, and Lydia put other purchases away before sitting in her computer chair and staring at my prison for a long moment.
        “I don’t know why, but….” she trailed off as she gently stroked the bottle, the act being sufficiently similar to rubbing it to set me free.
        “Greetings. I am named Alatriamedenaltavis, but you may call me Tria, owner.” Four thousand years, and I was at least allowed by my prison to give my owner a nickname.
        She recoiled and pulled her knees to her chest. I had taken human shape, close enough to my own: a curvy woman of middling stature with sea-green hair. Perhaps it was the shock of me appearing in her living room from nowhere? I had dressed appropriately for the time period, in a skirt and blouse that she might have worn herself, which I expected would put her more at ease. Perhaps, she was uncomfortable with my use of the term “owner,” but by the terms of my imprisonment, I could not refer to the person who released me as anything else unless they told me otherwise.
        After several moments of her hyperventilating, she managed to uncurl and slow her breathing. “Um. I guess you’re a genie or something? Just call me Lydia.”
        “As you ask, so shall I do. And while we do not call ourselves genies, it will serve to describe me. I am bound to serve you and grant you three wishes.”
        She spent the next several minutes swearing; I appreciated her use of invective, and even memorized some terms for later use. Interspersed with this, however, was a frustrated rant about how she couldn’t even keep a plant alive, what was she supposed to do with a person who also happened to be a genie?
        “Okay. There have to be rules, right? What are they? Or do I have to wish for that information?” As she stood then paced around the room, I studied her. Short brown hair, plain figure, glasses with chunky black frames, a gray sweatshirt declaring her fondness for chocolate by its chemical formula, simple black leggings, and sturdy black boots. 
        “There are indeed rules, and no, you do not have to wish for that. I am not allowed to affect the emotions of others without their consent. I cannot raise the dead. Parallel to that, I cannot undo the past. I cannot grant you more wishes.” 
        I paused, testing the boundaries of my imprisonment. “There are certain things I can advise you not to do. An example from my history is that you should not wish for me to cause the deaths of others, no matter how justified you, or I may believe it to be.” I struggled to say more, but could not tell her anything related to freeing me.
        She shuddered. “I’m so sorry you were put in that position. Hypothetically, what would happen if I did make that wish?”
        “In modern terms, it would be named the ‘law of unintended consequences’; even I cannot predict all the ripple effects of a death.” 
        “Hunh.” Lydia stared for a long moment at a picket sign propped on the wall, proclaiming her passion for human rights. “But I can’t just leave you bound like that,” she muttered, stalking around her living room.
        I noted that she caught my use of the word ‘bound’, but then my binding compelled me to ask, “Are you sure you wouldn’t like to have money? A more conventionally attractive form? Health and longevity?”
        She spun on me, glaring. “I’m guessing you’re stuck like this? I don’t care what you did, nobody deserves that. Unless you actually did something and this is penance, and even then there are better ways than forced servitude.”
        “Ah, no, own – um, Lydia.” I was startled enough to almost forget her instruction. “The magician who summoned me did so to make use of my magic, nothing more or less. He intended that once he was done with me, the bottle would find its way to others who could make use of it, and me.”
        Lydia stomped around some more, swearing about how some people gave humanity a bad name. Finally, she stopped and spun on me once more, narrowing her eyes.
        “I’m going to start with saying that while I do kinda want to look better, that’s not a wish I’m going to make. And yeah, I do need money, but again, not going to wish for it.” She grimaced. “It feels like exploiting you, and I’m really not comfortable with that, no matter what I might want.”
        “Your concern truly is for using me, and not the possible repercussions?”
        “Yeah. Even if you’re nice and don’t twist my words like in all the stories I’ve read. I’m guessing that me making wishes doesn’t actually help or free you.”
        I looked down at my feet. In four thousand years, only two others had ever considered my situation. I couldn’t tell her how to free me. The magician blocked that method of escape. But she could wish for me to tell her. I truly did want her to wish for that, even if I couldn’t tell her that she could.
        Meanwhile, she had gone to the bottle, my prison, and started looking it over. “I guess this wasn’t carnival glass after all. Hey, what’s this thing on the bottom? Looks like a maker’s mark or something.”
        I opened my mouth to speak, but no sound emerged. What a cruel binding by that magician! She’d found a clue and I could not tell her. But she did notice.
        “You can’t tell me, can you? Bet you it has to do with you being bound.” She rolled her eyes and swore a bit more before saying clearly, “I wish that you could and would tell me how to free you, the methods and means, and all the consequences.”
        Something loosened in my bindings, and I quickly said, “Don’t wish for my freedom, you’ll take my place and live a mortal lifespan inside that bottle. The magician also said that making an impossible wish would break the bottle and free me, but he never intended that to be possible. Finally, there is a key for my prison. It is in the form of a signet ring, and it fits the mark you noted, but it must be the specific key he created and not a copy. That is the only safe way to release me.”
        Lydia nodded. “He probably never thought of 3D printing, but yeah, there’s probably magic involved in the key.” She wandered around muttering some more, cradling the bottle like a precious thing, then asked, “With that first wish, does that include you telling me where the key is?”
        I blinked. “I… yes. I am allowed to tell you the key is buried beneath the foundations of the magician’s home, which is located near the border of the countries called Yemen and Oman in the present day. It is now a desert, which explains why it is undisturbed.”
        “Meaning, I can’t just go there myself. Am I permitted to ask you to retrieve it for me?”
        “I cannot touch the key myself,” I started sadly, only for her to roll her eyes again.
        “Because why would it be that easy,” Lydia muttered as she stomped off to another room (my magic told me it was her bedroom) and came back with a jar. “Look. If I have to wish for you to retrieve the key, I’ll do it, but unless the thing is idiotically big, it’ll probably fit in the jar. You go there, maneuver the jar around the key, close it off, and bring it back. Voilà, you didn’t touch the key at all.”
        “That’s… brilliant. Give me the jar. I can count the retrieval as a part of the first wish easily enough.”
        And truly, it took moments, most of those spent ensuring that the magician’s signet ring never made contact with me as I encapsulated it in the jar. Were I mortal, I would have been breathing far too quickly. My imprisonment was nearing its end.
        On my return, Lydia smiled at me. “Thank you. Let’s see here, I’m going to open this over a bowl. I don’t want desert sand all over my apartment.” She pulled the ring out of the mound of sand – a flat stone of the same color as my bottle, set into a heavy gold ring – and whistled. “This’d be worth a fair bit, but first things first. How do I unlock your prison?”
        I was capable of hyperventilating, it seemed, this close to freedom. “Align the marks on the ring with the bottom of the bottle, and once the ring is set, turn it clockwise a full three turns. And for whatever it is worth, I promise I will do you no harm once I am freed.”
        She grinned as she retrieved the bottle. “You’re kinda nice, I didn’t think you’d turn me inside out. Okay, here goes. Wait, clockwise relative to me, or to the bottle?”
        “Cunning. Your wish tells me that if you hold the neck of the bottle, so the bottom faces you, the ring should turn clockwise relative to you.”
        “Got it, and now to get you out of that prison.” She looked at me for confirmation at each step, and on the third revolution the bottle clicked, the stopper evaporated into mist, and the chains of my imprisonment dissolved around me.
        “Freedom,” I whispered, and Lydia – no longer my owner – put the bottle gently onto her table.
        “What now, Tria?” She smiled, but I saw her sadness for what it was. The adventure was about to leave her life, only a sea-green bottle and ring of unknown provenance as her souvenirs.
        Slowly, I answered, “I need, no, I want a guide to this time and place. And if you are interested, I would like for it to be you.”
        The woman who freed me blinked a bit. “Really?” She paused, and the grin came back to her face. “Yeah, I can do that. Just gotta figure out practicalities, but yeah.”
        I waved my hands, magic flowing, and the apartment gained another bedroom. “Magic triumphs over boring practicality, new roommate.”
        Lydia laughed. “Living with you, Tria, that’s going to be an adventure.”
