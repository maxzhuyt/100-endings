“Grendella is such an unusual name. Where are you from?”  Hrothmund awkwardly asked the striking woman who sat across from him. 
        They were sharing a mead in Heorot. Their date had been going badly. Hrothmund couldn’t quite put his finger on why the vibe was off, but it certainly was.
        Grendella was nervous. This was the first date she’d been on since committing to her human form. Her water-monster mother would never approve. However, her mother was gone. Grendella was free to make, and question, her own choices.
        Hrothmund had been planning this date for weeks. He had met Grendella by the port in town and hadn’t been able to stop thinking about her. He thought that Grendella would be impressed by Heorot. It was his go-to first date spot. All the other girls he had brought here had looked at the mead-hall with awe. The sheer size of the great hall was usually enough to impress the ladies. After some mead, he would show off his favorite woodwork designs, and then casually drop the fact that he had been the chief carpenter in charge of the renovation project.
        After all the bizarre tragedies that had taken place in the mead-hall last year, the king had paid him to “freshen the place up.” It had been tedious and sometimes gruesome work. The monster had not only killed thirty men, but he’d also made a horrific mess in the hall. When Hrothmund first accepted the job, Heorot was a gory disaster. However, Hrothmund was meticulous with his work. He’d gotten all the bloodstains out of the woodwork. He had also redesigned the layout of the main seating area and was proud of how much brighter and less cluttered the mead-hall now was. Overall, he’d done an admirable job of transforming it from a site of trauma into a joyful place to drink.
        Oddly, Grendella seemed far less impressed than anyone else he had brought to the Heorot. Hrothmund was surprised when Grendella looked around the hall with an air of disgust. Her normally beautiful face was screwed up, as if she had just gotten a whiff of something rotten.
        “I actually grew up near here,” Grendella said, her voice tight.
        “No way? It couldn’t have been in Hrothgar's domain. I would have noticed you earlier.” Hrothmund tried to deliver this line in a flirty way, but it didn’t quite land right.
        “No. I'm an outlander. I lived with my mom and brother a few leagues away in the eastern lake,” she said. “I mean, near the eastern lake,” she corrected herself.
        “Cool. Were you a fisherwoman?” he asked.
        “Kind of,” she responded. “I did some fishing, but I did more foraging. My mother did most of the hunting.”
        “Not your brother?” he asked.
        “Nah. My brother was a bum. He never did much work. All he was good at was getting into trouble,” she responded.
        “My brother is just like that too,” he said. 
        “Really? What kind of trouble does he get himself into?” Grendella asked.
        Hrothmund turned serious as he said, “Before all of that monster drama last year, he used to hang around here a bunch and serve mead to the nobles. He was always stirring the men up into boast battles. He was also pretty good at getting himself into bar fights. But, when all that crazy stuff went down last year, he disappeared. We all thought that he was one of the monster’s victims. It was awful. But then, five months later, my folks got a letter from a Geatland messenger. That loser was a stowaway on Beowulf’s boat and never even said goodbye.” As he spoke, he felt his temper rising in his throat. “I mean, it is one thing for him to abandon me. We always had a difficult relationship. But I still can’t believe that he did that to my mom.” Hrothmund’s voice started to rise in anger. “She thought he was dead. It drove her crazy.”
        Grendella looked at him. He could not read her expression. It was like no other expression he had ever seen, and yet he found something in it familiar. 
        “My brother died last year. And my mom went crazy when we lost him,” she said. Her tone was flat and unaffected, but Hrothmund saw tears in her eyes.
        “I am so sorry for your loss,” he said.
        “We were never close,” she responded. “I am more angry than I am sad. He made so many awful choices. Monstrous, really. He was a violent guy. We used to argue all the time, and to be honest, I was kind of scared of him. But I miss him. It is weird.”
        “I get that,” said Hrothmund. “When I found out that my brother was still alive and had just abandoned us, I almost wished he were dead. But that is not really true. I think I am so angry because I miss him.” 
        “Anger and grief. They can be hard to tell apart,” she said.
        “The anger feels monstrous. The grief feels human,” he said.
        “I think that this grief is making me feel more human,” Grendella responded, her voice softer now.
        “I think my anger is making me more monstrous,” said Hrothmund.
        “Maybe we are all a little bit human and a little bit monster.” Grendella looked right into Hrothmund’s eyes as she said this.
        He looked at her, really looked at her, as if for the first time. Who was this woman? She was unlike anyone he had ever met before. Both were silent for a long moment.
        “Can I get you another mead?” he asked.
        “Nah. This place freaks me out. Let’s get out of here,” she said, getting up from the long table. He took her by the arm and gently escorted her out of Heorot.
