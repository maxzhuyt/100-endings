Humans.
        They like to call those of us who aren’t human “monsters.” Yes, my claws mean that it’s difficult to use a touchscreen or turn a page in a physical book. But I never used them to harm humans, even when dragons were more common.
        But the world we live in now is entirely built for humans. The rest of us non-humans have had to figure out ways to adapt. The elves who stayed here in this world, they’re wilderness guides, park rangers, or other professions that limit their contact with iron and steel. The werewolves use moon tracking apps, form packs, and live in communal homes for mutual protection. I’d feel sorry for the vampires if they weren’t bloodsucking parasites. They need to feed on blood that’s free of foreign substances. Most humans use medicine that’s supposed to linger in the bloodstream, which means that even the simplest of painkillers makes their blood undrinkable.
        Dragons, though. There aren’t really that many of us left. We saw the writing on the wall, as it were, when the human known as Saint George started hunting us down. Most of us fled to other worlds. They recognized that some humans had trouble accepting anything that didn’t look like them, and even a small number of humans could be dangerous to dragons. 
        A few of us chose an extended form of hibernation, hoping that at some point, we wouldn’t be seen as a threat anymore.
        I’d always liked humans. Clever folk with endless curiosity and wonder at the world around them. Certainly, there were some humans who wanted to destroy anyone not like them; the rest were just trying to live their lives.
        Kind of like dragons, at least in those ways.
        By the human reckoning of time, I surfaced from my hibernation in March of 2015. My cave was isolated enough that no human had ever discovered it, or even if they had, they hadn’t gotten far enough in to find me. It took me several days to wake up to the point that I could check my defenses and confirm that I hadn’t been mistaken for an unusually shaped rock by an intrepid, exploring human.
        There was a care package, however. I’d made a deal with an elf with an unusual tolerance for iron to check in on me from time to time. I gave her a third of my hoard of valuables just before I went into hibernation, in exchange for her making sure that whenever I woke up, I’d be at least somewhat prepared for the world.
        Thankfully, the care package was nearly new. I guessed that she’d been to my cave sometime in the past five years, based on the dust that had accumulated. Her notes on the contents, and the state of the world, were extensive; it took me a few weeks to go through everything she’d written, and when I finished I was frankly amazed. 
        Humans had accomplished so much. They had created incredible ways to communicate with each other and record knowledge and expanded what they knew about the world, from extremely small things to incredibly large things and learned how to fight illnesses that would have devastated them when I was last awake.
        But they had also repeated the mistakes of the feudal governments I last remembered. They’d done terrible things to themselves in the name of “us, not them.” And they’d damaged the environment in ways that tempted me to go back to sleep and hope that in a century or two, things might be better.
        But Mirielle, being the clever elf that she is, had also equipped my cave with what I learned was called “the Internet.” Over the course of a couple of decades, she’d carefully built the infrastructure to ensure that when I woke and had absorbed everything she told me, I’d then be able to learn even more.
        I admit, I spent more than a month learning about what kind of world I’d woken up into. Some of that was catching up on what humans called “recent events” since Mirielle had last visited in 2013. Some of that was falling into so-called “rabbit holes” of information while losing hours clicking from link to link.
        And I found, again thanks to Mirielle, hidden websites, run by the non-humans for the non-humans. There were repositories of information detailing the experiences of the non-humans who’d stayed in this world and adapted to it, along with recommendations for newcomers and returning folk like myself.
        Mirielle visited after I established an email account, gave me a sum of human money in exchange for some coins from my hoard, and provided me with some suggestions on fitting in. Once she left, I started the process of changing my shape. It had always been a difficult and time-consuming process for dragons, best accomplished in privacy. By the end, I looked human, though I knew my silvery hair and sea-green eyes would likely make me stand out in any crowd.
        By June of 2015, I felt ready to attempt to rejoin the human world. Or at least as ready as I could be, given my nature. I had clothing chosen by Mirielle to blend in with the rest of the humans, sufficient modern money for necessities on a first trip, a smartphone with a number of useful apps, and a clear objective for my first venture into the modern human world — I would try to get coffee from a local place.
        The first difficulty was getting down from my cave. When I’d chosen this cave, I hadn’t considered that I might need to walk on two legs for any sizable distance. The nearest trail was a significant distance down from my cave, and I resolved to send Mirielle many thanks and a bit of my hoard for her wisdom in including hiking boots with my clothing. 
        By the time I was close to the trail, I’d added more to my intended gift. I’d forgotten mammals had the odd habit of sweating, and the athletic leggings and top I was wearing were not still wet the way wool would have been.
        I spent a fair bit of time watching the trail, waiting for a moment when I could get onto it without anyone noticing that I had been off the path. Perhaps the humans would not give it any thought, but perhaps they would. All I wanted in the moment was to blend in and not draw attention to myself. 
        But the humans walking along the trail were focused on their endeavors. One or two had dogs with them, which presented my second challenge: no matter how thorough the shift, certain animals can still smell the dragon underneath. So I had to wait for an opening in the flow of humans, and one where there wasn’t a dog.
        After a short amount of time, there was nobody on the trail ahead of or behind me. Exhaling in relief, I stepped onto the hard packed soil and began to casually walk toward what the signs assured me was the trailhead. 
        My first direct encounter with a human was someone dressed in shorts, hiking boots, and a jacket. The human waved at me but kept walking in the other direction. Clearly, nothing about me drew the hiker’s attention, and I grinned at my success. At least that part was going well enough.
        The third challenge was the parking lot. I’d never encountered asphalt before, and even though the parking lot was closed for the season, there were several people standing around, clearly waiting for something. I would usually stomp around to get a better feel of the material under my feet, but I was pretty sure humans didn’t really do that. 
        One of the humans approached me, curiosity in her eyes. She was dressed similarly to me, though her outfit was a vivid blue where mine was a more simple gray.
        “I didn’t see you on the bus?”
        Mirielle had explained that hikers took a bus to the trailhead from the local village and back again. I smiled without showing teeth – humans can be so uncomfortable at showing each other their teeth – and replied, “Oh, I got here early. I just spent some time sightseeing.” All of which was true, at least in some sense.
        She nodded, then looked at me more appreciatively. I’d chosen the form of a human woman in her early thirties, athletic but still curvy, and knew that I would be considered attractive by the current standards. This person seemed the sort to be attracted to women, which wasn’t an issue in my current form. I had no idea how she’d react to a dragon, though, and resolved to not let that secret come out.
        By the time the bus arrived, Jenna had introduced herself, told me a bit about her life, and seemed to be deciding whether she should verbalize her interest in me. Dragons are, on the whole, similar enough to humans in our orientations that I was open to the idea of pairing with a woman, a man, or anyone.
        I was purposefully vague about what I shared with Jenna. I mentioned taking an extended break after some travel supported by my investments. All true, though a bit vague about timeframe and other matters. 
        “So, Alix,” Jenna started near the end of the bus ride, and I knew she’d worked up the courage to ask for more of my company. “What are your plans for the rest of the day?”
        “Coffee first,” I said seriously, “but I didn’t really have plans after that.” I blinked slowly at her. “I’ve been enjoying your company so far. Would it be impolite to inquire after your plans?”
        I watched her blush. Such a fascinating mammalian response! And she hadn’t expressed any suspicion I wasn’t as human as her. I certainly wasn’t going to say, “Hello fellow human, I am also a human,” but I was still working on fitting in with the surrounding humans.
        After a moment, she coughed and said, “I’m meeting up for lunch at noon with a couple of friends I haven’t seen since high school.” I remembered the term for a place of education from my research, and nodded. “I don’t think they’ll be comfortable if I invite a stranger, but we could meet afterward?” Her blush deepened.
        Flirting! Another curious human behavior. Dragons were generally more direct in expressing our interest to each other.
        I replied, “Why don’t you come back to the coffee shop, say around four in the afternoon? Or is that not enough time to catch up with your friends?”
        Jenna laughed. “Four it is.” The bus squeaked to a stop, the village bustling with so many humans, the diversity was both impressive and distracting. She called, “I’m looking forward to it!” as she got off of the bus.
        I went to a coffee shop that Jenna had recommended, called The Bean Scene. The human behind the counter taking orders looked at me with some curiosity, but recommended a medium roast as one of their best. 
        I took the cup to an empty table, and resolved to watch the flow of humanity while I waited for Jenna. It was a new world, but I seemed to be fitting in well enough so far. There was so much to learn, so much to do, and so many new people to meet. I expected there would be challenges, but the thrill of adapting to this new flavor of humanity left me anticipating the future.
