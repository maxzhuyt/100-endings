I don’t believe in supernatural occurrences. I just don’t, but it still happened to me.
        It’s the job that led me to settle in New Orleans, and every house, indeed every building in the city that stood through the hurricane has a story. They’re all just stories, right? But there’s a heaviness to certain places, even a party destination like NOLA. I can’t explain it, and no, I still don’t believe it, even though it happened to me.
        “This one has a basement. That’s rare around here, but the Garden District is on a bit of a bluff, so nothing flooded too badly with Katrina. It’s been resealed several times since then with no leakage,” the real estate agent said. 
        “Not bad.” I looked around the kitchen. "The counters needed replacing, and this old intercom panel needs to be taken out, but otherwise it looks okay.” I pointed towards a door next to the pantry. “Does that go to the basement?”
        “Yes. It’s completely finished and adds another five hundred feet of floor space.” Clyde, the real estate agent, wore a dark green polo shirt and nondescript khaki pants. He opened the door to the basement and gestured for me to enter. “I’ll let you explore.”
        I hit the light at the top of the stairs and ventured down, careful to keep my hand on the rail. The steps felt pretty solid for basement stairs and creaked only a little. I clicked the second light switch on a panel when I reached the bottom. A weak light illuminated the main basement room, a bare space with a utility sink and a concrete floor. Two other doors led to other rooms. One hung wide open and appeared to be a workshop. I ventured inside to find a workbench and shelves lining one wall. A couple of metal shelving units stood against the opposite wall, housing only a couple of small jars of screws and nails.
        The other door off the main basement room was cracked. I crossed the space and moved to push the door open. It swung open. The room appeared empty, just more concrete floors and bare walls. An old intercom panel jutted slightly out of the wall next to the light switch. I moved to cross the threshold, and it felt as if I was being pushed away. Again, I attempted to enter, but it felt almost like a hand pressed against my chest, keeping me out of the room. My breath caught, but more out of surprise than fear.
        I tried again, and I almost felt a rebound effect, like I was pushed backwards. I felt a cold wave pass through my body, and the hair stood up on my arms.
        “Hey,” I called up the stairs to Clyde, “is there some kind of heating problem down here? I just got a chill.”
        The agent cleared his throat, almost a nervous sound. “Uhh, I believe everything is in working order, but we can add an HVAC allowance if you want to make an offer.”
        The normalcy of his words reassured me momentarily, and I moved to enter the room again, and this time I pushed past the weird shoving sensation and entered the room. The space itself appeared just like the rest of the basement, an empty room with recently painted off-white walls and a concrete floor. 
        That’s when I heard it. 
        No, I mean, no. I heard nothing because I don’t believe in supernatural nonsense. But I heard it all the same.
        “Don’t leave me here all alone. Please don’t leave.” A faint, small voice, possibly a child. As soon as I heard it, I questioned whether I had heard anything at all. It came out of nowhere and was just as suddenly gone.
        I exited the room and walked up the stairs calmly, as if nothing had happened.
        Clyde looked a little overly enthusiastic about selling me the place. “So, what’d ya think? Do you want to make an offer?”
        “Let’s keep looking. This place is a ‘no’ for me.” 
        That’s when I heard it again, faintly. “Stay with me.” It was like a whisper in the walls. I couldn’t tell if Clyde heard it or not. If he did, he didn’t acknowledge it. After that, I was more than happy to leave the house.
        Outside, the light of the late afternoon of the dreary October day had started to fade, and I reconsidered the prospect of looking at more houses.
        “Sorry, I do want to keep looking at places, but it’s probably better if we get a fresh start tomorrow. Do you have more places to show me?”
        “Yes, I’ve got lots. You sure you’re not interested in this house?” He seemed eager, which made me even less interested in the place. I felt the same chill as I had in the basement, but I was outside now, so I thought little of the shivery cold that passed through my body.
        I felt it again when I got in my car, so I readjusted my scarf and tucked it into the collar of my jacket.
        Back at my hotel, one of those extended-stay places near downtown, I took off my shoes and jacket and settled down to watch some news and check my phone for messages. 
        One of my texts came from “Unknown Number” and said, “Thanks for taking me with you. They never let me go out.”
        I shuddered. No, it couldn’t be. I responded simply, “Sorry, wrong number.”
        My heart jolted when the three dots appeared on my screen, indicating that someone was typing a message.
        “I’m here. You brought me with you. Thanks. They never let me go anywhere.”
        Someone was obviously messing with me. I tapped annoyedly into my phone, “Who is this?”
        The unknown messenger responded again. “It’s me, Millie. Can we go to the movies? I want to see the one about the girl from Kansas who goes to the place with the wizard!”
        I tapped at the screen again and said, “Okay, whoever this is can stop now. Is this some kind of joke?”
        The dots appeared again, and the words “No. Let’s go to the cinema. Please?”
        I turned off my messaging app—all of my messaging apps, as a matter of fact, but the word “please” showed up on my screen, repeated in three separate messages. I turned off notifications.
        The next day, before meeting Clyde again, I went to a phone store and showed them my messages, which now included ten unread messages from the same unknown caller. The phone company rep was baffled. “There’s no number for the other party. I can’t trace it, not even an IP address. They must be using some really sophisticated spoofing software.”
        I called Clyde to ask where we’d be meeting to start our day of real estate viewings, and I had to ask.
        “So this is going to sound weird, but is there something strange about that last house we looked at yesterday? Something about a kid, maybe?”
        Clyde let out an audible sigh over the phone. “I’ll level with you. That place has been in my inventory for a year. My sales record is great, but you’re right. The place is strange. Nobody comes up out of that basement wanting to buy the house.”
        “I certainly can understand that. What do you know about the room downstairs—not the workshop, the other one?” I felt the chill pass through me again.
        “I don’t know. There’s nothing in there. Nobody goes in it. They all say it’s cold, kinda like you did. The disclosure only goes back fifty years, but I heard that the daughter of the original owners had some kind of immune thing. They couldn’t touch her. Just brought her newspapers, books, and food. She died young, I think. She used the intercom to communicate with the family a lot, later the phone too.”
        But I went into the room. Everything in me told me not to go in there, but I pushed past it and went in anyway. The next day, I tried getting a new phone, even a new number, but Millie had gotten into the cloud, I guess.
        No, I don’t believe any of this. I felt ridiculous when I went back to my hotel on my last night in New Orleans and set up my phone with the camera facing the TV. Even more ridiculous when I pulled up the movie about the green wizard and the girl from Kansas and let it play. For her.
        As the credits rolled, my phone messages went active again. “Thank you. I’ve always wanted to see it. My brother told me all about it, but I never got to. Thank you.”
        I never got any more stray messages after that. I still don’t believe in supernatural occurrences. I just don’t, but it still happened to me.
