After Mom’s celebration of life ceremony, Ash and I stayed at the house to have a moment alone. Of course, we were both utterly drained from a long day of laughing, crying, telling stories, and hugging so many people.
        After everyone else cleared out and Ash’s husband took her sleeping kids home, Ash made eye contact with me and gestured towards the kitchen. We sat at the table, just me and my big sister. We each cracked a beer. Ash took off her shoes. I blew my nose.
        Ash took one of Mom’s old photo albums out of her bag. We had brought a whole stack of albums to the service. As she sipped, Ash and started absently flipping through the pages. Then, Ash asked me, “Who is this little lady with the silver hair?” She turned the book and showed me a photo of a distinctive, tiny, spritely looking woman.
        “No way! That’s Natalie!” I said.
        “Natalie?” she asked.
        “Wow. I had completely forgotten about her. Do you remember the woman I took the road trip with right before your wedding?” I asked.
        “You took this lady on a road trip before my wedding? What are you talking about?”
        “The off-the-grid mushroom lady, Natalie. Didn’t I ever tell you about her?”
        “You’ve definitely never told me about an off-the-grid mushroom lady,” Ash said, laughing.
        “I must have! She’s the one who makes her own fuel from the leftover restaurant oil. Remember?” I asked.
        “Is she in a weird cult or something?” Ash asked.
        “We are all in weird cults, whether we know it or not,” I laughed. “But Natalie’s great. I think you’d really like her.”
        “Wait, why did you take a road trip with her again?” Ash asked. 
        “Let me think… It must have been the spring of 2013. That’s when you got married, right?”
        “Yeah,” she said.
        “I was still finishing the anthropology master’s at NYU. I remember that I had to finish a huge group project right before getting ready to drive out to Milwaukee for your wedding. Right before I left, Mom asked me if I’d pick up her friend Natalie in upstate New York on my way and give her a ride to Chicago.”
        “Weird. That’s like a fourteen-hour drive, isn’t it?”
        “It was even longer. I thought it was a bizarre request. I mean, I’d never even heard Mom mention Natalie, and that’s a lot of time in a car with a random old lady I’d never met. I expected it to be super awkward. But you know Mom never asked for favors. Also, technically, I was still driving Mom’s car at the time, so I couldn’t really say no.”
        Ash snickered at me, “You never gave her that car back. You’re basically a car thief.”
        I laughed. “So Mom texted me these super specific directions to Natalie’s house. I told her just to give me an address, so I could put it in the GPS, but Mom told me that Natalie didn’t have an address.  I asked for Natalie’s phone number, so I could coordinate with her, but Mom said she didn’t have a phone! They had only communicated via letters that she sent to a P.O. box.”
        “Weird!” Ash said again.
        “If you think that’s weird, just wait. So, I follow these directions Mom sends me and I end up driving for, like, an hour on these little country roads in the Adirondacks. I finally found this amazing cottage. Ash, it looked like something out of a fairy tale! I remember it really clearly. It was a tiny little house painted bright canary yellow with a blue door. There were vines growing up the sides and wildflowers absolutely everywhere I looked. It was really beautiful. And, when I say she lived in the middle of nowhere, I mean, she was in the middle of nowhere! One tiny dirt path. No electrical wires. Just a house next to a pond and a sizable garden in a flowery glen in the woods. I knocked on the door and out came Natalie. She was wearing this frock kind of dress that definitely looked homemade and carrying a huge basket full of mushrooms. I can still remember the way they smelled. My car smelled like mushrooms for weeks after.”
        “You mean Mom’s car,” Ash snarked with a smile.
        “Mom’s car,” I said. “So, we started driving, and I have to tell you, we ended up having the best time. She was great. She told me all about living off-the-grid. She had some wild stories. This woman didn’t pay taxes. She foraged and had her own subsistence farm. Sometimes, she went weeks without talking to any other people. I think she was just excited to have an audience. Five minutes into the trip, she was already telling me about the medicinal properties of every mushroom in her basket. Apparently, she was on her way to some kind of mycology conference in Chicago.”
        “Wait. Like an academic conference?” Ash asked.
        “I think so?” I said, unsure.
        “If she didn’t even have power to her house, how could she coordinate all that?”
        “I don’t know. Mycologists are weird. But Natalie was surprisingly knowledgeable about data analysis. It was fascinating.”
        “Only you would want to listen to an old lady talk about mushrooms for fourteen hours,” Ash lovingly rolled her eyes at me.
        “I’m telling you, it was actually fun to hang with her. At one point, we stopped to get gas and I bought her a slushy. She lost her mind. She couldn’t stop talking about how amazing that slushy tasted. I don’t think she’d consumed anything with processed sugar in like a decade. After she drank it, she was buzzing. She started singing along with the radio, even though she didn’t know the words. It was hilarious.”
        “Sounds kind of annoying,” said Ash.
        “It’s hard to explain, but it really wasn’t. It was easy. So we finally got to Chicago really late that night. I dropped her off at an apartment building near the Loyola campus up in Rogers Park, and she went off, carrying her big box of mushrooms. And that’s the last time I saw her,” I told my sister.
        “Wait,” said Ash. “How did Mom know Natalie?”
        I tried to think, but my mind drew a blank.
        “I have no idea,” I told Ash.
        “You’re telling me that you spent a whole day in a car with this lady and never once asked how she knew our mother?” 
        “We mostly talked about mushrooms,” I said sheepishly.
        “And you never asked Mom?” Ash furrowed her brow at me.
        “I don’t think I did. I can’t believe I never talked about Natalie with Mom. What is wrong with me?” I felt a hot pressure in my chest.
        Ash put her hand on mine. “Mom was always a mystery, wasn’t she? There’s so much about her that we don’t understand.” Ash’s voice sounded far away.
        “Like how she was acquainted with an off-the-grid mycologist?” I asked.
        “Mom had a whole life outside of us, and we will never know about a lot of it,” Ash said, tears coming to her eyes.
        “Can I tell you something that Natalie told me about mushrooms?” I asked my sister.
        “Sure,” she said, her voice sounding small.
        “A mushroom is just the fruit of mycelium. The mycelium is this enormous, powerful network, woven through the soil. It is everywhere, under every step we take.”
        “Very poetic. What are you saying?” Ash asked.
        “Mom and Natalie, they were part of the same mycelium, a relational network. Some of that energy that was in Mom came from Natalie, and some of Natalie came from Mom.” There were tears in my eyes now too.
        “Some of Mom is in all of us,” Ash said.
        We were both quiet for a long beat, then Ash asked, “Do you think you could find it again?” 
        “Find what?” I asked.
        “That yellow house with the blue door in the upstate woods,” she said.
        “I don’t know. Maybe. Roadtrip?” I asked.
        She took my hand and said, “Roadtrip.”
