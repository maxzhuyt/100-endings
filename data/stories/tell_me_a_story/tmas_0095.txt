My family’s cabin in rural Colorado was my favorite part of winter when I was growing up, especially during our annual holiday vacation. It would snow for weeks by the time we’d arrive, and the impossibly long drive through icy mountain roads was always worth it when I saw how much snow we’d get to play in. I looked forward to two weeks chalk-full of cozy nights, snowball fights, and quality time with my dad and my little brother, Alex. I relished every moment there – apart from the nights the ghost family came to visit. 
        That’s a bit harsh. I didn’t have a problem with the ghost family themselves, it was just a difficult process to know how to accommodate them and how to act in their presence. My dad would give us a yearly reminder that they didn’t choose to visit us, the same way we didn’t choose to host them. If they had to come every year, we should at least make it a pleasant experience.
        As much effort as we’d put into it every year, it was never clear whether they were having a ‘pleasant experience’. They didn’t speak and never really changed expressions; they’d come, sit with us in the living room, float around the house, and eventually depart after midnight. They’d exit in the same way they’d entered, single file, in somber silence. The ghost family nearly mirrored ours: a father, a mother, a son, and a daughter. All that was missing on our end was our mother. 
        It was my mom’s family’s cabin, passed down through generations. She’d only come with us for one cabin vacation before she passed, the year she inherited the property. It was still our best visit that I can remember, even years later. It snowed that Christmas morning. She cooked us pancakes almost every morning and read us stories every night until we fell asleep.
        Returning to the cabin was its own difficult task. With it being her family’s cabin, her absence was impossible to overlook—especially since she’d been the one to instruct us on how to “properly” host the ghosts our first time visiting as a family. She never shared the reason behind these annual visits, insisting we’d have to wait until we were older. In the meantime, we were old enough to learn about ghosts.
        They knocked three times on our door the night of December twenty-eighth. That first night, my mother opened the door wide and greeted them as if they were neighbors or extended family. Dad would go on to do the same for the rest of our vacations.
        I’ll never forget how scared I was that first night, frozen in fear at my mom’s side, watching the vaporous outline of their legs float motionlessly past us single-file. They were all a pale, translucent blue with a dim glow. They each looked equally distressed, their expressions ranging from terrified to mournful.
        The ghost of the father, with a long brown beard covered in thick clumps of ice that never melted, would always sit on our leather couch. He’d stay there for hours on end, staring into the wall, sometimes groaning in low, grievous moans. The mother would float back and forth through the living room, eyebrows knit in deep frustration, occasionally wringing her quivering hands as she paced. The son and daughter sat listless on the floor, but sometimes moved around nine or ten o’clock to sit beside their dad.
        The children’s faces were the hardest to look at, maybe since in our first year there, I was too young to comprehend why they couldn’t cheer up. 
        It’s the holidays, I thought. I wondered if they’d be happier if Alex and I tried to play with them.
        We’d never get to try. The two of us were instructed to sit in silence while the ghosts were around.
        While we’d had quiet moments as a family alone while reading or watching television, the atmosphere of being surrounded by ghosts was not only physically colder, but oppressively melancholy. As a six-year-old at the time, sitting around was the last thing I wanted to do for an entire evening.
        I remember my mother insisting that we couldn’t read, watch TV, or even talk during their visit. We had to honor their grief, she said. These rules were nearly impossible to accept for my three-year-old brother and me. We were given constant shushes and often pulled into other rooms to have their reasoning re-explained to us. 
        “They’re grieving,” my mom had told me. “They’re really, really sad.” 
        I didn’t know what grief or grieving was, but I couldn’t imagine it’d be important enough to waste an entire night sitting and doing nothing. In the unavoidable ennui, I envisioned myself rising up from my body as a ghost, joining the other family with an eternally bored expression. 
        Far too soon, I’d learn about grief. While I was very young when my mom passed, I’ll never forget the confusion and the tears of adjustment. Returning to the cabin as if everything were normal was impossible; during some of our happiest moments, my brother and I would grow suddenly plaintive and depressed. Her absence was painful and pervasive, but we’d slowly learn to accept the change.                 
        Sometimes, it helped to sit in the silence of our sadness like ghosts.
        Despite my increased understanding of the ghost family’s struggle, I would come to resent their visits. It was hard enough at times to be in our mom’s family cabin without her. The last thing we needed was an entire evening of our annual two-week vacation dedicated to sitting with ghosts in unbearable silence. 
        Even when we’d done our best to enjoy ourselves, their arrival would spoil the moment. Years later, the three of us were in the middle of a round of rummy, a card game I wasn’t very good at. I was finally starting to win when I heard their loud knock. They’d come at the same time every year, but even if I were to forget their annual visit, the weight of the knock sounded in such a heavy, ominous echo that it could never be mistaken for anyone else.
        “Why do they have to come tonight?” I cried in my sudden agitation, and quickly, dad put his finger to his lips, throwing his playing cards in the discard pile as he went to answer the door.
        I remember tears prickling the corners of my eyes, so frustrated that our wonderful night would be snuffed out by these unhappy visitors. Why did they keep coming back if they didn’t even want to be there? By our early teens, Alex and I still weren’t told the reason for their annual visits, we only knew for certain that something awful happened.
        I would’ve tried to reason with my father, but he told me we should respect them and keep the necessary silence. He echoed our mother’s words.
        “They’re grieving,” he’d say. “They’ve gone through a lot.”
        I wanted to help them. The rare times I’d try talking to them, whenever dad reluctantly permitted it, they never responded. It seemed like they couldn’t really see us, appearing only vaguely aware of our presence.
        Two more years went by, and finally I decided to take my chance on altering our family tradition, but only after I’d learned the truth about their tragedy. Having both reached our teens, Alex and I asked again what the family’s story was. Finally, Dad shared all that he knew. 
        In the late eighteen-hundreds, the family had left their home to travel northbound, headed towards family in Wyoming. They unknowingly embarked right before one of the worst blizzards in Colorado history. Their damaged wagon was discovered near the cabin, though finding shelter hadn’t been enough to save them from the brutality of the storm. 
        Every year they return on the alleged night of the wagon crash, always at seven thirty-three, seemingly to re-enact their evening of terrifying uncertainty. My mom’s side of the family theorized that this was the only way the ghosts could process the tragedy, and instructed our family to continue hosting them so they could do so.
        While I wanted to delicately suggest that their process wasn’t working, my father didn’t take well to bad attitudes about the ghost family. I understood the need for our reverence. After all, it was only once a year that we had to see them. Still, I began to raise the argument that we should go about our lives as normal when they came to visit.
        Dad was never sold. He was always convinced it might anger or distress the ghosts if we were to celebrate in front of them. 
        “They might like it!” I insisted. “Maybe they’d enjoy watching us have fun or even try to join in. It could remind them of the better times they shared. Has anyone ever tried?”
        My father, running a hand through his thinning brown hair, shook his head. It was only a few hours until their annual arrival. 
        “I don’t know, hon. Your mother always said we shouldn’t. I’m not sure what we’d do if we provoked them.”
         “We’ll just talk amongst ourselves or play a game. I think if we just minded our own business, they’d do the same. They don’t seem to notice us as it is.”
        Alex interjected an agreeable ‘yeah!' after tuning into the conversation from his place on the leather couch. Dad looked at him, then glanced back at me. 
        While I could tell he was unconvinced, I watched him shrug.
        “I mean, we can try it,” he finally agreed. “But if anything bad happens, we stop right then and there.” 
        That evening, we ate dinner, played games, and joked around with abandon. When we heard that familiar knock, we let them in while “Silent Night” played softly in the background from my speaker. The ghost family didn’t seem to notice the difference, and I grew increasingly hopeful as the minutes passed.
         We kept our conversation going. For a while, we were talking about Mom.
        “Mom was so bad at rummy,” Dad remembered with a smile. “She needed a million refreshers every time on how the game was played, but they never did her any good.”
        Alex and I laughed, taking turns removing ornaments from the Christmas tree and putting them in a large cardboard box. “Sometimes I’d let her win,” Alex admitted, “Just to get her mood back up. She’d get so annoyed!”
        The ghosts sat in their same positions, but they didn’t seem as brooding anymore. It looked like they were simply staying quiet. The mother, though she paced back and forth between us and Dad, didn’t wring her hands. I wondered if I was simply hoping they were less somber, but I reminded myself to try not to focus on the ghosts.
        After Alex and I finished, we all played a round of rummy, and I felt our group atmosphere relax again. We were laughing and reminiscing as if we were all alone. 
        To the aghast groans of my brother and dad, I finally won the round. When I turned around to check on the other family, I noticed that they’d slightly faded in their glow. The mother, now no longer pacing, sat beside the rest of her family on the leather couch. I'd never seen her do that before. 
        We reshuffled the cards, and I waited before turning back around to look at them closely. I squinted to discern the lines of their ghostly bodies.
        Though faint, I could make out their changed expressions. Ever so slightly, they were smiling.
