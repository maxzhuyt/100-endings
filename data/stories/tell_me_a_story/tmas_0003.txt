Katya sighed at the butter churn in front of her, but kept at her work. Her family was well off enough to have cows, goats, and sheep – along with enough relatives to fill a farmstead and then some. Everyone who was able worked in every part of the dairy farm, from milking to making. Once you could hold a pail steady for someone else, you were expected to contribute.  Even if you broke an arm, you could still teach the unoccupied youngsters, keep records, or handle trade with other families.
        Katya’s family had been dairy farmers for centuries, back when Mirsk was a village and had not yet grown into a town. A few generations back, her blessed great-great-grandmother had invested in a cellar, the better to store products to keep them from going bad. At that point, the town elders had decreed that all of her family would focus on dairy for the townsfolk.
        It was like that in her town; indeed, it was like that in their entire nation. Folk would be born into a family with a profession, and would spend their lives in that profession, because tradition dictated it. Jorgen was a blacksmith because his mother had been, and her father, and so on. Misha the baker had a similar story, and so did everyone in town.
        “Stability and tradition,” the elders often said, “are the cornerstones of our town of Mirsk, and our nation of Byelobrad. To change things is to risk famine, or worse.”
        Winters were long, growing seasons short, and while there was grazing for the animals, it was a roll of the dice whether there would be enough grazing for the animals to produce butter and cream for everyone in town, or they’d make do with milk during the lean months. 
        Katya – like all the children of Byelobrad – had learned how to read, write, and count. She loved her family, but she loved writing and reading nearly as much, and more so than the goats or cows. But she hadn’t been born to the scholars in Mirsk, so in the eyes of the elders, what she wanted to do didn’t matter.
        “Katya!” her aunt Kisa barked. “That butter won’t churn itself. Where’s your head now?” Kisa wasn’t exactly the nicest person in the family, but she’d been in charge of making the butter now for a decade, and in her domain, she was empress.
        “Sorry, Aunt Kisa,” Katya said, ducking her head and getting back into the rhythm of churning. 
        Katya could feel her aunt narrowing her eyes, though Katya was focused on the churn. Unless something distracted Kisa soon, Katya’s aunt would unload whatever angry bile she’d stored up, and Katya would most likely be the target.
        “How was my writing for the recipe for the sheep’s butter, Aunt Kisa?” Katya had the best handwriting of any in the family, and Grandmother Lukya decreed that she would be the one to write down all the family’s records, in case another plague struck the town.
        A moment passed, then two. Kisa grudgingly allowed, “Legible. Concise. Accurate.” She then turned away from Katya, muttering but clearly not ready to begin a tirade against her niece. Instead, she turned her vitriol on a churn that had cracked a few days ago, swearing at the inanimate wood about needing to buy a new churn since it had been so faithless as to break while she was making a batch of the sheep’s butter.
        Katya was about to quietly breathe a sigh of relief when the door to the buttery opened, and Misha, her youngest nephew, called out, “A crier’s going round, someone’s come to visit the town! The elders want everyone to meet in the town square!” As he closed the door, he called out, “Gotta tell the rest of the family!”
        Kisa planted her hands on her hips, and announced, “Well, no helping this. Get done with what you’re doing, and we’ll gather the family. The elders probably want an assembly.” One by one, they finished as best as they were able, stored butter and buttermilk in sealed vats, and gathered near the road where the rest of Katya’s family had already assembled. Together, the twenty-six of them walked the short distance into town. Gossip was already flying fast among the adults as to why the elders would have called a meeting now, instead of waiting until the next rest day.
        Katya was old enough to walk with the adults, though she spent most of her time with them listening to speculation that ranged from the faintly worrisome to the mostly absurd. The emperor hadn’t called for a tax increase in a few years, Katya knew, and wasn’t likely to. Also, dragons hadn’t been sighted in a century, so Katya wondered why Uncle Vanyes would bring up the subject. She resolved to keep her eyes, ears, and mind open, and her mouth shut. Someone would probably need to remember and record the events of the assembly for her family.
        The town square was nearly full to capacity, but tradition required every family to have room to stand or sit as needed. Katya yielded her seat to Aunt Kisa, who arched an eyebrow but sat without complaint. “The family will need someone to see and listen,” she explained, and watched Kisa’s eyebrows briefly fly upward, then down into a frown.
        “You think this is more than just taxes?” her aunt asked.
        “How often has this happened, Aunt Kisa? Like this, with all the townsfolk here? And there’s a strange woman pacing the stage. She’s not in the emperor’s colors, and there’s that curious staff she doesn’t seem to be using to help her walk.”
        Kisa sighed and nodded. “You’ve got a brain, child. Most likely this won’t affect us, since everyone needs milk and butter. But keep your eyes open. We don’t want surprises.” 
        Katya silently reminded herself that to Aunt Kisa, everyone was a child, whether they’d seen twenty summers, or forty, or sixty. The woman had given her a set of fountain pens for her twenty-third name day last year! But there was no use arguing with Kisa, so instead she turned her attention to the woman walking around the stage. The woman kept looking at the crowd, seeming interested in everyone there.
        Katya was a bit too far from the stage to make out any fine detail; the woman seemed older than her but younger than her Aunt Kisa, certainly not old enough to need a staff to help her walk. Her clothes were fancy enough that Katya would have taken her for a merchant, but something about them seemed different in a way that made Katya long to get a closer look.
        The town elders filed onto the stage and waited for the crowd to fall silent. Elder Krenik raised his hands, and announced, “Our visitor today is named Lisbet! You will give her your undivided attention!”
        She’s not from Byelobrad, not with a name like that, Katya thought. And the elders should have said her profession.
        The woman stepped forward and bowed formally to the townsfolk. “Greetings to you, folk of Mirsk.” Her lightly accented voice easily reached the far corners of the town square, even though Katya was certain that she wasn’t shouting. “I come to you first from the nation of Oleppa to the west, and more recently from Emperor Nikholai’s court. He has granted me a writ of access for all the towns and cities in Byelobrad, to come to you and offer a chance to take on a new profession.”
        Katya heard the entire crowd gasp. New professions were unheard of, at least within the past century or more. The last one Katya knew for sure had been approved was associated with the printing presses that had come to Byelobrad a century and a half ago.
        Lisbet waited for the crowd to quiet down, and continued. “I know this comes as a shock, but I ask you to hear me out. When I went to the Emperor, he heard my request and granted it without hesitation.” 
        The crowd began murmuring, and Katya felt a bit faint. Like everyone, she had read the official histories. Immediate approval by the Emperor or Empress had happened fewer than five times in all of Byelobrad’s existence. Whatever this profession was, the Emperor clearly thought it was important enough to upset long-standing tradition.
        Lisbet smiled. “And so I come to you, Mirsk, first on my journey though other cities and towns were closer.” She paused to let this sink in. Katya wondered with a touch of cynicism whether it was true or simply showmanship.
        “Behold!” Lisbet announced, and lifted her staff so that it pointed into the air. A bluish-green ball of light sprang into existence above the woman, and the outcry from the townsfolk was enough to make Katya clap her hands over her ears. 
        Even through the noise of hundreds of people, Lisbet could still be heard. “Magic is real! Many have studied it, learned how it works, and learned how to work with it. And now I bring this opportunity to you, citizens of Mirsk!” She spun the tip of her staff in a tight circle, and the ball of light flew out across the crowd, moving well above their heads. “Do not fear, this is a spell of seeking! It will find those who have the potential to join this new profession, ones the Emperor has allowed me to call magicians.”
        While Lisbet spoke, the ball stopped several times, floated for a moment above certain people’s heads, and left a bit of itself behind. Maxim, the youngest of his family of bakers, shrieked in joy as the light marked him. Ilsa, one of the older bankers, very nearly fainted. One by one, the light stopped above the heads of people young and old, and marked them as having potential.
        And Katya could not find any surprise when the light marked her.
        She was the last; fifteen people of Mirsk had been marked, and all of them were caught up in emotions ranging from shock and fear to delight and excitement. Katya stared up at the light bobbing above her head. It reminded her of a painting she saw once, showing a great expanse of water that people called an ocean.
        “Hear me now, people of Mirsk,” Lisbet said over the rumblings from various townsfolk. “This indicates potential only. It does not mean that any of your people can immediately do magic. It takes a great deal of training to open up to the flows, much less do anything with them. Your Emperor has granted me one last boon: the choice. Those who have been marked may choose whether to stay in their profession or come with me, with no consequences for the people or their families levied by the elders or the Emperor.”
        The crowd went almost perfectly still, with the only sound coming from the miller Gretl’s newborn babe, yowling in hunger. It was one thing to be chosen for a new profession, but tradition demanded that a family be compensated in some way for the loss. Katya knew the stories of how families were punished for breaking tradition, even if the Emperor himself approved.
        Katya looked to her Aunt Kisa. The woman stared at her with a flat expression, and Katya mentally resigned herself to staying with her family and tending the dairy farm to the end of her days.
        Then her aunt shook her head and waved her hands, shooing Katya toward the stage. Into the growing sounds of the townsfolk talking at each other, she said, “Your mother would be proud of you. Just see if Lisbet will let you finish those records for us, yes?”
