My mama, Emma Louise, died in childbirth with me. 1955. Delbert, Texas. We didn’t have a hospital close by, and it all came on so suddenly. My aunt Arlene was helping Mama with me like she had helped with all my siblings before me, but there wasn’t a thing Arlene or anybody else could do for her. The doctor finally got to the house, but Mama had lost too much blood. My older brothers and sisters hated me for it. They told me it was my fault, that I took their mama away from them. As if she wasn’t my mama too. Daddy didn’t love me all that much either, but he loved our mama, and so he tolerated me. Some of the time, anyway.
        Every once in a while, he would talk about her when I was little, mostly at night. I had to be under six when he told me because Stepmother wasn’t with us yet. She didn’t like hearing about her competition, I guess, so he didn’t talk about our mama much more after that.
        “She loved goin’ to church, loved singin’ the hymns. She wanted one of y’all boys to be a preacher. Your two brothers are older and joined the Air Force, so it has to be you, son.”
        It was true. I think they thought Mama couldn’t have any more babies, but then there she was, pregnant with me. My sister Loudean is the closest to me in age, and she was five when I was born. She remembered Mama the least, so she was the nicest one to me. Loudean more or less raised me, truth be told. I’m twenty-three now, and she still buys me clothes when Tim’s Men’s Store in town has a sale. Charlie, her husband, owns a used car lot and doesn’t mind her soft spot for me. He let me work there in the summers, so I could go to college. I cleaned and polished cars, swept up around the shop, helped Sam the parts man when he needed me to fetch things. I studied business at school, and Charlie even started letting me do some of the books for him once I got up into college.
        A thing happens when you tell a little boy that his dead mama’s wish was for him to grow up to be some certain thing. It takes root. It becomes a part of him. Or at least it did for me. I never questioned my future after that. I was a stubborn little fella, and I decided that I would figure out how to be a preacher and then be one. One thing I had going for me was that I was good in school, as I found out not long after Daddy told me about Mama’s wish.
        I was in the second grade when I stopped to talk to our preacher one Sunday after service when he was shaking hands with everybody as they left.
        “Brother James, sir. Could you tell me something?”
        “What is it, son?” He was a kindly man, much gentler than my daddy, to be honest.
        “How do you get to be a preacher?”
        “Well, Marty, you gotta love the Lord, and I know you do.” I nodded. “And you’ve gotta go to college these days, so you can go to seminary. You can’t just get ordained. You need real training on how to run a church and minister to people.”
        That sounded daunting, but I pressed on anyway. “What’s a seminary?”
        “Oh, that’s a special school where you study the Good Book and learn how to give sermons and talk to people when life’s got them down.”
        And that’s how I ended up where I am now, at Greens Chapel in Cowtown, Texas, population 788. It’s a summer fill-in job while they find a new preacher. Brother James never told me what seminary was gonna be like. He never went, I guess. I’ve been there one year, and I've learned so much about the Good Book, like he told me I would. But he didn’t know what they teach at the seminary. I learned about all the translations, the different manuscripts, and the parts that some committee in the Middle Ages decided not to include. I never thought about how our holy book came to be; I guess I just thought it fell out of the sky. But it turns out, it was written down by people.
        All of that got me questioning, though. I wonder what kinda deity would start a little boy off in life with no mama, or let my mama die when she had five kids, three of them still at home. It’s a big universe, and I was just a little boy. I don’t know what I believe anymore, to be honest. And here I am supposed to be the preacher for the summer, to tell these people something about living their lives right.
        They set me up in a little room in old Miss Markum’s house. She’s a retired teacher and a longtime member of the church. On my first day, alone in the tiny church office, I got a call from a Mr. Davis, whose little girl is bad sick with some kind of blood cancer. I don’t know what omnipotent being would let a baby girl suffer like that, but they called me. 
        I don’t know what I believe anymore, to tell you the truth. But they need me to pray over little Anna, so that’s what I’m gonna do. The universe is too big for a sick little girl, and she needs me to make it smaller for her. And if I don’t give her that comfort, who will?
        I know what I’m here to do now.
