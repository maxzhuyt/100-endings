Irx and Diwo ran from behind one pile of rubble, leaving the shadow of an ancient pillar. They crossed the narrow meadow quickly to the next hill of debris.
        “Watch for Vampire Moths,” Irx said quietly. 
        Diwo looked up. Mother Fire still looked down on them from high in the sky, warming Father Earth and all the living things. “The blood drinkers do not usually show themselves until after Mother Fire has gone to look after her other kennels,” he said, panting loudly. 
        “Sit,” the older fetcher said. 
        Diwo crouched and sniffed. Sometimes the junk they came across contained more than just broken rocks. Occasionally, he found something interesting. 
        I have a nice collection under my bed.
        A colored shape or a strange shard of earth that didn’t break or chip but bent easily.
        Some things don’t come from Father Earth.
        Today they were out hunting for leafy greens. 
        Lettuce, maybe some spinach. Irx doesn’t like to take the time, but if I can find carrots, I’ll dig for them.
        Diwo pointed to an open area between two ancient pillars that had only partially fallen. “That looks like a good place,” the young fetcher said. A place they had not been before. The grasses were tall, and the purple, white and yellow flowers were bright in the midday light.
        Irx stared into the sky for another minute. ”Let’s go,” he growled.
        The pair dashed across the open space. 
        Halfway across, Diwo froze for a moment and looked up. 
        Did a shadow fall across the ground? 
        Nothing in the sky. A streak of clouds. He ran to catch up.
        There were fields of leafy greens there, growing wild. 
        Lettuce, cabbage, even spinach! Beets...  ew, I don’t like beets. And trees? Is that? It is! A nectarine tree! Diwo ran to it and breathed in deeply. Nectarines, all over the ground! He chomped down on one that didn’t look too bruised. He moaned with delight as the sweet juice filled his mouth.
        “Hey!” a voice called out. “That one’s mine!”
        Diwo looked up. He mumbled something around a mouthful of fruit.
        “I don’t understand you,” the figure said, shadowed in the branches of the tree. It jumped to the ground, landed gracefully and two-stepped over to a scattering of fruit nearby.
        Diwo spit out the fruit in his mouth. “I said, I didn’t know anyone was here.”
        “Well,” said the gatherer. “I am here. Prrrrrr. My name is Sabra.” She smiled and blinked rapidly as she collected the nectarines. Hands with sharp claws retrieved the fruit quickly and deposited them in a leather pouch tied around her waist. Still looking at Diwo, she licked the back of her hands, long claws retracting. 
        Diwo realized he was staring at a female. “I am Diwo.”
        Sabra’s long tail twitched. “Nice to meet you.” Her eyes darted around. “Where is the rest of your kennel, prrrrr?”
        That sound made the hair on his back stand up. Excitement? Fear? Diwo did not know. “Oh, we have traveled most of the morning. We are looking for new territory.”
        Sabra’s face twisted, and her eyes grew wide. “This place is mine.”
        Diwo noticed that Sabra’s long tail curled and moved with complexity. What was the word he had heard? Prehensile?
        Diwo spent a moment regarding his own stubby tail which did little, when a sound at the far end of the valley of rock pillars drew his attention. A strange sound started, then it ended just as quickly. Sabra also stared in that direction, motionless, listening.
        “Diwo!”
        Diwo looked around and saw Irx running towards them from across the field, carrying a sack of leafy greens.
        “Diwo, run!”
        Diwo looked over at Sabra with alarm. “Where is the rest of your clowder?”
        Sabra hissed and stuffed a few more nectarines in her pouch before tying it. “I prefer to hunt alone!” 
        “Diwo!” Irx was halfway across the field when a bolt of lightning shot out, and the ground exploded around him. 
        “Irx!” Diwo started to run to him, but Sabra grabbed his leg.
        “No! This way!”
        “But Irx!” Diwo shouted.
        “Irx is gone. Come on!”
        The ground trembled, and another bolt struck the nectarine tree, splitting the top in two. Diwo ran to follow Sabra. They fled into a gap in one of the pillars. 
        Diwo hesitated and looked at the concrete and stone that rose so high he couldn’t see the top. Straight into the sky. 
        “Come on!” Sabra urged.
        Diwo took one more look back. He could see where Irx lay, unmoving. He could smell blood and death. Then he slipped inside.

        Stomp adjusted the shard over his right shoulder. The armored battle suit should have fit together seamlessly, but the right shoulder plate never fit correctly. 
        The damn thing is too tight.
        He adjusted the strap around his rail gun so it rubbed against the loose shard a bit less, and he scanned the open field. 
        A second battle suit walked by, the micro-engines in the legs and arms humming to make the stride seem effortless.
        Stomp’s voice crackled over the intercom to his partner, Thumper. “See anything?” 
        Thumper flexed his proboscis back and forth above his head with an all-clear signal.
        They walked another five meters before Thumper’s trunk stood straight up. 
        Stomp saw it then. The body lay in the smoking crater.
        “These are primitives. They’re still living in caves out here,” Thumper scoffed.
        “Yeah, well, our job is to clear it out.” Stomp took the rail gun off his shoulder and held it at his side. That feels better. “We’ll take down these structures with charges and clear the rubble to atoms from the air. By this time tomorrow, there will be a new highway coming straight through here. The herd will have a new city to build.” He trumpeted proudly.
        Thumper looked around. “All these tall stone monoliths were built by monkeys?”
        Stomp looked at him with irritation. “They were called buildings. Built by humans.”
        Thumper shrugged. “Whatever. They’re dead now.” He took a bag from around his shoulder, opened it, and took out a blast disc.
        “They’re not dead. They left.”
        “Same thing.” Thumper tossed the disc into the air. It sailed and stuck to the face of a nearby building, worn by time down to its concrete and rock base. He threw another in the opposite direction. It stuck to the face of another building.
        “Ready?” Stomp asked.
        “Sure,” Thumper said. He threw his sack over his shoulder. “Say, did you see what I saw? Those two running into the — what did you call it — building?”
        “Yeah. It's better we clear this place out. Primitives like that can spread disease.”
        Thumper’s proboscis twitched in disgust. “Dogs and cats living together. Mass hysteria.”
