No dew drops would form on the crops this morning. Piter knew better than to hope for rain. It had been weeks since he had seen growth on the stalks. Too many moons came and went since a proper harvest had been reaped. 
        On the morrow, Piter would be forced to travel into town and beg for corn and wheat, so that he and his wife, Mary Jo, could have something other than fresh eggs and goat milk. He had done this only once, and the townsfolk had been kind enough to spare some grains and fruit, but it was the principle of the matter that did not sit well with Piter. He was a prideful man who felt great shame in asking others for something he ought to already have. Still, the drought and the return of the pestilent locust had left him no choice.
        Piter ran his fingers through the soft fur of his tabby cat, Thomasin. She purred as the sun crept over the horizon. 
        Mary Jo joined Piter by the fence he had built ten years ago when they picked this cursed plot of land to build their lives. She brought him a cup of herbal tea she had concocted.  The fragrant spices woke up Piter’s tastebuds. He drank and found his worries calmed. She rubbed his hunched shoulders and whispered in his ear, “We will find a way. We always do.” 
        Piter found comfort in his wife’s confidence, although he did not share her optimism. Winter was only two months away and if they did not harvest crops before the weather turned, they might not make it through to see the spring. As he sipped the tea, his mind drifted from present and future anxieties back into memories he and Mary Jo had made since settling in this strange new land.
        He began to grow sleepy as he remembered the times they spent building their cabin, herding their goats, the birth of their child, Neville. Neville had been a blessing to their lives. Oh, how Piter missed his baby boy. The thought of his disappearance made Piter's knees weak. 
        Mary Jo caught him under the arm and helped him into the cabin by the fireplace. She tucked blankets around him and brewed another cup of the herbal tea. After some time, Piter drifted off to sleep, an unusual occurrence this early in the morning. 
        When he awoke, it was nearly midday. As he stepped outside, he heard the unmistakable rumble of distant thunder. Sure enough, on the horizon, dark storm clouds were rolling in. 
        “What a stroke of luck…” Piter marveled to himself. “Mary Jo,” he called out, “Mary Jo, it’s a miracle!” But Mary Jo was not to be found. Piter checked in the stable, behind the woodpile, even back inside, but she was not there. He did his best not to worry, but as time passed and the dark rain clouds grew closer, he could not help himself from growing concerned.
        To distract his mind, Piter rounded up the animals. First, the goats, then the chickens, and finally the tabby cat. He called out for the cat, “Thomasin! Here kitty cat…” But after a few minutes without finding the cat, Piter abandoned that as well.
        In one final attempt to put his mind to ease, he packed his pipe with tobacco and struck a match. Before he could puff it, he stopped at the sound of branches snapping. He whipped his head around. Mary Jo emerged from the tree line, clothes dirty and barefoot. There were noticeable specks of blood on her smock. 
        Piter ran to her. “What is it? Is there something after you? What has happened to you?”
        “I’m fine, Piter. We’re going to be fine. I'll clean up and rest now.” She brushed Piter’s hands off and staggered toward the cabin. 
        Piter was speechless. He watched her go inside, and then he turned back to the densely wooded forest. Piter had hunted squirrels and rabbits there in the early days of moving here, but it was treacherous to maneuver through the thick brush, so he had abandoned that long ago. 
        That night, Piter fried eggs for dinner as it poured rain outside. He offered some to Mary Jo, but she claimed she was not hungry and that she would be happier and hungrier once the crops grew from this downpour of rain. This could not be true; she had not eaten in almost two days. He asked urgently, “What were you doing in the forest today?” 
        She dodged the question, pulling out her book of fairy tales that she used to read to Neville when he was just a baby. Piter had always felt the stories were too dark for such a young boy. Now, Mary Jo read them from time to time when she was thinking of Neville.
        Piter wanted to know what she was keeping from him. “Tell me what it was that you did in the forest today. I know it was no coincidence that I dozed off at such an early hour. Did you poison me then?”
        She responded without looking up from the book, “I would never do such a thing to someone I love.”
        “Do you lie to my face?”
        “I do not.”
        Piter fumed. He snatched the book from her hands. “Why is it that you keep secrets from me? What is it that you are hiding?” She stared back at him with icy eyes. Lightning illuminated the room. He continued pressing her, “Tell me what it is you are hiding! I command you!” 
        “I will tell you nothing, for I hide nothing.”
        “Did you take Thomasin the cat into the forest with you today?” He waited for a response, but all Mary Jo did was curl her lips into a thin smile. He pressed, “Did you take my cat from me?”
        “Aye. I did. And because of it we have been saved.” She gestured emphatically at the window where rain water seeped through the wood. “Look, Piter! We are saved.”
        Piter gulped. He did not want to believe the words he had just heard. “Why? What kind of witch are you, that you would say such a thing? Is this all a joke?”
        She sounded genuinely sorry as she responded, “I had to. For us.” 
        Piter stumbled backward, catching himself in a chair. The truth was still sinking in. The gears were still turning in his mind as he wrestled to understand.  
        Mary Jo knelt by his side and said in a sweet voice, “We are saved, my love. The rains have come and we are saved. Be still and rest.”
        Piter’s eyes welled up as he asked the question he had dreaded, “What of Neville? What of my son? And the improvement in the soil? And the change in the winds that drove the locust South? What of all the good fortune we had through the years? And all the misfortune that came along with it? My missing cat! My missing son! What of it all then, witch?”
        He stood up and made for the door. Mary Jo raised an outstretched arm and called out in a commanding voice that Piter had not heard prior, “Stop. You will not leave. Sit. Stay. You must!” Her voice softened, “Come here. I insist, my love.”
        Piter thought she was mad to think that he would listen to her. His mind was made up. She needed to be brought to justice and the townsfolk would see to that. 
        Yet, despite now knowing his wife to be a witch, Piter found himself involuntarily turning around and sitting back down. Mary Jo rubbed her fingers through his hair, which soothed him, even though internally Piter was still angered beyond compare.
        He whispered to her, “You devil.” 
        She kissed him and whispered back, “Sleep now.”
        His eyes closed, and he fell into a deep slumber for many hours. 
        When he awoke, he was disoriented and his mind was foggy. He noticed a cup of steaming tea beside him, so he sipped it and made his way outside. The rain had stopped, but the ground was soaked.
        Mary Jo was collecting eggs in her apron. She noticed him looking around wearily. “Are you feeling better?”
        He smiled. “Much better. I had the craziest dreams in my sleep. At least the rain proved to be real after all.”
        She replied, “Aye. Bless the rain. We are saved because of it. I told you we would find a way.”
        Piter felt she might be right indeed. “We always do.” He wrapped his arms around her and pulled her tight. Something flickered in the woods and caught his eye as they headed inside. He thought of it no more as his mind wandered to the whereabouts of his rascal tabby cat, Thomasin.
