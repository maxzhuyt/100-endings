I guess a bit of explanation is in order? Ghosts, which is what I am, tend to fall into a few different categories.
        First are the roamers. Hitchhiking ghosts are a good example. Roamers can travel, but mostly that’s because they have to. 
        Then there are the connected, or at least that’s what they call themselves. They’re not so common because they’re linked with a person, a family, or an entire bloodline. 
        And lastly, at least for the major groupings, are the bound. That’s what I am. I’m bound to a house and I can’t step outside of it. 
        Thanks to the occasional roamer or connected visiting my house, I know at least this much. 
        Back in the 80s, well, there was the AIDS epidemic, which strongly affected gay men. A bunch of us lesbians stepped up, since the families wouldn’t acknowledge their child existed, and we were all part of a community. We sat with them, brought them food, and stayed with them while they were dying so they didn’t have to do it alone. There were never enough of us, but we tried.
        Somewhere along the way, I accidentally got stuck by an infected needle. One of the nurses wasn’t being so good with biohazard materials, so I ended up dealing with it for this one guy, Jesse. AIDS hit me hard, probably because I’d been spending so much of my time and energy taking care of other people. Six months after I got infected, I died in my bed at home.
        Most ghosts have some kind of unfinished business, at least the ones I know of. I know I never really let go of that “taking care of people” energy even after death. Since I owned the house outright, it wasn’t like my parents could come in and take it over, but the property got tied up in probate for a while. My brother decided to contest the will, arguing that because I’d contracted AIDS, I wasn’t in my right mind. Eventually, the courts decided in his favor. It probably wouldn’t have fallen out that way if this hadn’t been the early 80s, but at the time nobody wanted anything to do with those of us who’d gotten the disease.
        I only learned about this after the fact, when he started moving his stuff in and getting rid of everything I’d ever owned. He said awful things to his lawyer, his real estate agent, and our parents, things I never expected to hear out of him. I don’t know how I ended up with such a hate-filled little brother, but he wasn’t shy about expressing his opinions.
        So he moved into my home, and I made sure he moved out in less than six months. I’ll admit, I played on his homophobia more than a little; even though I was dead, it was still my home, and he didn’t deserve to live there.
        Once my brother was out of the picture, one of the roamers, a young woman named Melinda, taught me everything she could. To this day, she comes by as frequently as she can, given that she can’t stay in one place for very long.
        I found out that my brother had sold the house only after this lovely gay couple moved in. Of course, they couldn’t just say they were gay, but despite telling the world they were just “roommates,” I picked up on it immediately. I didn’t know them from when I was alive, but I did everything I could to make them feel welcome and safe. Roger and Brian stayed for almost a decade, until Brian got a promotion that took him across the country. Thankfully, same-sex couples were slowly gaining acceptance, and Brian’s company didn’t drag their heels too much on the subject of him bringing his partner.
        By that point, though, my humble little home in San Francisco had shot up in value. And the man who’d bought the house, he’d promised Roger and Brian he’d keep things as they were as part of the deal. Only to gloat about “flipping” the house within weeks of closing the sale. 
        I’d gotten very good over the years at manipulating things inside my home, especially with Melinda helping. It didn’t take Brad all that long to get spooked about the house he thought would turn an easy profit once he “spruced it up a little.” I might have gone a bit too far at the end, where I painted one blank wall in blood with the words “Plague House.” But Brad certainly didn’t stick around all that long after he watched it happen right in front of him. I won’t deny that I got a certain amount of satisfaction watching him bolt outside, throw up in the neighbor’s garden, then get in his fancy Porsche and drive away, never to be seen again.
        Then my home sat empty for a couple of years. Sure, there were showings and the like, but after a decade or so of being dead, well, I’d learned to read people’s auras and figure out pretty quickly whether they were the sort I’d want in my home. And sadly, most of the people the real estate agent brought through weren’t the sort I’d have been nice to when I was alive. At least Melinda kept coming by to keep me company and fill me in on the state of things.
        Eventually, Lisa and Ken were the ones to make an offer the agent wasn’t interested in refusing, and were people I liked from the first time they walked into my home. They were a cute couple, deeply in love with each other, and happy with the way the home was set up. After moving in, the two of them started talking to the house, things like asking permission before updating the wiring or changing something else major. I wasn’t ever sure if they could see me, or if they just thought the house was alive, but it didn’t really matter. 
        And two years later, Ken came out to Lisa, so Kendra started dressing up around the house, to Lisa’s approval and relief. She once whispered that she knew Ken was transgender and was just waiting for her to feel safe and comfortable enough to come out. She thanked the house for being so good to them both. 
	I’ll admit, I got a warm glow from the whole thing. Back in my day, I’d known some folk who knew they were women or men all along, no matter what anyone else saw, and I did what I could to help them. I did the same for Kendra, even helped make things easier after the rounds of surgeries she did to make her body fit herself better. It helped that Lisa’s employer was very progressive, and that Kendra had a loving family. 
        Sadly, about five years in, Lisa’s parents got really sick, and she and Kendra moved out to take care of them. They kept the house as a rental, and did everything they could to vet potential renters, but this was in the days before viable videoconferencing was a thing. Which meant I got stuck briefly with Julie.
        She tried to be good, she really did. But she had the worst track record with men and women, bringing them over for a night or a weekend, and never noticing their bad sides. I tried to give her as many hints as I could, from things like putting Jake’s shoes next to the door to practically freezing Marla out of the shower. I don’t know why Julie’s radar for people was so bad, and I didn’t want to make her leave. But when Fergie tried to steal Julie’s rent money, I determined I’d had enough. Nobody I disapproved of was getting more than five feet inside my home. 
        Julie moved out after a month when she literally couldn’t bring any date inside. I swear, that woman needed some serious therapy and a good swift kick to the rear. The last I saw of her, she was sobbing on Amanda’s shoulders as the two of them walked the last box to the moving truck.
        Kendra came back for three months, and spent most of it cleaning and fixing things up. She also spent a lot of it talking out loud to me, well, the house. “Geez, I thought Julie was a good kid, that’s why we went with her,” she said after taking out the garbage for the fourth time. Or, “I wish Julie would have listened to you the way we did.” She told me all about Lisa’s parents, and how happy she was with Lisa, and the best I could do was make sure my home was as comforting as it always had been. 
        But eventually Kendra had to go back, and Sam moved in as the next renter. Deep down, Sam was good people, but she had a busload of anger and nowhere to put it except into her music. She introduced me to a broad variety of music, punk and indie and so many other genres I hadn’t known about while I was alive. At least the dead can learn new things, thank goodness. I did what I could, so the music wouldn’t disturb the neighbors, and Sam got a solid nine months of alone time before things started changing. 
        Sam brought Ivy home with her after a night at the club. Ivy was a goth. Ivy also said she was a witch. And even though Sam knew about the “spooky” history of my home, including everything I had done to drive my brother and Brad away, Ivy was the first to believe there was a ghost in the house.
        Sam didn’t think it was real until I started using the letters of her breakfast cereal to spell out messages. Me telling her not to freak out had the opposite effect, but Ivy bounced up and down like a giddy child.
        Ivy was good about trying to communicate with me. She tried a Ouija board, but that lasted about five minutes before we both started laughing too much. The magnetic poetry was a decent idea, but the kits are all really limited and not suited for, “Hey, don’t turn up the volume too much, the neighbors are starting to notice.” I certainly didn’t want to write in blood the way I’d done to scare Brad out of my home.
        We finally settled on a typewriter. I was very good at pushing material things after nearly twenty years as a ghost, and the newer typewriters required less effort. I think Sam would rather have used a computer, but there wasn’t any reasonable way to set one up only so I could talk with her.
        Kendra and Lisa came to visit about six months after we’d worked out how to communicate. The two of them were floored, but adapted fairly quickly. Typing was far slower than talking, but in a few hours I’d told them pretty much everything. Lisa at least knew the history of the AIDS epidemic well enough to tell the others, and we commiserated at the state of the world and how acceptance of LGBT people seemed to be taking forever. 
        “So why didn’t Julie work out? She seemed like a nice girl, and she was bi,” Kendra asked finally.
        I typed out, “She had horrible taste in men and women. They were all bad for her, one way or another. One of them even tried to steal her rent money.”
        I think at first they were worried that I was being biased or, in the words of Sam, “cranky” about Julie. But once I explained that I liked her, but the people she kept trying to date were actually harmful to her, the four of them looked at each other and nodded.
        “So how do we fix this in future? I don’t think Sam and Ivy are going to live here forever,” Kendra said dryly, with Sam blushing a bit.
        “Nah, my band is really taking off. They’re talking about a tour.”
        Ivy grinned at Sam, then looked over at the typewriter. “I’d like to volunteer, actually. To stay here, help our ghost determine who’s a good fit and who isn’t. It is a three-bedroom house. Maybe we can make it a safer space for LGBT people?”
        I typed with amusement, “That was my intent all along.” I paused. “And my name is Beverly.”
        Eventually, Kendra and Lisa permanently moved up to Seattle, into a house that was decidedly less haunted, but Ivy stayed, content with me as a roommate. She’s pretty special herself, and in the past couple of decades we’ve worked out a lot of things, including how to communicate, how to spot potential renters, and keep the people and my house safe from trouble.
        Which is why you’re reading this. Ivy met with you, and thought you’d be a good fit. How do you feel about living in a haunted house?
