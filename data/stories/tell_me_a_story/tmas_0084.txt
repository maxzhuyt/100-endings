Thomas pushed the cart through a dark and dusty aisle of Hectares Of Books, his boat shoes sticking to the cold concrete floor from the Dr. Pepper he’d spilled on them earlier. He had twenty-seven dollars to his name and no one to drink a beer with after work.
        What am I doing with my life?
        He had started in the Fiction Hall that morning and gotten through Aisle Ja—Kl before lunch. For the last year, it had been his daily morning routine to scan the shelves for books that patrons had put back in the wrong place. 
        He stopped and rolled his eyes, pulling a book from the shelf to his left. Was it so hard to know the difference between Ludlum and Vonnegut?
        He had the job because the owner noticed he spent most of his time there anyway. Heck, he had been reshelving books and discarding broken spines since high school, six years ago. He could walk down every aisle in the bookstore, more than fifty aisles in four different anterooms with bookshelves ten feet high—soulless, windowless, chambers—and pick a misplaced book out just by walking by.
        He stopped and grimaced. Here is L. Ron Hubbard on a shelf next to romance novels with pictures of Fabio. What were people thinking? He pulled the book from the shelf and put it in the cart.
        A word popped into Thomas’s head. Coddiwomple. To travel purposefully toward an as-yet-unknown destination. Thomas smiled. That’s me, traveling through the bookstore stacks.
        Mr. Whipple stopped at the end of Kl—Le, looking his way. God, why did he have to have a name like that? The old man was like a cross between Tyler Durden and the professor from The Paper Chase. The cunning demeanor and the smooth English accent, rolled into an ancient bookstore owner.
        “Mr. Anderson, the front staff have culled the latest donations. Many of them are not salesworthy.” 
        Thomas shivered. 
        Mr. Whipple made a sniffing sound. “They are in a rolling bin next to the restrooms. You have a bin of broken books as well, correct?”
        Thomas nodded. 
        “They need to be taken to the recycling dumpster in the alley,” Mr. Whipple said, approaching him. “Do you think you can manage both carts, Mr. Anderson?”  
        Thomas cleared his throat. “Can I do that after lunch, Mr. Whipple?”
        “Of course, Thomas.” Mr. Whipple gave him a sad smile before turning around. He stopped to look back at Thomas at the end of the aisle, licked his lips, then disappeared.
        Thomas let out a breath, and pushed the cart forward.
        After lunch, Thomas pushed his collection of books with torn or missing covers, broken spines, and missing pages to the rear fire door, and shoved it into the afternoon sun with a bang, dragging the donation bin behind him. At the recycling dumpster, he threw back the heavy lid, reached into the donation bin, and tossed an armful of books over the edge. After clearing the donations, he moved on to his discarded bin. About halfway down, he saw a copy of The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy. Thomas picked it up.
        I don’t recall seeing this one before. 
        Shrugging, Thomas started to toss the book into the dumpster.
        “You don’t want to do that!”
        Startled, Thomas dropped the book and turned around. Seeing no one in the alley, he called out. “Who said that?”
        “I did, you nincompoop.” 
        Thomas looked around, then behind the dumpster. “Is this a joke?”
        “No joke, nincompoop!”
        Thomas’ eyes fell on the book on the ground. His eyes wide.
        “It is I, or it is me, in the flesh! Or the pulp, as it were.” The cover pulsed with a bluish glow in sync with the words emanating from it.
        Thomas reached toward the book. “Can I pick you up?”
        “Of course. Just because I’m fabulous doesn’t mean you can’t touch me.”
        Thomas picked up the book and tried to open it.
        “Careful! I am not for reading. The quantum entanglement on my insides would astound you and confound your reality.”
        “What are you?”
        “I am a supernatural, interdimensional superior being.”
        Thomas turned the book over, then back to the cover.        
        “Why are you a book?”
        “Well, I can be a square.” In Thomas’ hands, the book transformed into a radiating square. “Or a sphere.” Then its shape changed into a shimmering sphere of the same size. Thomas jerked his hands up and the sphere floated to the ground.
        “Careful, nincompoop!”
        “You felt cold.”
        “Better than hot, nincompoop! I could be a record.” The sphere transformed into the Nirvana album NeverMind. “Pick me up!”
        Thomas held the record gingerly in his hands. “You can be a record? Do you play?”
        The record vibrated and shimmered. “Pssssh. Do I play? Of course! The best album of all time would deserve nothing less!”
        “I don’t know about the best album,” Thomas said. 
        The record morphed back into the original book.
        “Do you know music, nincompoop?”
        “No, and my name is Thomas. I just don’t think Nirvana has the best music.”
        The book sighed. “Well, Thomas, if you don’t know what you’re talking about, maybe you should just keep it to yourself. Okay?”
        What the hell? “What’s your name?”
        “My name? My name. Hm, I haven’t ever thought about that.”
        “What do you mean? How old are you?”
        “I have been on this planet for half a million years.”
        Thomas looked around. “Half a million. Get out of here. I don’t believe you.”
        “Half a million, give or take a millennium.”
        Thomas' eyes narrowed. "Are you immortal?"
        The book glowed. "Immortality is but ubiquity in time."
        “So you’ve seen everything.”
        “You bet.”
        “But you don’t have a name.”
        “Well, no. Good point. I haven’t had anyone to talk to.”
        “I’m the first person you’ve ever talked to?”
        “Huh. Well, I guess so.” The book hummed.
        “Why is that?”
        “I don’t know.”
        “Aren’t you an interdimensional supernatural superior being?”
        “Look, nincompoop. Don’t get too smart for a hairless monkey.”
        “You can’t have always been a book.”
        “No, I spent most of my time as a rock. Moving from place to place.”
        “And you haven’t been a book for very long.”
        “No. Things were just starting to get interesting.”
        “How long have you been a book?”
        “About a hundred and fifty years.”
        “Then you weren’t always the Hitchhiker's Guide.”
        “Oh no. When I started out as a book, I started out as Anna Karenina. Let me tell you, Count Vronsky is such a dude.”
        Thomas rolled his eyes. “How did you get here?”
        “Well, that’s a complicated question.”
        “I’m not stupid.”
        The book sighed. “Yes, but do you understand the origin of the universe, dark matter, and black holes?”
        “Well, a little,” Thomas said, scratching his head.
        “Let’s just say the gravitational wave signals you think you perceive are the secondary emissions from an intelligence occupying a higher plane of existence. Black holes are emitters of dark energy used by that intelligence. They are also revolving doors to other parts of the universe. I came to this planet through one of those black holes.”
        “I meant, how did you get to this bookstore?”
        “Oh, someone had purchased me as a first edition of Ayn Rand’s The Fountainhead, and then put me in a crate. When that hairless monkey died, I changed my title. The greedy progeny brought me here.”
        “Why haven’t you gone back home in half a million years?”
        “If I could, don't you think I would have, nincompoop?”
        “Stop calling me that. What should I call you? Zaphod?”
        The book let out a choking sound. “Ohhh, don’t you dare. I like Buckaroo.”
        “Buckaroo?”
        “For Buckaroo Banzai, you hairless monkey. You know the movie, right?”
        “I know it. I’m just surprised you do.”
        “Buckaroo Banzai in the eighth dimension! Of course, there are just a few more dimensions than that. But it’s still a good film.”
        “How do you know about movies?”
        “I spent decades in libraries with multimedia rooms.”
        “That's why you're a book?”
        “Hairless monkeys are stupid, but they can be very entertaining.”
        The fire door slammed open, and Mr. Whipple stood there.
        “Mr. Anderson, do you think you might finish your chore any time soon?”
        Thomas looked at the book in his hand. “Right away, Mr. Whipple.”
        “See that you don’t delay, Mr. Anderson.” With that, he turned away, and the fire door closed again.
        “I’ve got to get back to work,” Thomas said to the book.
        “Tell him to go pound sand. Don’t be a stupid, hairless monkey.”
        “What am I going to do without a job?” Thomas laid the book next to the dumpster and lifted an armful of books from the bin.
        “I can help you. If you help me.”
        Thomas lifted another armful of books and threw them into the dumpster.
        “Look, this is really weird. You’re just a book. What can you do?”
        “Well, what does the hairless monkey need?”
        “Look, Zaphod. Why don't you call me Thomas, and I’ll call you Buckaroo?”
        The book groaned. “Fine. Thomas. What do you need?”
        “I need money, Buckaroo,” said Thomas.
        “That’s easy. Pull out your cell phone. You have your bank app on there, right?”
        “Yes,” Thomas said, pulling out his cell phone and opening his banking app. “Okay.”
        “Login.”
        Thomas did so. “Okay.”
        “Now, how much do you need?”
        Thomas put his phone down by his side. “Wait. First, Buckaroo, what do you need?”
        “Well, aha. Ha. I just need access to a Hadron collider and something cyou hairless monkeys call element 115.”
        “How the hell am I supposed to get those?”
        “Look, you’ve got a superior supernatural interdimensional being with you. I don’t expect a hairless monkey, or you, Thomas, to know how to do that. I just need help getting there.”
        Thomas hesitated, then raised his phone and entered the password on the app.
        “How much do you need?”
        “How much do we need to find a Hadron collider? I don’t know. A million dollars!”
        “Done, Thomas!”
        “What do you mean?”
        “Look at your bank app.”
        Sure enough, when Thomas looked at his account, he had one million, twenty-seven dollars in his account.
        Whoa. “So what now?”
        “Well, now we go tell Uncle Fester that you are quitting. We go and collect your toothbrush, your towel, and your fanny pack, and we leave.”
        “I don’t have a fanny pack.”
        “Yeah, right. All adolescent hairless monkeys without friends have fanny packs.”
        Thomas raised the book towards the dumpster. “Maybe I should just put you in the recycle bin with the rest of the refuse.”
        “No! Just kidding. You don’t need anything. We just leave. Whatever you require, we can buy. What do you say, Thomas? Time to hit the road?”
        “Let’s do it, Buckaroo.”
