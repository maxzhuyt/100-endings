# My Apocalypse Buddy

---

The vending machine out front still had its little light on. Nobody knew why. The grid had been dead for two years, the city had been folded in half by whatever came after the city, and yet every morning Midas woke up to that faint orange glow bleeding through the tarp he used as a curtain, and every morning it felt like a personal insult from the universe. He had stopped trying to explain it. Some things earned their mystery.

He was eating crackers out of a can — crackers did not belong in cans, which was another thing he had stopped trying to explain — when Chewy spoke from the corner.

"You are crunching at an irregular interval," Chewy said. "It suggests anxiety."

"It suggests I'm eating."

"The two are not mutually exclusive."

Chewy was propped against the wall in the way he always was, slightly listing to one side, both glass eyes fixed forward with the serene vacancy of something that had been manufactured to delight children and had instead outlasted most of them. He was about the size of a large cat. His fur had gone gray-yellow in patches. His left ear rotated sometimes, slowly, for no reason anyone had identified. He was, technically, an animatronic koala bear, interactive model, batteries not included — except that the batteries had been included, in a way, because whatever was running Chewy now had long outlasted the D-cells he'd shipped with.

Midas had found him in a gift shop rubble pile sixteen months ago and had not had a quiet moment since.

---

"So here is the plan," Midas said, spreading the torn city map across the floor with the confidence of a man who had never successfully executed a plan. He tapped a crossroads three kilometers east. "We go here. We look at the thing. We assess."

"The thing," Chewy said. "You mean the graffiti."

"I mean the lead."

"You have described it as a lead four times since yesterday. Prior to yesterday you described it as a long shot. Prior to that, a hunch. The underlying evidence has not changed. Only your vocabulary has escalated."

"That's called optimism."

"It is called imprecision."

The graffiti had been there for at least a month, on the scorched wall of what used to be a dry cleaner: *FOLLOW THE LINDEN'S ROOT / THREE TURNS FROM THOMAS / YOU'LL KNOW BY THE* — and then it was gone, painted over by something that wasn't paint, crumbled by something that wasn't weather. But those words. *You'll know by the.* You'd know by the what. The what was the entire thing.

"It's pointing somewhere," Midas said. "Someone wrote that for a reason."

"Someone wrote it with a marker on a wall," Chewy said. "People wrote many things on walls in the last two years. Most of them were either warnings or profanity."

"This one's neither."

"I acknowledge that. I am noting that the sample space of wall-writing is not a reliable data source."

"Chewy."

"Yes."

"We're going."

A pause. The left ear rotated.

"I have logged my objections," Chewy said.

"Logged and ignored."

"As always."

---

They left at a little past eight, which was inauspicious from the first step because Midas walked directly into the door he had forgotten he'd rehung crooked, took the frame to the forehead, and bled a thin red line down to his eyebrow for the first ten minutes of the walk. Chewy observed this without comment for approximately one block before saying: "You are losing fluid."

"I'm aware."

"At a non-critical rate."

"Thank you."

The streets outside were the streets. There was no better way to put it. They were streets the way a skeleton was still a person — structurally familiar, elementally rearranged. A delivery truck had become a planter; something green and stubborn was colonizing the cab. Three storefronts down, a child's bicycle hung from a second-floor window by what appeared to be zip ties, rotating very slowly, and had been rotating very slowly every day Midas had seen it, and he had long decided this was either art or a threat and either way it was impressive. A dog that wasn't friendly watched them from a parking garage ramp. He noted it. He chose not to engage with it.

Chewy's right knee joint caught and skipped twice on the first curb drop, the grinding sound of a thing that had been walked too many miles.

"That is the third time this week," Midas said.

"It is functioning within acceptable parameters."

"You said that last week."

"The parameters have been revised."

---

The dry cleaner wall was on the corner of Linden and Marsh, which Midas had noted before and immediately forgot because he was bad at patterns. But Linden. *Follow the Linden's root.* He stood in front of the graffiti for a long moment, hands on hips, squinting at the text that remained.

"Root," he said. "As in underground. As in we go down somewhere."

"'Root' in older cartographic slang occasionally referred to a founding road — a main artery. Linden as a street would be the root. The origin point."

"So we follow Linden from its start."

"That is one reading."

"What's the other reading?"

"That 'Linden's root' refers to an actual linden tree, of which there were several planted along this corridor before the collapse, and 'root' refers to its base, suggesting proximity to a specific tree stump or surviving specimen."

Midas considered this. "That's more specific."

"It is more specific," Chewy agreed. "It is also less dramatic. I assumed you would prefer your interpretation."

"I do prefer my interpretation."

"I have noted that also."

So they went to the start of Linden, which was a cracked roundabout with a dry fountain at its center, and from there Midas counted three turns — *three turns from Thomas*, so from the Thomas Street junction, *three turns* — and the third turn put them at a loading dock behind what had been a hotel, its upper floors pancaked inward, its ground floor somehow still holding, a steel door sunk recessed into the concrete with a faded yellow stripe across it.

Midas looked at Chewy. Chewy looked at the door.

"You'll know by the yellow," Midas said.

"Possibly."

---

The door was blocked by a collapsed section of fire escape, which Midas moved by pulling on the wrong piece first, which brought the rest of it down on his left shoulder in a shower of rust and a sound like a dropped cutlery drawer, which echoed through the loading dock and possibly through the three adjacent city blocks, and which caused a colony of something — birds, rats, the vague rustling category of *medium-sized fauna* — to vacate a dumpster at speed.

Midas stood still. Listened.

Down the alley, a shape moved behind a fence.

"Human," Chewy said quietly.

"You sure?"

"The gait is bipedal and unhurried. Which suggests either confidence or obliviousness."

"Which is worse?"

A pause. "Contextually equivalent."

The shape did not come closer. Midas breathed. He had been breathing wrong for the last thirty seconds and his shoulder hurt in the way that meant bruise-not-break, which was the acceptable category of hurt. He looked back at the fire escape now blocking his path to the yellow-stripe door, assessed that he could climb over it but not quickly, and climbed over it not quickly, banging his shin on the top rail and losing the half-tin of crackers from his pack, which fell and scattered across the dock.

He stood before the door.

He tried the handle.

The handle turned.

He looked back at Chewy, still on the other side of the fire escape pile, both eyes fixed forward.

"Don't say anything," Midas said.

"I was going to say: proceed carefully."

"That's something."

"I revised."

---

The door opened into a stairwell leading down, and the stairwell opened into a space that had obviously once been a service basement, and the service basement was occupied.

By three people who were not happy to see him.

They were camped — properly camped, with a gas burner and rolled sleeping pads and a string of chemical light-sticks giving the space a dim green tint — and they were on their feet before Midas had finished registering them, and the tallest one had a length of metal pipe, and all three had the faces of people who had been found before and not enjoyed the experience.

"Wait," Midas said. "I'm not — I saw the graffiti. I was looking for the refuge."

Silence.

"The yellow stripe," he said. "Three turns from Thomas."

The tall one with the pipe said: "There is no refuge."

"Right, but—"

"We wrote that," the tall one said, "to draw people in."

Midas processed this.

"That is a trap," he said.

"Yes."

A beat.

"Well," Midas said, "that's — actually that's clever. I'm not being — I genuinely mean that's clever, as a trap concept."

From behind him, from approximately the fire escape area, Chewy's right knee joint made an extended grinding noise, followed by what could only be described as a squeak, followed by the sound of an animatronic koala falling sideways into a collapsed fire escape ladder, followed by a remarkable amount of secondary noise.

Every head in the room turned toward the doorway.

Midas ran.

He grabbed Chewy — who was on his side, legs cycling — threw him under his arm, crossed the fire escape pile with a speed he was confident no previous version of himself had achieved, and covered the first twenty meters of alley in a sprint while the three campers stood in their doorway, apparently too baffled to pursue a man carrying a malfunctioning koala, which in retrospect was probably exactly the right strategic asset.

He turned a corner. Stopped. Pressed his back to a wall.

Chewy's legs stopped cycling.

"Your improvisational decision to flee was," Chewy said, after a moment, "geometrically sound."

Midas breathed. "Thank you."

"You did step on my ear."

"I know. I'm sorry."

"The ear was already compromised."

---

They sat in an alcove between two sealed buildings while Midas pressed the heel of his hand against his shin, which had graduated from bruise territory to actual-consideration territory, and looked at Chewy, who was running through some internal process that manifested as a faint ticking sound and the left ear rotating in three slow increments.

"No refuge," Midas said.

"No refuge," Chewy confirmed.

"It was a trap the whole time."

"The clue was well-constructed," Chewy said. "Whoever designed it understood motivation. People seeking safety are credulous. The promise of a destination overrides the evaluation of the source."

"That's a fancy way of saying I got played."

"You got played," Chewy agreed. He paused. The ear completed its rotation. "However."

"However."

"You decoded the correct location from ambiguous text, navigated three kilometers without major incident—"

"I bled."

"Minor incident. And you extracted both of us from a three-on-one confrontation without injury to either party."

Midas looked at him. The glass eyes stared forward, serene, manufactured to be gentle, accidentally now being something else.

"You're doing the thing," Midas said.

"I am providing an accurate accounting."

"That's not what it feels like."

Another tick. "We should go back."

"Yeah."

"While there is still light."

---

They were halfway home when the dog from the parking garage ramp appeared again, this time in the road, this time not watching from a distance. Midas froze. The dog — medium-large, mixed origin, expression of profound personal grievance — looked at him with the specific focus of an animal that had made a decision.

Midas looked at Chewy.

Chewy said: "The animatronic roar function in my audio module is technically still operational."

"You have a roar function?"

"I was designed for a wildlife-themed attraction. I have six animal vocalizations. The bear is the largest."

"Why didn't you ever—"

"You never asked."

Chewy produced a sound from his chest speaker that was not quite a bear and not quite anything else in nature, but was loud and sudden and wrong enough that the dog sat down very fast, then stood up, then left.

Midas stared at the empty road.

"That," he said, "would have been useful at several earlier points today."

"Yes," said Chewy. "It would have."

---

They came home to the vending machine's orange glow. The tarp hung crooked over the door from where Midas had hit it going out. Inside, the cracker can was still on the floor, still half-full, and the map was still spread across the ground with Midas's hopeful finger smudge at the hotel crossroads.

Midas sat down heavily. He had a gash on his forehead, a bruised shoulder, a compromised shin, no crackers, and three fewer hours of operational daylight than when he'd left. Chewy sat beside him with a knee joint that would need attention and an ear that now only rotated in two increments instead of three.

The space was small. It was, against all odds and several structural codes, still standing.

"We need crackers," Midas said.

"We need several things," Chewy said. His voice had the slightly flattened quality it got when his battery draw was high. "Crackers. Knee lubricant. A more rigorous system for evaluating graffiti as an intelligence source."

"There could still be a real one out there," Midas said. "Someone else wrote one that was real."

Chewy was quiet for a moment.

"There could," he said.

"We just picked the wrong one."

"You picked the wrong one. I logged objections."

Midas leaned his head back against the wall. The vending machine's light pulsed, once, the way it sometimes did, as if considering turning off, deciding against it.

"Same time tomorrow?" he said.

Chewy's left ear rotated once, then stopped.

"I will begin revising my parameters tonight," he said, "so that tomorrow's acceptable risk threshold will be technically accurate when you exceed it."

---

Somewhere on Linden Street, in the rusted shadow of a former dry cleaner, the remaining half of a graffiti message waited under a sky that had no opinion about it either way. *You'll know by the.* The wall held. The words held. And whatever came after the *the* was still out there, probably, somewhere — tucked behind some yellow stripe, at the bottom of something, past three more turns, past a dozen more wrong guesses and a few more things going sideways in the specific way that things went sideways when Midas was involved.

The vending machine held its small orange light against the dark.

"You are crunching again," Chewy said.

"I found one in my pocket."

"Then you are not entirely diminished."

"High praise."

"It was meant," said Chewy, "precisely as given."
