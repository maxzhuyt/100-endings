# Carnival of Dangerous Intentions
### Coney Island, New York — July, 1937

---

She told herself it was the heat that had brought her back.

The city had been unbearable all week — that particular July cruelty that pressed down on the tenements and turned the air into something you had to fight through rather than breathe — and Coney Island in the evening offered the ocean, after all. The breeze. The particular mercy of a sky that opened wide over water rather than being carved into narrow strips by brownstone walls.

She told herself this on the subway. She told herself this walking from the station. She stopped telling herself this when she reached the shooting gallery and saw that he was already there, leaning with one shoulder against the counter, watching the boardwalk with the relaxed certainty of a man who had never once doubted that what he was waiting for would arrive.

The sodium lights buzzed and flickered along the promenade, casting everything in a color that was old amber, then briefly nothing, then amber again. Faces emerged from shadow and retreated. A woman laughed somewhere behind her, too loudly, and the carousel turned its slow mechanical circle in the middle distance, its painted horses rising and falling in their fixed, futile gallop. She could smell the burnt sugar from the kettle corn stand at the corner, and beneath that the salt air moving in off the Atlantic, and beneath that, as though the evening had a basement, something darker — rotting wood, machine oil, the particular marine decay of pylons that had been submerged and resurfaced too many times.

*His locks.* The phrase surfaced again, unbidden, and she felt the particular shame of it. Nineteenth-century language. The language of novels she'd read in her aunt's house as a girl, breathless and guilty under the bedcovers with a torch. She had not thought in those terms about anyone. Not until last Thursday, when a stranger had bought her a paper cone of fried dough on this same boardwalk and looked at her as though she were the solution to a question he had been quietly asking for years.

She hadn't given him her name. He hadn't given her his. They had talked for forty minutes about nothing that mattered and everything that did, and then she had gone home and lain awake until two in the morning cataloguing, with the methodical precision she applied to everything, all the reasons it would be foolish to go back.

She had filled four pages of the small blue ledger she kept beside her bed. Column one: reasons. Column two: counterarguments. Column three, which she had never used before and which she'd had to draw in herself with a ruler: the things she wanted and had not yet named.

Column three had only one entry, and it was not a word.

And yet here she was, her good shoes already suffering for it, the planks of the boardwalk uneven beneath her feet, the gaps between them wide enough that she had to be careful, the wood gone silver with age and salt exposure and the weight of a million ordinary summers.

He saw her before she'd taken three steps toward him. The lights held for a moment and she watched something move across his face — not surprise; he hadn't expected to be surprised — but a kind of satisfaction that he didn't bother concealing. He straightened from the counter and waited, and she had a moment, while the distance between them was still enough distance, to look at him the way she had been looking at him all week in her mind: in pieces, in fragments, the way you look at something bright by looking slightly away from it. The line of his jaw against the amber light. The way he held himself like a man accustomed to waiting, who had decided long ago that waiting was its own form of pressure.

Then she was close enough, and he was assembled whole, and she understood why she had preferred the fragments.

"You look like you've been arguing with yourself," he said.

"Only losing halves of arguments tend to show up to things like this."

Something shifted at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. More like a smile being considered and then carefully set aside for later. "What makes it losing?"

She didn't answer immediately. A group of young men pushed past on the boardwalk, loud and careless, and she stepped slightly closer to avoid them, which brought her closer to him than she'd intended, and she could smell him — salt, and something warmer beneath it, tobacco and something else she had no innocent name for — and she stepped back again, arranging her expression into something composed.

"I don't know your name," she said.

"You could ask."

"I'm asking."

He looked at her for a moment in a way that felt like being measured, though not unkindly. The lights stuttered and in the brief dark his face became only the planes of it, the bones, the black glint of his eyes. Then the amber came back.

"Daniel," he said. "And I already know yours."

Her pulse did something she chose to ignore. "How."

It wasn't quite a question. He seemed to understand this.

"You told the woman at the taffy stand to put your change toward whatever the next child ordered." He paused. "She called you by name when she thanked you. *Eleanor.*"

She had not noticed him there. That was the thing that stayed with her, that turned itself over in her mind like a stone being examined for what lived beneath it. She had not noticed. She was usually careful about noticing. She had made a habit of it, built her life on it — the carefully noticed detail, the route memorized, the room assessed before she committed to entering it.

She looked at him now. He returned the look without flinching, without performing discomfort.

"That's a surveillance you're describing," she said, keeping her voice light.

"That's a coincidence I'm describing." He glanced toward the water, then back. The ocean was a sound more than a presence — a low systolic wash beneath the music and the voices and the machinery of pleasure. "Though I won't pretend I wasn't watching you before you knew I was there."

The admission should have alarmed her. It arrived instead as something she could not name, though her body had several opinions about it, none of them particularly respectable, and the blue ledger, she thought, would require a fourth column, which she had not yet ruled.

"Why did you ask me to come back here?" she said.

"Why did you come?"

"I asked first."

The carousel music shifted keys, briefly dissonant, then resolved. A child's shout rose and dissolved into the general noise of the evening. He studied her face in the faltering light as though he were reading something handwritten that required patience and a certain quality of stillness, the way you read something you suspect you will only get to read once.

"Because I think you're someone who makes careful decisions," he said. "And I find myself wanting very much to be the exception to that."

*Leave.* The composed part of her said it clearly and without drama — the part that took the same route to work every morning, that kept accounts in a small blue ledger, that had never once raised its voice in company. *You can leave now. You came, which was the foolish thing. You can leave, which is the sensible thing, and there is still a version of tonight in which you go home and sleep and none of this happened.*

She looked at him. He was not watching her the way men usually watched women on boardwalks in July, the assessing look, the consumer's look. He was watching her the way someone watches a thing they already know they cannot afford, and have already decided to purchase anyway, and are only now beginning to calculate the cost.

She should leave. She understood that leaving was the correct decision, the legible decision, the one that could be justified to the composed voice and to the blue ledger and to the woman she had been at twenty-two who had promised herself she would always, always know what she was walking into.

She did not yet know what she was walking into.

"That's a dangerous thing to want from a stranger," she said.

"I know." He didn't look sorry about it.

She stayed.

There was a moment — she would mark it later, the way you mark the moment before a door closes rather than after — in which she could still have turned it into something else. Laughed, perhaps. Said something deflecting. Manufactured an engagement, a lateness, a better place to be. She was good at that. She had refinements in the management of exits that she had developed over years, small social machineries that could be deployed without appearing to be deployed.

She did none of those things.

"Are you going to walk with me?" he said.

She looked at the hand he had not yet extended.

Further along the boardwalk a vendor was closing his stall against the evening, folding back the striped awning with practiced economy. She watched him do it — the three efficient movements, the snap of canvas, the tying of a rope — and thought: there it is. There is the sensible thing, being done by someone who does sensible things.

She looked at Daniel.

"For a little while," she said.

They walked. He didn't take her arm and she didn't offer it, and the space between them was precise, calibrated, the exact width of a question that neither of them had asked. The boardwalk was crowded enough for cover, sparse enough for intention. The ocean sound grew louder as they moved away from the carousel.

He asked her how she spent her Sundays. She told him: the lending library on Flatbush, a walk if the weather permitted, cooking something complicated and solitary in her apartment kitchen because the precision of it satisfied her, the measuring, the sequence, the particular honesty of a thing that would either work or wouldn't.

He listened as though listening were an active enterprise, a thing he was doing with his whole body.

"You're good at being alone," he said.

"I've had practice."

"That's not the same thing."

She considered this. The planks shifted slightly beneath her feet, and she felt the ocean under the boardwalk, the deep cold of it, enormous and patient.

"No," she said. "I suppose it isn't."

There was a break in the lights ahead where a fixture had burned out and not been replaced. Twenty feet of relative dark between the amber pools, and as they approached it she was aware of a decision being made or not made, a fork that presented itself not dramatically but in the ordinary way that forks presented themselves — as the simple fact of continuing to walk in a given direction.

They walked through the dark together.

On the other side of it, in the returned light, he said: "Did you eat dinner?"

"No."

"Neither did I."

She almost laughed, and did laugh, a small surprised sound, because it was such a plain thing to say after everything that had not been said, and the plainness of it was its own kind of sophistication, and she was beginning to understand that he was sophisticated in ways she had not inventoried.

That was the thing she noticed, and should have asked about, and didn't.

There were things about him that didn't assemble cleanly. Small things. He had mentioned, early in the conversation, that he was staying nearby, but when she'd said *staying* as though to confirm the word, he'd looked past her for just a moment, not long enough to be evasive, long enough to be something. And his hands, when he'd straightened from the counter, had moved briefly to his jacket pocket in a gesture that was habitual and then consciously stopped, like a man who had trained himself out of a tell and occasionally forgot the training. And there was the matter of the watching — not the admission of it, she had made her peace with the admission, but the fact of it, the patience it implied, the quality of attention that preceded it.

She noticed these things. She filed them. She did not ask.

*Because you don't want the answers*, the composed voice said, with some accuracy.

The lights buzzed. The ocean moved in the dark beyond the railing, indifferent and enormous. The fried dough stand came into view ahead of them, the same woman behind the counter, and Eleanor felt him notice it at the same moment she did — felt his attention shift toward the small history they shared, last Thursday, the paper cone, the powdered sugar that had gotten on her sleeve.

He turned to look at her. She was already looking at him.

"A little while," he said.

"What?"

"You said for a little while." The lights held steady and his face was entirely legible and she wished, briefly, that it weren't. "I'm just noting that we've been walking for twenty minutes."

"I'm aware of that."

"And?"

She stopped walking. He stopped with her, half a beat after, which told her something. At the railing she could see the water below — not the ocean, just the harbor water between the boardwalk and the beach, dark and close and full of its own private motion.

She thought about the blue ledger.

She thought about column three.

"What do you do?" she said. "For work. You never said."

He looked at her. The evaluation in it was real and she felt it, the weight of a decision being made about her on the other side of the expression, the question of how much to offer and in what currency.

"I work for people who need things handled carefully," he said.

"That covers a great deal of territory."

"It's meant to."

She held his gaze. He held hers. A couple pushed past them on the boardwalk, bundled together, laughing at something private, and for a moment he and Eleanor were a still point in the moving evening, the sounds of the carnival continuing around them — the carousel, the mechanical bells of the shooting gallery, the distant cry of a barker — all of it present and irrelevant.

"I think," she said, "that you are a person who has reasons for things that you will not tell me."

"Yes."

"And you think I'm a person who will find a way to live with that."

He said nothing for a moment. Then: "I think you're a person who is deciding right now whether you are."

The lights buzzed. The ocean moved. Below the boardwalk there was the sound of water against wood, patient and recursive, the same stroke repeated without hope of a different result.

She had filled four pages in the blue ledger. She had made a list, which was what she did, and the list had told her clearly what the sensible decision was, and she was a woman who trusted her lists, who had built a life on the architecture of careful decisions, who had never once in thirty-one years made a move she could not justify by morning.

"All right," she said.

"All right."

She didn't specify what she meant. He didn't ask. 

When they turned back toward the lights and the crowd and the warm center of the carnival, she was on his left, and he adjusted his pace to hers without appearing to adjust it, and neither of them said anything for a long moment, and the carousel turned its slow circle, and the painted horses rose and fell in their permanent gallop, going nowhere, working very hard, wearing their fixed expressions with the commitment of things that had never known any other arrangement.

She could feel the boardwalk under her ruined shoes: the salt-silver planks, the gaps between them, the ocean beneath, dark and cold and very old.

Something had shifted.

She couldn't have said, if asked, what precisely it was. No line had been crossed that she could point to. No word had been spoken that couldn't be revisited and explained away. Her name and his name. A walk. Twenty minutes of conversation in the amber carnival light.

But she walked in the knowledge that she was not going to file the things she had noticed. Was not going to ask the questions she had not asked. Was going to walk through the lit and busy evening beside a man called Daniel who worked carefully for people who needed things, and she was going to let him buy her something to eat, and she was going to talk with him about Sundays and cooking and the precise dishonesty of the thing she had told herself on the subway, and she was not going to look too carefully at certain things, because she had finally, for the first time in a long and careful life, written something in column three, and it was not a word, but it pointed at one, and the word it pointed at was *yes.*

The lights buzzed.

The harbor moved beneath them.

They walked on.
