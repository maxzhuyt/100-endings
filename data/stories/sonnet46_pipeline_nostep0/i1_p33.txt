# The Last Outpost

## Chapter One: Last Call for Something

The Last Outpost smells like recycled air, burnt synth-coffee, and the particular brand of desperation that comes from being very far from anywhere that matters. I've grown fond of it. Some people need cathedrals. I need this — the low hum of the atmospheric processors, the clatter of mismatched bar stools, the sound of forty different species not quite getting along but making a decent go of it anyway.

I'm Nettle. Nets, if you've bought me a drink, which almost nobody has. I run this bar, for all practical purposes, even if Dorem's name is on the license. Dorem is three hundred pounds of Cephali who hasn't moved from his corner booth in eleven months. He eats, he watches, and occasionally he issues pronouncements. I pour. It works.

Tonight is a standard Thursday — which out here means nothing, since we don't use standard weeks, but I've kept the habit because it comforts me. Thursdays feel moderate. Manageable.

Old Hess is nursing his fourth Ganymede pale at the far end of the bar and explaining, again, to nobody in particular, how the asteroid belts of Andromeda were *meant* to be rich and the corporations *knew* it forty years ago and *suppressed* the data. He's not wrong, probably. He's also not interesting, definitely. I let him talk. It costs me nothing.

At table six, a pair of Vreth merchants are engaged in what I can only describe as a negotiation conducted entirely through aggressive nostril-flaring. One of them gestured too broadly about twenty minutes ago and knocked over a full glass of something blue and carbonated. I cleaned it up. I did not comment. Commerce is commerce.

The playlist is on its second cycle — something jazzy and indistinct that Dorem programmed when he was in what passes for a good mood. I'm restocking the speed rail, counting bottles by habit more than need, when I hear the door.

The Outpost has got sixty-three stools, twelve tables, two private booths, and one entrance. I know every sound that entrance makes. This one is quiet — barely a whisper of the pressure seal releasing — which means whoever just came in doesn't want to be noticed.

I look up.

She slides onto a stool at the far end of the bar, not Hess's end, the quiet end, and she does it like someone who has sat in the corner of rooms their whole life and gotten very good at taking up no space. Her dreads are something — thick, dark, tipped with bioluminescent nodes that should be glowing a warm amber but are flickering erratically, like a sign in a bad part of town. Tentacled, which puts her as Tilhari, probably outer-rim born given the length. One of the dreads is slightly askew, knocked loose from whatever arrangement it started the day in. She doesn't fix it.

She looks at me. I look at her.

"Vega whisky," she says. Her voice is low and controlled, which usually means someone is doing a lot of work to keep it that way. "Neat. Better make that a double."

"Better night or worse night?" I ask, reaching for the Vega. It's not cheap. She doesn't flinch when I pull the good bottle.

"Does it matter?"

"Helps me decide whether to talk to you or leave you alone."

She looks at me properly for the first time. Her eyes are the silver-grey of old mining equipment, and they do a full sweep of my face in about half a second — quick, practiced, professional. "Leave me alone, then."

"Sure," I say, and pour her the double. I set it down without clinking it too cheerfully. Some people don't want cheerful. I slide a water alongside it, unrequested, because that's just sense.

She wraps both hands around the glass and stares into it like it owes her money.

I move off. I refill Hess. I check on the Vreth merchants, who have resolved their nostril-flaring into something that looks almost like a handshake. I run a cloth along the bar in that long, slow arc that means *I'm working but I'm here*, which is most of what bartending actually is.

Then, from table four, a bright shatter — glass on composite flooring. Someone's elbow, a laugh too big for the space, and suddenly there's Corvin swearing and bending down with two hands full of broken pieces.

"Leave it," I call over. "I've got a broom, Corvin, don't bleed on my floor."

A small ripple of laughter. The room breathes. I reach under the bar for the dustpan.

When I straighten up, the stranger is watching me. Not everyone. *Me.*

"You own this place?" she asks.

"I keep it alive," I say. "Different thing."

She considers this. Takes her first sip of the whisky — not a nervous gulp, a real sip, someone who actually knows what they ordered. "How long have you been out here?"

"Long enough." I start toward table four with the broom. "You?"

"Not long enough," she says quietly. And then, like she didn't mean to say it: "Or maybe too long. I genuinely can't tell anymore."

I sweep up Corvin's mess. I don't push. Pushing is amateur work.

But when I come back behind the bar, I notice she's shifted — not toward the door, the way people do when they're thinking about leaving. She's shifted toward me. Just a few centimetres. The dread that was askew is still askew.

And across the room, in the second booth from the back, a man I don't recognize is not drinking his drink. He's been sitting with the same glass for forty minutes. Not reading. Not talking.

Watching the bar.

I reach for a cloth I don't need and think about how Thursdays used to feel moderate.

## Chapter Two: The Weight of a Glass

There is an art to not watching someone while watching them. I've had years of practice. Behind a bar you develop a kind of peripheral vision that isn't really vision at all — it's more like pressure, like the way you can feel a storm before the instruments confirm it. You learn to read a room the way you read weather. You learn which clouds are just clouds.

The man in the second booth is not just a cloud.

He's ordered nothing since I've been paying attention, which means either he ordered before I clocked him — possible — or he's been nursing the same drink for an unsettling length of time — probable. He's broad across the shoulders in a way that suggests either genetics or an occupation that requires it, and he's dressed in transit layers, which is what people wear when they want to look like they're just passing through. Everybody out here is just passing through. It's a self-selecting crowd. But most of them drink their drinks.

I put him at human-adjacent, which narrows it down to about seventeen species and tells me nothing useful. He's angled so that the booth's shadow falls across his face, which might be accident and might not be.

The stranger finishes her double. She sets the glass down carefully, precisely, at the exact centre of the coaster, and I read that as someone who is holding themselves together by attending to small manageable things. I've done that. I know the technique. It works until it doesn't.

I come back over without being summoned.

"Another?" I ask.

She looks at the empty glass like she's running calculations. "Water," she says finally. "Just water."

I pour it. "Long trip?"

"Mm." She wraps her hands around the water glass now with the same grip she had on the whisky — both hands, a little too tight. The bioluminescent nodes in her dreads flicker. One of them goes briefly dark and then recovers. Tilhari dreads are keyed to neurological state, which means hers are broadcasting whether she wants them to or not. "You could say that."

"Where from?"

Pause. "Further than here."

"That covers a lot of ground."

"Yes," she says. "It does."

I wipe down the section of bar between us with a cloth that's already clean. It's a way of lingering without appearing to linger. "I'm Nets," I say.

She looks at me. Something moves across her face — not warmth, exactly, but the precursor to it, the way the sky changes before sunrise. "Sable," she says.

"Sable." I test the weight of it. It's not a name. It's a name-shaped word that someone chose deliberately. I've had one of those. I understand their uses. "Nice to meet you."

"Is it?"

"Jury's still out," I admit, "but provisional yes."

That almost gets something from her. Not quite a smile. The *shape* of a smile, like a word in a language you learned too long ago to speak fluently anymore. Her nodes flicker again, and this time — just for a moment — they hold steady at a deep, bruised violet instead of amber. Tilhari neurochroma. I don't know the full vocabulary, but violet isn't good.

Behind me, Hess has progressed from asteroid belts to his theory about the Meridian Compact being a front organization for — and here his voice drops to a carrying whisper — "the real people who run things." I've heard this one. I let it run.

Sable's eyes drift briefly past me and then come back. She does it well. She's done it before, that particular motion of surveillance made to look casual. "Busy place," she says.

"For out here, yeah." I lean against the bar. "You looking for someone?"

The question lands harder than I intended it to. Her hands tighten imperceptibly on the glass. "No," she says. Then: "Why?"

"Way you keep scanning. Thought maybe you were meeting someone."

"Habit," she says. "Old habit."

I nod slowly, the way you nod when you're filing something rather than responding to it. She's running, not meeting. I'd already suspected it, but the denial confirmed it — she answered *no* before I'd finished the question. People who are just cautious don't do that. People who are being *hunted* do that.

"You eaten?" I ask.

She blinks. The question clearly isn't what she was braced for. "I — no. Not recently."

"Kitchen closes in twenty minutes. Dorem does a thing with synthetic protein and some spice blend he's never told me the name of. It's not bad."

She looks at me properly for the second time tonight. Same sweep of those grey eyes, same professional cataloguing, but this time something else underneath it — something more careful. Like she's deciding whether to trust a reading she doesn't fully trust herself to make.

"Why?" she asks again. Quieter this time.

"Because you're running on whisky and adrenaline and you look like you've been doing that for a while," I say, keeping my voice level and easy and entirely without judgment. "And because the food here is genuinely not bad."

A long pause. The atmospheric processors hum. Somewhere across the room Corvin is telling a loud story about something that happened on Pelagia Station, and the people around him are laughing with varying degrees of sincerity, and the Vreth merchants seem to have concluded their business and are settling in for a social drink, which is going to test my knowledge of Vreth customs fairly shortly.

"All right," Sable says. It comes out like a concession. Like something that cost her.

I call the order through to Dorem's side, and Dorem — who may be immobile but is not deaf — tilts one enormous eye in my direction and makes a gesture with two tentacle-tips that means *fine, but you owe me.* I already owe him seventeen things. One more doesn't move the dial.

When I turn back around, the man in the second booth is gone.

I scan the room in the practiced way I've had for years, automatic, unhurried in its appearance. He's not at the bar. Not at another table. Not in line for the facility. The entrance pressure seal is whispering its quiet breath, and I know he's just used it, and I know I didn't hear him stand up, which means he moved when the noise from Corvin's story peaked, which means he was waiting for cover.

Amateur, some part of me thinks. And then: *but not amateur enough to miss.*

I reach under the bar and rest two fingers against the emergency tag — a habit, not a decision. I don't press it. Not yet. But I know exactly where it is, which is the point.

I put a plate of Dorem's protein thing in front of Sable when it comes through, and I top up her water, and I ask if she needs anything else in a voice that gives no indication whatsoever that the evening has just changed shape.

She looks at the food. Her nodes flicker amber for just a second — gratitude, maybe, or something that wants to be gratitude but doesn't quite remember how to get there.

"Thank you," she says.

"Eat," I say. "We'll talk after."

## Chapter Three: Running Tab

She eats like someone who has been pretending not to be hungry for a long time. Controlled at first, measured, and then — about halfway through — just hungry, actually hungry, and something in her posture changes when she stops managing it. She loses about three centimetres of tension from her shoulders. Her nodes settle into a dim, steady amber that isn't happy but is at least no longer violet.

I give her the space to do it. I do a round of the room. I check on Hess, who has finally — and this is rare — moved on from his theories and is now simply sitting with his beer looking at the middle distance with the quiet melancholy of a man who has been right about too many things for it to bring him any satisfaction. I have a soft spot for Hess, I'll be honest about that. He's irritating and he's probably mostly harmless and life has clearly leaned on him until it got the shape it wanted.

I swing by the Vreth merchants, who want two glasses of something they call *thelvi,* which I don't have under that name but which I'm fairly confident maps to the dark Callian ferment I keep under the bar for specific occasions. I pour it and they make the facial gesture of satisfaction, which on a Vreth involves expanding the nasal ridge and goes from alarming to charming once you know what it means.

The man from the second booth doesn't come back.

I do a quiet, methodical sweep of the things he left behind: nothing. No glass still there — he took it with him. No shed clothing, no forgotten data chip, no personal effects of any kind. He could be nothing. He could be a cautious traveller who finished his drink and left. He could be someone who sat for forty minutes in a shadow watching the bar and then walked out when a stranger finished her whisky.

I've been in enough places to know which kind of nothing I'm looking at.

When I get back to Sable's end of the bar, the plate is mostly clear and she looks — not better, exactly, but more *present.* Like someone came back to an apartment and turned the lights on.

"Good?" I ask.

"Better than good," she admits, like making the admission costs her some small pride. "What is that spice?"

"Trade secret. I've been trying to get it out of Dorem for eleven months."

She glances toward Dorem's corner booth. Dorem is, as always, simply occupying his space, an enormous and baroque presence that the room has long since arranged itself around. "Which one is Dorem?"

"The one that looks like he was carved out of a coastal cliff."

Her nodes flicker — warm amber, brief. Not quite a laugh, but related to one. She sets her fork down and her hands fold on the bar and she looks at me with the expression of someone who has been putting off a thing and has just now decided to stop putting it off.

"The man in the back booth," she says.

So she clocked him too. "Yeah."

"He was watching me."

"Or the bar in general," I say, even though we both know that's not what he was doing.

She gives me a look that contains several dimensions of commentary about that suggestion. "He's gone now."

"He is."

"That's not better." She says it flatly, like a fact. "That is categorically not better."

"Agreed." I pour myself a glass of water, which is my version of sitting down.
