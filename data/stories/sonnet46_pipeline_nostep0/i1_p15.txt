# Therapeutic Boundaries

The table was upholstered in that particular shade of green that exists nowhere in nature except exam rooms, and Marcus had been staring at the ceiling for eleven minutes before he stopped counting. The paper beneath him made a sound when he breathed. He was aware of this and could not stop being aware of it—the small, humiliating crinkle of his own respiration.

He turned his head.

On the wall to his left, laminated and slightly warped at the corner where the adhesive had given up, was an anatomical diagram of the knee joint. Anterior view, posterior view, lateral cross-section. The structures were labeled in that clinical sans-serif that looked like it cost nothing. *Medial collateral ligament. Lateral collateral ligament. Anterior cruciate ligament. Posterior cruciate ligament. Medial meniscus. Lateral meniscus.*

He read it like a man reading an indictment.

The ACL was listed between a dotted reference line and a small black arrow. The meniscus label curved along the cartilaginous crescent like a banner. Both of his, on the left side, were currently in a state the surgeon had described as *significant structural compromise*, which was the medical community's way of saying: the thing that made you what you were is no longer the thing it was. He had watched the MRI images on the doctor's monitor for a long time without speaking. The images were gray and white and full of what was no longer there.

He was here because the team policy required it. The clause was in his contract, paragraph nine, subsection C: *player agrees to engage fully with all prescribed rehabilitation protocols as directed by team medical staff as a condition of continued roster status and insurance coverage.* He had read it four times. He was here because the alternative was losing the coverage that was paying for the surgery that had reconstructed the knee that had ended his season, which might end his career, which had already ended the version of himself he'd been building for twenty-six years. He had not chosen to be here in any sense of the word *choose* that he recognized.

The door opened.

He heard her before he registered her. The sound of the latch, a step, the particular efficiency of someone who moves through professional spaces with purpose—not hurried, not leisurely, exactly calibrated. He was looking at the ceiling again. He heard a clipboard lifted from its hook on the door, the faint compression of a pen click.

"Marcus Reeves." Not a question. A confirmation against a list.

"Yeah."

He turned his head. She was reading the chart. Her hair was pulled back. She wore dark scrubs and a lanyard with an ID he couldn't read from the table. The way she stood had that particular quality of someone who has learned to take up exactly the space they need—no more, no less. Her hands, when she turned a page, were precise. He noticed the hands clinically, the way you notice tools laid out on a tray.

She looked up.

The moment required less than a second to happen and it happened in his chest first, before his face did anything, before his brain completed the sentence. There was a gap—a white space—and then the name closed in around it.

*Dani Reyes.*

Not a name he'd thought in—he couldn't count. Four years. The image that arrived was not chosen. It simply arrived: a gymnasium in Phoenix, late round of regionals, the year he'd made All-State for the second time and she'd taken second in the state decathlon and they'd argued about whose sport required more from the human body and he'd been wrong and she'd known she was right and hadn't rubbed it in, which had been almost worse. She'd been seventeen. She'd had that same quality of spatial efficiency even then, and he'd noticed it without a framework for what he was noticing.

She was looking at him now with an expression so composed it was its own form of information.

"Hey," she said. The word contained nothing. She said it the way you say *hey* to someone you knew well enough once that the word is necessary and brief enough that it closes the door behind itself. Then she looked back at the chart. "Left knee. ACL reconstruction and medial meniscus repair. You're six weeks post-op."

"Six and a half."

She made a notation. "Are you weighting the leg?"

"Partial."

"How much?"

"Enough to walk."

She wrote something. She moved to the side of the table without looking at him and set the clipboard on the counter, and he tracked her peripherally while trying not to track her. She had *arrived* somewhere. That was the thing he couldn't find the right shape for. She'd gone to school, gotten certified, gotten a position with a program that placed her here, in this room, with that composure, on Thursday mornings. She'd taken the thing she was and built something adjacent and lateral and apparently livable from it. And he was on a table that crinkled when he breathed because the thing he was had been interrupted.

"I'm going to assess your current range of motion," she said, "and then we'll begin a manual therapy protocol based on what I find. You'll tell me when you hit your pain threshold."

"Okay."

"You're on the team's plan, so we'll see each other twice weekly to start." She glanced at him once. "I'm aware this is odd."

He said nothing.

"I'd rather say it directly than not say it," she said. "But it doesn't change the work."

"Sure," he said.

She moved to the foot of the table and positioned herself at his left side. She was taller than he remembered, or maybe he was horizontal, which had a way of adjusting perspectives. She placed one hand beneath his left heel—palm flat, the pads of her fingers along the tendon—and her other hand at the lateral aspect of his knee, thumb and forefinger bracketing the joint without pressure, just contact. The heel hand was warm. The knee hand was warm.

He breathed.

"I'm going to take it into flexion," she said. "Tell me when."

She began to bring the heel toward the table, raising the knee, and for the first two degrees it was nothing, and then it was a pulling sensation along the lateral compartment of the joint, and then it was—

He exhaled through his nose.

"That's twenty-eight degrees," she said. "Where are you on a scale of one to ten."

"Four."

She held it. He was aware of her holding it, the gentle maintenance of position—not increasing the pressure, simply sustaining it. She was looking at the leg. She was not looking at his face, which he was grateful for.

"Active range is just under thirty degrees," she said. "That's below where we'd expect at six and a half weeks. You've been favoring it more than you're supposed to."

"I've been walking."

"And you've been walking incorrectly," she said, without any edge to it. "Your hip is compensating. Your quad is not engaging the way it should. You've been protecting the knee by overworking everything around it, which is going to create secondary problems."

She lowered the leg. He had not noticed until she lowered it how carefully she was holding it, how she'd been bearing part of its weight so that the joint was unsupported only in the directions that were teaching it something.

"I noticed it when you came in," she said. "The way you walked from the door."

"You watched me walk in."

"That's what I do." She moved to the counter and opened a drawer. "I watch how people move. I've been doing it for a while."

He looked at the diagram on the wall. *Anterior cruciate ligament.* Two words and a dotted line. He had torn his at minute thirty-one of the second half against Charlotte, planting on a cut he'd made ten thousand times, and the sound had preceded the pain by half a second, a small industrial sound, and the pain had arrived so complete and sudden that he'd thought for a moment he'd been hit by something, a collision he'd missed, and he'd looked up expecting to find a player behind him, but there was nothing behind him, just the court and the crowd noise dropping and then arriving louder.

"Have you been doing the exercises from your outpatient discharge?" she asked, her back to him.

"Some of them."

She turned and looked at him.

"Most of them," he said.

"We'll find out," she said, and there was something so flat and unapologetic in it that it almost made him want to laugh, and then it almost made him want to say something that was not monosyllabic, and he stopped himself.

She told him to sit up slowly, to bring his legs over the side of the table. He did this with the performance of someone who has been pretending something doesn't hurt for long enough that the performance has almost become the thing itself. She watched him without comment.

"I want you to try to straighten the left leg fully," she said. "Seated position, without support. Just extend it."

He did.

The knee came to about a hundred and sixty degrees—eight degrees short of full extension—before the tightness in the posterior capsule and the scar tissue running through the joint delivered a specific, grinding complaint that was not sharp but continuous, a deep pressure that communicated clearly: this is the edge. He went to the edge and stopped.

"Okay," she said. "Hold it."

He held it. His quad was shaking. He was aware of the shake. He tightened against it, which made the shake worse.

"Don't fight the shake," she said.

"I'm not."

She looked at him.

"I'm aware of it," he said.

"That's the muscle trying to maintain a contraction it can't fully recruit yet," she said. "It doesn't mean you're weak. It means the communication between your nervous system and the quad is still re-calibrating after surgery. That's normal."

"I know it's normal."

"Then stop clenching your jaw," she said.

He had not been aware he was clenching his jaw.

He lowered the leg.

She had him lie back. She was moving around the table now with a kind of choreographic efficiency—she knew where she was going to stand before she got there, she knew where her hands were going to be before they arrived. She placed the leg flat. She positioned herself at his left side, facing the knee, and brought her hands to it—right hand cupping the posterior aspect of the knee, left hand just distal to the joint on the tibial crest. She was not pressing. She was learning.

"Tell me if this is uncomfortable," she said, and began a small posterior glide on the tibia—a gentle, rocking mobilization, her thumbs applying alternating pressure on the posterior joint line, assessing the joint capsule's compliance, feeling for restriction. The motion was small and oscillating, barely visible, barely—the word he found for it was *precise*. It was almost impersonal. It was almost the most personal thing that had ever happened to his body.

He looked at the ceiling.

"You have restriction in the posterior capsule," she said. "That's expected. It'll take consistent work to restore. Some of this is scar tissue, some is neural guarding—your body learned to protect this joint and it hasn't been taught to stop yet."

"How long."

"Months," she said. "Probably five or six before we'd consider you for return-to-play protocols. Realistically, if it works well, eight."

Eight months.

He said nothing.

She continued the mobilization. The pressure of her thumbs was specific—directed posteriorly and slightly inferiorly, targeting the posterior capsule's inferior recess, a small geography of tissue he had never thought about in his life and which now occupied an enormous portion of his attention. He noticed, at some remove, that the pain was not the only thing he was registering. What he noticed first was pain. Then underneath the pain, at a depth he could not immediately access with any clinical language, was the fact of her hands—the warmth, the sureness. He tried to reclassify it. He filed it under *therapeutic contact.* The filing did not hold.

She slowed the mobilization and stopped. She rested her hands for a moment—not moving them, just resting—and the stillness was different from the movement. He was not certain she was aware of this. Then she repositioned for the next intervention and the moment passed and he was not certain it had been a moment.

"I'm going to move into a passive stretch now," she said. "Hamstring and posterior capsule combined. I'll take the leg to the edge of your range and hold it. Your job is to not help me and not fight me. Don't engage the quad, don't resist. Just let the leg be heavy."

"Okay."

She lifted his left leg at the heel and the knee. She raised it slowly—fifteen, twenty, thirty degrees of hip flexion, the knee remaining at roughly the end-range of extension—and the pulling sensation was immediate and persistent, like a rope being drawn taut through the back of the joint. He breathed.

"Good," she said. "You're gripping."

"I know."

"Where in your body?"

"Hip."

"Let it go."

He tried to let the hip go. The difficulty of this—of surrendering muscular control to another person's hands while lying flat with your leg in the air and your entire self arranged in a position of pure structural dependency—was not a difficulty he had anticipated and could not entirely strategize against. He had spent twenty-six years in a body that he directed. A body that executed what he decided. The leg being held by her hands was his leg and it was also just a limb she was positioning, and the distinction between these two things was narrow enough and terrible enough that he had to study the ceiling with considerable care.

"Better," she said. "Stay there."

She held it.

In the silence of the hold, with the stretch burning low and continuous along the posterior chain, a gymnasium arrived uninvited—not Phoenix, a different one, away tournament his junior year, the practice facility, late, and Dani doing hamstring stretches on the floor beside him because they'd both stayed late for different reasons and ended up in the same room, and she'd been talking about something, a coach who'd told her she'd plateaued, and she'd said *I don't believe in plateaus, I think people just get comfortable with where they are* and he had thought at the time it was slightly arrogant and now on the table with his hamstring on fire he understood that she had been describing a discipline he hadn't yet needed and now needed entirely and could not access.

His body then: running so far inside its capacity that the effort was pure pleasure, a long-distance surplus, the kind of physical life that feels immortal specifically because it cannot conceive of its interruption.

His body now: thirty-degree passive hip flexion producing a burning six out of ten that he was reporting as four.

"Scale," she said.

"Four," he said.

She said nothing for a moment. She made a small, slow adjustment—barely increased the angle, perhaps two degrees—and held.

"Four," she said.

"Yes."

Another silence.

"Okay," she said, and he heard in the word something that was not belief.

She lowered the leg with the same deliberation with which she'd raised it, and her hands departed the heel and the knee in that order, and he was aware of the departure.

She moved to the counter and made notes on the clipboard. He watched the side of her face. She had an expression of concentration that was completely unperformed—she was thinking about something specific and showing no awareness of being observed, and in the three seconds before she turned back he found himself in the uncomfortable position of seeing her as a person who existed continuously, who had been somewhere while he'd been forgetting to think about her, who'd built something in the intervening years that had nothing to do with him but to which he was now subject.

"Last technique today," she said, turning back. "I want to try a resisted stretch. You're going to engage against me—I'll apply a stretch through the joint, and you'll try to produce a contraction against my resistance. Neuromuscular re-education. It's going to be uncomfortable."

"Fine."

She repositioned herself at his left side, closer now, her body angled toward the foot of the table. She placed her right hand on the anterior surface of his lower leg, her left beneath the knee. "When I say push, you try to extend the knee against my resistance. You won't get full range—that's not the goal. The contraction is the goal. On three."

"Okay."

"One, two—push."

He pushed.

The sensation was—it was different. The passive stretch had been something done to him. This was his own force meeting her force, the structures of the joint loaded in both directions simultaneously, his quad firing at partial capacity into the resistance of her hands, which were not yielding. She was applying a stretch as he was contracting against it, so his body was fighting in two directions, the muscle working and the joint opening and the scar tissue protesting the combination with something between a burn and a roar.

He heard himself exhale—a rough, abbreviated sound that he did not entirely choose.

"Good," she said. "Hold it."

He held it. His hands found the edges of the table. He gripped them.
