# The Azra Gambit
### *The Offer*

---

The transfer vessel's air filtration system hums with a persistent mechanical drone.

Arthur Neegan has been listening to it for six hours. He has counted the variations in its pitch — three, cycling every ninety seconds — and has decided that the middle tone is slightly flat, the result of a worn impeller bearing that nobody has bothered to replace. It is the kind of detail an engineer notices. It is the kind of detail that keeps an engineer from thinking about larger things.

Larger things, for instance, like the two armed men seated across the cabin, whose courtesy has not once flickered into friendliness.

They had come to his homestead at dawn — four of them, in a crawler that bore the dove-and-compass insignia of the Antares Diplomatic Corps on its flank. Arthur had recognised it immediately. Most colonists would have. The Antares Corps operated in the space between governments, which was to say it operated wherever governments preferred not to be seen operating. *Diplomatic* was, as a descriptor, doing considerable work.

Their leader — a lean woman who had introduced herself only as Coordinator Voss — had smiled the kind of smile that arrives pre-assembled. She had used the word *invitation* four times in two minutes. Arthur had counted those, too.

He stares now at the porthole. Mars has become a smear of ochre at the window's edge, shrinking with the patient indifference of a planet that has watched men scramble across it for thirty years and expects to watch them scramble for thirty thousand more. He thinks of the survey report folded in his breast pocket, though he does not need to read it. He has the numbers memorised.

*Vein depth: unconfirmed. Estimated surface yield: 40,000 metric tonnes. Projected extraction value at current Azra market rate —*

He stops the thought there.

---

The vessel docks with something larger. Arthur is not told what. He is escorted through two pressure locks and a corridor of brushed aluminium that smells of recycled air and machine oil, and then through a door that dilates rather than swings, and into a room that is trying very hard to suggest that none of this is sinister.

There are plants. Real ones — broad-leafed, impractical, expensive. A table of dark composite wood. Two chairs. A carafe of water with actual ice in it.

The man already seated rises as Arthur enters.

He is perhaps sixty, with the unhurried posture of someone who has not waited for anything in a long while, and the eyes of someone who has not been surprised in longer. His suit is grey. His hands, when he extends one, are steady.

"Mr. Neegan. Please." He gestures to the chair opposite. "I'm told the crossing is unpleasant. Do sit."

Arthur sits. "You have me at a disadvantage."

"Temporarily." The man pours water without being asked. "My name is Draven. I represent certain interests that have followed developments on Mars with considerable attention. Your survey, specifically, for the past eleven days." He sets the carafe down with the precision of a man who measures himself in small gestures. "We move quickly when the situation warrants."

"Eleven days." Arthur turns the glass in his hands. "Before I'd filed with the Colonial Land Office."

Draven smiles. It is a better smile than Voss's. More honest about what it is. "The Colonial Land Office is not the only institution with an interest in mineral surveys."

"No," Arthur says. "I imagine it isn't."

A silence opens between them with the quality of a calculated thing. Through the far bulkhead comes a low, subsonic vibration — the hum of something very large, doing something very deliberate. Arthur does not ask.

"Let me be direct," Draven says. "What sits beneath your claim, Mr. Neegan, is not a mine. It is, if our projections hold — and they will — roughly seven percent of all confirmed Azra deposits across the inner system. Seven percent." He lets the number breathe. "At present, the combined yield of every functioning Azra operation supplies perhaps a third of interstellar drive demand. Military demand goes unmet almost entirely. You understand what that means."

"It means whoever controls the vein controls a throttle," Arthur says. "On both commerce and armament."

Draven looks at him the way a man looks at a door he expected to be locked and finds open. "Precisely."

"And you want it."

"I want *you* to have it," Draven says, with the careful emphasis of a man who has rehearsed the distinction. "Legally, uncontestably, on record. The claim remains yours. We would provide — infrastructure. Capital. Security." He pauses. "There are other parties who will not be so considerate of the legal niceties."

Arthur thinks of the flat impeller bearing. Of things nobody bothers to fix until they fail entirely.

"What do you get?"

"Preferential extraction rights. Forty years, negotiable. A small oversight role in operations management." He opens his hands in a gesture of transparency so complete it almost resembles the real thing. "You become, Mr. Neegan, an extraordinarily wealthy man. Protected. Influential."

"Or?"

Draven tilts his head. "I beg your pardon?"

"You said other parties won't be considerate." Arthur sets his water down. "That implies an *or*."

The older man is quiet for a moment. Outside, the stars do not move. They never do.

"The vein will be extracted," Draven says finally, and his voice has lost nothing — not warmth, not precision, not that particular, terrible steadiness. "The only meaningful variable, Mr. Neegan, is whether you are present when it is."

Arthur looks at the plants. Real leaves. Real roots. Something alive, this far from anything living.

He thinks about what a man plants, and what gets planted around him.

"I'll need," he says slowly, "to see the terms in writing."
