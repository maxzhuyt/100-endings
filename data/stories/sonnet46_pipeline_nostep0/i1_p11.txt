# Overthinking It

The thing about being the kind of person who notices everything is that you have to live with having noticed it.

She had noticed, for instance, that the projector in Mr. Calloway's room made a sound like a question every twelve minutes or so — a high, thin squeak, almost querulous, as though the machine itself were uncertain about something. She had noticed this in September. It was now November. She had a spreadsheet's worth of data on the projector squeak and she had never once chosen to have it.

She noticed the way Marcus Fell tapped his pen — not nervously, not rhythmically, just occasionally, like punctuation, like he was agreeing with something in his own head. She noticed that he kept his notes in blue ink except for diagrams, which he did in pencil, probably because diagrams were provisional and he understood that. She noticed that he had once, in early October, held the door for someone who was still very far away, without making it weird, which was statistically difficult. She had a file on Marcus Fell the same way she had a file on the projector squeak. She had files on everything. This was not a skill she had developed. This was the factory setting.

"Pairs for the ecosystem project," Mr. Calloway said, consulting the sheet with the mild authority of someone announcing train departures. "These are assigned, not chosen, so please hold your feelings."

She was already holding her feelings. She was very experienced at this.

"Priya and Dani. Josh and Carter. Lydia—" and here she became briefly unable to hear due to blood— "and Marcus."

The projector squeaked.

---

She estimated she had forty-five minutes remaining in class. She had been paired with Marcus Fell for approximately eleven seconds and she was already tired in the way she got tired, which was a specific kind of tired that had nothing to do with sleep and everything to do with the sheer metabolic cost of being herself in proximity to something she cared about.

The assignment was straightforward. You selected an ecosystem. You divided the research. You would present in two weeks. This was not complicated. This was, in fact, the entire structure of every group project she'd ever done, and she had done them successfully, and she knew how to do this, and none of that information was currently accessible.

He had been seated three rows over. He was now going to move to the empty desk beside her. This was going to happen in approximately — she watched him gathering his notebook, uncapping and recapping the blue pen — eight seconds.

*Say something about the project,* she thought. *Something opener-y but not try-hard. You're good at language. You literally got a 98 on the last essay and Mr. Calloway said it showed "surprising sophistication of voice" which you've been privately proud of for two weeks, so use the voice, Lydia, use the—*

He sat down.

"Hey," he said.

"Hey," she said.

The voice was unavailable.

---

The thing she wanted to say — the opener she had constructed in the eight seconds, which was, she knew even then, too short a runway — was something like *I was thinking we could do coral reefs, there's actually a lot happening right now with bleaching events that would give the project some stakes.* This was good. This was specific and showed she'd been thinking without showing she'd been thinking about him specifically, which she had, but that was private information. It demonstrated investment in the quality of the work. It offered a direction without being bossy. It was, she was fairly confident, the correct opening sentence.

She did not say it because, upon running it a second time in her head, she heard the word *stakes* and thought: does that sound like she looked up the rubric and found the word *stakes* there? Because she had, in fact, looked up the rubric, but the word had also just felt right independently. But how would she prove that? She couldn't caveat it. *I think we should do coral reefs — and I mean that organically, the word stakes is just where I landed, I'm not gaming the rubric.* No. No, that's worse. Scrap the whole thing.

She would ask what he was thinking. Open question. Shows she's collaborative. Good.

But if she asked what he was thinking, and he said he didn't know yet, then the next move was naturally hers, and she'd be back to the coral reef problem having lost the initiative. And if he had a strong opinion and she agreed with it immediately, did that read as agreeable or as having no perspective? She had a perspective. She had many perspectives. She had possibly too many.

Maybe start with something logistical. *Do you want to meet outside of class, or just handle it through email?* That was—

No. Because *email* was almost certainly how they were going to collaborate, and choosing email over meeting was a way of structuring their interaction so that she would have time to draft and revise, and she was aware of this about herself, and she suspected, with no evidence, that he might also somehow be aware of this about her, which was paranoid, she knew, but the paranoia was part of the system, not a flaw in it.

The projector squeaked.

She looked at the worksheet.

---

She had been told, more than once, that she was funny. In text messages, in essays, occasionally in conversation when she was comfortable enough to load the right program. Her mother said she had her grandfather's way with language, meaning precise and a little dry. She believed this about herself in the way that you could believe something completely without being able to access it on demand.

The version of herself in her own head was fluent. The version of herself in her own head could have said *coral reefs, there's a lot happening with bleaching events,* and it would have landed as smart and engaged and slightly unexpected, and he would have said *yeah, actually, I was thinking something current,* and they would have been off, talking about the project, which would have been fine, which would have been enough. She didn't need it to be a moment. She needed it to be a conversation, just one, just to demonstrate proof of concept, that she existed in three dimensions and not just in the marginalia of group projects.

She wanted, if she was being precise about it — and she was always precise about it, that was the problem — she wanted him to know that there was a version of her that was worth knowing. Not the blue-pen version, not the monosyllable. The one that noticed the coral reefs. The one that had an opinion about the rubric and also didn't care about the rubric, at the same time, and could explain that contradiction if given the time.

This felt, she was aware, like a large thing to want from a science partner.

She arranged her pencil parallel to the edge of the desk.

Then she noticed she was doing that, and she stopped, and then she couldn't figure out where to put her hands, and then she was thinking about her hands, which were objectively just hands, they were fine, this was insane.

---

"So," Marcus said.

Her whole nervous system stood up.

"Ecosystem."

She had one and a half seconds. She knew she had one and a half seconds. She had, in a sense, been preparing for this conversation for eight weeks.

"Yeah," she said. "Did you have—"

"I was thinking tidal zones, maybe? My brother did coral reefs last year and his project got used as a display, so I figured if we do something similar we might be compared, but also tidal zones are kind of interesting because everyone forgets about them."

She looked at him.

"Tidal zones are genuinely underrated," she said.

This was not what she had planned. This was not scripted. She did not have time to find the flaw.

"Right?" he said.

"Like people always want to go dramatic — rainforests, coral reefs — but the intertidal zone has this insane range of stress tolerance and the organisms are basically doing extreme sports all day."

"Extreme sports," he repeated, and he wasn't laughing at her, he was doing a small, adjacent thing that was more like he was filing it away.

She felt, for approximately three seconds, like herself.

The projector squeaked.

She noticed him notice it. He looked up at the ceiling and looked back down.

*He's looked up at the ceiling before,* she registered. *He's heard it too. He's wondered about it.*

Or he'd looked up at the ceiling because there was a sound and looking was the default human response to sound. She had no way of knowing. She would have the rest of class to not know it.

---

They divided the research. She took chemical conditions and biodiversity; he took human impact and climate data. This was organized, actually. This was going fine.

She said "that works" when he proposed the division, which was slightly too little — she could have said something about the research being interconnected, which it was, which might have opened a secondary conversation — but it wasn't nothing. It wasn't a disaster. She was grading on a curve she hadn't disclosed to anyone.

Then she said, filling a pause that didn't strictly need filling: "There are good primary sources for the intertidal research if you want to just — I can send them."

"Yeah, definitely."

"Or I can put them in a shared doc."

"Sure, yeah."

"Both, maybe. Both is fine. That's — yeah."

She stopped talking.

He had already looked back at the worksheet. He was not replaying what she'd said. He was reading about ecosystem structure and possibly thinking about lunch. This was objectively fine. She replayed what she'd said twelve times in the next ninety seconds, which was also, she understood, not a productive use of her time, and she did it anyway, because production had very little to do with it.

---

The projector made its sound again, and she looked up at it automatically, and she thought about the twelve-minute interval, and she thought: *I have been sitting here for at least thirty-six minutes,* which meant class was almost over, which meant she had not done anything catastrophic, and she had not done anything remarkable, and those were, she supposed, equally true and equally hers.

She observed that Marcus Fell was tapping his pen. Just occasionally. Just punctuating something. He looked at the worksheet with an expression that communicated nothing except the presence of thought, faint and ordinary. He existed in his own body without apparent negotiations. She found this beautiful in a way she knew would sound unhinged if said aloud, so she kept it in the file.

He looked up briefly. She didn't move her eyes in time.

It might have been nothing. It was probably nothing. He might have just been surfacing from the worksheet the way you surfaced from a worksheet, blinking, and she happened to be in the direction he surfaced. Or he had noticed her. Or both, technically, in sequence. She had no access to his data.

She would think about this for probably four to six days.

---

When the bell rang, she was almost ready for it.

She was putting the worksheet into her folder — carefully, because she was the kind of person who was careful about that, and she had stopped apologizing for it — when he stood and held his notebook against his chest and said, very simply, as if he'd said it a hundred times, which he had probably said something like it a hundred times:

"See you Thursday."

Not *see you.* Not *bye.* See you Thursday. A specificity. A temporal commitment. An acknowledgment that Thursday was going to happen and they would both be participants in it.

"Yeah," she said. "Thursday."

He left. The door swung behind him.

She sat for a moment in the empty room, which was now mostly empty, the other students evaporating into the hallway with the ease of people who left rooms when rooms ended. She was still holding her folder. She was, she realized, smiling, just slightly, at the worksheet inside it, which was not embarrassing because no one was watching, and even if they were, she was going to allow herself this one small private thing.

*See you Thursday* meant nothing. She was absolutely certain of this. It was the conventional close of a school interaction between two people who would see each other on Thursday. It had the same informational content as a period at the end of a sentence. She would not build on it. She would not inflate it. She would carry it into Thursday like a very small stone in her pocket and not tell anyone it was there.

The projector squeaked.

She picked up her bag and went to class.
