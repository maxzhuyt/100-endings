# The Underhold

The door opened without resistance.

That alone should have told her something.

Aya's fingers had barely grazed the iron handle when the weight of it surrendered, swinging inward on hinges too well-oiled for a room supposedly sealed by both conjuration and padlock. The darkness beyond breathed out at them — cold, damp, smelling of brine and tallow and something older underneath, something that had been pressed down here long enough to forget what light tasted like.

"Easy," Simeon murmured behind her left shoulder. She could hear the tension in him, the careful calibration of a man deciding whether to reach for his flintlock. "Something's—"

"Wrong," Katra finished. Her voice was barely a thread. "Aya."

But Aya was already stepping through.

The underhold resolved itself in the light of Katra's lantern: a vaulted chamber, low-ceilinged and sweating stone, fitted along its walls with iron cradles that held gas lamps now burning at full flame. Someone had lit them recently. The shadows they cast were directional, deliberate — theatrical, almost — pooling at the feet of the four figures arranged in a loose semicircle at the chamber's far end.

Four figures, and at their centre, Councillor Voss.

*Oh,* thought Aya. *Oh, that's rather bad.*

Maren Voss, guildmaster of the Morska, was not in irons. She was not in a cell. She was dressed, if anything, better than Aya had ever seen her — black wool coat, brass-buttoned, the kind of garment that implied both means and malice. Her hair was silver at the temples and dark everywhere else, pulled back from a face that had long since decided it owed nothing to softness. The two bodyguards flanking her were Cray and the one they called the Deacon, both of them built like things that had been assembled rather than born. The fourth figure Aya did not immediately recognise — a man in a grey coat, standing slightly apart, watching with the still attention of someone tallying a ledger.

Voss regarded the three of them with the precise expression of a woman who had been expecting company and found it punctual.

"Close the door," she said.

No one moved.

"Close. The door." The second iteration was softer than the first, which made it considerably worse.

Simeon pulled it shut behind them. The latch caught with a sound like a held breath, released.

"Guildmaster," Aya began.

"You'll wait," said Voss.

Aya waited. She was aware of Katra at her right, perfectly still in the particular way that meant she was counting exits and finding none. She was aware of Simeon's hand hovering near his coat. She was aware, most acutely, of the artifact's absence — the dais at the chamber's far left wall stood empty, the conjuration locks hanging open, the velvet cradle bearing only an impression of where the thing had rested.

*Someone got here first.* The cold of that realisation spread down through her sternum. *Or never left.*

Voss walked toward them slowly, hands clasped behind her back, in no apparent hurry. She stopped three feet from Aya. Her gaze moved over the mask — hammered brass, fitted close, shaped in the neutral likeness that had become something of an emblem in Morska's lower wards — and something crossed her face that was almost appreciation.

"I have been watching little *Hollow-Face* for some months," she said. "Moving through my house like smoke. Taking work. Taking rank." She tilted her head. "Taking liberties."

*She knows.* How much she knew, Aya couldn't yet measure, but the word *liberties* sat against her ribs like a lit match.

Cray moved first. His hands were on her before she could summon the cold place behind her eyes where the immateria lived — thick fingers catching her wrists, wrenching them up and back, and then the mask was being pulled from her face with the matter-of-fact efficiency of a man shelling something. It came away with a sound like a cork drawn. The lantern-light hit her full and unmediated.

The silence that followed had texture to it.

Voss studied her. Behind those grey eyes, Aya watched her processing it — the youth, the bone structure, the cut of the coat beneath the guild-cloak — and she watched the moment it resolved into recognition.

"Hollowick," Voss said. Not a question. "Aldric Hollowick's daughter."

*There it is.* The name landed in the room like a coal dropped on dry wood.

Simeon made a sound. Katra did not.

Aya breathed through her nose. Her wrists were still pinned. She thought, with the detached clarity that appeared in her at moments of pure crisis, that she had perhaps four seconds before this became irreversible.

"Before you do whatever you're considering," she said, "you might want to think carefully about what I'm worth to you alive."

Voss raised an eyebrow. "Is that right."

"My father sits three chairs from the Trade Consul." She kept her voice even — not cold, not afraid, something more useful than either. "He has the ear of Harwick's harbourmaster and two of the four Port Authority commissioners. You have seventeen whalers logged with that authority this season, Guildmaster. That I know of." She let that settle. "Whatever you decide to do with me in the next few minutes, you will be doing it to a Hollowick. I'd encourage you to think about what that costs."

Far above and beyond the guild walls, past the cold stone and the gas-lit fog, a foghorn sounded across the harbour. Once. Twice. A third time, long and low, like something enormous passing in the dark.

Voss was quiet for a long moment. Her gaze moved from Aya to the empty dais, then back.

"Your mother," she said carefully, "had a certain kind of courage. I always thought it a great waste, what they did to her."

The match caught.

Aya said nothing. In the ringing silence where a response should have been, she felt the immateria stir behind her eyes like cold water finding a crack — and she kept it there, leashed, because she was not her mother, and she intended to prove it.

"Bind them," said Voss. "All three. We'll talk in the morning, when I've had time to consider the *value* of what I'm holding."

Cray's grip shifted to accommodate rope. Aya felt the cord bite her wrists and did not pull against it. She watched the man in the grey coat, who had not moved, who had not spoken, who had stood through all of it with the composed interest of a man watching weather develop over water. He met her gaze once, briefly, and looked away.

*You,* she thought. *I'll want to know who you are.*

They were walked to the chamber's right wall, where three iron rings were bolted at roughly shoulder height, and the rope was threaded through and knotted with competence. Katra was placed last. Through the whole of it she said nothing, only watched the room with dark, systematic eyes. Simeon said one thing — a short word under his breath that Aya declined to repeat — and then he too was quiet.

The Deacon extinguished two of the wall lamps. The shadows reasserted themselves, softer now and less theatrical, pulling the edges of the room back into ambiguity. Voss crossed to the far side of the chamber and conferred in low tones with the man in grey. Cray stationed himself near the door. The Deacon found a stool and sat on it, apparently capable of enormous patience.

Aya let her head rest back against the stone. It was cold enough to feel medicinal.

"Are you hurt?" Katra asked, barely audible.

"No." She moved her fingers experimentally. The circulation was restricted but present. "You?"

"I've been tied up in worse places." A pause. "Though I'd have trouble naming one at this specific moment."

Simeon exhaled carefully through his nose. "The artifact," he said. Very quiet. "It was already gone."

"Yes."

"Which means—"

"Later." She said it gently, because it wasn't that she disagreed with his thinking, only that working through it in front of Voss's people was a gift she had no intention of giving. She glanced at Cray, who appeared to be studying the middle distance with professional disinterest. She glanced at the Deacon, who had his eyes half-closed. Resting them, or pretending to.

She kept her own eyes open. From this angle she could see Voss's hands moving as she spoke — careful hands, deliberate, the kind that never gestured loosely. The man in grey had produced a small notebook from his coat and was consulting it rather than writing in it, which told her something she wasn't yet sure how to use.

*Think,* she instructed herself. *You have until morning. That's not nothing.*

The foghorn sounded again, distant and directionless. The tide was coming in; she could feel it in the damp, the way the stone seemed to breathe more slowly. Somewhere above, the city of Harwick was managing its ordinary night — harbour-hands and chandlers and the low late traffic of the wards, all of it proceeding without any knowledge of this room, this rope, this cold that worked its way methodically through her coat.

Her mother's name remained in the air. Voss had put it there precisely, had watched for the reaction, and that meant she'd known it before tonight. Had perhaps known for longer than Aya cared to calculate what it was she was slowly reeling in.

*What they did to her.* She turned the phrasing over. Not *what happened to her.* Not even *what was done to her.* The passive construction with its careful pronoun: *they.* Voss had been at pains to separate herself from whatever *they* were, which could mean several different things, and she did not yet have enough information to know which.

She filed it. She filed the notebook. She filed the empty cradle and the unlocked conjuration and the door that had swung open without resistance to hands that had not earned the right.

She waited. The lamp-light steadied. Cray breathed slowly near the door.

Aya worked the rope against the iron ring, feeling for give, and found, on the third careful test, precisely the quarter-inch of tolerance she needed.

She did not smile. She was not her mother in that way either.

She waited.
