# Love in the Limelight

The door opened the way doors don't, in Llandrindod Wells, on a Tuesday in November — all at once, with a man attached to it.

He came through sideways, pulling it shut behind him with both hands, his back pressed against the glass, chest heaving, looking out at the street through the gap in the *Local Interest* display with the intensity of someone who owed money to the street. Then he turned, and the intensity rearranged itself into something else entirely. Something practiced. He looked around the shop — the low shelves, the iron radiator ticking against the far wall, the smell of old paper and the particular quality of silence that only accumulates over decades — and he produced a smile that Meredith suspected had its own publicist.

"Right," he said, still catching his breath. "Hello."

Meredith looked at him over the top of her reading glasses. She had been doing the accounts on the counter beside a mug of tea she'd been failing to drink for forty minutes. She looked back down at the accounts.

"Door latches," she said, "if you push it fully."

He pushed it fully. It latched.

Outside, at the end of the high street, she could hear what sounded like a diesel engine idling and several pairs of shoes going quickly across wet cobblestones, and she had a reasonable sense of the general situation. She was a woman who read, which meant she was a woman who paid attention, and she had been paying attention since he came through the door.

"I just need—" he started.

"Five minutes," she said. "Possibly ten, depending how fast they walk."

He blinked at her. The smile faltered very slightly, recalibrated, and came back broader. "You're a lifesaver."

"I'm an accountant this afternoon," she said. "Lifesaving is seasonal."

He laughed, which she noted was genuine — surprised out of him rather than offered. He ran a hand through his hair, which was the kind of deliberately disordered hair that required a great deal of ordering first, and looked around the shop again as though he were doing it properly this time. He was very tall. He was making the ceiling feel lower.

"Can I—"

"Yes," she said.

He wandered toward the nearest shelf, which happened to be Welsh Poetry, and she estimated he had not opened a poetry collection since school, if then. He pulled one out and turned it over in his hands with the careful studiousness of a man at a gallery standing in front of a painting he is not sure about. She gave him thirty seconds.

"Shelf label's accurate," she said, without looking up. "It is what it says it is."

He looked up at her. Then at the shelf label. Then back at her.

There was a version of this — she was aware of the version — where she would have been flushing from the ears down. She'd have recognized him the moment he came through the door, because she *had* recognized him the moment he came through the door, and there was a tier of person at which recognition was automatic and physical, like a reflex, something that happened before consent was involved. Declan Doyle. She'd seen four of his films. Her sister had seen all of them twice and had opinions about his jawline that she'd committed to text at length. He was, in the way of a very small number of human beings, more vivid in person — colour-corrected slightly above real life, moving with an ease that suggested he had never, in thirty-five years, had to think about where to put his hands.

She had finished flushing approximately forty-five seconds ago, behind the reliable cover of the accounts ledger, and was now fine.

"I was going to buy it," he said, sounding only slightly aggrieved.

"You weren't," she said.

He put the poetry collection back. He came and rested his forearms on the other side of the counter, the way people did when they wanted to be charming and thought decreasing the distance was part of the strategy.

"You always this welcoming?"

"I welcomed you in," she said. "It's not a large building. There's a limit to how much further in you can be welcomed."

"Right." He studied her. She could feel him studying her the way she could feel a draught — directional, persistent. "You know, most people—"

"I'm sure," she said.

Something shifted in his face. Not offence — something more interesting than offence. She had the impression of a man encountering a locked door in a house where all the others had swung open at his approach, and who could not quite locate the handle.

"I'm Declan," he said.

"I know who you are," she said.

"Okay." A pause. "And you're—"

"Open Tuesday through Saturday." She turned a page in the ledger. "Nine to five, half-day Thursdays."

He laughed again. Shorter this time, less certain of itself. "That's not quite what I—"

"Meredith," she said. "Since you're staying."

He smiled at her — different from the first smile, fewer teeth — and she found she preferred it.

---

He didn't stand still well. He prowled the shop the way people prow who are accustomed to spaces shaped around their presence, adjusting himself whenever he felt the room stop looking at him, which in this case was immediately and constantly, because the room was Meredith and she was doing the accounts. He trailed his fingers along the spines of the Penguin Classics. He read the notice board. He spent some time in front of *Local Interest* holding a laminated map of walking routes in the Elan Valley with the expression of a man who had never knowingly walked to a destination he couldn't also have been driven to.

"What's up here?" he said, tapping a trail.

"Reservoirs. Kite country."

"Red kites?"

"We've not imported any other kind."

He put the map down. "You don't do—" He waved his hand. "Small talk."

"I do accounts," she said. "Small talk is the adjacent shelf."

"Right." He came back to the counter. He seemed, she thought, slightly restless in a way that wasn't about the paparazzi outside, or not only. He leaned against the shelf beside her end of the counter, arms folded, and looked at the ceiling beams, which were low enough that he'd had to angle his head once or twice when he'd gone near them. "How long have you had this place?"

She considered the question for its intentions and found them more or less genuine. "Eight years. Bought it from the man who ran it for thirty before that. He's still particular about where the Penguins go."

"Does he come in and move them?"

"Now and then. We've reached an arrangement."

"What arrangement?"

She looked over her glasses. "I let him move them. He pretends I don't move them back."

Declan Doyle smiled at the ceiling. It was slow coming this time, unrolled rather than deployed, and she thought: there it is. There's the actual one.

She looked at her ledger.

"Why the books?" he said.

"Better than the alternative."

"Which was?"

"Everything else," she said.

"That's very—" He tilted his head. "That's a way of answering without answering."

"Mm." She turned a page. "Runs in the family."

He laughed, and this time it caught in the back of his throat and he pressed his lips together and looked away, which she found — she noted, without voicing — unexpectedly endearing.

---

Outside, the diesel engine moved on.

Inside, neither of them mentioned it.

He had gone back to wandering, more slowly now, the performance of looking-at-books giving way to something more like actually seeing them. He stopped at the small biography section in the back corner and stood there for a moment with his hands in his jacket pockets.

"My mum has this one," he said, pulling out a paperback life of Marie Curie — her best-selling biography — and holding it up.

"Good taste."

"She has— She had it on her bedside table." He turned it over. His voice had done something small but distinct, a compression. "She died in March."

Meredith stopped writing.

He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at the back cover, which he wasn't reading.

"I keep starting it," he said. "And then — I get to about page forty and I—" He put it back on the shelf, carfully, spine out, straight. "I don't know. I put it down. She'd already read it twice. She'd have wanted to talk about it." He moved his jaw. "Sorry. That's—I don't know why I said that."

She took her glasses off.

The shop was very quiet. The radiator ticked. Outside, someone's terrier clicked past on the wet pavement.

"She sounds like she read properly," Meredith said.

He looked over at her. "What does that mean?"

"Reading something twice isn't finishing it. It's beginning it again." She set her glasses on the counter. "Most people read books. Some people live in them for a while. They're different activities."

He looked at her for a moment in a way she couldn't quite annotate.

"She'd have liked you," he said.

"She'd have liked the shop."

"Both," he said. His voice had gone simple. No curve toward a punchline. Nothing at the end of the sentence waiting to happen. "Both, I think."

Meredith said nothing. She found she didn't want to dilute whatever was sitting in the air between them by putting words in it. She had, she realised, been expecting someone essentially hollow, a beautiful echo chamber who had never in his life needed to be actual, because actual had never been required of him. She revised.

She picked her glasses back up and put them on and looked at the ledger, which she was not reading.

---

He came back to the counter and stood on the other side of it and looked at the shelf behind her — the one she kept her own books on, the reading copies, the ones with cracked spines and margin pencil and the occasional coffee ring — and he leaned forward before she could stop him and read the spines out at random. Chekhov. Le Carré. Three different editions of *Rebecca.* A Patrick O'Brian she'd been working through with more pleasure than she'd expected.

"Three *Rebeccas,*" he said.

"The editions matter."

"How do editions matter?"

"Different translators — no, wait, it's not translated. Different introductions. Different typesetting. A book you read in a paperback at twenty is a different conversation than the same book read in hardcover at thirty-eight." She paused. "I'm thirty-eight."

"I know," he said.

She looked at him.

He looked back. There was a beat where both of them were in the beat and knew they were in it, and she felt the counter between them as something more precise than furniture — a boundary, a negotiation, a thing she was grateful for and aware of simultaneously.

"The Patrick O'Brian," he said, pointing without quite reaching past her. "Is it good?"

"Intensely. Do you want it?"

"Only if you're done."

"I'm not done."

"Then I'll wait," he said, and they both knew he meant something else and neither of them said so.

---

His phone went with the specific vibration pattern of someone who'd been trying urgently for some time. He glanced at it, and his face did something complicated — the arrival of the outside world written across his features as something like reluctance. She hadn't expected reluctance. She'd expected relief, the natural habitat of a man re-entering the life that fit him. But he stood with the phone in his hand and didn't move for a moment.

"Your people," she said.

"Various of them," he said. He looked at the screen. "Car's come round."

"Good."

He came up to the counter. He put his phone face-down on it, which was a choice. "I read badly," he said. "Slow. I was embarrassed about it for a long time. Still am, a bit." He said it simply, no ornamentation, not looking for anything in return. "Don't know why that feels relevant."

"It doesn't have to be relevant," she said. "Things are allowed to just be true."

He looked at her again with that same unannotatable quality.

"I'll finish the Curie," he said.

"Good. She's worth the conversation."

He picked his phone up. He turned toward the door, and then turned back, and she got the impression this was not calculated — that the turn back was something happening ahead of the decision to do it.

"If I came in again—"

"Tuesday through Saturday," she said. "Nine to five. Half-day Thursdays."

His mouth did something that was almost a smile and was better than one. "Right," he said. "I'll bear that in mind."

He went to the door and did the thing where he checked through the gap in the *Local Interest* display — the way he'd come in, full circle — and whatever he saw satisfied him. He pulled the door open. Cold air and the smell of rain came in.

"Meredith," he said.

"Mm."

"Thank you. For the—" He gestured at the shop, the whole of it, all the things they hadn't said.

"The accounts," she said.

"The accounts," he agreed.

The door latched behind him.

---

She stood for a moment with her glasses in her hand.

Then she put them on and looked at the accounts, and the accounts were incomprehensible, and she looked at the shelf behind her — the reading copies, the margin pencil, the three editions that were the same conversation about different things — and her eyes snagged on the space on the poetry shelf where he'd replaced the collection, which he'd done with the spine facing in.

She went and turned it out.

*R. S. Thomas. Collected Poems.*

She thought about a woman with this book on her bedside table, a woman she would never meet, who had apparently read it twice.

She stood there with the reasonable winter quiet of the shop around her and the radiator's patient ticking and the feeling that was moving through her chest, which she declined to name, and which had the particular quality of something she had actively not shown, which meant — she knew herself well enough to know — that it was not going to be quick in going.

She put the Thomas back.

She went and sat at the counter.

She picked up her pen, looked at the ledger, and did not do the accounts.
