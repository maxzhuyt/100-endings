# Stolen Moments in Roma

---

The Trevi Fountain smelled of sunscreen and ambition.

Ben Aldrich stood at the edge of the crowd with his guidebook open to page 114, which had already told him three things he already knew and one thing that was almost certainly wrong. Around him, a thousand people performed the act of being somewhere famous. They held phones aloft like offerings. They jostled without apology. A child to his left was crying at a volume that suggested genuine injustice, and the child's father was photographing the fountain and not the child, and somewhere behind Ben a man was playing an accordion badly on purpose.

Ben had come to Rome to think. This was, he was now understanding, a structural error.

He was forty-one. He was on sabbatical from a position at a university that he had spent sixteen years making himself indispensable to and which had, without ceremony, offered him indispensability back in the form of twelve months in which to produce the book he kept not quite writing. He wore a blue linen shirt he'd bought specifically for this trip, already damp at the collar. His Italian, tested twice this morning — once at the hotel, once ordering coffee — had been received both times with the particular blankness of people who have decided that kindness is more efficient than engagement.

He looked at page 114 again.

The guidebook said: *The fountain's design is attributed to Nicola Salvi, though the project was passed between architects over several decades, reflecting the collaborative and sometimes contentious nature of Baroque civic ambition.*

Ben thought: yes.

He thought: that's exactly the kind of sentence I have been writing for sixteen years and I cannot feel it anymore.

He closed the book.

The scooter appeared from his left with no warning and insufficient physics. It threaded through a gap between two tour groups that did not technically exist, and when Ben stepped back — not forward, not to the side, back, which was the wrong direction — the front wheel stopped approximately six inches from his left shin with a shriek of rubber that seemed to silence even the accordion.

The woman on the motorcycle took off her helmet.

She had the specific dark-eyed expression of someone who has recently decided that the universe owes her an apology and is waiting, not patiently, for it to begin.

"*Che cazzo fai?*" she said. This and several things after it.

Ben said, "*Mi dispiace*," which he knew meant he was sorry, and then because the situation seemed to require more, he attempted to say that he hadn't been watching where he was going, and what came out was something closer to a statement about his personal direction being not good previously.

The woman stared at him.

"*Italian*," Ben said, uselessly, pointing at his own mouth. "*Non— I'm sorry. I'm— the book said—*" He held up the guidebook as though it were a document of legal relevance.

She looked at the book. She looked at him. Something moved through her expression that was not quite amusement and not quite contempt and occupied a privately maintained third category.

"You told me," she said, in English that had been learned from someone who enjoyed cinema, "that your direction was *not good*."

"Directional," Ben said. "My directional— my sense of—" He stopped. "Yes. I apologize."

"You are apologizing to me," she said, "when I nearly drove through your shin."

"You have more momentum," Ben said. "I felt the apology was more mine."

She looked at him for a moment. A tour group moved past like continental drift. The child behind Ben had stopped crying, apparently having concluded that grief was insufficient for the occasion.

The woman did not ride away.

---

Her name was Alessandra. She offered this the way someone might offer a light — casually and without attachment to what you did with it next.

The motorcycle's left side panel had a long scratch in the red paint, beginning near the headlight and ending somewhere beneath the footpeg, and through it and adjacent to it were three separate dents of varying circumference, the whole constellation of damage having the quality of a story that had been told so many times it had become a gesture.

"That looks—" Ben began.

"Yes," she said.

"Has it been—"

"Always," she said, and grinned at the fountain without elaborating.

She had parked the bike with the efficiency of someone who considered parking an argument to win, both feet flat on the cobblestones, and she was looking at him now with the appraising focus of someone taking inventory.

Ben was not, he knew, an impressive inventory. He was tall in a way that had never been particularly useful. He had good handwriting and an instinctive tendency to apologize preemptively, like a man walking through a room full of things he expects to knock over.

"Where are you going?" Alessandra asked.

He held up the book again. He was, he realized, using it as a talisman.

She plucked it from his hand before he could object, flipped to a random page, made a face not unlike the one you make when milk has turned, and handed it back.

"I know Rome," she said. "The real parts."

"I'm sure you do," Ben said, with the careful diplomacy of a man who suspects he is being offered something and is not yet sure what its cost structure looks like.

"I will take you," she said. "For two hours."

"That's— very kind, but I really don't—"

"*Dai.*" She was already putting her helmet back on.

Ben stood with his guidebook. Around him, Rome performed its perpetual theatre of heat and noise and stone worn to a gloss by four centuries of feet. A man nearby was arguing into a phone. A group of nuns passed in formation, purposeful and cool. A small dark bird landed on the rim of the fountain, looked at Ben, and left.

He put the guidebook in his bag.

"Right," he said. "Yes. All right."

She handed him her spare helmet and he noticed, without quite noticing he was noticing, that the woman standing two feet to his left — elderly, tourist, a plastic shopping bag from a souvenir shop looped over one wrist — was fishing in that bag with an expression of mild confusion. The woman turned the bag over. Turned it back.

A small glass figurine, a horse, blue-green, was not there anymore.

Ben blinked.

Alessandra was already on the bike.

---

The city from the back of a motorcycle was a different city than the city from guidebook page 114.

It had no margin notes. It offered no historical context. It presented itself in three-dimensional, thermodynamic immediacy — a blur of ochre walls and iron balconies and laundry strung at impossible angles between windows, and sudden eruptions of ancient stone appearing at intersections without apology or introduction, as if Rome had simply decided to store its past at street level because it had run out of museum space.

Ben held on with both hands and tried not to make a sound every time Alessandra passed through a gap in traffic that seemed governed not by physics but by confidence.

"You are very tense," she called back.

"You went through a red light," Ben called forward.

"The light was for automobiles."

"You have an engine."

"*Sì.*" She turned left. Ben leaned right in what he was already understanding was a counterproductive instinct. "Relax your hands. You feel every bump when you are like this."

"I feel every bump because this road has—"

"Rome's roads are old," she said, with enormous unconcern. "They remember when this was all different. They are not apologizing."

Ben thought: illegible. This woman is illegible.

He thought: I haven't felt anything in four months and I am currently feeling several things.

He loosened his hands, fractionally.

They went through another gap. Ben shut his eyes for approximately one second and then opened them because closing them was somehow worse.

The scratch ran down the bike's left side in his peripheral vision, and in the morning light it had a brief copper quality, like a scar. Ben opened his mouth to ask again.

Alessandra accelerated.

---

She took him to a *trattoria* that had no sign, only a handwritten card in the window that Ben suspected was in dialect because his Italian, already limited, couldn't parse it. She was greeted at the door by a man of approximately ninety who kissed her on both cheeks and called her something Ben didn't catch, and she touched the old man's arm with a specific gentleness she hadn't deployed yet, and Ben filed this away.

They sat in the back. It was dark and cool. The tablecloth was paper.

"This is not in any guidebook," Ben said.

"No."

"How long has it—"

"Since before I was born," she said. "Since before my mother was born, probably. *Enrico's* grandfather opened it." She looked at the menu, which was handwritten and laminated and had been laminated some time ago. "He died last year. The grandfather."

"I'm sorry."

"He was a hundred and one." She looked up. "He had a very good life." She said this with finality, the way you close a door on a room you want to remember intact.

Ben looked at the menu. He understood the word for fish and the word for wine.

"Tell me what to order," he said.

She said something to the ceiling, not out loud, and then told him.

When the food came it was extraordinary, and Ben said so, and she received this with neither modesty nor pride, just a nod, as if he had correctly identified something that had always been true and was only now apparent to him.

"What is the book about?" she asked, tilting her head at his bag. "The one you're writing."

He looked up. "How did you—"

"The other book. I can tell when someone is carrying a book they cannot look at." She tore bread. "What is it?"

"Baroque civic architecture," Ben said. "Specifically the relationship between spatial planning and political power in seventeenth-century Rome."

She looked at him for a long moment.

"You're mocking me with your eyes," he said.

"I am not mocking you," she said, and she almost wasn't. "You came to Rome on holiday to look at the thing you study for work."

"It's sabbatical."

"This is different?"

Ben poured wine. "It's supposed to be. It's supposed to be the year I finish the book I've been— yes." He stopped. He drank some of the wine. "I studied this city for twenty years and I arrived here five days ago and I've stood at four different piazzas and written nothing. I keep— the sentences come out and they're— they're correct. Every one of them. They're all correct. And I can't—" He put the glass down. "I can't feel any of them."

Alessandra was looking at him. Not with pity. With something more forensic.

"You are afraid," she said, "that you have studied the life out of it."

Ben sat with this.

"Yes," he said. "Possibly. Yes."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she pulled a small object from her jacket pocket and set it on the table between them. It was the blue-green glass horse from the Trevi Fountain. It caught the dim light and held it.

"I take things," she said, casually, watching his face. "Small things. Not valuable. I put them back, sometimes."

Ben looked at the horse. He looked at her. She held his gaze with complete equanimity, and the table between them was very small.

He picked up his wine glass.

"*The ride* mentioned trespassing," he said. "You said two hours."

She smiled. It was a private, involuntary thing, and she reclaimed control of it quickly. But Ben had seen it.

She put the horse back in her pocket.

"Finish your wine," she said.

---

The building was not technically open to the public in the hour between three and five in the afternoon, which Alessandra appeared to regard as useful rather than restrictive. It was a *palazzo* of the minor variety, the kind that appeared in Ben's academic texts as a footnote to more important buildings, but whose rooftop — she had explained, without explaining how she knew — offered an angle on the city that appeared in no photographs because no photographs had been taken from it.

"There is a caretaker," she said, at the service entrance.

"And?"

"He sleeps from three to five."

"How do you know that?"

"I know it because the last time I came here he was asleep." She said this as if it resolved the matter epistemologically.

"And if he isn't—"

"Then we say we are lost."

"We're on the third floor of a—"

"People get very lost in Rome," she said, and opened the door.

The roof was red-tiled and precarious in the way of all things that have survived by stubbornness over engineering, and you had to climb through a window to reach it, which Ben did with rather more commitment than he expected from himself, and then he was standing on it — the city beneath him in every direction, the dome of St. Peter's rising southwest across a jumble of rooftops turned gold in the declining afternoon light, the hills, the stone, the tremendous weight of accumulated time that Rome wore like a garment.

Ben stood very still.

He had studied this city from text for twenty years. He had read the documents and the plans and the histories of the men who built it and the men who paid for those men and the theological and political machinations behind every façade. He could reconstruct the genesis of any building in his eyeline from memory, root it in its moment, explain the forces that had shaped it.

He couldn't, right now, think of a single sentence.

Not a correct one. Not any kind.

He felt only the city. The extraordinary, unreasonable fact of it.

"*Allora*," Alessandra said, beside him. She was looking out at it too, and she had taken the glass horse from her pocket and held it loosely, turning it once.

"This is better than the guidebook," Ben said.

"Everything is better than the guidebook."

"Yes but—" He gestured at the view. "I could feel that. Right now. I could feel the whole thing."

She glanced at him briefly. Turned the horse again.

"I found that in someone's pocket," she said. "They had eight identical ones in a bag." She held it up. "I think they were going to sell them. Before me."

"That's not— that's not quite—"

"No," she agreed, pleasantly.

"Don't you feel—"

"I feel better when I have it," she said. She said it directly, without self-pity or particular shame, and the directness of it closed the conversation in a way that felt not like evasion but like a fact she had long since made peace with. "It was worse before. Now it is— smaller." She pocketed the horse. "And I return them."

Ben thought: she is not asking for absolution.

He thought: she is just telling me.

The sun moved behind something and the light on the rooftops changed register, cooler and more horizontal, and Rome made the adjustment without comment.

"I'm on sabbatical," Ben said, "because my wife and I separated. In February." He said it to the city. "She said I wasn't— she said I processed everything at a remove. Everything. That I was always— annotating." He was quiet for a moment. "She wasn't wrong."

Alessandra said nothing.

"I came here because it was where my work was," he continued. "And then I got here and the work wouldn't— wouldn't start, and I realized that might be the same problem."

The city made its sounds far below them.

"The book is an annotation," Alessandra said.

"Yes."

"And you came to Rome to feel Rome," she said. "Not to annotate it."

"I came to Rome to annotate it," Ben said. "I think Rome had other plans."

She said nothing to this. But when he looked at her, something had shifted in her expression — not open, exactly, but less defended, the way a room looks different when a window has been cracked.

She deflected it by moving toward the window they'd climbed through.

"One more stop," she said. "Before the light goes."

---

He didn't ask where they were going.

This was a development.

She wove through evening traffic with the same sovereign confidence, but the city around them was changing color, the shadows going long and warm, the tourist-noise thinning as the afternoon crowd retreated toward restaurants and hotels. Ben felt the shift in the air — Rome becoming its own thing again after a long day of being observed — and he felt also that he had loosened something in himself on that rooftop, some structural bolting, and the loosening was uncomfortable and also, under the discomfort, a relief.

They stopped on a narrow street in Trastevere where a bookshop had put its spillover stock in cardboard boxes outside — second-hand paper
