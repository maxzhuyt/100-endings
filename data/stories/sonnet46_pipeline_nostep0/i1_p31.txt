# Gone South

---

The Uber pulls away before Taylor's bag hits the sidewalk.

That's how it goes. That's how people leave — mid-sentence, mid-thought, tires already turning. Taylor knows this now like they know their own pulse: abrupt, involuntary, not a thing you can stop by wanting it to.

The street is loud in a way that feels personal. Like the city decided, specifically, to be too much. A horn somewhere. Two men arguing in a doorway across the street, and the argument sounds almost musical, rhythmic, call and response. A woman walks by trailing a cloud of gardenia and cigarette smoke and something fried, and Taylor's stomach responds in a way that is embarrassing given everything, given the full scope of everything, given that it has been forty-eight hours since Taylor ate a real meal and not just a gas station granola bar consumed sideways in the back seat of a stranger's Honda.

New Orleans. Okay.

Taylor had pulled up the map at the Memphis stop and put a finger down and this is where the finger landed. That's the whole plan. That's the complete and total plan in its entirety. Put your finger on the map. Trust the finger.

The finger has delivered Taylor to a sidewalk on Decatur Street at eleven in the morning in mid-July in Louisiana, which means the heat is not heat so much as a decision the atmosphere has made about you. It presses. It has opinions. Taylor's shirt is already doing something unfortunate and there's no fixing that, only accepting it, which is a skill Taylor is apparently developing whether they want to or not.

Okay. Inventory.

Cash: two hundred and fourteen dollars. The emergency fund from the mason jar under the bed that Mom didn't know about and now won't need to know about for different reasons. Two hundred and fourteen dollars sounds like more than it is. Taylor has a feeling it's about one-third of what it should be, but the feeling didn't have better ideas.

Phone: sixty-three percent battery, which sounds fine until you remember that the charger is at home. Home. That word doing something complicated. Taylor skips past it. Phone. Sixty-three percent. Data plan technically still active because Dad pays the family account and presumably hasn't thought to — hasn't had time to — forty-eight hours is not that long —

Skip.

The bag: one change of clothes, two changes of underwear, a hoodie that seemed necessary at 2 a.m. Nashville time and seems insane now in this heat but you can't just throw away a hoodie, a phone charger that requires an outlet to be useful, a battered copy of *Giovanni's Room* that Taylor has read four times and brought the way other people bring a photograph, the mason jar money minus what got spent on bus tickets and the Uber, and a half-eaten sleeve of Oreos.

What Taylor doesn't have: a plan. A contact. A single person in this city who knows their name.

The street moves around them and Taylor stands in it like a rock in water, everything splitting and rejoining downstream.

---

Here is the thing about the last forty-eight hours that Taylor has been not-thinking about since the Honda crossed the Louisiana state line:

It started with a notebook.

Not even — it started before that. It started with months of before, months of Taylor existing in the particular silence of knowing something large and unconfirmed, something that lived in the chest like a second heartbeat, something Taylor had been circling in the dark with both hands the way you circle something you're afraid to break. The notebook was just the place it lived. Private. Hidden behind the loose baseboard in the closet. Taylor had been so careful.

Mom has a habit. This is a charitable word for it. Mom has a *habit* of cleaning that is less cleaning and more a form of intervention, a removal of privacy disguised as love, which is — Taylor is not going to do this right now, not on this sidewalk, not in this heat —

Mom's face. That's the first image that comes when Taylor stops moving long enough. Not anger. Everyone expects the anger and the anger comes eventually but the first thing — the thing Taylor can't get out — is the face that isn't any expression at all yet. The moment of pure reading. Mom sitting on Taylor's bed with the notebook open to the middle and her face is just receiving information and then —

Skip.

Dad's silence is easier and harder. Dad is a man who does silence the way other men do speeches. It fills the room. It takes up furniture. Taylor had stood in the doorway of the living room and Dad had been sitting in his chair and he had looked at Taylor for a long moment with something passing across his face that Taylor could not name and then he had looked back at the television, which was off, and that was the whole conversation, and it was the loudest thing Taylor has ever heard.

The sibling: Brianna, fifteen, who said *I already knew* in a voice that was almost reassuring and then said *you're so selfish* in the same voice, and the whiplash of that — the way love in their family has always been a door that swings both ways without warning — Taylor still feels it, will probably always feel it, a specific bruise in a specific shape.

And then, somewhere in the second night, when the house had gone to the specific silence of people who've said too much and can't unsay it, Taylor had taken the mason jar from under the bed and the reading copy of *Giovanni's Room* and the hoodie and left.

The finger came down on the map. Here. New Orleans. Now.

---

Okay.

Taylor adjusts the bag strap and starts walking because standing still is not actually an option, standing still is just falling in place.

The French Quarter is — a lot. This is the professional assessment. A lot. Tourists in matching shirts. A man dressed as a living statue, spray-painted gold, absolutely motionless while a child tugs his sleeve. An entire shop dedicated to hot sauce, which, okay, hats off to the commitment. The buildings are old in a way that seems theatrical, balconies hung with ferns and what might be actual ghosts, wrought iron grilles that must be someone's dream and someone else's nightmare to maintain in this humidity. Everything smells like garbage and praline and something green and alive rotting happily underneath it all.

Taylor makes a wrong turn somewhere near a cathedral and ends up in an alley that is thinner than expected and then wider and then somehow ends at a courtyard they didn't mean to find. A man at the entrance says something in a pleasant voice about a tour and Taylor says no thank you, thank you, sorry, backing out, and then a woman outside asks if Taylor wants to try samples of she doesn't finish the sentence before Taylor is walking away.

Then the man.

Not a tour guide, not a sample person. A man in a Saints jersey who falls into step beside Taylor with an ease that is — Taylor notices this ease, files it immediately — the ease of someone who does this for a living.

"Hey, I can tell you're lost," he says, friendly like a sales pitch. "I'm from here, I can help. Where you trying to go?"

"I'm good," Taylor says. "Thanks." Keeps walking.

"You got people here? You need to know where to eat, I know all the spots, I'm local, locals know the real —"

"I'm really good," Taylor says, and then turns a corner hard and stops in a doorway and waits while their heart does something athletic.

After a minute the man doesn't appear. Good. Fine. Nothing happened. A warning, that's all. A note from the city: *I see you, tender foot, and so does everyone else, walk like you know something.*

Taylor doesn't know anything. But Taylor can walk like they do. The armor goes on, the spine straightens, the face arranges itself into an expression that a drama teacher in ninth grade once called *alarmingly opaque*, which was meant as a note and has become an asset.

---

There's a café on a side street off Bourbon — Bourbon is too much, Bourbon is the city doing an impression of itself for people who want to see an impression — that has a handwritten sign in the window: *COLD WATER FREE, WIFI FREE, ATTITUDE EXTRA.* Taylor pushes open the door and the air conditioning is immediate and violent and wonderful and Taylor stands in it for three full seconds with their eyes closed.

They order a coffee because ordering coffee feels necessary to justify the table. The woman at the counter doesn't look at Taylor differently than she'd look at anyone, which is not something Taylor can say about all encounters. Seven dollars including tip. Two hundred and seven dollars left. Taylor files it.

The table is in the back by the wall and Taylor sits with their back to the wall because — well. Because.

The phone comes out. Battery at fifty-nine percent now, the walk took more out of it than expected, or worry took something out of it. Taylor opens the messages.

Mom: *Where are you call me right now*

Mom: *Taylor please*

Mom: *I'm calling the police*

Mom: *Your father talked to me please just call*

Mom: *We love you I know that's hard to believe right now but we love you please come home*

Mom: *It's 3am please tell me you're safe*

Mom: *Baby*

Brianna: *are you okay*

Brianna: *i'm sorry about what I said*

Brianna: *please just tell me ur ok i dont care about the rest just say ur alive*

That one. The Brianna one. Taylor has to look away from the screen and look at the ceiling, which is tin and pressed in old patterns, and breathe for a minute.

There's nothing from Dad. This is not surprising. This is exactly as surprising as gravity.

Taylor types: *I'm okay. Don't call the police. I'll be in touch.*

Deletes it.

Types: *I'm safe.*

Sends it.

To Brianna only. Not Mom. Not the family thread. Brianna's name, a letter, two words. *I'm safe.* Sends it before thinking about it further and then turns the phone face-down on the table and drinks the coffee, which is strong enough to constitute a threat, and stares at the pressed tin ceiling.

The calculus of going back does not take long to run. What's there: a house with a specific silence in it. Two parents at the start of a process Taylor has no visibility into — the process of deciding what kind of parents they are, because not every parent knows until it happens, not every parent has decided what they believe until the moment it stops being abstract and becomes their kid in the doorway. Taylor doesn't know what that process will produce and doesn't know how long it will take and doesn't have the resources, the internal resources, to sit in that house in that silence while it runs.

So.

What's here: two hundred and seven dollars. A half-eaten sleeve of Oreos. A copy of *Giovanni's Room* in a bag on the floor. Taylor still in the body Taylor is in, which is not the body Taylor would have designed from scratch but is the body Taylor has learned to live in the way you learn to live in an old house, knowing where the floor creaks, knowing which windows stick.

*I'm safe.* Two words sent into the void. Taylor picks the phone back up and powers it to low battery mode and puts it in the bag.

The ceiling is very interesting.

---

Outside, the neighborhood just past the Quarter — Marigny, says the street sign, and Taylor files it — is quieter and more human-scaled. Second-line houses painted in candy colors. A beauty supply store where the window display is a masterpiece of organization and ambition. A mural on a corner building: a woman's face, enormous, serene, an eye the size of a manhole cover watching the street.

Taylor walks and tries to look like someone going somewhere.

The shop is small and would be easy to miss: SECONDHAND SAINTS, spelled out in hand-painted letters, with a cardboard sign below: *ALL BODIES WELCOME, ALL GENDERS WELCOME, WE HAVE AC.* The window display is haphazard and incredible: a mannequin in a beaded ballgown next to a rack of cowboy shirts next to a shelf of old cameras next to a string of streamers that spell out FABULOUS in silver.

Taylor stops.

Not because of the clothes. Not specifically. It's something else — the sign, maybe, or the window, or the particular confidence of the display, which announces without apology that the people who made it knew exactly who they were making it for and felt fine about that. This is not a place that hedged. This is not a place that qualified its welcome.

At home, the gas station bathroom has two doors and they both say things Taylor is not, technically, permitted to be.

Taylor looks at the window for a moment that goes longer than expected.

The street doesn't stop for it. Cars pass. A dog trots by on business of its own. Two kids zoom by on one bike, shrieking, the kid in back hanging on with the absolute faith of someone who has never fallen. The city doesn't particularly care that Taylor is having a moment. The city has seen moments. The city is made of them, laid up like sediment.

Taylor breathes out.

The feeling that comes is not happiness. It's more complicated than happiness. It's hope with a cut in it — the particular feeling of seeing something possible and knowing that possible is not the same as arrived, not yet, maybe not for a while, but *possible*, and possible is more than Taylor had yesterday. The grief is in there too, present, not going away: the grief of seeing something welcoming and knowing it is welcoming for the person Taylor is trying to become, which is not yet the person Taylor gets to be in the full, undefended, daily sense. That person is somewhere ahead. Taylor is walking toward them. The road is currently two hundred and seven dollars and one change of clothes and no clear plan.

Taylor doesn't go into the store.

But Taylor looks at it for a long moment, and something — not armor, something under the armor — accepts the looking.

---

The saxophonist is back on a corner near a small square, which might be Jackson Square or might not, Taylor's geography is still approximate. He's maybe sixty, wearing a hat that has lived a full life, and he's playing something slow and unidentifiable, something that doesn't have the shape of a song you'd know, something that seems to be making itself up as it goes.

Taylor had passed him this morning, coming off the ride-share, and stopped then too. For less time. More disoriented.

Now Taylor sits on a bench at the edge of the square and listens.

The music doesn't ask anything. That's still true. That was the thing this morning and it's the thing now: the saxophone enters the air and it doesn't want Taylor to be okay, doesn't want Taylor to be anything in particular, doesn't want anything except to be heard. Taylor can sit inside that non-requirement and just — be. No expectations about the shape of the sitting.

The tourists take photos of the cathedral. The pigeons conduct their debates. The heat does its patient work on everything.

Taylor puts their arms on their knees and their chin on their arms and listens to a man play music for the city in the middle of the day and doesn't think about anything, and after a while doesn't do that consciously either, just stops.

---

"You've been sitting there for like forty-five minutes."

Taylor looks up.

The person who said it is leaning against the iron fence about six feet away, backpack at their feet, watching Taylor with the specific expression of someone who noticed something and decided to mention it rather than not. Which is either reckless or brave, Taylor hasn't decided. Possibly both.

They're tall. Dark brown skin, close-cropped hair, wearing a denim jacket covered in pins — too many pins, a truly irresponsible number of pins — over a band shirt Taylor can't quite read from this angle. Maybe seventeen. Maybe a young-looking twenty. The ease of the lean doesn't come naturally; there's something performance in it, something slightly too constructed, which is the kind of thing you notice when you're doing the same thing yourself.

"Was I in your spot?" Taylor asks.

"No such thing as spots out here." They look at the saxophonist. "He's good."

"Yeah."

"You from here?"

Taylor almost laughs. "Extremely no."

"Visiting?"

"Also no."

They look at Taylor for a moment. Something turns over behind their eyes — a small internal movement, a file opened and briefed and closed. "Same," they say, and sit down on the other end of the bench.

The saxophone plays.

They sit in it together for a while, which is unusual — two strangers on a bench who've just acknowledged a mutual unnamed condition and then agreed to not speak about it while also not leaving. Taylor watches the saxophonist and feels the other person doing the same thing without looking.

"Nova," they say eventually. Not turning. Like it's information offered to the air.

"Taylor," Taylor says. Same.

"Where you from, Taylor."

"Tennessee."

"How long you been in town?"

"Four hours. What about you?"

"Three days."
