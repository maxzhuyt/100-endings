# Blood Debt

---

The temple had been dying for decades before anyone thought to fight in it.

Water had found the roof seams first, then the walls, patient and inevitable, splitting the mortar grain by grain until the stones listed inward like old men against winter. The floor buckled in three places where tree roots had shouldered through from below. A fig tree had seeded itself somehow in the eastern corner and now spread black branches across the ceiling, its roots woven into the rubble like fingers closing around something precious. The whole structure smelled of damp and cold ash and something sweetly animal — bat droppings, perhaps, or the slow wet rot of wood that had once been a door. Whatever god had lived here had packed up and moved on long ago. The space it left behind had the particular quality of emptiness that invites desecration.

The single torch was wedged into a crack in the northern wall at shoulder height, jammed there by whoever had arrived first — a length of pine pitch wound with oiled cloth, burning with a fat orange flame that threw more shadow than light. It caught the edges of broken stone and abandoned them. It turned the fig tree into a crouching shape that changed every time a draft moved through the room. When the crowd pressed in through the gap that had once been the southern entrance, the torch shuddered and the shadows lurched across the walls, and for a moment the whole ruin seemed to breathe.

The crowd split itself naturally, the way crowds always do when they know which side they're on.

The Jigen-ryū men took the eastern wall — the side with more standing stone, the side where the floor was flattest. They were well-dressed in muted colors, and they stood with a stillness that was its own form of contempt. A few of them were swords-school men who had come out of professional curiosity; most were merchants and minor officials who had backed the school with money and wanted to see their money justified. They did not shout. They murmured to one another in low voices and placed their bets in silver with the ease of men for whom silver is not a large thing. One of them produced a folded piece of paper and wrote on it with a brush he kept in his sleeve. The others nodded and sealed it without ceremony. Together they had the quality of men watching something they already know the outcome of — not cruel, exactly, but indifferent to the possibility of surprise.

The Piguaquan men had the western wall and the broken ground and the fig tree, and they made noise enough for three times their number. They were craftsmen and stevedores mostly, men with wide hands and scarred knuckles, and they betted in copper with the urgency of people who have thought carefully about whether they can afford to lose. They passed a jar around. They argued with each other about technique. A young man at the back was shaking visibly — not from cold — and his neighbor gripped his arm once and said something close to his ear that made the shaking slow but not stop. There were women on this side too, standing toward the back with their arms folded, watching the empty center of the room with expressions impossible to read.

The torch guttered. The shadows jumped. Everyone went still for a moment, and in that stillness the space between the two factions became visible as a thing unto itself — a corridor of air nobody was standing in, charged and specific, the physical shape of everything that had brought them here.

Then sound resumed, and the fighters entered.

---

The Jigen-ryū man came first.

His name was Sato Kenji and he was thirty-one years old and the son of a master, and he moved into the center of the ruined temple the way water moves into a container — filling it, becoming its shape, immediately at home. He was not tall but he was broad through the chest and shoulders, and he wore a dove-gray jacket with the sleeves rolled to the elbow, and his forearms in the torchlight were ropy with muscle and a few clean old scars. He had shaved that morning. His hair was pulled back tight. Everything about him that could have been disordered had been ordered, and the effect was not elegance so much as compression — a man who had taken everything superfluous and pressed it into a smaller space, packed it down into something dense and waiting.

He did not look at the crowd.

He stopped in the center of the room and stood with his feet close, almost together, weight riding slightly forward on the balls of his feet, arms at his sides. He was still in the way a drawn bow is still — the stillness was the form that the movement would take before it took it. He breathed slowly through his nose. His gaze moved once around the room, noting the floor hazards with no apparent feeling about them, and then fixed itself on the southern wall as if he were waiting for something already decided to arrive.

He had been practicing since he was seven years old. He had practiced the same three techniques for twenty-four years, the same way a stone river rounds itself against the same bank — not acquiring more but becoming more completely itself.

He believed he had already won. This was not arrogance in the common meaning. It was more like faith.

---

The Piguaquan fighter came in through the gap behind a pair of his supporters and stopped at the edge of the crowd for a moment before stepping into the open space, as if he were tasting the air.

His name was Chen Liang. He was twenty-six and originally from a city three provinces away, and he had the look of someone who had been traveling for long enough that travel had become his natural condition — lean and watchable, with eyes that moved constantly over the room, cataloguing. He wore a dark tunic with the collar open and no jacket, and the cold didn't seem to reach him. His feet were in soft leather shoes, not sandals, and they were already in motion even as he came to something approximating a rest — a small continuous adjustment, his weight rolling fractionally from one foot to the other, his knees slightly bent, his body organized around its own center of gravity the way a man on a boat learns to stand.

He was not composing himself. He already was himself.

He found Sato Kenji across the eight feet of broken floor between them and held the look. His shoulders dropped half an inch. The corners of his mouth moved in something that was not quite a smile — it was the wrong face for a smile, too sharp — but it communicated clearly that he had already seen what he needed to see and found it neither more nor less than expected. It was a look that said *I know exactly what you are*.

Sato Kenji turned his head ninety degrees and looked at him for the first time.

The silence was brief and total.

"Your master," Sato said quietly, "taught a dead man's tricks."

Chen Liang said nothing. But the motion under his feet changed — almost imperceptibly, the way a cat's hind legs adjust before a spring — and the sound of the crowd suddenly became very far away.

---

There was no referee. There was no ceremony. Nobody had bothered to establish rules because everyone present knew what the rules were: you stopped when you couldn't go on, and you went on as long as you could.

The crowd-appointed signal was a man named Gu who had organized the event and therefore owned the problem of starting it. He was a broad-faced man who ran a gambling house and understood when an audience had waited long enough. He had been watching the two fighters stand in the torchlight for forty seconds and he could feel the crowd behind him reaching the edge of what anticipation could hold before it curdled into something worse.

He raised his arm.

For Sato Kenji, winning meant an argument ended — not the argument in this room, but the one that had been running for three years since a public demonstration where he'd been passed over for a student appointment in favor of a recommendation from a Piguaquan practitioner he'd never respected. It meant the validation of everything he'd compressed himself into becoming. It meant being right about the way things worked.

For Chen Liang, winning meant sending money home. There was more than that, but that was where it started, and starting there kept it real.

Gu's arm came down.

---

Sato moved before the arm finished falling.

It was not a step. It was a collapse of distance — the stored energy in his back leg releasing through his hip through his shoulder through his arm and into a single overhand strike aimed at the centerline of Chen Liang's skull, with his weight behind it to a degree that made it less like a punch and more like being hit by something thrown. The sound it would have made, connecting, was not a sound anyone in the room wanted to hear produced by a human head. The distance closed in less than a heartbeat.

The strike connected with air.

Chen Liang was six inches to the left of where he'd been, his front foot sweeping out in a quarter arc, his torso unwinding away from the line of attack at an angle that shouldn't have been available to a body at that speed. Sato's extended arm passed through the space where Chen's face had been and continued into the emptiness beyond it, and for a fraction of a second Sato's full weight was on his front foot with his elbow pointing at a wall.

The crowd made a sound like an intake of breath.

Sato recovered in a single sharp motion — he was good enough that the over-extension lasted barely a hundred milliseconds — and turned to find Chen already a step and a half away, circling leftward, loose and diagonal, his feet describing a shallow arc around the outer edge of the combat space. Not retreating. Repositioning. His arms were up but relaxed, elbows soft, hands open at roughly throat height, and as he moved they swung slightly in a lazy pendulum rhythm that looked almost casual until you tracked where his hands were relative to Sato's head and realized the casual was doing a great deal of work.

Sato attacked again — fast, faster than the first time, a straight thrust followed by a rising swing that cut back across the centerline. Using the close distance. Using his weight. Piguaquan is a long-range style and he knew it and he intended to take that range away.

Chen slipped the thrust by rolling his torso, caught the swing on a rotating forearm that redirected it outward rather than blocking it — letting the energy past, then turning inside its arc — and as Sato's arm continued its course, traveling somewhere it was no longer needed, Chen's right arm was already moving. A whipping outward swing, loose from the shoulder, the arm nearly straight, the hand open and describing a circle that terminated against the left side of Sato's jaw.

Not a hard blow. Not a finishing blow. The kind of blow that reminds a face that it is a face.

Sato's head snapped sideways. He felt a bright sharp heat at his jaw that was less pain than announcement. His molars had clicked together.

He took a step back.

From the western wall, sound: a rough shout, several voices together, then individual calls in Chen Liang's dialect. From the east, silence. Then one voice, quiet and precise: *Steady.*

Sato touched his jaw with two fingers. Looked at his hand. Nothing there.

His eyes found Chen Liang and whatever adjustment happened behind them did not cross his face.

He moved in again, and this time he moved differently.

---

He had not won the school's reputation on belief alone. He had won it on the particular intelligence that can only be grown through decades of one practice — the intelligence that is entirely kinesthetic, that lives in the body and not the head, that does not think but *sees* and *moves* with the same action.

What he had seen: the Piguaquan man needed room. Room to the side, room to pivot, room for that lazy horrifying arm to build speed before it arrived. The footwork that had put him offline had covered eighteen inches of lateral space before Chen Liang was clear of the attack. He needed eight inches minimum to generate the redirect, twelve to generate the counter.

What he intended: deny it.

He tightened his approach angle, keeping his own body between Chen and the open ground to the left. He watched the feet. He walked Chen toward the eastern wall, step by deliberate step, narrowing the space — three feet of open ground, then two, the broken stone forcing Chen's arc into a short semicircle rather than a full pivot. Close enough that Sato could smell the other man's sweat, sharp and fresh.

He felt the crowd sense the shift. The noise from the west had dropped; the noise from the east had not risen but had changed in quality, deepened somehow, a murmur that sounded like satisfaction.

Chen felt the wall coming and let Sato see him feel it — a brief look back over his shoulder, weight shifting — and then reversed direction sharply, cutting right instead of continuing left, using Sato's own advance as the distance he needed.

His right foot came down hard on a section of the floor where a root had buckled the stones, and the stone turned under him.

He dropped, caught himself on one knee, was up again in the same second, but in that second Sato was already inside the distance and already swinging.

The strike hit Chen Liang across the right forearm and then his shoulder, three inches from the neck. Not the neck. Not what Sato had intended. But the force was a real force and Chen stumbled right, into a section of wall where two stones had fallen onto each other to form a low step, and his shoulder blade hit stone and he made a sound and then was moving again, back into open ground, but moving with everything reset by the pain — cautious, not loose, his right arm held slightly higher than it wanted to be.

The torch flared as a draft came through the root-gap in the eastern wall and for a moment both fighters' shadows stretched enormous across the ceiling, and the fig tree lost its shape and became part of the dark, and a man on the eastern wall mistook Chen Liang's position and shouted something prematurely that died when the light steadied and showed the two fighters still close and both still upright.

Then they were into each other again.

---

This was the part the stories never told correctly.

Later, the men from both sides would describe this middle portion in terms of their fighter landing blows and the other absorbing them, but the truth visible to anyone watching neutrally was that both men were doing both things with roughly equivalent frequency, and neither was absorbing the blows well. Combat is expensive in ways that training only approximates. The body spends its reserves on a different currency when the thing hitting it means it.

Sato committed fully to each technique — it was not a choice so much as the nature of the style, the way it held its power only in the committed form, the absolute weight of the body behind the strike. When he connected it was terrible. When he missed, the extension took him a fraction past recovery, and Chen was in that fraction twice, a hard circular elbow to the right side of Sato's ribs that landed like a door slamming, and an open-hand strike to the cheekbone that peeled the skin there without breaking the bone but started a thin bleed that ran into his eye if he tilted his head.

Chen's evasions worked until they didn't. Three times he found the angle and turned Sato's force past him. The fourth time Sato adjusted mid-technique in a way that shouldn't have been possible and the strike that should have passed over Chen's right shoulder hit him in the sternum instead, and Chen's feet left the ground for a moment and he landed badly on the uneven floor, and the sound of his heels hitting the stone rattled around the ruin and the western wall erupted in noise, not celebration, alarm —

— but he was moving again, working backward, using the distance he'd created with the fall, his face strange and concentrated with the work of breathing through whatever had happened to his sternum. Working backward and working sideways. Finding the room's dimensions again with his feet.

Sato followed, rib pain showing now in how he moved, left arm slightly protective over the site. The cheek bleeding slowed but had left a dark trail.

They found each other twice more in the middle of the floor. The first time Sato turned Chen against the wall again and they exchanged something fast and close and ugly and emerged from it with new damage neither would fully assess until later — Sato's right hand had stopped closing into a complete fist; Chen's left eye was swelling, pinching the visual field on that side. The second time Chen finally used the shadows the way he'd been trying to: dragging Sato perpendicular to the torch until his own body blocked the light, stepping offline into the dimness on the far side while Sato's eyes adjusted, striking from the angle that the dark had made temporarily invisible.

But Sato had spent twenty-four years learning to sense distance without seeing it.

The strike landed, but not clean — Sato had moved his head enough that the forearm caught mostly jaw and ear rather than the temple Chen had been angling for, and there was a hot high ringing in Sato's left ear but he was still there and still standing and he turned again into Chen with everything he had, no timing, no tactics, just the style at full expression, which was: absolute commitment to ending the distance and ending the thing —

And they separated.

Both men, without ceremony or conference, stepped back by identical amounts.
