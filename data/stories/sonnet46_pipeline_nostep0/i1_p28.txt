# The Rewind Watch

The worst part wasn't failing the test.

The worst part was Ms. Hendricks handing it back facedown, the way she did for everyone, and Jaylen Briggs sitting right next to Connor and saying *"What'd you get?"* before Connor could stop him — and Connor, for some absolutely brain-dead reason, flipping it over instead of covering it with his arm.

Fifty-one. Big red ink. A fifty-one with a circle around it, like Ms. Hendricks wanted to make sure the number knew what it had done wrong.

Jaylen looked at it. Then he looked up at Connor. He didn't say anything, which was worse than if he'd laughed.

Connor shoved the test into his folder and spent the rest of class staring at the whiteboard without seeing a single thing written on it.

---

He'd gotten the watch three days ago.

It showed up in a small brown box on the front step, no return address, no note, just his name written in handwriting that looked old — not old like a grandparent's, but old like something that had been written slowly and deliberately, each letter formed with too much care. His mom assumed it was from his uncle. His uncle assumed it was from his mom. Nobody investigated further, because it was a watch, and Connor was eleven, and it was sleek and black with a strange angular face and looked expensive, so Connor strapped it on and went to school.

He'd been wearing it for three days without thinking much about it.

He was thinking about it now.

Sitting in the hallway outside the science room, staring at the fifty-one in his folder, Connor turned his wrist and looked at the watch. Its face was mostly normal — hour hand, minute hand, the small digital display showing the date. But there was a button on the left side he'd never pushed. He'd assumed it was for the stopwatch function. He'd never checked.

Above the button, pressed into the casing in tiny letters almost too small to read, was a symbol he didn't recognize. Not a brand logo. Something else. Like two arrows eating each other's tails.

Connor pressed it.

---

The world made a sound like a cassette tape being rewound — that warbling, compressed chirp of audio running backward at speed. The lights in the hallway went wrong. Not dark, just *inverted*, like a photo negative, colors swapping for their opposites for half a second before snapping back. Connor's stomach dropped the way it does on a roller coaster, that gone feeling, and he grabbed the wall —

And he was sitting in Ms. Hendricks' class. Test facedown on the desk. Jaylen turning to him.

*"What'd you get?"*

Connor slammed his hand over the paper. "Not great," he said. "Don't worry about it."

Jaylen shrugged and looked away.

Connor sat there for a moment breathing hard, heart slamming, test hidden under his palm. Then he looked down at the watch. The button sat there, ordinary, waiting.

He felt incredible.

---

He almost didn't notice the cat.

It was sitting on the narrow concrete ledge outside the hallway window — the window at the end of the hall where the fire door never opened all the way, the one that looked out on the side lot where the janitors parked. Just a gray cat. Medium sized. Connor glanced at it on his way to his next class and the cat looked back at him. Didn't blink. Didn't move. Its eyes were a pale, pale yellow, almost white.

Connor made a face at it and kept walking.

---

The quiz came back on Thursday.

Different class — Mr. Petrova's history. A ten-question quiz on the Revolutionary War that Connor had started studying for and then gotten distracted from, and the result was a seven out of ten, which wasn't catastrophic, but the thing was that seven out of ten was the *exact same grade* he'd gotten on the last quiz, and he was already losing ground in history, and his mom had specifically said —

He pressed the button in the bathroom between classes.

The rewind was faster this time, or he was more prepared for it — still the warping sound, still the color inversion, still the gut-drop feeling, but he stayed on his feet. He was back in class. He circled different answers. Two of the wrong ones he could correct from memory. The third one he guessed on and got it right anyway.

Eight out of ten.

He was walking to the cafeteria when he stopped in front of the water fountain and caught his own reflection in the metal panel above it. He leaned in slightly. His face looked back at him.

He studied it for a second.

He looked — and this was a strange word, he knew, but it was the one that came to mind — *longer* than he had this morning. Not taller. Just longer somehow. His jaw. The line of it. He pressed a finger to his chin.

His shirt felt tight around the shoulders.

He tugged at the collar and went to get lunch.

---

The cat was outside the cafeteria window, on the ledge above the dumpsters. Same unblinking yellow-white eyes. Watching the doors like it had been there for a while.

Connor pointed at it and said *"That cat again"* to nobody in particular, and a kid he didn't know glanced over and looked unimpressed, and Connor sat down and ate his sandwich.

---

It was Diego who said something first.

Diego, who had been Connor's best friend since third grade, who had seen Connor throw up on a field trip and never brought it up, who knew every embarrassing and ordinary thing about him — Diego looked at Connor across the lunch table on Friday and said, "Did you do something to your hair?"

Connor's hand went to his head. "No."

Diego squinted. "You look different."

"I don't look different."

"You look older," Diego said. He said it the way you say something when you're not sure you should.

"I'm the same age as yesterday," Connor said.

Diego looked at him for another second, then picked up his fork. "Never mind," he said.

But he'd said it.

---

Connor checked his hands in the bathroom after lunch.

He did this slowly, in the way you do something when you half expect to find what you're looking for. He held his hands out and looked at them. His fingers were longer. He was sure of it. His knuckles looked knobbier. The skin was stretched a little differently over them, the veins a little more visible than they'd been.

He looked at his face in the mirror.

He looked older.

Not dramatically older. Not like a movie where someone wakes up forty years in the future. But the soft roundness that had been in his cheeks was reduced. His eyebrows were heavier somehow. He had a pimple forming at the corner of his jaw, deep under the skin, the kind his brother got.

His brother was fifteen.

Connor stood very still.

He looked at the watch.

He looked at his hands.

He looked at the watch again.

He didn't press the button. Not this time. He went to class and sat down and did not raise his hand when he knew answers, which was unusual enough that his English teacher, Mrs. Park, asked if he was all right and looked at him with the careful attention of an adult who isn't sure they're looking at the same kid who was here yesterday.

"Fine," Connor said.

She nodded, slowly, and moved on.

---

Tuesday.

The high-stakes thing happened on Tuesday.

The hallway between sixth period and seventh, the narrow one by the gym — Connor was walking the long way to avoid Liam Stout, who'd been on his case about something stupid for weeks, and he came around the corner and found Liam already there, but Liam wasn't looking at Connor. Liam was looking at Diego.

Diego had his back pressed to the lockers and his lunch bag clutched to his chest and his face arranged into the careful blankness he used when he was actually scared. Three of Liam's friends were standing in a loose semicircle and Liam was saying something Connor didn't catch, and Diego wasn't saying anything back.

Connor stopped.

He should get a teacher. He knew he should get a teacher. He'd had the conversation with his mom, with his dad, with basically every responsible adult who'd ever been asked to discuss middle school conflict, and the correct answer was always *get a teacher*.

But the hall was empty and the nearest classroom was around a corner and through a door, and Diego's face looked like that —

He looked at the watch.

He thought about Diego pointing at him at lunch and saying *you look different*.

He thought about his hands in the bathroom mirror.

He pressed the button.

---

The rewind was wrong from the start.

The sound stretched too long — that warbling chirp extending into something lower, something that vibrated behind his teeth. The colors inverted and then inverted again, cycling, and Connor felt the gut-drop not as a single fall but as a tumbling, like he'd missed more than one step on a staircase. He grabbed the wall but his hand went through the light switch, and then the light switch was there and so was his hand and the hall reassembled itself —

But it wasn't the hall.

It was a hall. The same school. But the lockers were a different color — painted a yellowed beige instead of the blue they'd been all year, the blue they'd been his whole life — and the trophy case by the gym entrance held different trophies, older ones, dull with age, and the bulletin board had different paper on it, different font, different —

"Hey."

Connor spun around.

A girl was looking at him. She was roughly his age, hair pulled back, backpack on both shoulders, an expression of polite confusion. He did not know her.

"Are you lost?" she said.

"I go here," Connor said.

She looked at him. "I've never seen you before."

Connor took a step back. "Where's Diego?" he said.

"I don't know who that is."

The hall was mostly empty. Not the empty of between-classes — the empty of after school, or early morning. Like the day had been shifted.

"What period is it?" Connor asked.

"It's four o'clock," the girl said, like this was obvious, like she wasn't sure about him. "School's over."

School was over. Connor had been in between sixth and seventh period. He'd pressed the button and —

He found a bathroom. He needed to find a bathroom.

---

The mirror was worse than Thursday's bathroom mirror.

He was taller. He could tell without even looking — his shirt was pulling at the hem, his sleeves were riding up his forearms. His face in the mirror was lean in a way that his face had never been, jaw angular, cheekbones distinct, a ghost of something dark along his upper lip that was not quite a mustache and was deeply wrong in a way he could not fully look at.

He looked sixteen. Maybe seventeen.

He was eleven.

He pressed his hands against the sides of the sink and breathed.

The watch face had changed.

The hour and minute hands were gone. In their place, a series of numbers were ticking upward on the digital display, small and fast, like a counter running forward. And there was a crack — a hairline fracture across the upper left quadrant of the crystal, thin as a spider's thread, but there.

He looked at himself in the mirror.

He went to find Diego.

---

Diego was by the bike rack.

Connor saw him from across the parking lot and walked toward him fast, already arranging what he would say, already thinking about how to make this make sense, and Diego looked up and his expression went through three phases in about two seconds: unfamiliarity, confusion, and then a careful wariness that was somehow the worst of the three.

"Hey," Connor said.

"Hey," Diego said, in the voice you use for someone you sort of recognize.

"It's me. It's Connor."

Diego looked at him. "Connor," he repeated.

"Connor Waverly. We've been friends since third grade. You sat next to me at my brother's soccer game and we found that dead bird and built a little grave for it and called it Gerald. You're terrified of mannequins. You cried at *Finding Nemo* last year and made me swear not to tell anyone."

Something moved through Diego's face — not full recognition, but something proximate to it. A hesitation. A pulling-at-the-edge-of-memory look.

"Connor's eleven," Diego said, slowly.

"I know."

"You're not eleven."

"I *know*," Connor said. "That's what I'm trying to tell you. Something is wrong."

Diego stared at him for a long moment. Then he took a step back, just one step, the way you step back from something you haven't fully categorized yet.

"I don't —" Diego started.

"It's the watch," Connor said. He held up his wrist. "Something's wrong with the watch and I need to fix it and I don't know how."

Diego looked at the watch. His expression flickered.

The gray cat was sitting on the top of the bike rack.

Connor turned.

It was the same cat. He was certain — the same gray fur, the same medium size, the same absolute stillness. But it wasn't on a windowsill now, not half-hidden on a ledge. It was sitting directly in the center of the bike rack, six feet in front of Connor, and it was watching him with those pale yellow-white eyes and it was not moving and it was not blinking.

Connor looked at it.

It looked at him.

Up close, the cat looked wrong in the way that old things look wrong. Not sick, not injured — old. Its eyes had depth to them, not animal depth but something else, the kind of depth you see in very old photographs of faces that belonged to people who'd been through things. There was a collar around its neck, worn so thin and soft it almost wasn't there, and on the small metal tag, barely visible, was a symbol pressed into the surface.

Two arrows eating each other's tails.

Connor looked at the watch.

The crack had grown.

---

He understood it the way you understand something in a dream — not through logic but through the sudden, complete, and horrible sensation of *of course*.

Each rewind cost time. Not the time going back — the time going *forward*. Every reversal didn't erase anything. It just moved the debt. The universe wasn't giving him those minutes back. It was lending them. And collecting.

Three presses. The first one, one button push in the hallway with the test. The second, the bathroom between history class and the cafeteria. The third, the one that went wrong — the one that had shifted the whole thing sideways in time.

He was aging forward every time he pushed it back.

To fix the timeline — to put Diego back where Diego was supposed to be, to put the lockers back to blue, to make the trophies current and the bulletin board right — he'd have to push it again.

And every push cost him years.

He stood in the parking lot and calculated his face in the mirror and understood that he was already not eleven anymore, not really, not his body, and if he pressed it again —

The cat watched him.

Diego watched him.

Connor looked at the watch.

He thought about Gerald the dead bird and the little grave they'd made with sticks, and how Diego had actually cried a little, which Connor had never brought up and never would, and how Diego right now was looking at him with that stranger's wariness, that careful not-quite-recognition, and how the blue had been stripped from the lockers and replaced with beige and how it was four o'clock when it should be two.

He pressed the button.

---

The sound was beyond warbling this time — it was everything at once, every frequency, the air itself unraveling into its components and then reweaving, and the colors went through every inversion available and the ground vanished for what felt like a very long time before returning. Connor kept his eyes open because he thought if he closed them he would lose something important about who he was.

Then the hallway.

Blue lockers. His hallway. The bulletin board with the right paper. The rush of between-classes noise, sneakers squeaking, someone dropping a book, the distant percussion of a gym class from somewhere above.

Connor grabbed the wall.

Liam Stout was walking away from Diego. Diego was adjusting his backpack strap, and his face was its normal face — not scared, not wary, just Diego. He saw Connor and lifted his chin in greeting.

"You okay?" Diego said. "You look —"

"Don't say it," Connor said.

Diego frowned. "Pale. I was going to say pale."

Connor looked down at his hands.

They were not his hands.

He mean — they were his. He recognized the scar on his index finger from the time with the fishing hook, recognized the hangnail he'd been picking at for days, but they were the hands from the mirror. The wrong hands. Long, veined, the knuckles of someone who'd finished growing.

He walked to the nearest bathroom without explaining himself to Diego. Diego called something after him that he didn't catch.

---

The mirror.
