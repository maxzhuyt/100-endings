# Blood Moon

---

The moon rises wrong.

Not in angle or arc — it rises as moons do, slow and inevitable above the eastern treeline — but in color, in weight, in the wet red it pours over the village of Drahim like something that has already been decided. The light does not silver. It does not comfort. It settles on the thatched rooftops and the cobblestones and the still black water of the well like a stain that will not lift with morning, and the village receives it in total silence, which is the most wrong thing of all.

A medieval village at the cusp of harvest is never silent. There are animals that do not understand the courtesy of sleep, children who won't be reasoned with, the particular percussion of communal human life that continues whether anyone wants it to or not. Drahim has none of this. The silence here has texture — dense and held, like breath kept too long. Three houses stand with their doors open to the red night air, and whatever opened them did not come back to close them. A cart sits at the crossroads with one wheel still lazily spinning, and the oxen that should be hitched to it are simply not present. Not wandered. Not fled. *Absent*, in the way that things become absent when something has decided they should be.

The well has been used recently. The rope is still damp.

Yennefer of Vengerberg crouches at the well's edge and does not touch the rope. She doesn't need to. She reads the scene the way other people read text — sequentially, with full comprehension, moving from detail to implication without sentimentality. Her violet eyes track the damp rope, the disturbed moss at the well's lip, the faint parallel scoring in the stone that might be claw marks or might be something she doesn't have a name for yet. She catalogs it all and sets it aside for later use.

She stands, and the red light finds her — catches the white streak at her temple, the severe architecture of her profile, the stillness with which she holds herself that reads, at a distance, like calm but is in fact something considerably more precise. She is wearing traveling clothes that cost more than most villages earn in a month. This is not an accident.

She extends one hand, palm up, and whispers two words that are not in any language she was taught. They predate her teachers. A small orb of purple-white light ignites above her palm and hovers there, casting a second shadow that moves independently of her body and points in a direction she notes carefully before extinguishing it.

The air around her smells of lilac. Of gooseberries. Lush and incongruous against the rot-undertone the red moonlight seems to carry.

She knows something emptied this village. She knows it was not soldiers — there are none of the signs she's learned to read for soldiers — and not plague, and not the kind of creature she came here expecting to find. Whatever this is sits at the edge of her taxonomy and declines to step fully into the light, and this interests her considerably more than it frightens her.

She is calculating the direction her spell indicated when the air changes.

Not the wind. The wind has been still since she arrived, the whole night holding itself arrested. This is something else — a shift in pressure, a drop in temperature so localized it prickles the back of her neck specifically, as though something has positioned itself behind her with great care and then decided to let her notice. The shadows at the mouth of the nearest alley lengthen by a degree that has nothing to do with the moon's position. She watches this happen in her peripheral vision and does not turn around.

She lets him have that.

Then she turns around.

---

The first thing she registers is not a person but a composition. The wide-brimmed hat, the coat the color of arterial blood that drinks the red moonlight and gives nothing back, the orange lenses of the glasses catching the night like two small embers. He is tall in a way that seems less physical than *decided*, and he stands at the alley's edge with his hands loose at his sides and the particular quality of stillness that belongs to things that have forgotten what urgency feels like. He is not leaning. He is not posed. He simply *is*, in the way that very old things simply are, with no performance of presence because presence is no longer something they have to perform.

He has been there, she understands, since before she cast the spell. He watched the whole thing.

The orange lenses do not move. Behind them, in the red dark, she cannot see his eyes — only the impression of attention, complete and unblinking, the full weight of something that has been cataloging *her*.

He smiles. It is too wide and absolutely unhurried and contains rather more teeth than the situation requires.

"The village was already empty," he says. His voice is a low, layered thing, the accent cultured and strange and carrying centuries of somewhere else beneath it. "You're not here about the village."

She meets his gaze through the orange lenses.

"And you're not here about the village either," she says. "Which makes us both liars with the same cover story. How refreshing."

A beat. Something in his posture shifts — minute, imperceptible to anyone who hadn't been reading human bodies, and inhuman ones, for decades. Not a flinch. A recalibration.

"You knew what I was before I spoke," he says, and it is not quite a question.

"I knew what you were when the temperature dropped." Her voice is dry, unhurried, bored in precisely the performative way that signals the opposite. "The coat is a bit much. Is the theater mandatory, or do you simply enjoy it?"

"Both," he says immediately, and something in the word is genuinely pleased, a man hearing the right answer to a question he's been waiting years to ask. He tilts his head. The hat brim shifts. "A sorceress. Not local — the accent is Aedirn-adjacent but you lost it somewhere expensive. You came for the same anomaly I did and you've already ruled out three explanations in the time I've been watching you." He takes one step forward, out of the alley's shadow and into the full red moonlight, and in it he is worse — more present, more *there*, the coat catching the color of the sky like it was made for exactly this light. "You're not afraid."

"No," she agrees.

"Most things are afraid of me."

"Most things," she says, "have less to lose than I do, which makes them more easily impressed."

---

He closes the distance between them at a pace that is just slightly wrong — not fast, but *continuous* in a way that bypasses the normal metrics of approach, so that he is suddenly near enough that she has to revise her sense of his height without remembering the intermediate steps. She feels the cold radiating off him, a cold that has nothing to do with temperature, and she feels the wrongness of his presence the way you feel the wrongness of a picture hung crookedly in total darkness — registering before you can articulate why.

From his back, from the spaces between his coat and his shadow, something moves. Tendrils of absolute dark, darker than the night around them, feeling outward along the cobblestones with a curious, boneless motion. They do not reach for her. They simply extend, as though to demonstrate what reaching would look like if he decided on it.

She watches them reach a dead sparrow near the gutter and stop there, coiling briefly. She watches the sparrow disappear.

Then she lifts her hand.

The magic that comes is purple and cold and precise — not a warning shot, not a display for its own sake, but a working with edges and intention behind it, a contained geometry of force that she positions six inches from his face so that the light of it throws violet shadows across his jaw and the lenses of his glasses go dark against it. She doesn't push it forward. She holds it there, very still, and lets him read what it contains.

The tendrils retract.

He doesn't step back. But he goes still in a new way — the stillness of something that has made an involuntary reassessment, the attention shifting in quality from the attention of a predator reading terrain to something more focused, more personal. He looks at the orb of magic and then he looks at her and his smile does something she wasn't anticipating.

It *deepens*.

She drops the magic and her jaw tightens, which she permits herself because he is not looking at her jaw.

"There it is," he says softly. He is not speaking about the magic. He is speaking about something he believes he now understands about her, and the accuracy of it is what she should have expected from something this old, something that has watched enough people practice their defenses long enough to recognize when a defense is also a confession. "You built that," he says. "The magic. The bearing. The coat that costs a village's earnings." He tilts his head the other way, an unsettling motion, birdlike and boneless. "You built all of it very carefully."

Her voice, when it comes, is absolutely level.

"And you," she says, "kneel."

His smile holds. But the quality of it changes — something beneath it shifts, the first thing she's seen in his face that is not controlled, and she watches it pass through him the way weather passes through landscape. There is a master. There is a leash, and at the end of it there is a hand that holds it, and he has chosen this and she will not pretend she doesn't find this interesting.

"We've all made our arrangements," he says.

"Yes," she says. "We have."

The moon pours red. The village holds its breath. Between them, in the silence, there is the particular quality of air that exists between two people who have had, without planning to, an honest conversation — who have named something real about the other and found the return accurate and who are now standing in the specific danger of being *understood*.

Neither of them has anyone to be honest with very often. This does not need to be said.

It sits between them in the red dark and they both leave it where it is.

---

The sound, when it comes, is structural — a groan deep in the stone of the building to her left, the kind of sound that precedes rather than accompanies collapse. Yennefer is moving before she finishes identifying it, reading the lean of the wall and the direction of likely fall and the thing that caused it, which is neither the wall settling nor an accident but something *inside* the building that has been waiting quietly for company to arrive.

It comes through the wall instead of the door — an economy of movement that bypasses courtesy entirely — and it is large and it is the wrong number of limbs and it carries with it the cold particular to things that feed on absence, that grew fat on whatever happened to Drahim, and it is faster than its size suggests.

Yennefer hits it with the full working before it clears the rubble. The magic punches through it in a way that would stop most things but this simply *redirects*, reorganizes itself around the working, and she is already revising as the shadow beside her unfolds.

Alucard does not fight the way she fights — not force against force, not controlled geometry. He fights the way weather fights, expansive and total, the darkness coming off him in a wave that engulfs rather than strikes, and the thing that came through the wall discovers in a brief and final moment that it has miscounted the number of opponents by one and the nature of that opponent rather badly. It makes a sound that is less a cry than a reversal — something proceeding suddenly in the wrong direction — and then it is simply not present anymore.

The rubble settles. Red dust drifts through red light.

Yennefer straightens her coat, a gesture that takes two seconds and communicates a complete sentence's worth of composure. She does not look at the space where the thing was.

Alucard reforms at her left side — *reforms*, she notes, not approaches — and stands there with the coat settling around him as though it answers to gravity at its own discretion.

Neither of them speaks.

She is aware of him reading her efficiency. She is aware of herself reading his, the casual totality of what he did, the absence of performance in it. That is what it looks like when something doesn't need to prove itself to its own power. She recognizes this from the inside. She finds the recognition uncomfortable in the specific way of things that disturb something you believed distinguished you.

He says nothing. She says nothing.

But when she checks the direction her earlier spell indicated and takes three steps toward it, he falls into place at a distance behind her left shoulder that is not coincidental, and she does not object, and they move through the red village in a silence that has changed its character entirely.

---

They reach the edge of where the anomaly was—*was*, resolved now, perhaps by whatever they just removed. She stops. He stops, at that same measured distance, and she turns to look at him because she's done allowing him positioning advantages she's acknowledged.

The orange lenses are dark in this light. The coat moves in a wind that isn't blowing.

"The thing you're looking for," he says, "isn't here anymore." He pauses in the way of someone offering an addendum he hasn't decided on yet. "There will be another. These manifestations travel."

"I know," she says.

"The next convergence point is three days' ride northeast." He says this with the ease of someone reading from a document she can't see. "I've been tracking the pattern for two weeks."

She looks at him for a long moment. He looks back with unlimited patience, which is maybe the most disquieting thing about him.

"Is this an invitation?" she says.

"It's a provision of information." He tilts his head, and the smile acquires a quality she hasn't seen yet — oblique, layered, amused by something she suspects is the sincerity beneath his own performance. "What you do with information is your business."

"Yes," she says. "It is."

She holds his gaze through the orange lenses for a three-count that she uses to make entirely sure she is leaving because she is choosing to leave, because the next thing she needs is northeast and she was going northeast regardless and because she has decided that this conversation is concluded. Not because anything is resolving. Not because she trusts him, or he her. Not because she is comfortable with what he is or with how accurately he read her or with the particular quality of intelligence in the face beneath that ridiculous hat.

She turns.

She walks — back straight, pace even, the red moonlight laying her shadow long behind her across the cobblestones. The lilac-and-gooseberry scent moves with her, trailing in the still air, marking the space she occupied.

From the shadows at the mouth of the alley, from the specific darkness that has organized itself around him the way darkness tends to when given the opportunity, Alucard watches her walk the full length of the main street and does not speak and does not move. The orange lenses catch the moon. He does not let things go.

He is watching the particular way she chose this — walked through the door rather than waiting to be shown it — and the smile on his face is not the performance smile, not the too-wide grotesque thing he deploys to establish hierarchy. This is something older and quieter and considerably more sincere, residing in him the way sincerity resides in things that have decided they can finally afford it.

*Interesting*, he thinks, and the word is insufficient and he knows it.

---

Above the empty village of Drahim the blood moon continues its slow transit, indifferent and red, pouring its rust-colored light over the still well and the spinning cartwheels and the three open doorways and the rubble where a wall used to be. It is the same moon that opened the night — the same weight, the same wrong color, the same atmosphere of dread that suggests something has already been decided.

But the village no longer feels empty.

It feels *occupied*, by something that didn't announce itself and didn't leave a name, and whether that is more or less frightening than what came before is a question the moon does not answer.

The red light holds.
