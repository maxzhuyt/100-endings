# High Seas and Low Vices

---

The Pearl River at dawn smelled of bilge and salted fish and something underneath both of those — something organic and ancient that had nothing to do with the sea and everything to do with what the land had been before the factories came. Merrick lay on his bunk and breathed it through the gap in the window shutter and knew, without moving, that it was going to be a bad morning. The knowing had a specific texture by now. It was not pain exactly. It was the memory of the absence of pain, which was worse.

He was already holding the figurine.

He did not remember reaching for it. It was jade — pale green, almost white at the belly, carved into a form that might have been a fish or might have been a woman depending on the light and the angle and how much he had smoked the night before. A Hong merchant's wife had pressed it into his hand eight months ago in exchange for a word said in the right ear about a customs matter, and he had kept it because it was the first bribe he had ever taken that fit in his palm. He found, since then, that he needed to have it in his hand the way other men needed to have the horizon in their eyes. It was not superstition. It was not sentiment. He didn't know what it was.

Commander James Merrick, Royal Navy, thirty-four years of age, four years on the Canton station, six months behind on every promise he had made to himself, lay in his bunk with a piece of stolen jade pressed into his left hand and waited to feel capable of standing.

Outside, the factory bells began their first sequence. Somewhere below the window a Portuguese voice was arguing with a Chinese voice about the weight of something, and neither of them, he thought, was probably right.

He stood.

---

The uniform was in better condition than he was. This was almost funny. He had kept it maintained through a kind of muscular habit that operated independently of the rest of him — the good buttons, the brushed wool, the epaulettes that still held their shape despite the humidity that ate everything else on the station. He stood at the small mirror above the washstand and put it on piece by piece, and the man in the mirror assembled himself with a competence Merrick watched from some slight distance, as if consulting a portrait of someone he had once known quite well.

His hands shook a little on the buttons. Not violently. Not enough to be visible across a deck. He had calibrated that — knew exactly how bad it would need to get before someone else could see it, and he was not there yet. He was perhaps four hours from there, which meant he had four hours to not be.

There was a knock.

"Sir." Mids Phelps, seventeen, earnest in the particular desperate way of seventeen-year-olds who had decided that the navy was going to make something of them. The voice through the door had the careful loudness of a subordinate who had already knocked once and not been heard. "Sir, Lieutenant Graves sends his compliments and asks whether you'll be at the inspection of the *Andromache*'s cargo manifest at nine. She came in last night, sir, and apparently there are — there are some irregularities with the manifest that Captain Fordyce thinks want a senior officer's eye."

Merrick tucked the last button. "Tell Lieutenant Graves I'll send word within the hour."

A pause. "He said to say, sir, that Captain Fordyce specifically requested—"

"I heard you, Phelps." Not sharp. Measured. The voice of a man who was busy, not a man who was hiding. He had worked on the difference. "Tell him within the hour. Thank you."

Footsteps retreating. The boy had the sense not to push it, which was either good instinct or already a small corruption of his own. Merrick hoped it was instinct.

He looked at the washstand. The figurine was in his right hand now — he had moved it at some point during the dressing without noticing. He put it in his breast pocket, over his heart, which was not sentiment. It was proximity. He could feel it there, the slight solid weight of it, the way it pressed through the wool when he moved.

Cargo manifest irregularities. He knew what that meant. He had written the letter that created them.

He would not be at the inspection at nine.

He opened the door and walked down the corridor in the correct way — measured, unhurried, a man moving purposefully toward something — and turned right at the end where turning right led toward the officers' mess and left led toward the stairs down to the street, and he turned left.

---

The boundary between the naval compound and the city was a low wall and a gate with two ratings on it who came to something like attention when they saw his epaulettes and something like confusion when they saw the direction he was heading. He gave them the same look he had given Phelps — the look that said *I am a man with reasons, and the reasons are not for you* — and they let him through without a word, because this was Canton and there were enough legitimate reasons for a senior officer to be moving into the city that they couldn't cost themselves the assumption.

The street took him immediately. That was always how it happened — the compound had a particular smell of oil and boiled naval everything, and then he was through the gate and the city was instead the city, which had no single smell but was rather a continuous argument of smells that changed every twenty feet. Dried fish hanging in the still air. Cookfire smoke from the vendors already set up along the factory fringe. The river, a constant presence beneath everything, its particular dark vegetable rot coming and going on the breeze. Baled cotton stacked against the wall of one of the hoppo's outer buildings, the burlap already darkening with humidity. A sugar merchant's cart, the reek of raw cane and the sharp edge of something fermenting under it.

He walked.

The Thirteen Factories district in the early morning had a different character than it had at noon, less performative, more honest. The compradors and shroffs moved through it without ceremony, knowing the geometry of the commerce before the Europeans had woken to it. Two men were arguing over a measure of something gray that might have been raw cotton and might have been something else, standing in the narrow gap between the English Factory and its nearest neighbor. A woman was washing something in a bucket, and the water running out across the stones caught the early light and held it briefly before it was gone.

The colors were — Merrick noticed this and noted the noting — the colors were too vivid. The red lacquer on the tea merchant's sign across the street was nearly aggressive. The blue of a man's jacket was the blue of something that wanted to be sky. This was how he knew, always, approximately how bad it was. When he was well the city was the city. When he was not well the city became this — a series of sensory assertions that his body couldn't quite metabolize. He pressed the pad of his thumb into the jade through the wool of his jacket and felt it push back.

Four hours, he thought. Let's say three, to be safe.

He turned into the narrower streets behind the factories, where the pidgin gave way to Cantonese and the compradors gave way to people who were not interested in compradors, and he moved through it in the way he had learned to move, which was to say as though he lived here, which he did not, but to say also as if he belonged here, which in some sense he was no longer certain he did not.

*Mm-goi*, he said to the man who stepped back to let him pass a narrow stretch. *Hóu*, said the man, looking at his uniform in the practiced way of someone who had learned exactly how much the uniform mattered in this particular geography, which was: less and less, the further you walked.

---

Drummond was sitting outside the tea merchant's back entrance on a crate that the tea merchant probably didn't know Drummond was sitting on, drinking something from a clay cup and watching the alley with the attentive vacancy of a man who had nowhere to be and had made a philosophy of it. He had been a clerk with the East India Company until he was not, which had to do with a warehouse inventory that hadn't tallied and a company auditor who had tallied it correctly, and now he did other things. He was perhaps forty-five, perhaps fifty-five — it was genuinely difficult to say, because the years had landed on him selectively, collapsing certain features while leaving others oddly intact, so that he had the eyes of a young man and the hands of something older than seemed possible.

He saw Merrick and his face did not change, which was itself a kind of greeting.

"You look terrible," Drummond said.

"I look fine."

"You look fine for a corpse." Drummond drank from his clay cup. "But not for a naval commander."

"Nobody important is looking." Merrick leaned against the wall. The crate Drummond was on had *Batavia* stenciled on its side and smelled faintly of cloves, which were probably not what it had contained. "I need you to take something to Ferreira."

Drummond's eyebrows moved in a way that was not quite skepticism and not quite interest and was possibly both. "Ferreira is in a mood."

"Ferreira is always in a mood."

"This is a specific mood. He's had a problem with the docking papers for the *Santa Cecília* — someone told the hoppo's man that the tonnage declaration was wrong, and now there's an inquiry, and he thinks—"

"I know what he thinks." Merrick looked at a point above Drummond's head, which was his habit when he was organizing what to say. "Tell him the inquiry is being handled. Tell him the man who raised it is going to reconsider his account of the tonnage declaration."

"Will he?"

"He will if the letter I've written is given to the right person, and the right person is going to receive it this morning through channels that Ferreira's man maintains in the second factory."

A pause. Drummond drank again. He had the manner of a man who had been told many things in many alleys and had developed a professional appreciation for the quality of the lie. He was assessing this one, which was not a lie, which Merrick found marginally comforting.

"That's tidy," Drummond said finally.

"It's the job. Or it was the job. The job has expanded."

Something flickered in Drummond's expression — something wry and almost warm, the particular dark fondness of men who have recognised each other across a distance. "The job always expands," he said. "That's the remarkable thing about it. You take one small step outside the proper perimeter and when you look back the perimeter has moved."

"Philosopher."

"Realist." But he was smiling a little. "All right. I'll take it to Ferreira's man at the second factory. What's in it for me?"

"I'll attest that the clay you had in the *Drummond and Company* warehouse on the fourteenth was in fact ceramic supply for the potters on Guangzhou East Road and not the other thing."

"The other thing was also for the potters."

"I believe you absolutely."

A beat. Then Drummond set down his cup. "Give me the letter."

Merrick passed it. Drummond took it with his old-man's hands and made it disappear into his coat in a movement that had a professional elegance to it, the elegance of someone who had been doing something long enough that the mechanics had become invisible.

"You need to eat something," Drummond said. "You look like you're running on principles alone, and I don't think that's accurate."

"I'm going."

"I know where you're going." Not accusation. Not pity, which would have been worse. Something flatter than both — simple information, held without judgment, which was the most accurate description of what Drummond extended to the world. He picked up his clay cup again. "He raised the price, by the way. I heard it last week."

Merrick stopped. "How much."

Drummond told him.

Merrick looked at the wall. Then he walked on.

---

Chen Baoliang operated out of two locations that Merrick knew about and possibly more that he did not, and on Tuesdays he was at the back of a printing house near the river whose front half printed almanacs and astrological guides for the wholesale market and whose rear half served a different wholesale interest. He was perhaps thirty, perhaps a little older, with the smooth economy of a man who had never wasted a movement in his life, and he wore his hair in the merchant's style and his jacket was better than Merrick's own and his expression, when Merrick entered and sat across from him on the low bench, was one of perfect unconcerned attention.

The printing house was quiet at this hour. The smell of ink was very present, and underneath it the paper smell, and underneath that something else — something faint and sweet and specific that Merrick's body recognized before his mind catalogued it, and his hands, which had been resting on his thighs, pressed flat.

"Commander," Chen said. He said it in English, with the careful precision of a man using a foreign tongue as a tool rather than a courtesy. "It has been—" he considered "—eleven days."

"I know how many days it's been."

"You look as if you know." He studied Merrick with something that was not quite clinical and not quite sympathetic — a compound interest, as if Merrick were a specimen worth observing but also a business problem worth solving. "You have the money?"

Merrick had most of the money. He put it on the bench between them. He had calculated it on the walk over and done the mathematics three times and the mathematics did not improve with repetition. He was short. Not badly short, not insultingly short, but verifiably short.

Chen looked at the money without touching it. Then he looked at Merrick. "You know what I told you last time about the price."

"Drummond told me."

"Then you know you are short."

"I know."

A long pause. The printing presses in the front room were still; the men who worked them had not yet arrived. Through the walls Merrick could hear the river, which was always audible here, a low constant sound like a crowd heard from a great distance.

"There is a matter," Chen said, "of a naval patrol on the sixteenth. The lower river, between the second and third anchorages." He said it quietly, not as a threat, not as a demand — as a man introducing a topic for discussion. "It would be useful to know the timing."

Merrick looked at him.

"The patrol's timing," Chen continued, "is the kind of information that a man in your position would have access to without any particular effort."

"That's a different category of thing."

"Many things are different categories of thing, and yet here we are in the same room with all of them." Chen's expression had not moved. It was not cruelty. It was a commercial precision that was, in its way, more unsettling than cruelty would have been. "I am not asking you to alter the patrol. I am asking you to know something you already know, and to share that knowledge."

Merrick sat with it. The jade was in his left fist, which was resting on his thigh, and he was pressing his thumb along its carved belly in a slow movement he was aware of only when Chen's eyes moved briefly to his hand and then back to his face.

"That's—" He stopped.

Chen waited.

"The fourteenth," Merrick said. "The patrol leaves at the fourteenth hour. They're at the second anchorage between the fourteenth and the sixteenth hour and then they move upstream."

Chen looked at the money on the bench. Then he looked at Merrick. Some interior calculation completed itself. He reached into the case beside him and produced what Merrick had come for, wrapped in the plain way, and set it beside the money, which he then moved to his own side of the bench.

"Come in six days," Chen said. "Not before six days. Your body needs the interval more than it thinks it does."

"I'm aware."

"I don't think you are." He looked at Merrick again — that compound look. "The figurine," he said. "May I see it?"

Merrick's hand closed to a fist.

"I've seen the type before," Chen said, without urgency. "There's a craftsman in the interior who makes them for the merchant wives. They carry a particular mark on the base — a character from the old catalogue. I'm curious only."

"It's not for sale."

"I didn't say—"

"It's not for sale." He stood. He picked up the wrapped thing, which was what he had come for, and put it inside his coat. The jade went with it, in his fist, which did not unclench until he was out the door and into the street and already walking.

He had given away the timing of a naval patrol for opium. He understood this. He filed it in the same place he filed the other things he understood, which was a place that was getting full.
