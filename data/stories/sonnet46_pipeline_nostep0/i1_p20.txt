# Gunpowder Rendezvous

---

The jukebox had been playing the same track for forty minutes.

It was a bad track — something with too much guitar and not enough sense, recorded in a studio that probably smelled like cigarettes and disappointment, pressed onto vinyl that had been warped by too many summers in too many bars exactly like this one. The neon housing the jukebox was red on one side and dead on the other, which meant the light it threw was less illumination than suggestion — a reddish interference across the bottles behind the bar, the water-stained ceiling, the back of the woman sitting at the corner stool who had thus far consumed four shots of something the bartender poured from an unlabeled bottle and who showed no signs of stopping.

Her name was Revy.

The bartender knew this the way bartenders in certain establishments came to know things: through survival instinct rather than introduction. She'd come in an hour ago, dropped herself onto the stool like she was doing it a favor, slapped a folded piece of paper under her shot glass without looking at it again, and proceeded to drink with the focused efficiency of someone treating alcohol as a professional obligation. The tattoo started at her left shoulder and moved in the direction of her elbow — a dragon, blue-black, detailed enough to suggest someone had taken considerable time and pain to put it there. Her hair was damp at the temples. The two pistols in her hip holsters were not concealed.

The bartender had stopped asking people like her to check their weapons. It was faster, in the long run, not to.

The door opened at 10:47.

The man who came through it did so without urgency — a loose arrangement of limbs that managed to look both ready and entirely indifferent to the concept of readiness. He was tall. He wore a jacket the color of something that had once been blue. He paused just inside the door with his hands in his pockets and let his eyes move across the bar in the way that wasn't looking but was, in fact, seeing everything.

They stopped on the woman at the corner stool.

Moved to the folded paper under her shot glass.

Moved to his own jacket pocket, where his hand rested against a fold of identical paper.

His face did not change. He crossed to the bar, sat down one stool away from her, and said to the bartender, "Whatever she's having."

The bartender poured. The jukebox finished its track.

The needle lifted.

Found the beginning again.

---

She didn't look at him until his drink arrived. Then she looked at him the way she looked at most things — directly, without preamble, in the manner of someone who had decided somewhere along the way that social lubrication was a resource to be allocated elsewhere.

"You're in the wrong seat," she said.

"There are twelve empty stools," he said. He picked up his drink. "I'm probably in the wrong seat for the right reasons."

She looked at his jacket pocket. He looked at the paper under her glass. There was a pause in which both of them assessed the full geometry of the situation with the speed and precision of people who were very good at assessment.

"Yuen Tak-fu," she said. "Three hundred thousand."

"Give or take," he said.

"Give or take isn't something I give or take."

He drank. Set the glass down. Looked at the bottles lined up behind the bar for a moment, as if they were mildly interesting rather than entirely irrelevant. "You know where he is?"

"I know where he's going to be."

"Then you're ahead of me."

"I'm always ahead of you." She said it without inflection, as a statement of ontological fact. "I just met you."

"That's true," he said. He didn't appear particularly troubled by it.

The jukebox ground through its chorus. She turned back to her drink. He turned back to his. Neither of them had introduced themselves. The kind of people who needed introduction were a different kind of people.

---

The second round arrived because she signaled for it and the bartender, with the practiced instinct of someone who wanted the evening to stay at its current temperature, poured two without being asked. She didn't acknowledge this. Neither did he. They drank.

"You want to tell me your read?" he said.

"No."

"Okay."

She waited for him to push. He didn't push. This was, she found, more irritating than if he had pushed.

"Macau connection," she said, eventually, with the air of someone conceding a point in an argument that hadn't technically started. "He's got a handler here who moves money through a restaurant in the port district. Tak-fu cycles through every couple of weeks to skim his percentage off the top. He'll be at the Celestial Garden by midnight."

"You've done your homework."

"I do my homework or I'm dead. Those are the options."

He nodded slowly, as if she'd said something that confirmed a theory rather than provided new information. It was an infuriating nod. She picked up her shot and looked at the middle distance and reminded herself that shooting someone for having an infuriating nod was not proportionate.

"You been doing this long?" he said.

"Define *this.*"

"Bounty work. Guns. Whatever it is you'd call it."

She turned and looked at him properly for the first time. He met the look with an expression of genuine, mild curiosity, which was somehow worse than if he'd been challenging about it.

"Since I was fourteen," she said.

He didn't say *that's young* or *Jesus* or any of the other things people said, which briefly rearranged her opinion of him by a small margin. He just turned his glass in a slow circle on the bar top.

"You?" she said.

"Different work," he said. "Same general environment."

"Syndicate?"

"Something like that."

He said it in the way people said things they were done saying, with a particular quality of finality that had nothing to prove. She recognized it. She had a version of it herself, for different rooms. She didn't push.

The jukebox restarted.

"I'm going to shoot that thing," she said.

"It's not worth the bullet," he said.

She looked at the jukebox. Looked at the gun on her hip. Considered the arithmetic. Put her hand back around her glass.

"Don't tell me what's worth my bullets," she said.

"I wouldn't dare," he said, and he said it with such complete and utterly weightless sincerity that she looked at him again, trying to locate the joke, and couldn't find one.

---

They drank through eleven o'clock and past it.

Not shoulder-to-shoulder. Not warming toward each other in the gradual way of people who are becoming something to each other. The stool between them stayed empty, held its ground like a demilitarized zone both parties had agreed to without a document. But neither of them left, and rounds arrived, and the bar around them thinned as the hour progressed until it was mostly them and the bartender and a pair of old men in the far corner playing cards with the absorbed patience of people with nowhere else to be.

She talked about the Philippines once, briefly, and then appeared to realize she was talking about the Philippines and stopped. He didn't reach for it. Months ago she would have respected that less. Now she found it — not uncomfortable, exactly. Something.

She wasn't going to examine what something.

"You do it alone?" she said.

"Mostly," he said. "I have a ship. Crew, technically. It's complicated."

"Everything's complicated when there are other people."

"That's one way to put it."

"What's another way?"

He thought about this with apparent seriousness, looking at the bar top, and then he said: "Everything's complicated."

She almost laughed. She took a drink instead, which accomplished the same thing with less information exchanged.

The physical fact of him was something she was aware of in the way she was aware of exits and entrances — involuntary, tactical, continuous. He was built in a way that looked casual until you looked for longer, until you noticed the way he occupied space with the stillness of something that had already decided how it intended to move. She was not, in general, a person who thought about other people's bodies. She thought about threats. She thought about money. She drank and looked at the middle distance and was exceptionally irritated to find that the distinction was currently blurring.

He was, she was fairly certain, equally aware of her.

Neither of them was going to do a goddamn thing about it.

---

The man who came through the door at 11:22 was not Yuen Tak-fu.

He was twenty-five, maybe, with new boots and an old face and the particular posture of someone who had been sent to do a thing they weren't quite capable of, buoyed by the confidence of whoever had sent them. He had two companions behind him who held themselves slightly better but not by much. He swept the bar with his eyes and they stopped on Revy and he reached under his jacket.

She was already moving.

Two pistols, both hands, the motion so continuous and reflexive it had no beginning — she was on her feet and between her stool and the bar with both guns up before the man had cleared leather, and the crack of her first shot put a bottle exploding off the shelf six inches from his right ear as a formal introduction.

He dove left. His companions went right and right.

The one going right-and-far raised a shotgun and Revy pivoted and she heard rather than saw the other one drop because she was already on the shotgun and there was a voice that said, somewhere behind her, flat and calm in the noise: *down,* and she dropped without thinking and something passed through the air where her head had been and the shotgun man folded.

She came up.

The first man was backed into the corner, disarmed, with a hand pressed to his ear which was bleeding from the proximity of her warning shot, and there was a gun pressed with minimal drama to the side of his jaw. Spike was holding it. He had moved from the barstool to the corner of the bar to the position he was currently in, and she hadn't seen a single step of it. His expression was the same as it had been when he was discussing the price of bullets.

"Who sent you," Revy said.

The man told them.

It was not a name either of them recognized, which meant it was a name below her usual threshold of people worth knowing, which meant this was a low-altitude problem attached to a high-altitude target.

"Go away," Spike said to the man, with the complete weariness of someone who had better things to think about.

The man went away. His companions, those capable of going anywhere, went with him. The bar was empty except for the bartender, who had achieved a state of stillness behind the bar that was practically artistic, and the two old men in the corner who appeared to have continued their card game without pause and who either had extremely good nerves or extremely poor hearing.

The jukebox was still playing.

Revy holstered both guns and looked at the door and looked at Spike and looked at the body of the shotgun man on the floor, which was producing exactly as much blood as a body in that condition produced. Then she looked at Spike again.

He had already put his gun away. He was looking at his glass, which had survived the engagement unbroken on the bar, and he seemed to be weighing whether to finish it.

He picked it up and finished it.

"Referral problem," he said.

"Yeah," she said.

"Someone told someone we were here."

"Or someone's running countersurveillance on Tak-fu."

"Which means Tak-fu already knows someone's running a ticket on him."

"Which means," she said, "we're on a clock now."

"We're on a clock."

There was a beat. She was breathing slightly faster than usual. Not from exertion. The adrenaline had nowhere to go, which was its own specific kind of problem, and she was aware of him in the immediate aftermath of the gunfire with the hyperclarity of someone whose nervous system was doing its own accounting.

He appeared to be experiencing something similar, or containing something similar. The glass went back on the bar.

"Split the bounty," he said. "Fifty-fifty. We take the job together, we split the take, we go separate ways."

"Generous of you."

"It's not generous. It's math."

"Your math assumes I need you."

"It assumes we'd each do better not watching our back all night." He looked at her. Level, not quite patient, something in his eyes that was too tired to be arrogance and too precise to be anything else. "You don't need me. I'm not suggesting you need me. I'm suggesting this goes faster and cleaner with two."

"I know what you're suggesting."

She stood there. The jukebox restarted. She thought about the three hundred thousand and thought about the man with the shotgun and thought about the name they'd been given, which connected upward to other names, which suggested that between now and midnight the topology of this job had changed.

"Sixty-forty," she said. "I found the location. I did the homework. You ride my read, you take forty."

"I just saved your head."

"I was already dropping. I didn't need you to tell me."

"Then why'd you drop?"

She looked at him. He looked at her. It was a very short pause.

"Fifty-five, forty-five," she said. "My fifty-five."

"Done," he said.

The jukebox played. Neither of them looked at it.

---

The motel was three blocks from the port district and two blocks from the Celestial Garden, which made it functional. The room was what the room was — a double that smelled of old smoke and the specific exhaustion of a thousand people who had slept badly in it, lit by a single lamp with a bulb of insufficient wattage throwing a yellow circle on the ceiling. A window looked out at an alley and a wall. The mattresses were pushed to opposite sides of the room. She didn't remember doing that. Neither, probably, did he.

She sat on the far bed with her back against the headboard and one gun across her knee and a cigarette going. He sat on the near bed with his legs stretched out and his ankles crossed and one hand resting near his holster and a cigarette going. The window was cracked. It was 11:40.

They had twenty minutes.

The room did not feel like twenty minutes of time to spare. It felt like a held breath.

"You always work solo?" she said.

"Generally."

"Your crew doesn't run jobs with you?"

"Sometimes." He looked at the ceiling. "It's different."

"Different how."

"Different," he said, and she recognized the cadence as the end of that direction.

She smoked. He smoked. Outside a boat moved through the water and its horn sounded once, dull and far away.

"You said you were dead," she said. "Earlier. At the bar."

He looked at her. "I said something like that."

"What does that mean."

"It means I died a while ago and everything since has been the extra part." He said it the way he said most things — offhand, without drama, as if he were commenting on the weather in a place where the weather was rarely interesting. "I don't think about it too much."

She looked at him.

She was not, in general, a person who was caught by things. She had a system — not a conscious one, not something she'd built deliberately, but a system nonetheless — for not being caught by things, for keeping everything at a distance that was workable. The system was very good. She had been running it for a long time.

She said: "I died a while ago too."

She hadn't meant to say it. She took a drag from her cigarette and looked at the window instead of him and the thing she'd said hung in the air between them with the particular weight of the accidentally true.

He didn't say *I know* or *I can tell* or *me too* or any of the things that would have made her pick up the gun and end the evening in a completely different way. He just let it be there, in the space between the two bad beds in the bad light, and she appreciated this in a way she was not going to examine.

"You get used to it," he said, finally.

"Do you."

"No," he said, after a moment. "But you stop expecting to."

She nodded. The cigarette was most of the way gone. Across the room from her he was a long geometry of angles and that specific stillness, and she was aware of the distance between them — not large, not small, precisely what it was — and aware that she was not going to cross it and aware that he was not going to cross it and aware that this was the correct outcome and the only outcome and she was, in some registered but uncategorized part of her chest
