# Hell Is Other Demons

The morning's damnation quota was forty-seven souls short, and Lucifer had already signed his name so many times that the quill had begun to smoke at the nib.

This was not unusual. The quill always smoked when he was annoyed, and he was, this morning, in the specific way one is annoyed by a thing one has been annoyed by before and expects to be annoyed by again: the mild, almost comfortable annoyance of a recurring pattern, like weather, like the tides, like the forty-seven-soul shortfall that appeared on the third Tuesday of every month because Beelzebub refused to acknowledge that the Southwestern Acquisitions department was understaffed and always had been.

"Gerald," he said, without looking up.

The hellhound paused its assault on the left foreleg of the Iron Throne. It was a large animal, Gerald, the size of a draft horse and in possession of three heads, each of which expressed a subtly different personality. The leftmost was alert and ashamed; the middle was calculating; the rightmost had already resumed chewing.

"Gerald."

The chewing stopped. Briefly.

Lucifer signed the forty-eighth page and moved it to the completed stack. Outside the throne room's high windows — the windowless windows, the glass that showed only the burned orange sky of the Outer Rings regardless of where in Hell you were — something screamed. Something always screamed. It was, as he had explained to a new intake of souls precisely once before growing tired of explaining things, simply the ambient noise of consequence. One stopped hearing it the way city dwellers stop hearing traffic.

He did not look at the chair to his left.

He had stopped looking at it approximately nine days ago, which was when not-looking at things became a discipline rather than an accident. The chair was there. It was empty. The second cup of black tea he'd had Prax bring in at dawn sat beside his elbow, cold now, which was his own fault for optimism. He'd sent Prax away rather than admit the tea had been for anyone.

The chair was simply a chair. The absence in it was simply an absence.

The forty-seventh shortfall in Southwestern Acquisitions was the actual problem. He wrote a note in the margin of Beelzebub's quarterly report: *This is the third month. We have discussed this. Hire someone.* Then he crossed out the last three words and wrote: *The department is structurally unsound and I will not continue to absorb the deficit from Central.* Then he crossed that out as well and wrote: *See me.* Then he put the report aside.

The argument — not the argument he was thinking about, the other argument, the one accounting for the cold tea — had started over something that was not small, exactly, but had been treated as small, which was perhaps worse. He had made a decision about the eastern border expansions without consulting anyone, which was his prerogative, it was absolutely his prerogative, Hell was not a democracy, had never once claimed to be a democracy, the name of the form of governance operating here was in fact printed on the letterhead, and if Lilith had wanted to be consulted she might have expressed that desire in any of their previous fourteen discussions of eastern policy, none of which she had attended because she had been, in her words, *otherwise occupied with things that actually matter*, and the fact that she had then materialized four days later with a sixteen-point objection to the expansion—

Gerald made a sound. All three heads made it simultaneously, which happened only when they agreed on something, and what they agreed on now was that someone was at the door.

Lucifer looked up.

---

The door opened, as it always did for her, before the knock.

The throne room's temperature did not change. It was already warm — it was always warm, it was Hell, warmth was largely the point — but the character of the warmth shifted the way a room shifts when a window is opened onto wind. The seventeen minor functionaries who had been lurking at the edges of the room waiting for audiences found suddenly and unanimously that they had other places to be. They moved toward the secondary exits in a way that they would each have described, if asked, as coincidental.

Lilith walked as if she had been somewhere more interesting and was tolerating this.

She was dressed for travel, which meant she had come from elsewhere, or wanted him to think she had. Her hair was pinned with three obsidian combs she had had since — longer than Lucifer cared to calculate, because calculating it led to other calculations he preferred not to run. She was carrying a leather satchel. The satchel meant business. The satchel meant she had bothered with a reason.

Gerald watched her from the dais.

The left head flattened its ears. The middle head was very still and very interested. The right head, the chewing head, the pragmatic head, got up and trotted down the steps and went and put its face against her hand.

She acknowledged this in the way she acknowledged most things — without making it a production, with a simple, absent press of her palm against the fur — and then she looked up at Lucifer.

"I need the Third Compact," she said. "The original."

"Good morning."

"It's not morning."

"It's always morning somewhere."

"Not in Hell."

"No," he agreed. "Not in Hell."

She waited. She was very good at waiting. She had, as far as Lucifer could calculate, invented patience, although she would dispute both the framing and the attribution.

"The Third Compact," he said, setting down the quill, "is in the archive. You know where the archive is. You helped design the archive."

"I need your seal on the retrieval request. New protocols." She said this without inflection, the way one relays factual information.

"Since when."

"Since the audit. Which you would know about if you read the memos I send."

"I read your memos."

"You respond to them."

"That implies reading."

"You respond to *the subject lines* of my memos," she said, "which is not the same thing and produces responses like the one I received in September, which addressed an entirely different document than the one I had sent."

There was a pause. It was true. He had done that. The subject line had read *Re: Eastern Compact Archive / Filing Protocol Revision* and he had responded to what he took to be *eastern* and *protocol* and *revision*, which were the words he had read, because the paragraph beneath had opened with a subordinate clause and he had, briefly, stopped paying—

"The retrieval form," Lilith said, and held it out.

He took it. His hand did not touch hers in the taking. He was aware of this as a fact.

The form was correct. It was, as she'd said, a new form, stamped with the archive seal and the date of the audit, and there was a line for his signature and a line for the purpose of retrieval and a line, inexplicably, for a secondary witness.

"Why is there a witness line," he said.

"I don't design the forms. I just brought you the form."

"The form is redundant. The archive already logs—"

"Lucifer."

He looked at her.

"Just sign it," she said.

He signed it. He slid it back across the desk. She picked it up, glanced at his signature with the professional attention of someone checking a box rather than the personal attention of someone looking at something someone had written, and tucked it into the satchel.

"Thank you," she said, which was technically polite and contained nothing.

"You could have sent a courier," he said.

"The courier would have needed your seal anyway."

"The courier could have waited."

She looked at him. A long look, the kind that in another context, in a better version of this morning, in any of several thousand previous mornings stretching back to before the word *morning* had meant anything — that look would have carried something different in it. He was fluent in her silences the way one is fluent in a language one has not chosen to stop speaking.

"I was in the area," she said.

---

The area, in this case, was nowhere near the Outer Rings. The area, in this case, was the Central Processing district, which was adjacent to the Fourth Ward, which did not connect to this wing of the palace by any efficient route. Lucifer knew this because he had personally approved the architectural plans for this wing, sixteen centuries ago, in a meeting at which Lilith had also been present, at which she had said the northeastern corridor was structurally inefficient and she had been right and he had not said so at the time.

He did not say this now.

What he said was: "The eastern expansion."

What she said was: "We're not doing this."

"I'm not doing anything. I'm noting that the Third Compact is directly relevant to the eastern expansion, which you objected to."

"My objections are in the memo."

"Your objections are in the seven-page memo you sent the week after the policy was set, yes."

"The policy was set," she said, "in a meeting to which I was not invited."

"There was no meeting. I made a decision."

"You made a decision. On the eastern border. Where I have maintained three outposts for the last two hundred years."

"Your outposts are in the northern quarter of—"

"They *were*," she said, "in the northern quarter of the eastern sector, which is now the eastern sector's *western* border, because you moved the line."

Gerald chose this moment to sneeze. All three heads produced separate sneezes in rapid succession, and the effect of this on the pile of completed paperwork was immediate and total. Pages lifted, separated, described brief arcs, settled. The forty-seventh shortfall report skated off the edge of the desk and landed, open, on the floor.

Lucifer looked at it. Lilith looked at it. They both looked at Gerald.

"Gerald," Lucifer said, with the tone of a man who is choosing not to have the further reaction he is having.

Gerald's middle head located the report. Gerald's middle head picked up the report. Gerald's middle head trotted to Lilith, because Lilith was closer, and deposited the report at her feet and looked up at her with the expression of someone who has done a good thing.

She picked it up. She straightened it against her hand. She looked at it for a moment — he saw her eyes move, saw her actually reading it — and then she held it out toward him and said, "You're still running the Southwestern deficit."

"Beelzebub is running the Southwestern deficit."

"Beelzebub runs what you allow him to run. You've been covering it for — this is three months?"

"That's not relevant to—"

"You should restructure the department." She was still holding the report out. "Break the acquisition cycle into two separate pipelines. You've been staffing for a unified process and it doesn't work at scale."

He took the report. His hand did touch hers in the taking, briefly, at the corner, the paper between their fingers for a moment before she released it. Neither of them acknowledged this.

"The restructure would need sign-off from three senior—"

"I know," she said. "I redesigned the process the last time." And then, quietly, more quietly, still looking at the report rather than at him: "You could ask me."

The throne room was very still. Even the ambient screaming, which was always there, had the courtesy to dip slightly, the way conversation dips in a room when something real has been said.

He looked at her.

She was not looking at him. She was looking at the middle distance, past his shoulder, the practiced neutrality of someone who has said a thing and is waiting to see what happens to it, and he knew this, knew the look, knew what it cost her because he knew what it would cost him, and he opened his mouth—

"The outposts," he said.

Her expression didn't change. It didn't need to change. The thing that had almost happened moved through the room like a draft and then was gone.

"Yes," she said. "The outposts."

"I can review the boundary."

"The boundary," she said, back in the register of professional neutrality, "is already documented in my objection memo. Which you didn't read."

"I read it."

"You read the subject line."

"I read the first—" He stopped. "I read most of it."

"Right." She picked up the satchel. The Third Compact retrieval form was inside it. Gerald's right head had resumed chewing the throne leg. Everything was returning to its position. "I'll have the outpost survey sent over. If you want to review the boundary, the survey is the starting point."

"Lilith."

She had turned, half-turned, the way she sometimes turned — not toward, not away, the exact angle of someone who has not decided.

"The chair," he said, and stopped.

She waited.

"You don't need to send a courier next time," he said. "For the archive requests."

A pause. Not a long one.

"The new protocols require your seal," she said. "So I know."

And then she walked to the door, which opened before she reached it.

She paused at the threshold, not looking back — but the pause was there, the fraction of a second that was not travel time, that was something else, that he chose to note and chose not to name — and then she was through, and the door was—

The door was not closed.

It stood open, perhaps eight inches, the warm orange light from the corridor leaning into the throne room. Gerald's left head looked at it. Gerald's middle head looked at Lucifer. Gerald's right head chewed.

---

Lucifer reordered the paperwork. He re-sorted the nightly quota reports, placed Beelzebub's document at the top of the review pile, and picked up the quill, which had stopped smoking.

He did not send anyone to close the door.

The cold tea sat at his elbow. He drank it, because it was there. Because waste seemed, in this particular moment, an argument for something he wasn't prepared to make.

Gerald settled at the foot of the dais, turned in two complete circles, and lay down. The chewing stopped. All three heads rested on the stones. The right head sighed.

Lucifer signed the first page of the revised damnation schedule. Then the second. Then he wrote, in the margin of Beelzebub's quarterly report: *See me. We will discuss the pipeline.* He did not cross this out.

The screaming in the Outer Rings continued, at its ordinary pitch, at its ordinary distance.

The chair to his left was empty and he did not look at it, and the door at the far end of the room stood open, and the orange light came through.

He dipped the quill and signed the third page, and the fourth, and outside the windowless windows the sky of Hell burned the same color it had always burned, and he worked, as he always worked, with great and flawless attention, and if there was something in the set of his shoulders that the paperwork could not account for, there was no one in the room to see it.

Gerald slept. The throne lost another inch of iron to the right head's dreaming jaw.

The door stood open.
