# Rehearsing Rejection

---

The concealer is wrong. She's been blending it for four minutes and it's still wrong, a pale crescent under her left eye that looks, in the bathroom light, like something is being hidden rather than corrected. Which it is. She's been awake since four.

Her phone says 6:47 PM. Her phone also says 23%, and she meant to charge it before leaving but the charger is in her bag and her bag is in the hallway and she'll do it in a minute, she'll do it before she leaves, there's time. Nadia's text said seven-thirty. There's time.

Nadia.

She sets down the sponge and looks at herself.

Nadia has been—not absent, exactly. It isn't absence when someone technically answers. It's more like the quality of the response has changed, the way temperature changes in a room before you can name it as cold. A text that used to come back in minutes now takes an hour, two hours. The voice notes have stopped. The voice notes used to be her favorite thing, Nadia talking while she walked somewhere, mid-thought, *oh wait I forgot to tell you*, the ambient sound of Nadia's life spilling in through the speaker. Silent for three weeks.

She knows what this is. She's felt this shape before.

*They're pulling away*, she thinks, and the thought has the particular quality of a long-held note—certain, sustained, low.

---

There was a period, last winter, that she returns to the way you return to a photograph. Coffee at Nadia's kitchen table, a Saturday, neither of them anywhere to be. Nadia had a blanket around her shoulders and kept reaching across the table to squeeze her hand mid-conversation, apropos of nothing, just occasional punctuation of affection. They talked for five hours. At some point it got dark and neither of them turned on the light and they just kept talking in the increasing dimness until Nadia said, *I think we've lost the ability to see each other*, and they both laughed so hard it hurt.

That was four months ago.

She collects evidence the way some people collect injuries, cataloguing without meaning to. The read receipt on the Tuesday message—she'd sent a long one, about the thing at work, and it sat there for six hours before a reply came back that said *ugh that sounds so hard, more soon, slammed today*. More soon had not materialized. The birthday of a mutual friend they'd planned to attend together; Nadia had cancelled the afternoon of, *so sorry, family thing*, which was fine, of course it was fine, except that it wasn't fine because why hadn't she texted the next day to check in, a month ago she would have texted the next day.

*Evidence*, she thinks, arranging it. *Building a case.*

She catches herself and feels, briefly, the flicker of recognition—*I know I do this*—and then the recognition passes like a cloud and the evidence remains, solid, real, irrefutable.

She does this. She knows she does this. But she also knows things other people don't, which is how endings feel from the inside when you're practiced at surviving them.

---

She checks her phone out of habit. Still 23%. She should plug it in.

Instead she picks her jacket off the hook and tries on, in the mirror of her own mind, what she will say.

Because Nadia will bring it up, probably. Or Nadia won't bring it up, which is somehow worse, which is its own kind of verdict. There will be a moment, some hinge in the evening, and Nadia will say—

*I feel like we've been in different places lately.*

That's how she'll say it. Gentle, the way Nadia does things. The kindness as preparation, the way they put a warm blanket over you before surgery.

And she'll say—she sees herself saying it, absolutely steady—*I've noticed that too. I wasn't going to bring it up.* A pause. *I wasn't sure it was worth mentioning.* Which means: *I wasn't sure you'd care.* Which means: *I know you don't.* Which means: *I want you to hear it in my voice that I am fine.*

The argument assembles itself with extraordinary efficiency, the way these things do, vivid as any real memory: Nadia's face in that particular arrangement of trying-to-be-fair, her own voice cool and uninflected, the specific pleasure of being the one who ends the conversation rather than loses it. She can feel her own heart rate in the imagining of it. She can feel the satisfaction of the exit.

Her phone says 19%. She hasn't moved in six minutes.

---

The cancelled plan in March.

At the time she'd said, *no worries, let's reschedule.* At the time she'd meant it, or believed she meant it. Now she sits with it differently. Nadia cancelled because she was already—what? Already tiring of her? Already beginning the internal calculus that precedes withdrawal, logging the cost of the friendship against its return?

She goes further back, a month before that. A dinner where Nadia had been on her phone more than usual. She'd assumed work stress—Nadia has a relentless job, she knows this—but now the assumption feels naïve, the kind of generosity you only extend when you haven't seen it yet.

She was on her phone. At dinner. Talking to someone else.

*You see*, she thinks, as though addressing a courtroom, *you see how it lines up.*

The friend in her mind is now a fully realized character, consistent and coherent, whose motivations make sense when arranged in this particular order. She understands this version of Nadia better, maybe, than she understands the one she's about to meet. This version has clear intentions. This version confirms what she already knows.

*I'll be fine*, she thinks. *I'll be completely fine. If that's what tonight is, I'll just be—* she searches for the word—*cool. Unbothered. I won't need anything.*

She is aware, for just a second, that she is rehearsing emotional withdrawal from a person who has not yet done anything. She is punishing someone inside her own head, building the cell before committing the crime. She is—

Her phone says 17% and something sharp passes through her, urgent and physical like a gear catching, and the thought dissolves.

She needs a charger. The charger is in her bag. She'll find a café with an outlet near the restaurant, she'll deal with it when she gets there, it's fine.

She puts the jacket on. She goes.

---

The train is the wrong place to think and she thinks the entire way.

Her body—hands, legs, the mechanical negotiation of stairs—carries her toward the restaurant while her mind holds its own proceedings in the dock. She watches the dark window of the train and in it sees her own face, blurred, and behind it the images: Nadia at the kitchen table, four months ago, the room going dark around them. Nadia cancelling in March. The read receipt. The shortened replies.

*Running late*—Nadia's text from an hour ago, which arrived with a heart emoji. A heart emoji, she'd thought at the time, is currency so debased it's almost insult.

And then—

A memory she didn't invite: walking home from a film last fall, she'd said something, she can't remember what now, something self-deprecating, probably too honest, the kind of thing she says when she's tired and the guard's low. And Nadia had stopped. Right there on the footpath, people parting around them, and taken her face in both hands—cold palms, she'd been wearing fingerless gloves—and said, *You are so much more than that, do you hear me?* Not performance. The particular quality of meaning it.

She feels it land again now, on the train, and for a moment the case she's been building has a hole in it.

A tender person does not do what you've decided she's doing.

She closes her eyes.

*Tenderness is the trap*, she thinks. *Tenderness is how you get close enough to leave.*

She watches the dark tunnel go past.

---

The restaurant is warm and slightly too loud and Nadia is already there.

She sees her from the door—Nadia hasn't spotted her yet, looking down at the menu with her reading glasses on, the ones she's slightly embarrassed about, pushing them up her nose with one finger, and there is something so specifically Nadia about the gesture that the breath catches somewhere in her chest.

Then Nadia looks up and sees her. And her face—

Her face does the thing it does. Opens. That's the word, the only word: opens, like a window from inside.

"Hey." Nadia is already half out of her chair. "Hey, I'm so glad—come here."

The hug is long. Nadia is a hugger in the way some people are, full and unironic, arms actually holding rather than the performative press that passes for embrace. She smells like the coat she always wears in winter, wool and something faintly cedar. She is so familiar that the body responds before the mind can intervene.

*Oh*, she thinks, helplessly. *Oh.*

The relief is embarrassing in its scale. She feels it move through her the way a very hot shower moves through you when you've been cold for a long time—first the surface, then deeper, the particular animal ease of being held by someone who knows you.

"I missed you," Nadia says, pulling back to look at her. "You look good. How are you, actually, and I mean actually—"

*Why is she being so warm.*

The thought arrives immediately, the filing-in of a standing army.

*Why is she being this warm tonight in particular. After weeks of nothing. Why tonight.*

She knows why tonight. She thinks she knows why tonight. This is what it looks like when someone is managing a transition carefully, turning up the warmth before they dim it, so that the ending lands softly, so that later they can say *I was kind to her, I was good to her, I made sure she felt loved before I—*

"I'm good," she hears herself say. She sounds entirely normal. "Sorry I'm—yes. Good. I'm really good."

"Liar," Nadia says, affectionately, sitting back down. "You're always terrible at that. It's one of my favorite things about you."

She watches Nadia pour her a glass of water. She watches herself receive it. She observes, from some slight remove, the tenderness of the gesture and its simultaneous dismantling—the way she can hold *this is real* and *this is performance* in both hands at once, and squeeze them both, and not be able to tell which breaks first.

---

Halfway through the meal, Nadia sets down her fork and says: "I feel like I've been neglecting you."

It lands like a stone dropped into water.

"I've been in my head this past month. Work has been a lot, but also—I don't know. I get like that sometimes and I hate it, I go quiet. I should have said something instead of just sort of disappearing." She looks directly at her. "I'm sorry. You matter to me and I'm sorry."

The words are specific. The words are *personal* and *specific*—not the soft blur of a social apology but something oriented, aimed. Something that requires actual knowledge of her in order to land the way it does.

She feels her throat close in the way that isn't sadness exactly, more the body's recognition of being seen, of being wanted in spite of all available reasons not to be.

*I need this*, she thinks, and the need feels almost unbearable, this raw and involuntary craving, this impossible situation of wanting someone while being terrified of how much you want them, while knowing that wanting them this much is itself the danger, that the depth of the want guarantees the depth of the eventual—

*"You matter to me."*

She hears it again. She turns it in her hands.

*This is real*, she thinks. And then: *This is what it looks like before it ends.*

Her phone screen lights up briefly in her pocket, notification for nothing important, and she feels her heart lurch before she remembers: 11%. She left it alone. She forgot to find an outlet. She's going to lose it before she gets home.

"You don't need to apologize," she says.

And she means it and she doesn't mean it and she watches herself mean and not mean it simultaneously and it is exhausting, the bifurcation of it, the constant split-screen of living with herself.

---

She sees it clearly, briefly, like a room lit by lightning.

She is in the loop. She can see the exact shape of it, the architecture: the evidence-gathering, the rehearsed confrontation, the preemptive grief, the warmth received and immediately interrogated, the way even kindness gets processed as data, as threat, as the soft footfall before the door closes. She's been here before—she's always here—this exact room with its particular angles and its familiar wallpaper, the pattern: abandonment as premise, and everything else arranged around it.

*This is the pattern*, she thinks, sitting across from Nadia who is telling a story about something at work, gesturing with her hands the way she does. *This is the script. This is not necessarily real.*

She holds that. She actually holds it.

There is a moment of extraordinary quiet inside her.

---

But the imagined confrontation knows her better than real life does.

The version of tonight she constructed on the train—that version has incorporated everything: Nadia's warmth, the apology, the hands-on-face memory from last fall, the cedar coat, everything—and retrofitted it. The story is more complete now, more internally consistent. *You were warm tonight because you've made the decision. You apologized to clear your conscience. You held me because you already know this is ending.*

The real Nadia, here, laughing at her own story, reaching across the table to flick a breadcrumb dramatically off the tablecloth—this real Nadia is contingent, unpredictable, full of data that doesn't yet resolve. The imagined Nadia is knowable. Follows from premises. Confirms what she already suspects.

She cannot help which one feels more real.

The spiral takes her back without drama, without fanfare. It doesn't sweep her away—it simply proceeds, the way a tide proceeds, and she stands in it.

*You were kind to me tonight*, she thinks, watching Nadia's face, *because you're about to leave.*

And she can feel the argument rising from the foundation: *I knew before you arrived. I knew from weeks of silence. I built a version of you that was honest about what you were doing. That version is more truthful than the one sitting in front of me, laughing at a breadcrumb.*

The fortress goes up. She watches it rise. She knows how to do this—she is very good at this—the smooth management of need, the face that reveals nothing, the heart that is already relocating, making arrangements, sorting through what it will keep.

She will be fine.

---

The check comes. They put on coats. The evening has the slightly elegiac quality of winters ending or summers ending or things ending in general, the particular golden light of a thing as you're still inside it but can already imagine its absence.

At the door, Nadia reaches for her hand. Not a farewell gesture—habitual, easy, just reaching for her hand while she says something about a weekend in February, some plan, a specific date. As though there will be a February. As though she is making commitments.

She feels it—the hand—and what moves through her is not interpretable as anything: not relief, not terror, not suspicion. Just Nadia's hand, which is cold because they just stepped outside, and the specific weight of it, which she knows.

*I'm so tired*, she thinks.

Not of Nadia. Of herself. Of the machinery of herself, the perpetual motion of it, the way it runs and runs and runs on almost nothing and produces this—this sprawling, vivid, well-argued nowhere. She is so tired of the intelligence of it. She is tired of how convincing it is. She is tired of knowing, at the top of her mind, that she is doing this—knowing it clearly, clinically, naming the patterns by name—while some deeper engine runs undisturbed below.

She will go home tonight and the evening will disassemble.

She knows this. She will lie in the dark and play it back and by some hour of the morning the warmth will be evidence and the apology will be strategy and the hand will be a kindness extended so as to make the withdrawal more graceful. She knows what she will do with tonight. She knows how she works.

She holds Nadia's hand on the street.
