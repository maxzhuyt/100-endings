# Lost & Found in Osaka

Den Den Town on a Saturday was its own frequency — the overlap of a hundred different audio sources flattening into something that was almost white noise if you let it. Mio didn't let it. She walked slowly, headphones around her neck, picking apart the layers: the chime of a pachinko parlor two floors up, the canned voice announcing a sale on HDMI cables, someone's muffled YouTube video leaking through a propped-open door. The neon made everything look slightly wet. She liked it here. She liked that everyone was looking at something else.

She was trying to find a specific VCO module — discontinued, available used if you were patient and willing to walk — and she was on her third shop when the guitar sound hit her.

Not the fact of a guitar. The *tone* of it. A particular mid-range brightness, something almost nasal, almost vintage, and she registered it the way she registered a wrong note in a song she knew well: immediately, involuntarily, before she had decided to care. Then the melody started and she knew it — the opening phrase from the *Neon Genesis* title sequence, arranged for single guitar in a way that stripped out everything sentimental and left only the architecture of it — and her feet had already turned her around before she'd thought about it.

The busker was maybe twenty, twenty-two, with the guitar strap adjusted too low in a way that somehow still looked intentional. She played with her eyes half-open, like she was listening to something behind the song, and the small crowd around her was doing that thing crowds do when they're not sure if they're allowed to enjoy something — standing at a slight angle, not quite committing. The busker didn't seem to notice or care. She bent a note at the end of the phrase and held it a half-beat longer than the original, and Mio's headphones slipped off her shoulder and she didn't catch them in time and they hit the pavement and she grabbed them too fast, face warm.

The crowd shifted. Someone with a stroller needed through. Mio didn't move, and when the gap closed she was three feet closer to the case on the ground and the busker's eyes found her mid-phrase, just for a second, no interruption in the playing.

The song ended. Applause, a few coins. The busker crouched to adjust something in the case and said, without looking up, "You caught the bend."

Mio's brain produced several responses and discarded all of them. "I — yeah."

The busker stood. She was taller than she'd looked sitting. "Most people don't. They just know it sounds a little different." She said it like she was continuing a conversation they'd already been having. "What'd you think?"

The headphones slipped. Mio's hand went up automatically to catch them, pressing them against her collarbone. "The resolution's different. In the original it goes back to the root. You went to the sixth."

A pause. The busker tilted her head very slightly. "Yeah," she said. "I did."

"It's less — it doesn't close. It keeps going somehow, even after it stops."

"That's exactly it." The busker sat back down on her case, hooking the guitar across her knee. "You play?"

"Synthesizers. Mostly." Mio looked at the middle distance. "Not performing. Just — building things."

"What kind of things?"

"Textures, mostly. Like — " She stopped. Started again. "Not songs exactly. More like weather."

The busker was quiet for a moment, which was different from the silence of someone waiting for more. It was the silence of someone actually thinking. "Okay," she said. "So do you tune to a key or do you just — "

"Both. Depends on whether I want it to sit inside something or just exist by itself." Mio heard herself talking and recognized that she was doing the thing she couldn't always stop, the going-too-specific thing, and she closed her mouth.

"I've been trying to figure out how to make a guitar do that," the busker said. "Exist by itself. I use a loop pedal but it still sounds like a guitar doing a thing. You know? It still has intent."

"Synthesis can remove the intent," Mio said. "Or — hide it. Make it feel like it was always there."

"I'm Riku."

"Mio."

"Mio." Riku said it like she was testing the weight of it. "What brought you to Den Den Town?"

"A VCO module. A Doepfer A-110. Discontinued." She paused. "Old, but the oscillator has a warmth that the new ones — " She stopped again. "Sorry."

"Don't be." Riku was watching her with attention that wasn't pressuring anything. "I know what a VCO is. My first guitar teacher quit to build modular synths. We still talk. He'd lose his mind over what you just said about weather."

Mio looked at her directly for the first time. "Really."

"He'd want to collaborate immediately and it would be a disaster but in a good way." Riku stood, started loosening her guitar strap. "Are you hungry? There's a kaitenzushi about five minutes from here that's actually good, which is rarer than it sounds."

Mio's first instinct assembled itself — the no, the maybe another time, the I should really keep looking — and it was smaller than usual, and she noticed that it was smaller, and she wasn't sure what to do with that. "I haven't eaten since — "

"Then yes." Riku said it simply, already sliding the guitar into its case.

"Your headphones," she said, as she clicked the latches closed. "Are those the Sony MDR-7506s?"

Mio's hand went to them. "Yeah."

"Do you always wear them down like that or is that a today thing?"

"I — " She thought about it. "Always, I think. They're — I don't know. It's easier."

Riku smiled, not unkindly. "Sure," she said, and picked up her case.

They walked south through the thinning afternoon crowd, and Riku said something about loop pedals and Mio said something back, and by the time the kaitenzushi's glass door slid open — the warm smell of vinegared rice, the soft mechanical hum of the belt — they were mid-sentence, and the city was doing its thing around them, and Mio's headphones were around her neck, and she hadn't noticed.
