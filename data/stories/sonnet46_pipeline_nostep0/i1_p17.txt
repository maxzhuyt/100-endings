# Court of the Dreaming

The court had been there before courts existed. This was evident in the architecture, which did not entirely agree with itself — the columns too tall, the vaulted ceiling at a height that suggested whoever had designed it had been thinking about something else at the time, something vast and preoccupying, and had simply let the ceiling continue upward until it became somebody else's problem. The floor was dark stone that reflected nothing. The air tasted faintly of the hour before sleep.

Dream sat at the bench.

He had the look of someone who had been informed, at great length and with great enthusiasm, about a problem he did not consider a problem, and who had nonetheless been compelled by forces obscure even to himself to convene this proceeding. His pale hands were folded. His eyes were the deep and depthless black of a sky that has forgotten it was ever anything else. He was, by any observable measure, utterly still.

His irritation was also utterly still. It simply occupied the same space.

From somewhere near the ceiling — from some junction between one impossible arch and another, some crease in the stonework where the geometry had stopped trying — there came a sound. Brief, ascending, distinctly wet at the edges. A cackle, technically, though it had ambitions beyond the word. It bounced once off the far wall and dissolved.

Dream's eyes moved upward by perhaps two degrees.

Then he looked back at his folded hands.

---

First Love entered from the left.

She was rosy-cheeked in the way that suggested she had been rosy-cheeked for a very long time and had made peace with it as a defining characteristic. She wore something that managed to be both practical and slightly flustered — a jacket with too many pockets, all of them full, and a sash that had started the morning tied correctly and had since developed opinions. She carried a leather portfolio that was visibly overstuffed, several pressed wildflowers having escaped their intended position and now protruding from the top at angles that suggested they had been filed with feeling.

She took her position at the plaintiff's table, set down the portfolio, squared it with the table's edge, then immediately picked it up again and squared it more firmly. She looked up at Dream with the expression of someone who had been wronged in a way that was difficult to explain without context, and who had prepared an enormous quantity of context.

Mora entered from the right.

She was ancient in the way that certain things are ancient — not ostentatiously, not performing it, simply having been around long enough that time had stopped bothering to leave marks and had moved on to easier subjects. She was small and she was not small. Her gray hair was pinned with something that was not a pin. Her face held the particular composure of someone who has seen every possible human expression of moral outrage and has found the collection, on balance, charming but repetitive.

She was eating grapes.

The grapes were not, as far as anyone could determine, from anywhere in the Dreaming. She had simply produced them, and she ate them one at a time, with an air of meditation, as she settled into her seat at the defendant's table and looked around the courtroom with the mild interest of someone who has been to many courtrooms and found them, on balance, fine.

The space between the two tables was the space between a pressed wildflower and a grape. It was not a small distance.

---

"We are convened," Dream said, "in the matter of Dreamscape Four-Seventeen-Subsection-Meridian." He paused. "Which I understand has been called, colloquially, *The Field.*"

First Love's hand rose immediately. "I named it. That's actually relevant to—"

"It is not yet time for you to speak," Dream said.

Her hand lowered. Her cheeks went slightly rosier.

"The plaintiff will present her case."

First Love rose. She opened the portfolio, made a small distressed noise, and reorganized the wildflowers before producing from beneath them a sheaf of documents covered in handwriting that grew progressively more emphatic toward the bottom of each page.

"Thank you," she said. She smoothed the top page. She looked up at Dream with earnest, prepared eyes. "I'll start at the beginning."

"I assumed you would," Dream said.

She started at the beginning.

The field, she explained, had taken considerable time. Years, in the terminology that mattered here — not human years exactly, but the kind of years measured in accumulated emotional experience, in the slow accretion of longing, in the particular quality of light that exists in memory just before something important happens. She had done the light herself. She was proud of the light.

"Golden," she said. "But not oppressively golden. Aspirational golden. The golden that makes you think something is about to—"

"I am familiar," Dream said, "with golden."

She moved on. The wildflowers had been individually placed. The specific angle of the hill. The temperature — and here she produced a document — had been calibrated to a precise warmth that would read, to a dreaming sixteen-year-old boy, as *summer before everything changed,* a sensation she had been refining since approximately the wheel. The girl in the dream — not a real girl, a dream-construct, a representation of First Love's own better instincts — had been positioned at the appropriate distance from the boy for the appropriate ambiguity. Not too close. Not so far that the dream resolved into comfort. The tension of *almost.* The craftsmanship of *not yet.*

First Love paused. She looked down at her notes. She looked up.

"It was good work," she said. "It was genuinely good work, and I would like that acknowledged."

"Noted," Dream said.

"Then *she*—" First Love's composure developed a hairline fracture. She pointed, not quite at Mora, but in Mora's general direction, which was the concession she was willing to make to professional decorum. "She came in."

The crack in the composure widened slightly. First Love described it as she had described it in the filing, as she had described it to her colleague Romantic Longing at length and with gestures on three separate occasions: the field, undisturbed, the golden light, the almost-kiss, the construct-girl and the boy close enough that it mattered — and then *something shifted.* The weight of the construct-girl changed. The quality of attention in the dream changed. The girl looked up, and her face was the same face and then it was not the same face, and she moved in a way that was not the way First Love had designed her to move, and then she was sitting on the boy's chest.

"On his chest," First Love repeated, in case this had not landed.

"I have read the filing," Dream said.

"She was sitting on his chest, and she was — there was a quality to the air, to the breathing, it was — he was breathing and she was—" First Love stopped. She turned a page. "The dream-construct was feeding. On his breath. Which is — that is not what that dream was for. That dream was not a pantry."

From somewhere above and to the right, a sound descended briefly, richocheted off a column, and vanished.

Dream's eyes moved upward. Then back.

"At this time," First Love said, "I would like to present exhibit four, which is a description of the defendant's appearance during the incursion, for the court's consideration."

She shuffled to the relevant page. She cleared her throat. There was a pause that was almost imperceptible and then, in the pause, something happened to First Love's expression that was not quite what she had intended.

"The defendant," she read, "during the incursion — and I want to be clear that I find this entirely irrelevant to the legal question — appeared in a form that some observers might characterize as—" She stopped. She cleared her throat again. "The form was, by certain visual metrics, which should not be taken as any kind of endorsement or—" She looked up. She looked back down. "She was beautiful," she said, quickly, as though if she said it fast enough it wouldn't count. "The defendant appeared in a form that was beautiful, which is — that's beside the point, and I want to return to the point, which is the chest. The sitting. The unauthorized—"

"Thank you," Dream said. "You may be seated."

She sat. She squared the wildflowers.

---

"The defendant will present her case."

Mora set down the grapes. She rose without haste. She smoothed nothing. She looked at Dream with the expression of a woman who has taken this particular meeting ten thousand times, in ten thousand forms, and has decided once and for all that she will not be performing anxiety on anyone's behalf.

"A girl's gotta eat," she said.

She sat back down.

Dream looked at her. The look contained several things, most of them precise.

"Is that the totality of your defense?"

Mora considered. She picked up a grape. "There's more," she allowed.

She rose again.

The dreamscape in question, she explained, had considerable existing emotional infrastructure. First Love was a talented craftsperson — here she inclined her head toward the plaintiff's table in a way that was almost a compliment — and the field had been built with exceptional attention to the quality that made dreams *sticky.* The ambiguity. The almost-ness. The sustained, carefully calibrated *wanting.* 

"All of which," Mora said, "is extremely useful to me."

She had not entered the field as a vandal. She was not in the business of vandalism — vandalism required caring enough about the original structure to want to unmake it, and she had no such feeling. She had entered as an opportunist, which was, she suggested, a different category, and possibly a protected one.

"I didn't destroy the field," she said. "The light is still there. The wildflowers." She gestured toward First Love's table, at the literal wildflowers protruding from the portfolio, with an expression of mild acknowledgment. "The girl is still there, technically. I just — adjusted the nature of the encounter."

"You sat on his chest," First Love said.

"Plaintiff," Dream said.

"She sat on his — I just want to make sure the court has not—"

"The plaintiff," Dream said, "will not speak again until invited."

First Love closed her mouth. She squared the portfolio.

Mora continued. There was, she suggested, an educational dimension to her version of events that First Love's original dreamscape notably lacked. The boy — she understood his name was Max — would have learned from the first dream what wanting felt like when it was safe and golden and still. A useful lesson. She did not diminish it. But what he was learning from her version of the dream was something more nuanced: that want and dread could occupy the same space, could in fact be the same space, could be a field in warm light with a girl who was a girl and then was something older sitting on your chest and taking your breath and the taking was terrifying and the terror was not, entirely, unwelcome.

"That's a life skill," Mora said. "You could argue I'm doing him a favor."

From the ceiling, a sound. Something between a laugh and water moving in an old pipe. It rebounded twice and then was gone. Nobody looked up. Nobody mentioned it.

"Furthermore," Mora said, "and I think this is really the crux of it—" She looked at Dream directly. He was already looking at her. "He keeps coming back."

The courtroom held this sentence for a moment.

"The boy," Mora said, with the patience of a woman explaining gravity, "goes to sleep, and he comes back to the field, and he comes back to whatever version of the dream is currently running in the field, and he does this voluntarily, or as voluntarily as anything a sleeping sixteen-year-old does can be called voluntary, and he has been doing it for three months." She tilted her head. "If the plaintiff's version was the one he wanted, I would think — and I don't want to presume — but I would think he would perhaps prefer to dream about something else."

She sat.

She picked up a grape.

---

Dream looked at his folded hands for a moment. He appeared to be consulting an interior document of some kind.

"I have questions," he said, "for both parties."

He asked First Love several questions about the construction of the dream, precise and brief, which she answered at approximately three times the necessary length. He asked Mora several questions about the mechanism of the incursion, which she answered with monosyllables and occasional shrugs that somehow conveyed complete information.

Then he paused.

He paused for long enough that it became a different kind of pause — the kind that preceded something he had not written down because writing it down would have required acknowledging that he was going to need to ask it.

"The breath-feeding," he said. "Describe the mechanics."

Mora looked at him with calm interest.

"Does she need to be—" First Love started.

"Plaintiff."

"I just mean, she could demonstrate in a way that—"

"Plaintiff, if you speak again before I invite you to speak, I will find against you on principle."

First Love became very still. Her cheeks, against all apparent possibility, went rosier.

Dream looked back at Mora. He asked the question with the same precision with which he asked all questions, and the words were the right words and they were in the right order, and something in his expression suggested that he found the asking of them a mild but meaningful inconvenience to his general sense of the dignity of this proceeding, which was his proceeding, in his court, and he had consented to hold it and still found himself, somehow, asking whether the breath passed through the nose or the mouth.

"Both," Mora said. "Depending."

Dream held this answer for exactly as long as it took to register it. "Depending," he said, not as a question.

"On the dream."

He moved on.

---

The central problem emerged the way problems do in court — not announced, but surrounded, by both parties, until it stood alone in the middle of the proceeding with nowhere to go.

First Love had been building toward it with increasing agitation. Mora had been waiting for it with the patience of someone who had already arrived.

"The boy," Dream said, "returns to the dream."

"Yes," said Mora.

"Yes," said First Love, more miserably.

"Voluntarily."

"I would argue—" First Love began.

"She would argue," Mora agreed, pleasantly.

"I would argue that at sixteen, during sleep, the question of what constitutes voluntary—"

"He is asleep," Dream said. "Not compelled. Not trapped. He returns to a dream that has, by the plaintiff's own account, been demonstrably altered from its original design. He returns to a version of the dream that includes the defendant. He continues to return."

First Love looked at her wildflowers. "Yes," she said quietly.

"Does the court need to know," Dream said, with the air of someone who had decided to simply go through this door directly and get it over with, "what exactly the boy is currently dreaming? The original vision? The defendant's incursion? Some—" He paused. A very brief pause. "—hybrid?"

Both parties looked at each other. The look was, despite everything, briefly united.

"I haven't looked," First Love said.

"I haven't needed to," Mora said.

"Neither of you," Dream said, "knows what the boy is currently dreaming when he enters the field."

"No," they said, almost simultaneously.

A silence that nobody elected to fill.

"Good," Dream said, with finality, suggesting very strongly that he also would not be looking.

---

Dream was quiet for a moment. Then he delivered his ruling.

He delivered it in the tone with which one reads coordinates of distant places — accurate, unhurried, impersonal, and with an undercurrent of private knowledge about the territory that he had not been asked to share and would not be volunteering.

The defendant, he said, retained access to Dreamscape Four-Seventeen-Subsection-Meridian on a conditional basis. The condition was not defined by schedule or frequency or the consent of the plaintiff, which had been requested and noted and set aside. Mora could enter as she entered. She could eat as she ate.

First Love's creative credit for the dreamscape's original construction was affirmed and formally recorded. The light was hers. The wildflowers were hers. The hill, the temperature, the calibrated ambiguity of the almost-kiss — all of it was hers and would remain attributed to her in whatever records such things were kept in. Her craftsmanship was exceptional, and this too was noted.

First Love's chin came up slightly.

"However," Dream said, "effective immediately, the dreamscape is subject to a standing modification."

He looked at neither of them when he said it. He looked at his hands.

The field would retain the light. The field would retain the wildflowers. The girl-construct would retain her original positioning. All of this would remain intact in the initial establishment of the dream — First Love's architecture, whole and credited. But the dream would now include, at a moment of its choosing, a third element, one that had not been designed by either party
