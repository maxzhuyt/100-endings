# Caribou Season at the Lodge

---

## Chapter Three

He woke before dawn to the sound of something moving on the porch.

Not the wind—the wind had died sometime in the small hours and left behind a cold so complete it felt structural, like the cold was holding the world together. This was footsteps. Deliberate, unhurried, crossing the porch boards from one end to the other and then stopping directly outside the back room window.

JT lay still. He breathed through his mouth.

After a long moment he turned his head toward the window. The glass was fogged at the corners. Beyond it: darkness, the suggestion of treeline, and nothing else. No shape. No movement.

He got up at five-thirty when he heard his father in the kitchen, and he said nothing about it at breakfast.

---

The second morning they went out in two directions—his father and Mike taking the southern meadow route where they'd had luck on the second day two years ago, JT going north and east on the pretext of scouting the ridge. It wasn't a pretext he'd entirely planned. He'd heard himself suggest it at the table and watched his father consider it with an expression he couldn't read.

"Stay off the north cut," his father said.

"I know," JT said.

He went east, toward the ridge. Or he started that way. By the time he'd cleared the first tree line and entered the mixed growth beyond it, he understood that he'd known, before he even spoke at breakfast, that he was going to end up somewhere else.

He told himself he was watching for sign. It wasn't wrong. He was watching for sign.

What he found, forty minutes from the lodge, at the edge of a shallow depression where a frozen creek ran under a shelf of ice, was a bull caribou standing alone in the open.

JT stopped.

The bull was large—larger than the one in the clearing, though he might have been misremembering the clearing. It stood in the pale morning light with its head turned partly toward him and its breath making soft clouds in the cold air. At this distance he could see the flank rising and falling. He could see the eye.

It had found him before he'd found it.

He was downwind. He'd been moving quietly. And yet the animal had oriented on him with an unhurried awareness, the way a person turns from a window when they hear their name spoken in another room. Not alarmed. Already knowing.

JT had his rifle. He was aware of it in the way he was aware of his own hands—present, automatic, part of the machinery of what you did out here. He didn't raise it. He stood at the edge of the depression and the bull stood in the center of it and they looked at each other, and somewhere in the middle of that exchange something changed in JT's chest. A loosening. A giving-way.

*You could just walk down,* he thought. The thought came in a voice that was almost his own. *It's not going to run. You know that. It's been waiting. You could walk down and you could—*

A branch cracked somewhere to his left.

JT flinched so hard he nearly lost his footing in the snow. His hand went to the rifle, gripped it, and he turned toward the sound with his heart hammering—but there was nothing there, only birch trees and shadow and the gray light coming down through bare branches.

When he turned back, the bull was gone.

Not running—there was no sound of it, no broken crust of snow, no expelled breath hanging in the air where it had stood. Just gone, as though it had simply stepped out of being while he wasn't looking.

JT stood at the edge of the depression for a long time.

He thought about what he'd almost done. He couldn't name it. He understood enough to know that *walking down there* was not really what the thought had been about, that the thought had been a translation of something that didn't have words in the language he'd grown up speaking, and that translation was already unreliable.

He thought: *my father had a rifle in his hand and didn't raise it either.*

He thought: *Mike's eyes when he was field-stripping the rifle. The way they'd gone somewhere else and then come back.*

He turned north, toward the old birch stand, and walked until the trees changed around him.

---

Nobody went to the north cut anymore. That was what Mike had said, and JT could feel why before he'd been in it ten minutes. The standing birches were old enough that their trunks had thickened into something architectural, white columns rising to a canopy so high it was barely visible. Sound behaved strangely here. His footsteps came back to him fractionally delayed, like an echo from something much farther away. The cold had a different quality. Denser, somehow. Less like weather and more like presence.

He found the cabin in a small clearing where the trees pulled back in a near-perfect circle.

It had been a trapping cabin, originally, built in some decade before JT's father was born—a single room with a low pitched roof, one window boarded from the inside with exterior-grade plywood, a stovepipe still projecting from the peak though the metal had gone the color of old blood. Mike was right about the roof. A section on the north side had buckled inward under the weight of accumulated winters and stayed there. The front wall was intact. The door—or what served as a door, a sheet of corrugated steel that someone had fitted into the frame with no visible hardware—was flush in its opening and showed no gap around its edges, which meant no light inside, which meant it had been fitted carefully.

JT stood at the tree line for a full minute.

Then he crossed the clearing and moved the steel sheet aside.

---

The smell hit him immediately: old ash, something organic he couldn't categorize, and underneath both of them something metallic and sweet that coated the back of his throat. He held the sheet aside with one hand and let his eyes adjust.

The stove was in the corner. It was a small, corroded thing, nothing more than a barrel stove with a cast iron door, and it had been used recently—JT could see the ash was dense and fresh, a gray-white mound that had spilled out onto the wooden floor. Someone had been burning things in it. Lots of things. Often.

The walls told the rest of it.

They'd been marked. Not spray paint, not markers, not anything that came in a can or a pen or anything manufactured—it looked like charcoal, or ash mixed to a paste, or perhaps blood in its various stages of drying. The marks were not graffiti. They were not the work of bored teenagers or a troubled drifter with time to kill. They were systematic. Some of them were repeated enough that JT began to perceive them as units, as a notation, though for what system of thought he couldn't begin to guess. Among them, at intervals, were images he could read: an antler, rendered simply. A standing figure that might have been a man or might have been an animal caught at the moment of change between one thing and another. A shape like an eye—oval, bisected, repeated in rows across the length of the back wall.

On the floor: bones. Arranged. Not scattered or left haphazardly but placed in configurations that mirrored certain of the wall marks, radiating outward from the stove in an organization too careful to be accidental. Hooves. A jawbone. A skull with the antlers cut at the base, both sides, leaving two flat scarred circles above the eye sockets like closed eyes or old wounds.

In the center of it all, between the stove and the skull, something JT looked at for a long time before he understood what he was seeing.

A bundle. Wrapped in burlap. About the length of a man's forearm. It had been there long enough that the burlap had dried and stiffened around whatever was inside and he was not going to open it and he did not open it, but he crouched over it and looked at it for a long time, and while he did he thought about how many hunting seasons there had been in years he hadn't come along, and about the cow caribou that had stood and waited, and about his father's reaching hand, and about what Mike's eyes looked like now compared to what they'd looked like when they were boys.

He replaced the steel sheet carefully behind him when he left.

He walked back through the north cut and neither hurried nor slowed.

What he felt was not entirely fear. He didn't have a name for the rest of it.

---

## Chapter Four

His father was back before him.

JT saw the old man's tracks in the snow coming up from the south, from the meadow route, and the tracks were wrong—he could see that before he'd even registered what was wrong about them, some animal-level pattern recognition firing bright before the reasoning followed. The stride was off. Too long, or not long enough in the wrong places, or pausing where it shouldn't pause. His father had stopped three times between the tree line and the porch. JT could see the divot of each boot's shift in weight, the slight disturbance in the snow where he'd turned.

He'd been stopping to look at something. Or stopping because he'd forgotten what he was doing.

JT went inside.

His father was at the table with a cup of coffee and the hunting license paperwork spread in front of him, filling in the kill record. He looked fine. He looked exactly like a man filling in paperwork.

"Good ridge?" he said without looking up.

"No sign," JT said. "Where's Mike?"

"Coming up. He got a shot at a cow, tracking it." He put a mark in the record. His penmanship was precise, the way it always was. "Should be back by noon."

JT took off his boots and put them by the door and stood for a moment in the middle of the room without doing anything else.

"Dad," he said.

His father looked up.

What JT had planned to say—had been walking back through the birch stand composing, arranging, selecting the words that might make it through whatever was between them—was not what came out. What came out was something much smaller and less formed, the stripped-down thing underneath the argument, like a bone after the removal of everything else.

"Are you all right?" he said. "Are you—is everything okay?"

His father held his gaze for a moment. Something moved through his expression, too fast to read—not quite surprise, not quite recognition. Something that might have been the beginning of something else, if it had kept going.

"I'm fine," he said. "Good hunt this year. Animals are showing themselves."

He looked back down at the paperwork.

JT said, "Yeah." He said, "Good." He went and sat down on his bunk with his rifle across his knees and stared at the wall and felt the distance between what he knew and what he was allowed to say widen like a crack in ice.

---

Mike came back at one in the afternoon trailing blood in the snow. The cow had run a long way before she dropped—he'd tracked her a mile south before he found her, and by then she'd been down long enough that she'd gone stiff in the cold, and he'd had to gut her alone. He came through the door with it all over his coat and his lower arms and the front of his waders, and he sat down heavily in the chair nearest the door with the look of a man who'd forgotten to remember something essential.

"Get the job done?" their father asked.

Mike nodded. "She's tagged. I'll need help with the drag."

"After lunch."

"Yeah."

JT watched his brother. He was watching for the thing he'd seen in the clearing, that going-away and coming-back behind the eyes—but Mike didn't look like that now. He looked tired, only tired, in the boneless and genuine way of someone who'd done hard physical work in the cold. He looked like Mike. He looked like the brother JT had eaten cereal with in front of the TV on Saturday mornings for an entire childhood.

That was almost worse.

"Hey," JT said. His voice came out lower than he intended.

Mike looked at him.

"When you were tracking her," JT said. He stopped. He started again. "Did she run? The whole way? Did she just—"

Mike's eyes did something. Not the same thing as before. Something subtler—a recalibration, a quick and almost invisible closing—that JT saw because he was looking for it and because he'd grown up studying his brother's expressions in the same way you studied weather.

"She ran," Mike said. He turned back to the table. "That's what they do."

There was a silence that didn't have any room in it for further questions. Their father put a plate of food in front of Mike and Mike ate it and JT said nothing, and outside the pines stood absolutely still.

---

## Chapter Five

The third night, he didn't sleep at all.

He lay in the narrow bed and listened to the lodge and tried to make an accounting of what he knew versus what he'd inferred versus what he'd felt, and the problem was that felt kept bleeding into the other categories until they weren't separate anymore. Here was what he could say with certainty: the caribou were not behaving normally. His father had experienced something in the clearing that had left no apparent trace in him afterward. Mike was concealing something that JT couldn't fully describe but could not unfeel. Someone—or something, and he was no longer investing much energy in the distinction—was maintaining the cabin in the north cut. Had been for long enough that the marks on the walls had layered into something sedimentary, something that had been added to and added to across seasons, across years, perhaps across the span of JT's entire remembered life.

And then there was the other thing, the thing he had more trouble thinking about directly:

He had stood at the edge of that frozen depression and felt the pull of it himself. Had felt, for the duration of several breaths, something in his chest unlock like a door.

He had not wanted to walk down to the bull. That wasn't right. The urge had not come from want in any sense he recognized. It had come from somewhere that operated under want, in the substrate, in the part that didn't use language.

*You could just walk down.*

He stared at the ceiling and understood: it wasn't hunting. That was the thing he'd been orbiting without landing on. They had come here to hunt, had been coming here for years, and what they were doing was not hunting. What the woods were doing with them was something that wore hunting's clothes—the season, the ritual, the kill—the way something might wear a face it had taken from somewhere else. The caribou were not being hunted. They were being placed. The humans with their rifles and their licenses and their generational inheritance of this particular masculine ceremony—they were not predators. They were the mechanism. The means by which whatever this was accomplished whatever it was accomplishing.

And the mechanism was working. And had been working. Maybe since before JT was born.

He got up at four in the morning and sat at the kitchen table in the dark without turning a light on.

He was sitting there when he heard the door to his father's room open.

---

His father moved through the dark kitchen with the confidence of someone who knows a room by heart, which he did, which they all did—you always learned the dimensions of the lodge in darkness within the first two nights, because the lodge taught you. JT watched him cross to the coat hooks by the door. Watched him take down his coat. Watched him sit on the bench and begin, slowly and with careful hands, to put on his boots.

JT did not move.

His father's face in the darkness was not legible. He was simply a man putting on boots in the dark at four in the morning, and there was nothing unusual about it until you asked yourself where he was going, and asked yourself that question while holding in mind everything else you knew.

He finished with the boots and stood and took his rifle from the rack.

"Dad," JT said.

His father turned, and in the darkness JT couldn't see his expression—could only see the shape of him, the outline, his father's familiar width and posture, the rifle held in the easy way of someone long accustomed to its weight.

The shape of him paused.

"Go to sleep," his father said.

His voice was even. It was his voice. It was the voice of a man telling his son to go to sleep in exactly the tone of voice he had used for thirty years, and that was the thing that moved JT out of his chair.

"No," JT said. He was on his feet. He was across the room. He put himself between his father and the door, and in doing this he became visible to both of them—there was no more watching from concealment, no more filing things away—and the moment had a quality of irreversibility that tasted like copper. "No. You're not going out there."

His father looked at him. In the darkness, what JT could see of his face was still, the way the clearing had been still.

"JT," he said.

"Where are you going? It's four in the morning. Where are you going?"

"I heard something," his father said. "Outside."
