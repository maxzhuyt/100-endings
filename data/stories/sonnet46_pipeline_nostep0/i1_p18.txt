# Sisyphus Gives Advice

---

*The following is a faithful transcription of the column "Practical Concerns," as dictated by Sisyphus of Ephyra and annotated for publication by Crow, who wishes it known that she is doing this under protest and also that her beak hurts.*

---

The boulder was approximately two-thirds of the way up when Sisyphus said, *"Next one."*

Crow hopped sideways along the rock face, holding the papyrus in one talon and a reed pen in the other, which was an arrangement that worked considerably better in theory. She squinted at the letter.

"This one's about a husband," she said.

"They're often about husbands."

"Do you want to know what kind?"

"The usual kind, I expect."

Crow read it aloud.

---

**Dear Practical Concerns,**

**My husband and I have been married eleven years. He is not a bad man. He remembers my birthday, he is kind to our children, he has never once raised his voice in anger at me or anyone else. But I have begun to feel, in the last two years or so, that I am speaking to him from across a very large field, and that he can see me waving but cannot make out what I'm saying, and does not think to walk closer. I have tried to explain this to him. He says he understands, and I believe that he believes this. Nothing changes. I don't know if I'm asking for advice or just to be heard.**

**— Waving From Across the Field**

---

Sisyphus was quiet for a moment. His feet found their next placement. His shoulders settled into the familiar geometry of the push.

"She's asking to be heard," he said. "Write that first. Not as a preamble — as the answer. Then write this:"

Crow's pen moved.

"The field is real, but the field is a description of a feeling, not a fact about your marriage. What it usually means, when we find ourselves in that field, is that something has changed in what we need — not necessarily in our partner, not necessarily in the marriage. Two years ago, something shifted in *you*. That's worth sitting with before you ask him to move.

"That said: a man who walks no closer is still a man standing still. Your job, in the next conversation, is not to explain the field again. Your job is to say one specific, concrete thing that would make you feel less far away. Not the feeling — the thing. A Wednesday dinner with no children. A question you want him to ask you. Something he can *do*, so he stops practicing the understanding face and starts actually understanding.

"He sounds like a man who wants to do right by you. Give him something to do."

He paused. "Is that enough?"

"It's a lot," Crow said.

"It's what she needs." Crow was fairly certain he wasn't aware of the distinction he'd drawn. "Read me the next one while I come back down."

The boulder had reached the top. It sat there for a moment, in the way that boulders do when they are deciding, and then it rolled back down the hill with the unhurried confidence of something that has done this many times before.

Sisyphus followed it at a walk, hands in his pockets.^(1)

---

^(1) *He doesn't have pockets. I don't know why I keep writing this. — C.*

---

**Dear Practical Concerns,**

**I work in the municipal records office. I have worked in the municipal records office for nineteen years. Every morning I file the same categories of forms into the same categories of boxes. Every evening I go home. On the first of each month, my supervisor Demetrios delivers a speech about the importance of accurate record-keeping, and I stand there and I nod, and then he makes the joke about the file that went missing in the fourteenth year of the Archon Solon's administration (which I have now heard forty-seven times, I have kept count), and everyone laughs, and on the second of the month everything is exactly the same as it was on the first.

**I am not miserable. I want to be clear about that. I am not crying at my desk. I have simply begun to wonder if there is something else I am supposed to be doing with my life. Do you have thoughts?**

**— Forty-Seven Times**

---

"I like her," Crow announced.

"She's funny."

"*He's* funny."

"He is. The counting is what makes it funny." Sisyphus put his shoulder into the boulder. "Write this:"

*The fact that you're not crying at your desk is more important than it sounds. You're describing something subtler than misery, which is harder to address and also harder to dismiss. What you're describing might be the feeling of work that absorbs your hands but not your mind — and the question isn't whether to leave your work, but how to find the part of you that isn't in the records office when you're in the records office.*

*You mention Demetrios's joke. You have heard it forty-seven times. What you have not mentioned is whether you have ever laughed at it. Genuine feeling — even irritation, even the small pleasure of being right about something predictable — is the thing that keeps us present to what we're doing. It doesn't have to be joy. It just has to be real.*

*Try this: find one part of your work that you do well enough to find interesting — not profound, not meaningful, just technically interesting. The specific way you know the system. A filing logic that required a solution nobody thought to document. Whatever it is. Pay attention to that part as if it were the whole job for one week, and see if the job changes, or if you do.*

*Some of us, it turns out, are built for depth rather than breadth. There's nothing wrong with the records office. There might be something slightly wrong with how you're standing in it.*

The pen stopped moving.

"Did I say something?" Sisyphus asked.

"No," Crow said. Her voice was strange. "Keep going."

"That's everything. Sign it off."^(2)

---

^(2) *At the phrase "absorbs your hands but not your mind," Sisyphus had both hands on the boulder and was looking at nothing in particular. I don't think he noticed. I am putting this here because someone should. — C.*

---

The light on the hill was changing — that particular quality of late-afternoon light that makes limestone look briefly like something it isn't. The rock under Sisyphus's palms was warm from the day, slightly rough at the upper edge where something had broken away once, long ago.

He turned the letter over himself, which was not customary. Crow watched him read it.

---

**Dear Practical Concerns,**

**There is a woman in my village who has done something I think was wrong. It wasn't wrong enough for anyone to say so. It was the kind of wrong that happens between people who know each other — a small cruelty, done where no one could see it, against a person who couldn't respond. I saw it. I haven't said anything. I tell myself this is because it's not my business. I think actually it's because she frightens me a little, and because I don't want to make things complicated, and because she makes a very good point about the person she was cruel to, who is actually quite difficult, and sometimes I find myself agreeing with her, which makes me feel sick. She is also my friend. I am also her friend. I don't know what to do.**

**— Complications**

---

Sisyphus read it twice.

"Write: *You already know what you did, and you already know that you did it for the reasons you've given, and it's worth noticing that you can name all of those reasons clearly. That clarity is not the same as excusing yourself; it's just accurate. Hold both things.*

*Your friend did a small wrong. Small wrongs matter — not because they require tribunals, but because they're the kind that compound. You don't have to say anything grand. You can ask her, the next time you are alone, whether she thought about how that landed. Just the question. You don't have to have an answer ready. She might surprise you.*

*The person she was cruel to is, in fact, probably difficult. That is also true. Difficulty is not protection from cruelty. You already know this.*

*You are not a coward. You are someone who found a complicated situation complicated, which is what complicated situations do. But you wrote this letter, which means you haven't decided to stop thinking about it. That's the important part. Don't stop.*"

He handed the letter back without comment.

Crow looked at him.

"Next one," he said.^(3)

---

^(3) *I've been doing this job for four years. I've never heard him raise his voice. I've never heard him complain. I've never once heard him say anything about himself, his life, his particular situation, or the opinions of the gods. I sometimes think: what would it cost him to be angry, just once. And then I read back the letters. I think I am beginning to understand that this is the wrong question.

I am writing this note here instead of somewhere Sisyphus might see it, because I don't want to talk about it, and also because he would probably give excellent advice. — C.*

---

**Dear Practical Concerns,**

**I am thirty-one years old. I have a good job, a family I love, enough to eat, a city I enjoy. I am aware that these are not small things. But I find myself, at unexpected moments — washing dishes, walking to the market, just before sleep — seized by the feeling that I don't know what I'm *for*. Not what I should do with my life; I'm not asking about occupation. I mean something underneath occupation: whether there is a reason I specifically exist, or whether I am one instance of a general category, interchangeable with thousands of others, and whether that would be bad if it were true.

**I'm sorry if this sounds self-indulgent. It might be self-indulgent. I wrote it anyway.**

**— One Instance**

---

The boulder was near the top again. The sky had gone to copper.

Sisyphus considered the letter for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was the same as it always was — unhurried, even, with the quality of a man who has time.

"Write:

*Don't apologize for the question. It's one of the better ones.*

*Here is something: the feeling you're describing — that seizing, before sleep, over the dishes — that is not absence of purpose. That is what purpose feels like when it's working. The people who have no sense of their own meaning never ask. The question is only available to someone who cares what the answer is, and caring what the answer is is already an answer of a kind.*

*You want to know if you're interchangeable. I think you mean: would anything be different if you had never been born? I can't tell you the large version of this. No one can, and anyone who offers to is selling something. But I can tell you the small version: you were shaped by specific things — specific mornings, specific losses, specific conversations that no one else was in — and the things you do, you do from that specific shape. It doesn't make you cosmically necessary. It makes you unrepeatable, which is not nothing.*

*The feeling will come back. It's supposed to. Let it come. Ask it what it wants, and then wash the dishes anyway. That's not resignation. That's just Tuesday.*"

Crow had stopped writing before he finished. She had the papyrus flat on the rock and was looking at it.

The boulder reached the top.

It sat.

It rolled.^(4)

---

^(4) *(Nothing to add here. — C.)*

---

Sisyphus had just handed Letter Four to Crow for folding and filing when she unrolled the next one, went still, and made a sound that might charitably be described as a professional clearing of the throat.

"This one requires a content advisory."

"Read it."

"I'm going to read it."

She read it.

The letter, which will not be reproduced here in full, concerned a young man, a neighbor's wife, a quantity of olive oil, and what the young man described as an *administrative question about consent and property rights* that was, upon very brief examination, not an administrative question at all.^(5)

Sisyphus listened. He nodded once or twice.

"Write: *The question you are asking is not about property rights. The question you are asking is whether you are the kind of person who would do a thing that you have already decided to do, and whether my answering will change that. It won't, but I'll answer anyway.*

*Tell her. Ask her directly. Use simple words, in daylight, with your hands visible. If the answer is yes, the olive oil is irrelevant. If the answer is no, the olive oil is still irrelevant. These are all the relevant facts.*

*Also, and I say this with no judgment and complete sincerity: return the neighbor's oil."*

"That's it?"

"That's it."

Crow rolled up the letter. She unrolled it again. She rolled it back up.

"Sisyphus," she said.

"Mm."

"He said — and I want to make sure I'm reading this correctly — he said she keeps the oil in a *—*"

"Next letter," said Sisyphus.^(5)

---

^(5) *I need everyone reading this to understand that I, specifically, have questions about the oil situation that were not addressed in the response, and that my professionalism is the only reason I am not going into further detail, and that if this column ever decides to run a follow-up series I have a list. — C.*

---

The copper had gone out of the sky. The hill was quiet in the way hills get at the end of a day, as if they've been doing something vigorous and would like not to discuss it.

Crow had sorted the remaining letters into three stacks — *soon*, *also soon*, *probably about the oil* — and was doing the thing with her wings that meant she was finished for the day and not especially willing to argue about it.

Sisyphus walked to the boulder.

He put both hands on it. He found the place — the slightly rough patch at the upper edge — and adjusted his grip until the weight of it settled across his shoulders correctly, and then he began to push.

From behind him, without turning around, he said: "Same time."

"I know," said Crow.

She picked up the stack labeled *soon* with one talon. She looked at the hill, at the man on it, at the last of the light doing what it did to limestone. She thought about what she'd written in footnote three and decided not to re-read it.

She tucked the letters under her wing and said, to no one in particular: "Mail's in."

---

*"Practical Concerns" publishes irregularly, on account of the boulder. Letters may be submitted to any crow, who will determine if they're worth his time. He has not yet declined one.*
