# Darkroom Revelations

---

The smell gets you first. That's what Dani always thought — not the heat of the safelight or the particular quality of silence that only exists underground, in rooms with no windows, but the chemistry of it: acetic acid and developer, a sharp tang with something underneath it that was almost floral, almost wrong. She'd been in darkrooms for six years and she still loved that smell the way you love something that doesn't entirely make sense to love.

The safelight threw everything red. Her hands moving over the enlarger looked like someone else's hands. Elliot's white t-shirt — layered under a flannel that he'd taken off an hour ago and left draped over the paper cutter — had gone the colour of a fading bruise.

She knew who she was. That had never been a question. Dani Reyes, twenty-five, which was only six years older than some of the undergrads in this elective but felt, sometimes, like a different continent. She'd come back to school for the MFA after two years shooting editorial work she didn't care about, and she had opinions about grain and about light ratios and about which chemicals the department had been ordering at the wrong dilutions for going on three semesters now. She was the person in the room who knew things, and she had never once found that uncomfortable.

She found it a little uncomfortable right now.

Not for any reason she was ready to name.

---

This was the fourth late session she and Elliot had shared. The assignment was simple enough — or Professor Hayashi had described it as simple, meaning it was the kind of simple that required you to dismantle something you'd believed — a single roll, shot in collaboration, developed together. The premise being that the labour of making an image was already a form of intimacy. She'd rolled her eyes at the syllabus note when she'd first read it. She wasn't rolling her eyes now.

Elliot Marsh. Nineteen. She'd worked that out without asking, from a comment in the first session about a high school teacher, a graduation date implied. He dressed like someone who'd learned to shop secondhand from necessity and then kept doing it for love — the flannel, a distressed crewneck the week before, always some layering that added softness to a slight frame. Silver rings on three fingers of his left hand. Nails that were sometimes painted, sometimes partially painted, sometimes bare in a way that looked like a decision rather than an oversight.

She'd been watching for weeks without calling it watching.

What she'd told herself, at first, was that it was her photographer's instinct, the habit of noticing — she catalogued everyone. That was true. She did. But she catalogued them once and moved on, and with Elliot she kept returning. Kept revising. There was a way he stood when he was uncertain, weight shifted back, one hand finding the opposite elbow, that she'd now seen enough times to recognise from thirty feet away. There was a way his face went very still when someone said something interesting, a stillness that looked like nothing from a distance and from close up looked like complete attention. He was one of those people on whom stillness read as depth rather than vacancy.

This was not, Dani knew, her type.

Her type, insofar as she had one, tended toward certainty. People who moved fast and thought they knew things. She'd dated two women and a man in the last four years and they had all been, in their different ways, decisive to the point of occasionally being wrong about everything. She'd found comfort in that sureness. She recognised it as a companion to her own.

Elliot was not certain about much that she could see. He offered opinions in a way that included the listener — *I think, but tell me if I'm wrong, I might be wrong* — and some small part of her that she wasn't proud of had initially read this as a weakness. Some other part, larger or just louder lately, read it as an invitation. Not a romantic one. Or not only. More like: here is a mind that has room in it. Come in if you want to.

The protectiveness had arrived around the second session, which had embarrassed her. She didn't have siblings. She didn't mentor. She had no business feeling that way about someone she barely knew, someone who, for all she knew, had perfectly good people in his life who already performed all the functions she kept imagining performing. It embarrassed her and she catalogued it and kept it separate, the way you keep a developing image away from the white light, knowing that exposure would only ruin it.

She had not examined too closely what exactly she was afraid of ruining.

---

"Hypo or wash first?" Elliot asked.

"Wash, then hypo, then wash again," Dani said. She paused. "My way. You can do it differently."

"No, yours makes sense." He adjusted the tray. His rings clinked against the rim. "Why did you do it the other way when I watched you last Tuesday?"

She looked up. "What other way?"

"You went hypo, then wash."

Dani stood with that for a moment. "I was rushing."

A small sound from him that might have been a laugh. Not a mocking one.

"I'm allowed to rush," she said. "I'm not a methodology."

"I know," Elliot said. "I just like watching how you do things."

He said it simply, which meant he probably hadn't registered how it sounded, and Dani made a decision to treat it as simply as it had been offered.

"I'll perform more consistently in future," she said.

"Don't." He shook his head. "That's not what I meant."

There was a small warm thing sitting in the middle of her chest that she was not going to look at.

They worked. The enlarger clicked through its exposure count. The first prints went into the developer tray and they watched them the way she never stopped loving — that slow blooming from grey silver to image, the emergence of the thing you'd already taken on faith was there. The particular print was a shot she'd taken of the fire escape outside the studio building, which she'd liked in-camera without being entirely sure why, and which continued to resist her attempts to pin down its meaning. There were things like that. You bracketed them and kept shooting.

"What aperture for the third negative?" Elliot asked. He was leaning over the contact sheet with a loupe, the focused intensity of someone who'd learned the skill recently enough that it still required active thought.

"Try f/8 to start. It's a little dense, so maybe plus a half stop."

"Okay." He made a small adjustment. She didn't watch him do it. She was watching the developer tray.

She was watching the developer tray and thinking about the protectiveness and trying to trace back to where it had turned into something less clean.

She thought it had been around the second session, yes, but she thought the initial thread had been pulled earlier than that — first day of the elective, Hayashi doing intros around the circle, and when he got to Elliot, Elliot had said, *I'm mostly just starting to figure out what I want to do, so I'm here to figure it out*, and everyone had nodded in the polite way you nodded when you were waiting for your own turn, and Dani had thought: what a terrifying thing to say out loud in a room full of people with opinions. And then she'd thought: what a brave thing. And then she'd thought about it again on the way home.

She'd identified the attraction about ten days into the semester.

She'd been identifying it silently ever since.

It wasn't the youth — she wasn't interested in the youth specifically, and nineteen was old enough to be a colleague rather than a problem but young enough to make her cautious. It wasn't the softness. Or not despite it. It was because of — something in him that had not yet been edited down by other people's preferences about what he was supposed to be. He was still becoming. She'd been so certain for so long that she'd forgotten that becoming was a thing you could still be doing.

She envied it and she wanted to put her hand over it like something breakable; these two impulses were related in a way she had not finished working out.

---

She was setting a fresh stack of paper at the edge of the counter when Elliot reached past her for the tongs and his arm caught the edge of the box.

It happened fast. The box went over the enlarger ledge and the paper spilled — the safelight caught it as it fell, the whole slide of it exposed to red before either of them could react, and Dani turned and saw it and said nothing, because there was nothing to say. A box of photo paper, not cheap, now approximately worth the cardboard container it had come out of.

Silence.

"Oh god," Elliot said. His voice was very quiet.

"It's okay," Dani said automatically.

"I ruined —"

"Paper," she said. "Just paper. We have enough for tonight without it. It's fine."

He had not moved. He was standing with his hand still half-extended toward where the tongs were, body arrested in the posture of the moment before the mistake, like he was trying to inhabit the alternative version of events. His face in the red light was very still.

It was not about the paper. People's faces did not look like that about paper.

Dani felt the instinct rise in her immediately: fix it, make it right, say the thing that would put him back at ease. She had things to say. She was good in moments like this, she knew the right tone, she'd done it before. Her hand moved an inch toward his arm.

She stopped. She set her hand flat on the counter instead.

*This is not yours to fix*, she thought. She thought it clearly, which meant it was worth examining — when did fixing become a need of her own rather than a kindness to someone else? When did she start needing to be useful? She held still and she waited and she let him stand there for however long it took.

It took maybe forty seconds. Then he let out a long breath.

"Sorry," he said. The word had layers in it.

---

"Don't be." She turned back to the developer tray but not quickly, not so quickly that it would look like escape. Just a natural redirect. "We'll work with what we have."

Another silence. The timer went. She lifted the print — the fire escape again, and it was still saying something she still couldn't name — and moved it to the stop bath with the tongs. Elliot, slowly, came to stand at the other end of the counter. She heard him begin to gather the spilled papers, the useless ones, stacking them with a care that served no practical purpose except to give his hands something to do.

"The darkroom's strange," he said. Not going anywhere with it yet.

"Yes," Dani agreed.

"It's like — " He paused. "I don't know. You can't quite see each other's faces. It makes things easier to say."

"I know," she said. "I've thought that. It's like a confessional."

"I was going to say that."

"Priests discovered red light before we did."

He did laugh at that — a real one, brief, and she felt the warmth of it against the side of the evening. Then quiet again.

"I've been —" He stopped. Started again. "I've been having a weird time of it lately. Feeling-wise. About myself, I mean."

Dani moved the print from stop bath to the fixer. Her hands were steady. She didn't turn around.

"Yeah?" she said. Neutral. Open-ended.

"I don't — I'm not sure I have language for it yet. Maybe that's the problem." A pause. "I don't know if this makes sense, but. I've been wondering whether some of the ways I've always understood myself are — you know. Fixed. Whether they're actually mine or whether I just ended up with them."

She thought: *I know this feeling*. She almost said it. She didn't.

"Like which ways," she said instead.

"I guess — okay. I've been thinking about gender. About whether the way I think of myself is really how I experience it, or whether it's just. A default." He said *default* with particular care, like he was testing the word. "I've been wondering if it's strange to be — not sure about that. At nineteen. Most people seem to have sorted it out."

"Most people are pretending they've sorted it out," Dani said.

"Maybe." She could hear him leaning against the counter. "I talked to my friend about it and she said — she was really nice about it, she said it was brave to be questioning. But I think she thought I meant something more defined than I do. Like she heard *questioning* and thought *decided*, and I'm really not. I'm just. I've been wondering."

His voice was careful, picking its way through unmarked terrain with no expectation of arriving anywhere. She knew that she was not the first person he'd said this to — he'd mentioned the friend — but she understood that she might be being given something relatively new. Still rough-edged, unprinted. The negative before the image.

"You're not strange," Dani said. "For not knowing."

"I feel a bit strange."

"That's different from being strange."

A beat.

"That's a really good distinction," he said, and there was a small dry quality to it — the faintest irony, affectionate — that was entirely his, that existed nowhere in her idea of him, that she filed away somewhere.

---

Her first instinct had been to affirm him. She recognised this happening inside herself even as she was doing it — she was assembling reassurance, she was reaching for certainty on his behalf, and the impulse was genuine, she did want to reassure him, but she was watching herself do it fast, watching herself build toward a confident statement about gender and identity and how this was all okay, and she had enough self-knowledge to ask: who is this for?

Not entirely for him, she admitted. For her too. For the feeling of being useful. For the comfortable shape of being the one who knew things.

She held the tongs over the fixer and didn't say the assembled reassurance.

And then, because she was twenty-five and not a saint, she said it anyway. Or the start of it.

"You know that what you're feeling is very comm —" She stopped. She heard herself. "Sorry. Don't do that."

"Don't do what?"

"I was about to do a thing where I tell you how your feeling fits into a framework." She turned to look at him. His face was half in shadow, half in red, and he was watching her with the close attention she'd catalogued. "That's my reflex. It doesn't mean your feeling is small."

"I didn't think you meant it that way."

"I know, but I meant it the wrong way anyway."

He considered this. "What's the right way?"

"I don't know," she said. "I'm not sure I have one. I think the right way is probably just — " She spread her hands over the counter, palms down. "It sounds hard and it sounds like something that doesn't have an end point yet and that's hard to sit with, and you're sitting with it. That seems like the thing to say."

"That's a better thing," Elliot said.

"Thank you for the performance review."

The small laugh again. Some of the tension in the room turned to something else, not gone but changed, and she felt her own shoulders drop by about half an inch. The timer went.

"These are still probably terrible," Dani said, moving toward the tray, "given that I accidentally —" She stopped. "I may have pushed the exposure."

"How much?"

"Optimistically, half a stop."

Elliot came to stand beside her over the developer tray. She was hyperaware of the distance — six inches, maybe eight, the two of them bent over the emerging image, the warmth of the chemicals rising and the warmth of him beside her and the red light making everything inside a kind of other time, a kind of space that didn't have the same rules as outside.

The first print was, as she'd predicted, a little dark. But there was something in it. An image she'd taken outside the building, the stairs and the railing and behind them, through the grid of iron, the street — motion-blurred at the street level, sharp at the metal, two different sharpnesses occupying the same frame. It wasn't what she'd planned.

"I like it," Elliot said.

"I overcorrected."

"I still like it."

She looked at it for a long moment. "Yeah," she admitted. "I do too."

Neither of them moved. The space between them was the size of a held breath and they were both holding it. She could feel him there without looking, that particular sensory specificity of being beside someone you've been watching without calling it watching. The ruined papers sat in a pile off to her right. The photo she hadn't planned expanded slowly in the developer tray.

She thought: I could close this distance. Not dramatically. Three inches. And it was possible he would not make it nothing — it was possible
