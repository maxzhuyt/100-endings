# The Mare's Eye

The oil had gone rancid in the lamp again, and that was how I knew it was morning.

I lay on the stone shelf that served as my bed and breathed the smell — sour fat and old smoke — until the sound of iron on iron from the training yard told me the earlier risers were already at it. My left knee announced itself, as it did every morning now, with that grinding complaint just below the cap. I pressed two fingers into the joint, felt the pop, and swung my legs over the edge. The floor was cold. The floor was always cold, even in July, because the ludus walls were three feet thick and the sun had to climb a long way before it reached us.

I was a Murmillo. That meant I carried forty pounds of equipment into the arena — the curved scutum, the helmet with its high crest that made me look, according to the other men, like a fish someone had stood upright. It also meant I had survived fourteen bouts, which was the kind of number that made lanistas look at you differently. Not with affection. With the calculating eyes of a man reassessing the price of a horse.

Before I stood, I looked at the small figure on the shelf's far end. Epona. I had carved her myself from a piece of oak that a merchant's cart had shed outside the wall — just a fragment, but enough. She sat sidesaddle on her mare, arms open, the way I remembered her from the post beside our door in Gaul. My father had kept a painted clay version there, and every morning before he walked to the paddock he would touch her knee with two fingers. I did the same now. The wood was smooth where I'd touched it ten thousand times.

*Watch the road*, I told her. It was what my father used to say. As if she might, from this shelf in a Roman barracks, still see the hills above the Moselle.

---

In the yard, Varro was already talking.

He was the lanista's man — not the lanista himself, who visited perhaps once a month and smelled of a senator's money — but the one who ran the daily machinery of us. He stood on the raised step by the armory door, arms crossed, reading from a wax tablet in the particular way that meant he wasn't really reading but wanted to appear to be.

"Games," he said, when enough of us had gathered. "Fifteen days. The editor is a man named Licinius Crassus Minor, giving the munera in honor of his father's memory and also, conveniently, in honor of his own candidacy for the aedileship." He set the tablet down. "Twenty pairs. We provide six."

I watched the men receive this. Sorio, the Thraex who slept in the cell beside mine, went very still. He was young — twenty, perhaps — and he had fought twice. Around him the older men shifted their weight and said nothing, which was the only language that mattered.

I was not afraid in the way I might once have been. Fear had changed in me over four years. It had become a kind of arithmetic.

"The crowd will be large," Varro added, almost to himself, snapping the tablet closed. "Crassus needs a spectacle. He's running against a man the Emperor favors." He looked at us directly then, and for a moment I thought I saw something behind his administrator's eyes — some awareness of the geometry he was describing. Then it closed. "Train well."

---

I was sharpening my gladius near the east wall when Sorio sat beside me.

He said nothing for a while. Below us, through a gap where two courses of brick had never been properly mortared, we could see a slice of the street: sandaled feet, bare feet, the gilded leg of a litter being carried in the direction of the Forum, a dog investigating something at the base of a column. The sound of the city came through the gap too — Latin in a dozen accents, a woman selling something from a basket, the distant groan of wagon wheels on stone, and beneath it all the low, continuous sound of thousands of people existing simultaneously. I had grown used to it, the way you grow used to tinnitus.

"Do you pray?" Sorio asked. He was from somewhere in Syria, taken in a border action, and he spoke Latin the way a man picks up a tool that doesn't quite fit his hand.

"Yes," I said.

"To which god?"

"Mine," I said. "Epona. A goddess from home."

He was quiet, thinking about this. A senator's litter passed through our narrow view and the feet on the street parted around it without breaking their rhythm, the way water moves around a stone. The men carrying it were slaves. I watched their calves flex and release, flex and release.

"Do you think she can hear you?" Sorio asked. "This far from home?"

I thought of my father's hands. The way the horses in our paddock would come to him unbidden, pressing their great soft faces against his chest. I thought about the fourteen bouts, and the knee, and the rancid oil, and the fact that I was still here to think about any of it.

"I think she can see farther than we can," I said.

He nodded slowly, as if this were enough.

---

Late afternoon. The yard had emptied. I sat with my back against the wall beneath the shrine shelf and watched a sparrow investigate the packed dirt near the gate. It pecked at something I couldn't see, found nothing, tried again. The shadows from the wall's crenellations lay across the ground like bars, moving almost imperceptibly as the sun continued its indifferent arc.

Above me, Epona sat on her oak mare.

In the myth — in what I remembered of it, half-heard from my grandmother beside a fire — she traveled the road between worlds. Not the road the living traveled and not the road the dead took. The road between. The one that ran alongside both, in the thin country where nothing was yet decided.

Fifteen days.

The sparrow found what it was looking for, or gave up. It rose suddenly and was gone.

I pressed two fingers to the carved knee and held them there.
