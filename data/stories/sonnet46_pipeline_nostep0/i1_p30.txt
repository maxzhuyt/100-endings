# Útiseta

There are things you tell yourself at seventeen that you keep telling yourself at thirty-four, not because you believe them anymore but because the alternative is a door you'd rather leave shut. I'm telling this now because last week I drove past a burial mound on the E4 — unremarkable, roadside, a low hump in a farmer's field — and had to pull over. My hands were shaking. That's all. That's why.

---

The summer I spent in Norrland I was supposed to be bonding with my father's side of the family, which meant three weeks in a village so small it was less a place than a collective habit, a decision a handful of people renewed every morning by staying. My cousins had their own language for everything — not just dialect, though the vowels alone were enough to mark me, my Stockholm mouth rounding where theirs went flat — but references, shortcuts, a shared mythology of local incident and landscape that I could not access. I wore the wrong shoes. I said *hallå* instead of *hej* with an intonation that apparently meant something different there, something slightly comic. I tried for two weeks and failed steadily and quietly, the way water fails to become ice; nothing dramatic, just a persistent wrongness of state.

What I wanted was Astrid's approval. I understood this clearly even then, which didn't help. She was my second cousin, a year older, and she possessed that specific kind of confidence that has nothing to do with performance — she didn't need you to see it, which made you need to. She decided things. Small things, usually: where to walk, when to go home, what was worth laughing at. I followed. Everyone in her orbit followed. I told myself this was because I was a guest and didn't know the terrain, and this was partly true, and mostly not.

---

Astrid was already sitting on the fence when I came around the side of the house at eleven-thirty, waiting with the patient quality of someone who has been there longer than they've agreed to be. She didn't check her phone — she'd left it at home, she'd told me to leave mine, and I had, and I experienced a particular combination of compliance and anxiety about this that felt embarrassing even in memory. She had a canvas bag in her lap. She was looking at the tree line.

"You're late," she said, though I wasn't.

This was Astrid's grammar. I answered it by falling into step beside her.

She was not goth. She was not dramatic. She wore her grandmother's interest in old things the way you wear a practical jacket — it served a function, you didn't think about it much. The grandmother, Maj-Britt, was eighty-one and still sharp in a way that frightened people; I'd spent one afternoon in her kitchen and come away feeling I'd been assessed for something specific and my score withheld. Astrid spoke of her practices with a casualness that precluded questions. She didn't invite me to find it interesting. She just told me things.

"Farmor says you have to bring something woven," she'd said that afternoon, unpacking and repacking the bag with a focused economy. "For sitting. Not synthetic." She held up a folded rectangle of grey wool, turned it over, put it back. "And something from the ground here — I have a piece of bark, she said bark is fine. And the offering has to be something you've made or grown yourself, not bought." She'd produced a small wax-paper parcel that turned out to hold three flatbreads, slightly uneven. She'd baked them herself. She said this without pride, just as specification.

It sounded learned. Too specific to be invented. The flatbreads especially — I could not have arrived at flatbreads on my own.

---

We walked. The midsummer sky above the trees was the colour of the inside of a shell, pale and faintly luminous, not dark in the way a city night is dark but not light either — something else, a third state without a name in any language I'd learned. The quality of it was hard to look at directly. It made distances uncertain.

Astrid talked as we walked, instructions delivered in her ordinary voice, the same voice she'd use to tell me a bus route.

"Once we sit, we don't talk. Not until it's over."

"When is it over?"

"You'll know."

"How?"

"You'll know," she said again, without impatience, as though this were genuinely the complete answer. "We don't stand until then. We don't move around. And —" she paused, not for effect but as though ordering the information — "if anything comes from the left side, don't look at it. Don't acknowledge it. Don't turn toward it."

I waited for more.

"That's it?"

"That's the important part."

"What counts as acknowledging?" I asked, because I was from Stockholm and could not let a rule be imprecise.

Astrid considered this. "Don't think about it," she said finally. "If you have to ask what counts, you'll make it count."

The specificity of the prohibition was doing something to the back of my neck. I noticed this and categorized it as: susceptibility, imagination, the way any set of rules gains gravity once spoken aloud. I was seventeen and I knew exactly what was happening to me and the knowledge was not total protection.

"You know this is probably —" I started.

"Yeah," Astrid said, without looking at me. Just: yeah. She didn't finish my sentence or hers. She walked.

I thought about my phone in my cousin's kitchen and I thought about texting my mother tomorrow and I thought about whether there was a word in Swedish for the particular embarrassment of finding yourself in a scenario you know is teenage mythology and being unable to locate your own skepticism. I fell quiet.

We had almost half a kilometre left to walk. For maybe a hundred metres of it we were both just ourselves, sixteen and seventeen, girls in the dark with a bag full of wool and flatbread and bark, and Astrid said something quietly funny about a boy at school and I laughed and the sound came out wrong in that open air, too loud, a city sound. Astrid's face closed, not unkindly. Just closed. Time to be serious now, the expression said, or said nothing and let me read it.

I followed.

---

I had imagined the mound as taller. I don't know why — something in the word itself, *hög*, the idea of something that asserts itself, monument-like, commands the landscape. But this was low. It rose from the ground by maybe a metre, perhaps a little more, gently graded, grass-covered and unremarkable in the way of things that have been there long enough to stop defending their strangeness. In daylight I might have walked across it without noticing. At midnight its flatness was different. Its lowness was different. I had wanted something I could dismiss and this was not that. A dramatic mound would have given me something to push against.

Astrid chose the position with quiet deliberation, walking the circumference once before settling on the south face. She spread the grey wool, set the bark at the edge of the cloth, placed the flatbreads carefully in front of it.

"Sit," she said.

I sat.

The wool was coarse and slightly damp from the night air. My ankles crossed in front of me. Astrid settled beside me, close but not touching, and then she went somewhere — not physically, just internally — and the girl I'd been walking with was present still in body and simply not available. I can't describe it better than that.

The sky was doing the same indeterminate thing it had been doing. Somewhere across the fields, water was moving — a stream, maybe, or birch leaves in a wind I couldn't feel from the mound. I could hear our breathing, hers slower than mine. The sentences in my head shortened. I stopped narrating. I noticed what was in front of me: grass, darkness soft as cloth, the crease where the mound met the field.

The silence was full. That was the thing — it was not empty, was not the mere absence of sound but a pressure, a substance, the kind of quiet that makes you aware of the small machinery of your own body. My pulse in my wrists. The minute sound of my own swallowing. Astrid's concentrated stillness beside me, and the way that stillness was not relaxed but *applied*, deliberate, maintained. I understood then that she was not performing. Whatever she was doing, she was doing it. The understanding arrived without drama and stayed.

---

Nothing happened.

For a long time, nothing happened, and I mean this precisely: not that I was waiting and time dragged, but that the nothing itself was textured, specific, present. The grass did not move. The light did not change. Astrid did not move. I became aware that I was cold in my shoulders and that I had been cold for some time without registering it, and this seemed like information.

Then my mind began its particular work.

The thing about real darkness, even this half-darkness, is that it doesn't take long before the edges of your vision start to draft shapes from available material. I watched this happen with a clinical remove I did not fully feel. I saw the peripheral suggestions of heights and depths that were not there, or were there and ordinary, birch trunks and fence posts and the slope of a small hill — or weren't. The cruelty of it was the inventory: each item considered, assessed, assigned a probability. That's a tree. That is almost certainly a tree. The *almost* doing its work.

There was a sound like something settling its weight into the ground, maybe forty metres to my left, maybe less. Or there wasn't and I had generated it from wind in the grass.

The cold in my shoulders had moved inward.

I wanted to laugh. I wanted to say something to Astrid, anything, even her name. I wanted to stand up and walk away through the field and feel the unromantic gravel of the path under my shoes and smell the woodsmoke from someone's house and return to being a girl who had almost done something strange and then decided not to. The want was physical, a contracting in my chest. I sat still. I don't know why I sat still. The rule had been given to me by a sixteen-year-old girl drawing on her grandmother's practices, which I had identified ten minutes ago as probably teenage mythology. I sat still anyway. This is the part I've never been able to account for satisfactorily — not that I felt something, which is easy to explain, but that I followed a rule whose source I didn't trust. Later, I would mean to think hard about this. I didn't.

Astrid's profile beside me in the half-light. I'd been looking at the field and when I turned to look at her it took a moment to locate her face, to read it. It was the stillness that frightened me. Not the mound, which was grass and mud and two thousand years of patient presence. Her. The way she was attending to something. How serious she was. How old she looked.

---

The church bell rang once.

I knew the church. I had walked past it twice; it was Lutheran and white and held services on Sundays and Wednesday evenings, not on midsummer night at midnight. I knew this as a fact and I filed it and I cannot explain what I did with it afterward except to say I held it carefully, the way you hold something that might break.

We had been sitting for a long time. Or not long at all. I had lost the capacity to judge it; my sense of duration had softened like heated wax and taken no clear shape.

The bell rang once and stopped.

Before it: I was afraid of my own imagination. After it: I was afraid of something else, and the difference was in me, not the field, not the mound, not the specific quality of that Baltic summer light lying ghostly across the grass. Something had shifted in my capacity to be certain. That's all. That's everything.

Astrid turned her head and looked — not at me, not at the field, but at a middle distance that I could not find. And her face did not do what I expected. There was no fear in it. There was no performance. There was something that I can only call recognition, the expression of someone who has heard a sound they were waiting to hear, a train arriving, a name called — something that has arrived on schedule. That was the most frightening thing in the whole night. Not what I heard or thought I heard or saw or thought I saw. Her face in that moment.

---

I won't describe what happened next with false precision. I'll describe it as I experienced it, which is the best I can do.

Something was present at the edge of the field to my left.

I did not look at it. I don't know what it was. It could have been — I have spent seventeen years generating explanations and none of them are airtight and none of them are impossible. My body responded before my reasoning did: my skin tightened, my hands curled in my lap, my breathing stopped for several seconds of its own accord, not held but suspended. This is what I mean when I say *fear*. Not the thought of fear but the biochemistry, the thing that happens below the threshold of decision.

I could not have spoken. I want to be honest about this — I tell myself sometimes that I chose the rule, that I chose silence, but I'm not sure I was capable of sound. Sitting still. I was not certain whether I was complying with Útiseta or whether my legs had simply stopped working. I am still not certain.

The presence on my left — and I'm calling it that, presence, because *thing* is too definite and *nothing* is false — was there for a length of time I could not measure. I didn't acknowledge it. I existed very hard in my own body, in my own hands and my own heartbeat and the cold wool under me.

Astrid leaned, very slightly, toward me. Her mouth near my ear.

"It comes," she whispered. She had broken her own rule. "Don't be afraid."

I have been turning that sentence over for seventeen years. *Don't be afraid*. It could mean: there is nothing here to fear. It could mean: what is here requires your courage. I cannot tell you which she meant. I am not sure she could have told me either.

---

We left without ceremony, which I knew was wrong. There was supposed to be something — a closing, a formal ending, Astrid had not specified but I understood from the specificity of the opening that openings and closings were paired. We left anyway, walking quickly without running, and I felt the incompleteness behind me like a door left ajar. I knew it, and I didn't say it, and Astrid didn't say it, and we walked.

The path back was ordinary and slow to reassert itself. Gravel underfoot, pale in the light. A birch tree beside the track that was definitely a birch tree. The smell of someone's woodsmoke from a house we couldn't see. These things arrived like film developing — not all at once but in sequence, the world returning to me in pieces, not fully trusted until I was past it.

Astrid talked about something. I can't remember what — a film she'd seen, something her brother had done, something from school. Her voice had its normal quality, the same voice she'd used on the bus route instructions, pragmatic, a little dry. She was entirely herself. She was fine.

I could not find my way to where she was. I tried — I made sounds that qualified as responses, I think, I told myself each footstep was another increment of return. I was still partly on the mound and I knew it and I could see from Astrid's face that she either didn't know or had already closed it off, and I could not decide which was worse:  that she was better at this than me, or that she didn't understand what she'd opened.

---

I'm thirty-four. I drove past a low mound in a field last week and my hands shook and I pulled over.

Here is what I know: something crosses when you sit on old ground in good faith and follow old rules. Whether the crossing is in yourself or in the world, I've never been able to decide, and I suspect the question is less distinct than I'd like. I used a practice I didn't own, for a reason that had nothing to do with its purpose — for Astrid's approval, because I wanted to belong somewhere, because I was afraid of going home for three weeks and remaining a stranger. I used the old thing.

I have thought for a long time about which is worse: that nothing answered. Or that something did.
