# The Last Outpost — Chapter One: Dreads Askew

The glass hits the floor somewhere behind me — a sharp, bright shatter — and I don't even flinch. After four years behind this bar, broken glass is just punctuation.

"Sola, sweep that up before someone loses a toe," I call over my shoulder, already reaching for the Vega whisky without looking. I know where everything lives. The bottles are the most reliable relationship I have.

The stranger who just sat down is watching me with the particular exhaustion of someone who has been awake for too long and thinking even longer. Their tentacled dreads — eight of them, maybe ten, the bioluminescent kind that usually pulse a gentle amber — are dim and listing to one side like a satellite dish knocked loose in a storm. I place the double in front of them and lean against the back counter, giving them the professional bartender's gift of nearby silence.

They drink half of it immediately.

"Rough transit?" I ask, because that's the safe opener. Everyone out here has either just arrived or is about to leave, and both conditions are stressful.

"Rough everything." Their voice is low, genderless, with the slight harmonic doubling that tells me they're Velhari — a species I don't see much this far out. They lift the glass, look at the remaining whisky like it owes them something. "You're Nettle?"

I feel the small, familiar tightening in my chest that happens when a stranger knows my name before I've offered it. I keep my face pleasant. I'm good at that.

"Nets," I say. "Most people call me Nets. And you are?"

"Cass." They set the glass down. "Cassiel, technically, but nobody calls me that anymore either."

"Cass." I nod. "You want to tell me how you know my name, or should we do another round first?"

One of the dreads twitches — a Velhari micro-expression I roughly translate as *embarrassed* — and Cass wraps both hands around the glass. They have four hands, two sets, the smaller pair folded tight against their sternum like they're holding something in.

"Piotr Menn told me to find you," they say.

The name lands in my stomach like a dropped anchor. I take a breath through my nose, slow and even, and I reach for a glass of my own — water, always water when I'm working — and I take a sip before I answer.

"Piotr Menn," I repeat, "is a man I haven't spoken to in three years."

"Two years, eleven months," Cass says. "He's precise about it. He said you would be too."

"He said a lot of things." I set my glass down. "What does he want?"

Cass looks around the bar then — a quick, professional sweep that I recognize because I do it myself, cataloguing exits and threats and the distance between one body and another. The Outpost is mid-evening busy: Davo and his crew are loud in the corner booth, celebrating a good haul from the Kepler seam. Old Mirette is nursing her usual at the far end of the bar, apparently asleep but almost certainly not. Two merchants I don't know are arguing quietly over a datapad near the door. The ambient noise is comfortable, layered, the kind of sound that swallows conversations whole.

"He didn't tell me what he wants," Cass says carefully. "He told me what *I* need. And apparently those two things involve you."

I laugh, short and dry. "That sounds exactly like him."

"He said you'd say that too." For the first time, something moves across Cass's face that isn't exhaustion — something wry and reluctant. "He said to tell you the debt still stands. That you'd know what that means."

I do know what it means. I wish I didn't.

"Finish your drink," I say. "I've got to do a round."

I take my water and move down the bar, checking in with Mirette — who is definitely asleep — and refilling Davo's table with the cheerful efficiency that is, genuinely, one of my better qualities. Davo tries to get me to sit down and hear about the haul and I tell him I'm working, which is true, and that I'll hear about it later, which may or may not be true depending on how this night unfolds.

When I come back, Cass has finished the whisky and is sitting very still in the way people sit when they're trying not to look like they're falling apart.

"Another?" I ask.

"Not yet." They look up at me. "Can I ask you something?"

"You can ask."

"The debt Piotr mentioned." Cass hesitates. "Is it the kind that gets people hurt? I need to know what I've walked into."

I study them for a moment — the dim dreads, the folded hands, the shadows under their eyes that speak of days without proper sleep. Whatever they're carrying, they didn't choose it lightly. Nobody comes to the outer rim of Andromeda on a whim. The transit alone takes three weeks and costs more than most miners make in a month.

"The debt," I say slowly, "is complicated."

"That's not an answer."

"No," I agree. "It's not." I lean forward on the bar. "Tell me why Piotr sent you specifically. Tell me what you need. Then I'll tell you whether you should be worried."

Cass opens their mouth, and then — from the direction of the merchant table near the door — comes a sound that is not an argument anymore. It is something harder and quieter than an argument, and when I look up, one of the merchants I don't know is on their feet, and the other one is very still, and the datapad is on the floor between them, and the bar's ambient noise has dropped by half without anyone deciding to drop it.

Davo's crew has noticed too.

I straighten up, and I keep my voice easy, the way you do when easy is the only thing standing between a bad moment and a worse one.

"Cass," I say quietly, "hold that thought."
