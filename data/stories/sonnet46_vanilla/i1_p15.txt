# Therapeutic Boundaries

The paper on the table crinkles every time I shift my weight, and I've decided I hate that sound more than anything else in the world. More than the pop my ACL made. More than the silence after.

Maya sets her clipboard on the counter without looking at me, and I watch her wash her hands at the small sink, her back straight in the way it always was on the track—that particular posture that meant she was thinking, organizing something internally. She was a 400-meter specialist. I was decathlon. We trained adjacent to each other for four years, shared water from the same coolers, complained about the same coaching staff. Now she has a framed license on the wall and I have a reconstructed knee and a referral form that says *mandatory* in the box next to attendance.

"How's the swelling today?" she asks, still not looking.

"Fine."

She turns and looks at me the way she used to look at a stopwatch—with complete, impersonal assessment. Her hair is pulled back. She's wearing navy scrubs. None of this helps.

"Marcus."

"Reduced. The swelling is reduced."

Above the treatment table, a laminated diagram of human musculature stares down at us—the skinless man, red and labeled, every fiber named and categorized. His quadriceps are highlighted in the diagram's legend. Mine are currently atrophied in ways that make me feel like a building that's been condemned.

"Lie back," she says. "We're starting with passive range of motion today before we load anything."

I lie back. The paper crinkles. I stare at the ceiling.

Her hands come to my ankle first—one palm cradling the heel, the other resting lightly on my shin just below the knee. Her grip is clinical. Warm. She begins to flex the joint slowly, drawing my heel toward my body, and I feel the familiar drag of resistance in the joint, the reconstructed ligament pulling taut like a wire that hasn't learned it's supposed to be part of me yet.

"Tell me when you hit a seven," she says.

We use numbers. Numbers are good. Numbers are not personal.

"Four," I say. She continues. The angle increases. I can feel the capsule of the joint stretching, the scar tissue around the surgical site pulling in a way that is not pain exactly but is the cousin of pain, the warning that pain is nearby and has your address. "Six."

She stops. Holds. Thirty seconds of her maintaining that position while my body argues with itself about whether this is acceptable. I look at the diagram. The diagram's vastus medialis is labeled in clean sans-serif font. Mine is failing to fire correctly and has been for eleven weeks.

"How's the medial side?" she asks.

"Tighter."

She adjusts, rotating my lower leg slightly outward, and the sensation changes character entirely—sharper, more specific, running up the inside of my knee like a seam being tested. I exhale through my nose.

"Still six?"

"Six and a half."

She holds it. I am aware of her hands in a way I'm working very hard not to be aware of them. The heel of her palm against my calf. The slight pressure of her thumb near the tibial plateau, monitoring the joint space. This is her job. I know it's her job. I also know that four years ago she beat me in a footrace across a parking lot because I made a stupid bet, and she laughed with her whole body, and I thought about that laugh for longer than was reasonable.

"Okay," she says, lowering my leg back to the table. "Sit up for me."

I sit up. The paper crinkles.

She moves to my side and places one hand flat against my thoracic spine and the other on my sternum, and says, "I need you to extend back against my hand. Slowly. We're opening the hip flexors from the chain."

I lean back into her palm. I can feel each of her fingers individually against my vertebrae. The stretch pulls down through my lumbar spine, through the hip, into the anterior of my thigh where the quad connects to the hip flexor—the whole kinetic chain elongating like something being unrolled. It is uncomfortable in the specific way that correct things are uncomfortable.

"There," she says quietly. Her voice is close. She's standing directly behind me to support the movement, and I am aware of the proximity in my peripheral vision, the navy fabric of her sleeve at the edge of my sight line. "Hold that."

I hold it.

"You competed at nationals three times," she says, and her voice is carefully neutral. "Your body knows how to do hard things."

"That body is gone."

"No. It's modified." A pause. "There's a difference."

I don't answer. The stretch deepens as she applies the gentlest additional pressure with the hand on my sternum, and the sensation climbs to a seven—a real seven, not a polite one—and I breathe through it the way I breathed through the last hundred meters of every event when my lungs were dissolving and the only thing left was mechanics. She feels me tense and holds the pressure exactly where it is. Not retreating. Not advancing.

"Breathe," she says.

I breathe.

The tension releases by a degree. Just one. Just enough.

She guides me back upright slowly, her palm leaving my spine last, and steps away to make a note on her clipboard. I look at the skinless man on the wall. He has no expression. Every muscle perfectly intact, perfectly labeled, perfectly in its place.

"Same time Thursday," she says, not looking up.

"Yeah," I say.

The paper crinkles as I ease off the table.

She's already writing. Her posture is straight. She is thinking, organizing something internally. She was always good at that—at putting things in their correct categories, at knowing exactly what a thing was and what it wasn't.

I'm still learning.
