# THE SISYPHUS COLUMN
## *Practical Wisdom for the Practically Overwhelmed*
### As Dictated to Crow, Corvid of Letters, Keeper of the Seal, Reluctant Employee

---

The mountain, as mountains go, was unremarkable except for the man on it, and the boulder ahead of the man, and the crow sitting on the boulder eating what appeared to be someone's forgotten sandwich.

"That's the third letter from someone whose husband won't do the dishes," said Crow, through a mouthful of what had once been egg salad. "I'm beginning to think the dishes are not actually about the dishes."

"They never are," said Sisyphus, and leaned into the rock.

---

**Dear Sisyphus,**

*My coworker Gerald takes credit for all my ideas in meetings. Last week he presented my entire proposal for restructuring the inventory system as his own, right in front of me, and our manager praised him for it. I've tried speaking to Gerald directly but he just smiles and says he "built on my foundation." I don't know what to do. I've considered quitting but I need this job.*

*— Invisible in Indianapolis*

---

**Dear Invisible,**

Gerald knows exactly what he is doing. The smile tells you everything. A man who genuinely believed he was acting in good faith would look confused when confronted, not pleased with himself. You are not dealing with a misunderstanding. You are dealing with someone who has correctly identified that you will absorb the cost of his behavior rather than create conflict, and has made a business decision accordingly.

So: change the math.

Before your next meeting, send your manager a brief email. Not a complaint about Gerald — not yet. Simply a summary of your proposal, your reasoning, your timeline. "Wanted to make sure you had the background on this before Thursday." Then, in the meeting, when Gerald presents, you have documentation with a timestamp and your manager has already read your name attached to those ideas. Gerald's smile will become somewhat more complicated.

As for whether to stay: that depends on whether your manager is the kind of person who, once they understand what's happening, will be embarrassed they didn't notice, or the kind who already knows and finds it convenient. Those are two very different jobs.

*With genuine regard,*
*Sisyphus*

*[Ed. note: Gerald sounds exhausting. — C.]*

---

The boulder reached the top, paused in that particular way boulders have when they're about to do something everyone present already knows they're going to do, and went back down the mountain.

Sisyphus followed it at a comfortable pace, reading the next letter.

"This one's about a marriage," said Crow, flying alongside.

"Most of them are, in one way or another."

---

**Dear Sisyphus,**

*My wife and I have been together for nineteen years. We don't fight. We're not unhappy, exactly. But last Tuesday I realized I couldn't remember the last time either of us had laughed at something the other one said. Is this just what long marriages become? Am I being unrealistic?*

*— Comfortable but Concerned in Cork*

---

**Dear Comfortable,**

You are not being unrealistic. You are being honest, which is harder and rarer, and the fact that it frightens you a little is a reasonable response to an honest thing.

Nineteen years is long enough that two people can stop *performing* for each other — the exhausting early work of being interesting — and settle into something quieter. Sometimes that quiet is warmth. Sometimes it is distance that has simply learned to sit still.

The question worth asking is not whether you laugh, but whether you are still *curious* about her. Do you know what she's worried about this week? What she's looking forward to? Not the logistics — not the dentist appointment and the thing about the gutters — but what is actually moving through her mind?

If the answer is no, and you'd like it to be yes, then the work is surprisingly small to begin: ask her something you don't already know the answer to, and then listen as though the answer matters. Do this on a Tuesday. Not a special occasion. Tuesdays are underrated.

If she asks why you're suddenly asking, tell her the truth. The truth is a good place to put nineteen years.

*Warmly,*
*Sisyphus*

*[Ed. note: I've never been in a nineteen-year marriage, being a crow, but this seems right. — C.]*

---

**Dear Sisyphus,**

*I'm 34 and I still don't know what my "purpose" is. Everyone around me seems to have found their thing. I feel like I missed a meeting.*

*— Behind Schedule in Brisbane*

---

**Dear Behind Schedule,**

You did not miss a meeting. The meeting was never scheduled. Someone simply told everyone there was a meeting and now everyone is pretending they attended.

Purpose, in my experience, is not a destination that announces itself. It is something you notice, afterward, in the things you kept returning to even when no one was watching and there was no particular reward. What have you kept returning to?

I would also gently suggest that several of the people who appear to have found their thing are in fact people who found *a* thing, committed to it publicly before they could reconsider, and are now too embarrassed to mention the reconsideration. This is not cynicism. This is observation.

Thirty-four is not behind schedule. Thirty-four is old enough to stop taking other people's certainty personally.

*With encouragement,*
*Sisyphus*

*[Ed. note: He's right. I watched a pelican spend six years "purposefully" fishing the same empty inlet. Commitment is not the same as correctness. — C.]*

---

The boulder went up. The boulder came down. Sisyphus retrieved it from a thornbush with the practiced ease of a man who has long since made his peace with thornbushes.

"Last one," said Crow. "It's from someone named Marguerite. It's a bit—"

"Read it."

Crow read it.

There was a pause.

"Well," said Sisyphus.

"Indeed," said Crow.

---

**Dear Marguerite,**

What you are describing is, I want to reassure you, not as unusual as you fear. The human body is inventive and the heart is not always tidy about what it attaches to, and there is no shame in finding yourself at fifty-one surprised by your own enthusiasms.

My practical advice: have the conversation you are afraid to have. You will find it goes one of two ways, and either way, you will finally be able to sleep.

The rest I leave to your own good judgment, which — based on the care and specificity with which you wrote to a dead man on a mountain — I suspect is considerable.

*Yours sincerely,*
*Sisyphus*

*[Ed. note: I am not transcribing the specifics. I am a crow with standards. — C.]*

---

The sun moved, as it does, indifferent to columns and boulders and the small persistent work of being alive.

Sisyphus put his hands on the rock.

"Same time tomorrow?" said Crow.

"The letters will be there," said Sisyphus. "They always are."

He pushed.
