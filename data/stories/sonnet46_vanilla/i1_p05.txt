# Chapter Two

That night, over plates of beans and salt pork, Uncle Denny brought up the Halverson cabin.

JT had been staring into his food, listening to the fire spit and settle in the stone hearth, when the name surfaced in conversation like something floating up from deep water. He wasn't even sure he'd heard it right at first—the wind had picked up outside, shouldering against the lodge walls with a low, persistent moan—but then his father set down his fork, and the particular quality of that silence told JT he hadn't misheard anything.

"Nobody goes near the Halverson place," his father said. Not a question. Not quite a warning. Something in between.

"Wasn't planning on it." Denny reached for the salt without looking up. He was a big man, Denny, with hands that had been broken and reset so many times they'd taken on a sculptural quality, knuckles like river stones. "Just saying I saw smoke up that way this afternoon. Coming from the chimney."

Mike looked up from his plate. "Somebody squatting?"

"Could be." Denny shrugged, but it was a careful shrug, the kind that meant he'd already thought about it more than he wanted to admit. "Could be nothing. Trick of the light."

JT wanted to ask what the Halverson cabin was, exactly, and why the mention of it had made his father's jaw tighten like that. But the table had already moved on—Mike asking about the weather coming in, his father refilling coffee—and the moment sealed itself over the way moments did in this family, smooth and impenetrable as scar tissue.

He lay awake that night listening to his brother breathe in the bunk above him.

---

He found out about the cabin the next morning from Denny, who seemed to have decided overnight that JT was old enough to know things.

They were splitting wood behind the lodge in the grey pre-dawn, breath smoking, the axe rising and falling in a steady rhythm that Denny kept even when he talked, which JT had always found both impressive and unnerving.

"Place has been empty since the late eighties," Denny said, not looking at him. *Thwack.* "Halverson—old Rudy Halverson—he came up here every season, same as us. Good hunter. Quiet man." *Thwack.* "One year he came up alone. Wife said he needed the time." A pause. The axe rested on the block. "They found his gear. Rifle, pack, all of it, laid out real neat on the porch. Like he'd just set it down to go inside." Denny picked up the split halves and stacked them without hurry. "Never found Rudy."

JT thought about the buck in the clearing. The way his father's rifle had hung at his side.

"What do people think happened?"

Denny looked at him then, and for just a moment something moved behind the older man's eyes—not fear exactly, but its more seasoned cousin. Wariness. "People think all kinds of things," he said. "People up here got long winters and short days and too much time to fill the dark with stories." He handed JT a log. "I don't put much stock in stories."

But he hadn't answered the question, and they both knew it.

---

JT saw the smoke himself that afternoon.

He'd drifted from the others again—he had a talent for it, or maybe a compulsion, the way you probe a sore tooth with your tongue even knowing it'll hurt—working his way along the ridge above the frozen creek when he stopped and looked northeast and there it was. A thin grey thread unspooling from the treeline maybe half a mile out, rising until the wind took it apart. Lazy and even. The smoke of a fire that someone was tending.

The cold seemed to sharpen around him.

He stood watching it for longer than he should have. The trees between him and the source were dense spruce, dark and crowded, the kind of forest that swallowed sound. He had the sudden, absolute conviction that if he walked into those trees he would not come out the same way he went in—not changed in any way he could point to or explain, but different in some cellular, unalterable sense, the way meat is different after you've run a current through it.

He turned around and walked back toward the sound of the others.

---

At dinner his father seemed better. More himself. He told the story about the '09 season when Mike had slipped on the ice crossing the creek and lost his rifle to six feet of black water, and Mike groaned and said *Jesus, Dad, not again,* and even JT laughed, and for a little while the lodge felt the way it was supposed to feel—warm and close and sufficient against the dark.

But later, washing dishes at the deep iron sink, JT watched his father standing at the window that faced northeast. Just standing there, hands loose at his sides, looking out at nothing—nothing visible, anyway, just the treeline gone black against a sky that offered no stars. His father's breath had fogged the glass, and he hadn't wiped it clear.

"Dad."

His father turned, and his eyes were fine. Normal. A little tired. "Yeah, bud."

"Nothing." JT turned back to the dishes. "Thought you said something."

He hadn't, of course. And his father hadn't been doing anything wrong. Just a man standing at a window, looking out at the dark. There was nothing wrong with that. People did it all the time.

JT scrubbed at a pot that was already clean and listened to the wind work its way around the eaves of the lodge, patient and exploratory, the way something moves when it's learning the shape of a place. When it's deciding whether to come inside.

Outside, northeast, the smoke had stopped.

Whatever had been tending that fire had let it go out—or had no further need of it.
