# Carnival of Dangerous Intentions

She had told herself, walking the six blocks from the boarding house, that she was simply taking the evening air. That the salt off the Atlantic was good for the constitution. That Coney Island in July was where half of Brooklyn came to breathe, and her presence here meant nothing particular, nothing decided.

She had almost believed it.

The shooting gallery clattered and popped ahead of her. A row of painted ducks jerked along a track, indifferent to the rifles aimed at them, and the man running the booth — thick-necked, with a barker's practiced contempt — called out to the passing crowd in a voice like gravel in a tin can. She stopped just short of the light spilling from the booth's awning. Behind her, the carousel organ ground through something that might once have been a waltz. The sodium lamps along the boardwalk had begun their nightly argument with the dark, flickering on in stutters, throwing yellow light and then withholding it, so that the faces of strangers appeared and disappeared like thoughts she couldn't quite finish.

*His locks.* There it was again.

She pressed her clutch purse against her ribs and made herself breathe. It was a ridiculous phrase, the kind that belonged in a dime novel or a psalm, and she was a practical woman — a secretary at Brennan & Associates, a woman who kept her receipts and read the newspaper and had never in twenty-six years done anything that required an explanation she couldn't give. The phrase had no business lodging itself in her mind. Neither did he.

She had met him here four days ago, on a Wednesday afternoon when she'd come with her friend Dorothea, who had immediately wandered off after a sailor. He had been leaning against the railing at the edge of the boardwalk, watching the ocean with the focused attention most men reserved for things they intended to acquire. Dark curls. Darker eyes. A white shirt with the sleeves rolled to the elbow, which in 1937 still carried a suggestion of carelessness that bordered on the provocative. He had turned and looked at her with the directness of someone who had already decided something, and she had felt, absurdly, like one of those painted ducks — already in the sights, already struck, still moving along the track.

They had talked for forty minutes. She could not now reconstruct the full content of that conversation, which troubled her. She remembered the quality of it — something pulled taut between them, a current running just below the ordinary words about the heat and the crowd and whether the new roller coaster was worth the line. She remembered that he had not smiled easily, that when he finally did it was like a concession he hadn't planned to make. She remembered that when Dorothea had reappeared, flushed and laughing, he had looked at her — only her — and said, quietly, *Come back Saturday. Same time.*

Not a question.

She had said nothing. She had walked away with Dorothea, and she was here now, on Saturday, at the same time, and she had not told Dorothea.

The sodium lamp directly above her buzzed and went dark for a full three seconds. When it came back, he was there.

He was perhaps ten feet away, hands in the pockets of dark trousers, watching her with that same settled attention. He looked, she thought, like a man who was accustomed to waiting and had made his peace with it — which was somehow more unsettling than impatience would have been.

"You came," he said. He didn't move toward her.

"I was in the neighborhood." The words came out with more composure than she felt, which was its own small relief.

Something shifted in his expression. Not quite a smile. "You live in Flatbush."

She felt heat move up the back of her neck. "You asked Dorothea."

"She volunteered it." He took one step toward her, unhurried. "Does it bother you?"

*Yes,* she thought. *It should.* "I haven't decided," she said.

He stopped at a distance that was neither intimate nor quite comfortable — close enough that she had to make a choice about where to look. The carousel waltz lurched into a new key behind them. In the flickering light, the planes of his face arranged and rearranged themselves, shadow filling the hollows differently each time the lamp stuttered, and she had the disorienting sense that she was looking at someone who was not entirely fixed — not dangerous, exactly, but not safe in the way that safety had always been defined for her.

"I don't know your name," she said.

"No," he agreed. He said it as though this were an interesting condition rather than an oversight.

"That's — " She stopped. *Irregular* was the word that came to mind, the secretary's word, the word of receipts and explanations. She discarded it. "Don't you think that's strange?"

"I think," he said, "that you know something more useful than my name." He tilted his head slightly. "You know you came back."

The lamp above them buzzed and darkened again. In the brief blackout, the carnival sounds pressed closer — the rifle cracks, the barker's gravel voice, a child crying somewhere with the specific exhausted misery of too much summer. When the light returned, he was watching her with an expression she couldn't categorize, patient and intent and carrying some private knowledge she hadn't consented to give him.

*You know you came back.* 

She did know. That was the difficulty. She had walked six blocks and told herself a series of comfortable lies, and she was standing here anyway, and the knowing of it sat in her chest like something warm and slightly dangerous, like a match held too long.

"What do you want?" she asked. Her voice was steadier than she had any right to expect.

He considered the question with apparent seriousness, which was not what she had anticipated.

"To show you something," he said finally. "If you'll let me."

The carousel ground on. The sodium light flickered. She did not walk away.
