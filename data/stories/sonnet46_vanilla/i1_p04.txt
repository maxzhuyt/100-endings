# My Apocalypse Buddy

The world had ended, more or less, on a Tuesday. Nobody had planned for Tuesday. That was perhaps the central tragedy of it all.

Midas sat cross-legged on what had once been a traffic median, consulting Chewy on the matter of their immediate future. Chewy sat opposite him, glass eyes catching the flat grey morning light, fur the approximate colour and texture of something that had been through several apocalypses, which, technically, it had.

"The way I see it," Midas said, unfolding a hand-drawn map that had been wrong about three separate occasions already and showed no signs of improving, "we go north, find the place, collect whatever's there, and come back."

Chewy said nothing, which Midas had long since learned to interpret as cautious scepticism.

"The graffiti was very specific," Midas continued, in the tone of a man defending a position he didn't entirely believe in himself. He pulled a scrap of fabric from his jacket pocket. He'd copied the message down carefully, which had taken some time, because whoever had written it had been either visionary or deeply unwell, and the two categories had become increasingly difficult to distinguish.

The original graffiti, discovered on the collapsed wall of what had been a petrol station, read: *WHERE THE THREE-LEGGED DOG TURNS LEFT AND THE RIVER ARGUES WITH ITSELF, THE STILL PLACE WAITS. BRING WHAT YOU ARE.*

"Poetic," Midas had said, when he'd first read it.

"Unsettling," he said now, rereading it.

Chewy's left ear was slightly more forward than the right, which meant nothing, objectively, but which Midas had come to associate with the expression of mild alarm.

"Right then," he said, and stood up. "North."

---

North, as it turned out, had moved slightly since the map was drawn. This was not geographically possible, and yet the evidence was compelling. The road Midas had marked as their primary route had been comprehensively eaten by a sinkhole, which had then been colonised by a family of extremely territorial geese who had apparently decided that the apocalypse represented, for geese, a significant upgrade in circumstances.

Midas retreated with what he felt was admirable dignity, given that he was moving backwards quite quickly with his arms over his head.

"They weren't there before," he told Chewy, who was tucked under his arm and therefore could not verify this claim. "I would have noted the geese."

They went around.

The three-legged dog was, against all reasonable expectation, real. It was sitting at a fork in what had been a dual carriageway, one lane of which had become a meadow and the other a sort of accidental canal. The dog regarded Midas with the weary professionalism of something that had been doing this for a while and found the work beneath its abilities.

It turned left.

Midas followed it for approximately forty minutes before the dog sat down, licked itself with tremendous focus, and declined to move further.

"This is where you live, isn't it," Midas said.

The dog confirmed this by going inside a collapsed AutoZone.

Midas stood in the road for a moment. He looked at Chewy. Chewy looked at the river, which was visible through the treeline and which was, in some hydrological sense that Midas couldn't quite articulate, arguing with itself. Two channels, splitting around a collapsed bridge, rejoining badly, creating a small and perpetually furious whirlpool that seemed personally offended by the laws of physics.

"That must be where the river argues with itself," Midas said.

He crossed it on a log, which was the kind of sentence that sounds straightforward until you are actually on the log.

He made it across. He also made it into the river, briefly, and then across again, which he felt should count for something. Chewy, held above his head during the crossing, remained dry, which Midas considered a reasonable distribution of outcomes.

---

The Still Place was a greenhouse.

It had been, at some point, attached to a garden centre that had since become largely theoretical. But the greenhouse itself stood, improbably, intact. Inside, in the particular silence of growing things, someone had built a small library. Books on shelves made of pallets. A note on the door, in the same handwriting as the graffiti: *TAKE ONE. LEAVE ONE. OR DON'T. IT'S FINE EITHER WAY.*

Midas stood in the warm, green-smelling air for a long moment.

He had been expecting supplies. Tinned goods, perhaps. Medicine. The practical currency of the collapsed world.

Instead: books.

He looked at Chewy.

Chewy's expression, as ever, was one of synthetic serenity, but Midas had the distinct impression that if the koala could have said something, it would have been *well, what did you expect.*

He picked up a copy of a novel he'd never heard of, left a pocket knife he'd been carrying as a spare, and sat down on an upturned crate for twenty minutes reading the first three chapters, which were about a man who kept making poor decisions in increasingly scenic locations.

He felt seen.

---

The journey home was, in the technical sense, worse.

The geese had expanded their territory. The log had moved. Midas's left boot developed a philosophical disagreement with his left foot somewhere around the two-hour mark. It began to rain, specifically and with apparent intent, on Midas.

He arrived back at the median as the light was failing, wet in the specific way that suggests the universe has made a point, carrying one book and the memory of a warm greenhouse.

He sat down. He set Chewy opposite him.

"Good day," he said, in the way that means the opposite.

Chewy's glass eyes caught the last of the light. His left ear was, perhaps, very slightly less forward than before.

Midas opened the book to chapter four.

The man in the novel made another poor decision.

Midas read on, and felt, in the damp ruin of the world, something that was almost, if you didn't look at it directly, like comfort.
