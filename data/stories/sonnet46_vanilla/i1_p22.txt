# Prison Strings

The fluorescent light overhead stutters like a dying heartbeat, casting the corridor in alternating pools of sickly yellow and shadow. The facility smells of rust and something older—something that has no name in any language Jolyne has encountered, and she has encountered several since Stone Free first split her skin into thread.

She doesn't know how she got here.

That fact alone keeps her jaw tight.

Jolyne Cujoh has woken in unfamiliar places before. Prison cells. Hospital rooms. The backseat of her own miserable memories. But this corridor belongs to no architecture she recognizes—the walls breathe at irregular intervals, the concrete expanding and contracting like something alive and patient, and the cell doors lining both sides bear no numbers, only symbols that rearrange themselves when she isn't looking directly at them.

She's been walking for eleven minutes. She's counted.

"You seem tense."

Jolyne stops.

The voice arrives before the woman does—soft, unhurried, the vocal equivalent of a hand placed gently on your shoulder by someone you haven't given permission to touch you. Jolyne turns slowly, because she learned from her father that slow turns communicate more danger than fast ones.

The woman stepping from the adjacent corridor is young-looking. Auburn hair. Eyes like something ancient wearing a costume. She's dressed cleanly, impossibly cleanly for a place like this, and she's smiling the way people smile in photographs—technically correct, emotionally hollow.

"Most people are, when they first arrive," the woman continues. "It passes."

"Does it." Jolyne doesn't phrase it as a question.

"I'm Makima." She tilts her head slightly, the gesture engineered to appear curious. "And you're Jolyne Cujoh. Jotaro's daughter."

The name of her father in this stranger's mouth feels like fingers closing around something private. Jolyne's hand drifts toward her wrist—toward the string already beginning to unravel beneath her skin, subtle as a whisper.

"You've done your homework," Jolyne says. "Good for you."

"I find it useful to know who I'm sharing space with." Makima's smile doesn't waver. It is, Jolyne realizes, the most stable thing in this entire corridor. More stable than the walls. More stable than the light. "Your ability is remarkable. Unraveling yourself into string. The applications must be—"

"Why are you here."

Not a question either. Jolyne watches those ringed yellow eyes absorb the interruption without any visible irritation, which is itself a kind of answer. Normal people react to rudeness. They tighten, or they retreat, or they match it. This woman simply continues, the way water continues around a stone it has already decided to eventually wear down.

"The same reason you are, I imagine," Makima says pleasantly. "Circumstances."

"Circumstances." Jolyne lets the word sit between them like evidence. "Right."

She starts walking again, keeping Makima in her peripheral vision—a habit inherited from years of watching her back in Green Dolphin Street, refined by fighting things that didn't have the decency to stay in one direction. Makima falls into step beside her with the ease of someone who has been invited.

She has not been invited.

"The facility responds to supernatural ability," Makima offers, conversationally. "It's why the walls move. It's cataloguing you."

"And you know this how."

"I've been here longer."

Jolyne glances sideways. Makima's profile is serene, her hands clasped loosely in front of her, and there is something about the way she occupies space that makes Jolyne's teeth ache—like she is simultaneously present and entirely elsewhere, like the body walking beside her is a very convincing forwarding address.

"You're not a prisoner," Jolyne says.

A pause. Fractional. The first real timing error Makima has made.

"What makes you say that?"

"Because you're not acting like one." Jolyne stops walking and faces her fully, letting Stone Free's string coil silently around her fingers inside her sleeve. "Prisoners case exits. Prisoners watch doors. You're watching *me*." She tilts her head, mirroring Makima's earlier gesture deliberately. "So either you're staff, or you put yourself here. Neither one makes you someone I want walking next to me."

Something shifts in Makima's expression. Not much. A degree or two of recalibration, the smile softening into something that is probably supposed to read as impressed.

"You're perceptive," she says. "Jotaro raised you well."

"Jotaro raised me *badly*," Jolyne says flatly, "and I turned out fine anyway. Don't try to compliment your way into my good graces. I don't have any."

"I'm not trying to manipulate you."

"People who aren't trying to manipulate you don't say that." Jolyne takes one step back, creating distance that she intends to keep. "They don't need to."

The light flickers violently overhead. In the strobing dark, Makima's eyes catch the light differently than they should—reflecting it back in rings, in layers, like something with depth that has no bottom. The corridor exhales around them.

"Your string," Makima says softly, and now the pleasantness has thinned just enough to show the architecture beneath it—vast, cold, certain of itself the way only very old things are certain. "I wonder how much of yourself you can afford to unravel before there's nothing left to pull back."

Jolyne's string is already moving.

It traces the corridor walls in threads too fine to see, mapping exits, testing the breathing concrete, threading through the gaps in cell doors. She has been doing this since the conversation began. She learned patience from watching her father, and she learned paranoia from surviving without him.

"I wonder," Jolyne says, her voice carrying the particular steadiness of someone who has decided, "how many people have stood where I'm standing and thought you were on their side."

Makima looks at her for a long moment.

Then she smiles, and this time it reaches her eyes, which is somehow so much worse.

"Not many," she admits. "You're the first in quite some time."

The light goes out completely.

Jolyne pulls her string tight and gets to work.
