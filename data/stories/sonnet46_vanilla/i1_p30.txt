# Útiseta

I should tell you that I was fifteen, and that fifteen is old enough to know better. I've told myself this for years, the way you press a bruise to confirm it still hurts.

---

Astrid came for me at eleven-thirty, throwing gravel at my window with the practiced accuracy of someone who had done this before, for other people, on other nights. I'd been awake already, lying in the particular darkness of a Swedish autumn that has no equivalent in Stockholm—a darkness with weight to it, with texture, as though the forest pressed itself against the glass and breathed.

She was waiting at the garden gate with a canvas bag over one shoulder and her grandmother's old reindeer-hide coat hanging to her knees. Her breath made small ghosts in the cold.

"You wore wool," she said, checking my sweater with a critical eye. "Good. Nothing synthetic. It confuses things."

"What things?"

She was already walking.

---

I'd been in Norrland for three months by then, long enough to understand that I would never belong, not short enough to still be numb to it. Stockholm felt like a photograph I'd left in a drawer. My father talked about the transfer as an adventure. My mother had stopped talking about it at all.

Astrid was the only person at school who'd spoken to me without the careful distance the others maintained, and I'd been so grateful for this that I hadn't asked, early enough, what the friendship would cost.

The midnight games had begun small. A circle of ash on the floor of her grandmother's barn. Names written on birch bark and buried at crossroads. She'd told me we could use them to know things—who liked whom, who was lying, what the teachers said in the staff room. Teenage currency. I'd told myself it was just theater, that we were playing at something rather than practicing it.

But Astrid never played. That was the thing I kept forgetting.

---

The burial mound sat at the edge of Persson's pasture, a long low rise in the earth that the farmer mowed around without discussion, the way you navigate around something you've agreed not to acknowledge. It was older than the church, older than the village's name. The locals called it *Döshögen*. The dead hill.

"Sit at the center," Astrid said, unpacking her bag with the efficiency of someone laying out surgical instruments. "Facing north. Your back straight, your hands open on your knees. You don't speak unless spoken to."

"Spoken to by what?"

She looked at me with the patience she reserved for questions she found exhausting. "By whatever comes."

She laid out a circle of stones around the mound's crown, small and pale, collected from the stream. She placed a bundle of dried herbs—juniper, I thought, and something else I didn't recognize—at the northern point and lit it with a match, cupping the flame against the wind until the smoke rose thin and steady. She set down a small clay dish with something dark in it. I didn't ask.

"The útiseta," she said, settling cross-legged beside me, "is how the *völva* received knowledge. You sit through the night. You open yourself. Things come to fill the space."

"Your grandmother taught you this."

"She taught me the shape of it." Astrid's voice shifted slightly, the way it always did when she mentioned her grandmother. "She said the shape is what matters. The intention fits inside the shape like water in a bowl."

The herbs burned down to a coal and then to nothing. The cold settled around us with purpose.

---

I don't know how long we sat. Long enough that the cold moved from my skin inward, long enough that the tree line lost its definition and became simply *dark against dark*. Astrid was motionless beside me. I focused on keeping my hands open, palms up, the way she'd instructed, and tried not to think about how exposed the position felt—how it removed the option of making fists.

The first thing I noticed was the silence. Not the absence of sound, but silence as a presence, a cessation that felt chosen rather than natural. The wind had been moving through the spruce trees steadily since we'd arrived. Then it wasn't.

The second thing was the smell. Something underneath the juniper smoke—older, mineral, like turned earth but warmer than earth should be in October.

"Astrid," I whispered.

"Don't," she said. Her voice was completely level.

I watched the tree line and the tree line watched back, and I was aware, with the specific clarity that fear produces, that I had made an error in judgment whose dimensions I couldn't yet measure. We had come to this place and announced ourselves. We had made a shape and put our intention inside it. We were fifteen and we had done this as though it were nothing, as though old things stayed where you left them, as though a door was just a door until you knocked.

The church bell in the village struck once.

There was no service scheduled. I knew this because my mother had started attending, looking for community, and had complained that the church was only open Sundays. It was Tuesday. The bell had not rung since I'd arrived in the village.

One stroke. Clean, deliberate, and then silence resumed its weight.

Astrid exhaled slowly beside me. Her hands, I noticed, were not open on her knees. They were pressed flat against the earth of the mound, and her eyes were closed, and her lips were moving without sound.

I looked north, where the herbs had burned.

The cold dish was warm.

---

I want to tell you nothing happened. I want to tell you we were two teenage girls frightening ourselves in a field, that the bell was a mechanical fault, that the dish had retained heat from Astrid's hands.

I've been telling myself these things for thirty years.

I'm not sure I believe them.
