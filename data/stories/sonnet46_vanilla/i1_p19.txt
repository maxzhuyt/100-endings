# The Dragon's Accounting

The morning tasted of low tide and burnt sugar.

I knew the smell before I opened my eyes — that particular marriage of the Pearl River at ebb and the charcoal braziers of the cook-stalls along Thirteen Factories Street, threading through the gap in my shutter where the latch had broken three weeks ago and I hadn't bothered to fix it. I lay still for a moment, cataloguing myself. Fingers: trembling slightly, not badly. Stomach: a slow, turning wrongness, like a compass needle seeking north and finding only more north. Temples: a tightening. Nothing yet that would make me useless, but the arithmetic was already running in my head, the same calculation I performed every morning before I was properly awake.

What I had. What I needed. What the distance was between those two numbers.

I dressed without shaving. My uniform coat hung on its hook with the authority of someone else's life — the brass buttons catching what light there was, the epaulettes sitting straight and correct. I put it on anyway. The coat was useful. People saw the coat before they saw me.

---

Fong met me at the corner of Old China Street, where the lane narrows to the width of two men walking abreast and the upper floors of the buildings nearly touch overhead, making a kind of perpetual twilight even at nine in the morning.

"You are late," he said, in his careful English, without looking up from the cage of songbirds he was examining at a vendor's stall. He was a small man, perhaps fifty, with a face that had settled into an expression of permanent mild disappointment.

"I'm not late," I said. "We said no time."

"I said morning. This is late morning." He selected one finger to point at the sky, where the sun was already well clear of the rooflines. "You have the letter?"

I produced it from my coat — a sealed dispatch from the factory offices, addressed in the formal hand of a company clerk, bearing a seal that was not quite genuine but would pass any inspection short of a careful one. I had spent forty minutes the previous evening on that seal, pressing wax with a button I'd had a craftsman modify on Hog Lane. My hands had shaken throughout.

Fong took it without examining it. "The comprador at Conseequa's warehouse. You know him?"

"We've met."

"He will ask you about the cargo manifest for the *Reliance*. You will tell him the figures I gave you."

"The figures you gave me are wrong," I said. "If anyone checks against the harbour master's records—"

"No one checks." He finally looked at me. His eyes moved to my hands, which I'd pressed flat against my thighs. "You need something before you go."

It wasn't a question. I hated that it wasn't a question.

"After," I said.

"Of course." A pause that lasted precisely long enough to remind me who was conducting this negotiation. "After."

---

The warehouse district sat behind the factories, a labyrinth of narrow godowns smelling of camphor, wet hemp, and the faint ghost of pepper from some older trade. The comprador was a young man named Chen who wore a Western waistcoat over his changshan and spoke better English than half my midshipmen. He received me in a small office barely large enough for a desk and two chairs, a ledger open before him.

We talked for twenty minutes about numbers that meant nothing to me. I gave him Fong's figures. He wrote them down. I watched his pen move and felt the jade figurine in my coat pocket — a small Guanyin, no longer than my thumb, that I'd taken from a curio stall on a better day — and I pressed my thumb into the carved folds of her robe. The nausea was building now into something with more intention behind it, a slow insistence in my gut and behind my eyes.

"You are unwell, Lieutenant?" Chen asked, without looking up.

"Perfectly well. The heat."

"It is not hot today."

"No," I agreed. "No, it isn't."

He closed the ledger. "Tell Fong the arrangement is confirmed for Thursday. The lighter will be at the second anchorage, not the first. The first anchorage is — complicated."

"Complicated how?"

He smiled politely and said nothing, which was its own kind of answer.

---

I found Fong's man at the tea house on Respondentia Walk — not Fong himself, but a boy of perhaps sixteen who spoke no English and handed me a small paper parcel with the transaction already concluded, Fong having apparently decided my portion of the morning's work was worth a specific quantity of his generosity. I didn't count it. I knew the weight of it in my palm the way I knew the weight of a sextant.

I took a room above a chandler's shop for an hour. The proprietor knew me by a different name and didn't look at my face.

---

Afterwards, in that brief country between relief and return, I sat on the edge of a rope-strung cot and looked at the Guanyin in my palm. The craftsman who'd carved her had given her an expression of absolute equanimity — that particular Buddhist serenity that always struck me as either profound or simply blank, depending on my condition. Today she looked merely patient.

I thought about the *Reliance* and her manifests. I thought about Thursday's lighter at the second anchorage. I thought about the letter I hadn't written to my father in four months, and the one before that which I'd started three times and abandoned.

Outside, Canton went about its enormous business — the calls of the river boatmen, a dispute in Cantonese from somewhere below, the bells of some vessel making her way downriver toward the open sea and whatever honest purpose she served.

I put the Guanyin back in my pocket.

I still had the afternoon's accounting to do.
