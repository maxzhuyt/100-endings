The Trevi Fountain was already crowded at nine in the morning, a glittering bowl of coins and cameras and sun-splashed stone. Ben stood one step back from the tide, holding his guidebook open like a shield.

He’d underlined three sentences about Bernini the night before. He was trying to find the exact angle from which the guidebook’s photo had been taken, to compare light and shadow like it mattered, like it was research and not merely a way of keeping himself occupied in a foreign city.

He turned a page.

A horn snapped through the air like a whip.

Ben looked up, too late.

A red-and-black motorcycle cut across the cobblestones with a confidence that bordered on criminal. The rider leaned in, knee almost kissing stone, and for one unsteady second Ben saw only a visor and the flash of dark hair escaping beneath a helmet.

He stepped back instinctively—straight into a tourist with a gelato.

“Mi prendi in giro?” came a voice, sharp as a thrown coin.

The bike stopped so close the heat from the engine brushed Ben’s shins. He could see the front fender: a bright, childish dent painted over with swirls of turquoise and yellow, like someone had tried to turn a wound into art. It didn’t look accidental. It looked like a souvenir.

The rider lifted her visor. She was not smiling.

“You—uh—sorry,” Ben said quickly, hands up, guidebook clutched. “I’m terribly sorry. I didn’t—”

“Non hai visto niente, eh?” She tipped her head toward the guidebook, then the fountain, then Ben, like she was cataloging his entire existence in three glances. “You read. You die.”

Ben blinked. “I—what?”

She sighed as if he’d failed a basic exam. “Britannico.”

“How did you—”

“Those shoes,” she said in English now, clipped and amused. “And that face you do. Like you are always apologizing to the air.”

Ben’s ears warmed. “It’s—habit. Also you almost ran me over.”

“I did not.” Her mouth curved just enough to suggest she enjoyed the argument. “I almost educated you. Different.”

Around them, Rome flowed as if nothing had happened. Coins flicked into water. A street vendor called out prices with lazy desperation. The fountain roared, indifferent.

Ben tried Italian, because he was here to learn, because embarrassment made him reckless. “Mi… dispiace. Ma—eh—lei… guida… molto… veloce.”

She stared at him for a beat, then laughed, a short burst like a match struck. “Madonna. You are adorable.”

Ben straightened, unsure if he’d been insulted or praised. “I’m not sure that’s—”

“I’m Alessandra.” She nodded at the motorcycle like it was an extension of her body. “And you are… Professor?”

“No,” Ben said automatically, then realized that was what he was, technically. “Ben. Ben Carter. I’m—on sabbatical.”

“Sabbatical.” She rolled the word in her mouth as if tasting it. “So you are here to be lost.”

“I’m not lost,” he said, too quickly.

Alessandra glanced at the guidebook again. “You are holding a map like a drowning man.”

“I just like to understand—”

“Understand.” She pulled the key from the ignition, then—without any warning—reached past him and plucked something from the fountain’s edge.

Ben’s eyes followed her hand. A small brass coin lay in her palm. Not a Euro. Something older. It glinted with a worn profile.

“Did you just—” Ben lowered his voice. “You can’t take that.”

Alessandra tucked it into her jacket pocket with the smoothness of someone who’d practiced being careless. “It was out. On the stone. Not in the water.”

“That’s not the point.”

“The point,” she said, stepping off the bike, “is you are too stiff. You will crack. Like—how you say—biscotti.”

Ben tried to gather himself. “I’m perfectly capable of enjoying Rome in a—lawful manner.”

She looked him up and down again. “Lawful is boring. Come.”

“Come where?”

She started walking, helmet under her arm, not waiting to see if he followed. She moved like she owned the street, like the city’s chaos was choreographed for her.

Ben hesitated. He had plans: the Pantheon, then a quiet lunch, then reading in the shade. He could almost see his day neatly stacked like books.

Alessandra stopped and looked back. “Professor Ben.”

“I said I wasn’t—”

“You want to see Rome or you want to read about Rome?”

The question hit him in a place he hadn’t known was tender.

He closed the guidebook, then—before he could talk himself out of it—fell into step beside her.

---

By late morning they were in Trastevere, the streets narrowing into a maze of laundry lines and scooter exhaust. Alessandra walked fast, pointing without explanation.

“That church,” she said, jabbing a finger. “Pretty. Too many weddings. That bar—bad coffee, good gossip. That window—” She paused, eyes narrowing at a second-floor balcony where basil spilled from terracotta pots. “That is where my cousin threw a plate at her boyfriend.”

Ben tried to keep up. “Why?”

“He deserved.” Alessandra said it as if it were universal logic. Then she glanced at Ben’s careful steps on the uneven stones and slowed, just slightly. “You are afraid of falling?”

“I’m not afraid,” Ben lied. “I’m cautious.”

“Same thing with better suit.”

Ben huffed a laugh despite himself. “You’re very… direct.”

“I am Roman,” she said. “We don’t have time for soft words.”

At a small piazza, Alessandra stopped at a market stall crowded with scarves and leather bracelets. The vendor greeted her like family. She leaned in, said something rapid in Italian, and the vendor’s grin widened.

Ben hovered behind her, hands in his pockets, trying to look relaxed.

Alessandra picked up a narrow strip of leather—simple, dark, with a metal clasp. She held it against Ben’s wrist.

“No,” Ben said reflexively. “I don’t—”

“Shh.” She fastened it before he could pull away. Her fingers were quick, warm against his skin. “Now you look less like a lost child.”

Ben stared down. The bracelet was understated, almost scholarly. The absurd affection in the gesture made his throat tighten.

“That’s very kind,” he said.

Alessandra waved him off, already moving. “It is nothing.”

Ben turned to thank the vendor, but Alessandra was blocking his view—and the vendor’s hand moved, exchanging cash that Ben didn’t see.

When they were a few steps away, Ben glanced back. The vendor was still smiling. Alessandra was whistling, innocent as a saint.

Ben’s eyes fell to her jacket pocket, where the outline of something small made a little bump.

He said nothing, because he was not certain. Because calling her out would end whatever this was. Because, disturbingly, he didn’t want it to end.

---

They crossed the Tiber at noon, the river a slow ribbon under the bridges. Alessandra stopped beside a street musician playing a battered accordion.

Without asking, she took Ben’s hand and placed a coin in his palm. “Give.”

Ben looked at the coin. It wasn’t Euro. It was old, brass, worn.

“Alessandra,” he said quietly.

“What?” Her eyes were bright, challenging.

“This—where did you—”

She stepped closer, crowding his space until he could smell something clean under the city: soap, maybe, and lemon. “Professor,” she murmured, voice low enough to be private. “Sometimes you have to borrow luck.”

Ben swallowed. The musician’s song wove around them. People passed, brushing shoulders, not noticing.

Ben dropped the coin into the musician’s open case.

The accordionist nodded in thanks.

Alessandra watched Ben’s face as if she’d just pushed him off a ledge and was waiting to see if he’d fly.

Ben exhaled, shaky. “This day is going to get me arrested.”

Alessandra’s smile finally arrived fully, wicked and delighted. “No. I will get you arrested. You just come.”

---

In the afternoon heat, Alessandra led him to a courtyard behind a building he couldn’t identify. The gate was ajar. Inside, sunlight slanted over a patch of wild flowers and a cracked marble statue missing its hands.

“This is private property,” Ben said.

Alessandra lifted her eyebrows. “Now you can read signs.”

“There’s a lock,” Ben pointed out.

“Broken,” she corrected, pushing it open with a finger. “Come.”

Ben followed, heart thumping. It felt ridiculous—trespassing like a teenager—yet the thrill was undeniable. His usual internal narration, the endless caution, had quieted to a stunned whisper.

In the courtyard, Alessandra climbed onto a low wall and sat with her legs dangling.

Ben stood awkwardly below her. “Why are you doing this?” he asked.

“Doing what?”

“Dragging me around. You don’t know me.”

Alessandra looked at him for a long moment. In the sudden stillness, her mischief softened into something harder to read.

“You looked sad at Trevi,” she said finally, almost matter-of-fact. “Not sad-sad. More like—stuck.”

Ben’s chest tightened. “I’m not stuck.”

“Sure.” She swung her boot against the wall. “And I am not trouble.”

Ben surprised himself by stepping closer. “Are you trouble?”

Her eyes flicked to his mouth, then back to his eyes. “Yes.”

“Should I be worried?”

Alessandra leaned forward, close enough now that Ben could see a faint freckle near her cheekbone, a small imperfection that made her feel more real. “You are already worried,” she said. “But you are still here.”

Ben let out a breath he didn’t know he’d been holding. “I don’t usually do things like this.”

“Like what?” she asked, voice softer.

“Follow a stranger,” Ben said. “Trespass. Skip my plans. Let someone—” He glanced down at his wrist, at the bracelet. “—decide my day.”

Alessandra’s gaze lingered on his wrist too. “You like it.”

Ben met her eyes. “Yes.”

The confession hung between them, bright and dangerous.

Alessandra slipped off the wall. She landed close, boots scraping stone, and for a heartbeat Ben thought she might kiss him—thought he might actually tilt forward and meet her halfway, forget every rule he’d ever rehearsed.

Instead she reached past him, plucked a small white flower from the wild patch, and tucked it behind his ear with exaggerated care.

“There,” she said, stepping back to admire her work. “Now you are ridiculous.”

Ben touched the flower, half laughing, half stunned. “You’re impossible.”

Alessandra’s grin flashed. “And you are—how you say—smitten.”

Ben’s laugh died on his tongue. He didn’t deny it.

---

Near sunset, they returned to where it had started. The Trevi Fountain was even more crowded now, the water tinted gold. Alessandra’s motorcycle waited at the edge of the street like a poised animal. The colorful dent caught the last light, bright as a bruise made beautiful.

Ben stood beside her, reluctant in a way that surprised him. It had been one day. It felt like a crack in his life had opened and let oxygen in.

Alessandra swung her helmet onto her head, then paused. Her hands hovered at her chin strap. She looked at Ben as if measuring something.

“This is where you go back to your hotel,” she said.

Ben nodded slowly. “And you go back to… causing havoc.”

“Work,” Alessandra corrected, though her eyes said yes, havoc. “I deliver packages. Sometimes I deliver people from their boredom.”

Ben swallowed. “Will I see you again?”

Alessandra tilted her head. “You want?”

“Yes,” Ben said, too honest. “I think so.”

She stepped closer. In the roar of the fountain, her voice was a private line drawn straight into him. “Then you come tomorrow,” she said, “at ten, same place. No book.”

Ben tried to smile, but it came out shaky. “That sounds… terrifying.”

“Bene.” Alessandra reached into her jacket pocket. Ben’s pulse jumped—expecting a coin, a stolen trinket, proof.

She pulled out something else: a tiny keyring shaped like the Colosseum, cheap and glittery, the kind tourists bought in bulk.

She pressed it into Ben’s palm. Her fingers closed around his for a second, firm.

Ben stared at it. “Is this—”

“A souvenir,” Alessandra said quickly, too quickly. “You need something to remember you survived.”

Ben looked up. “Did you steal this?”

Alessandra’s eyes sparked. “No.”

He waited.

She sighed, exasperated, then leaned in and kissed him—fast, warm, unapologetic. It wasn’t gentle. It was a dare.

When she pulled back, Ben was still trying to breathe.

Alessandra tapped the guidebook sticking out of his bag like an accusation. “Tomorrow,” she repeated. “No book.”

Then she swung onto the motorcycle in one fluid motion. The engine growled. The colorful dent flashed again, a bright mark of some past adventure Ben could only guess at.

As she pulled away, she called over her shoulder, “And, Professor—try not to die.”

Ben stood with a ridiculous flower in his hair, a cheap keyring in his hand, and a bracelet snug on his wrist, watching her disappear into Roman traffic.

He realized—too late, and with a startled, delighted dread—that he was already planning his entire life around ten o’clock tomorrow.
