A dim overhead light flickers in the prison corridor at irregular intervals. Each blink cuts the hall into slices—concrete, bars, peeling paint, then shadow again—like the place can’t decide whether it wants to be seen.

Jolyne Cujoh walks like she owns the corridor anyway.

The cuffs on her wrists clink with each step, not because she’s scared, but because the facility insists on reminding you who is supposed to be in charge. Her green prison jacket hangs open. The butterfly tattoo on her arm sits bright against her skin, a little flag that says she’s still herself in here.

This isn’t Green Dolphin Street.

The guards call it a transfer annex, but the air feels wrong—too clean, too still, like a sealed jar. The walls are stamped with symbols that look halfway between warding runes and corporate branding. A prison built by people who believe the supernatural is a management problem.

Two men in grey uniforms flank her. Their eyes don’t meet hers. They keep their hands near holsters that aren’t guns.

“You’re acting like I’m radioactive,” Jolyne mutters.

One of them swallows. “Just keep moving.”

The light flickers. When it comes back, there’s a woman standing ahead as if she’s always been there.

Auburn hair, neatly cut. White shirt, black tie, coat that doesn’t belong in a prison. Her face holds a polite, gentle smile that makes the concrete look cheaper by comparison. Her eyes are the worst part—yellow rings nested inside yellow rings, like a target that keeps pulling you inward.

“Good evening,” the woman says, voice soft enough to sound kind even in this place. “You must be Jolyne Cujoh.”

Jolyne stops. The guards stop too, but their shoulders loosen the way people relax around authority they recognize. That alone makes Jolyne’s skin crawl.

“And you are?” Jolyne says.

“Makima.” She inclines her head. “I’m here to oversee your intake.”

Oversee. Not process. Not assist. Oversee.

Jolyne lets her gaze drag slowly over Makima’s hands. Clean nails. No cuffs. No badge. No weapon. She looks like she could walk into any room and people would start offering her chairs.

Jolyne smiles with her mouth only. “That’s funny. You don’t look like a prison worker.”

Makima’s expression doesn’t falter. “I work with difficult cases.”

Jolyne hears it: *You’re a case. Not a person.*

The light flickers again. In the dark, Jolyne feels something like a tug across her spine, like invisible thread tightening. When the light returns, Makima is still smiling, and the two guards are staring ahead with blank patience.

Makima steps closer. “I read your file. Framed for murder. Poor thing.”

Jolyne’s jaw tightens. Sympathy from strangers in a facility like this is never free.

“Don’t ‘poor thing’ me,” Jolyne snaps. “You don’t know shit about me.”

Makima’s smile warms, like Jolyne has given her something cute. “Then let me learn.”

The corridor feels narrower. Jolyne feels the boundaries of the air, the way sound doesn’t travel right. A contained space. A controlled space.

Makima turns her head slightly, and Jolyne notices—down the hall, behind a grated window in a door—figures stand motionless. Not guards. People in plain clothes with collars, the kind used for dogs.

Leashes.

A thin leather strap runs from one of their necks to Makima’s hand, slack like decoration. The person holding it doesn’t blink.

Jolyne’s stomach drops in a way she hates. Not fear. Recognition.

“You keep pets in a prison?” Jolyne says.

Makima’s tone stays tender. “They’re very helpful. They don’t cause trouble.”

Jolyne’s fingers twitch. The cuffs are heavy, but Stone Free doesn’t need a lot of room. She can feel her Stand under her skin like a coil. If Makima is some kind of Stand user or worse—something else—then this corridor is a trap with a smile painted on it.

Makima steps in close enough that the guards behind Jolyne shift as if to make space for her. Jolyne doesn’t.

Makima’s gaze drops to the butterfly tattoo, then to Jolyne’s hands. “Your ability is fascinating,” she says, as if they’re discussing a hobby. “String.”

Jolyne’s pulse thuds once. She hasn’t used Stone Free since they dragged her in here.

“You’ve got the wrong bitch,” Jolyne says. “I don’t knit.”

Makima’s eyes narrow with interest, not irritation. “String is stronger than people assume. It binds. It cuts. It connects. It makes puppets.”

Puppets. Jolyne hears the word and sees those leashes again.

The light flickers. In the darkness, Jolyne moves.

Stone Free unwraps from her forearm in a whisper of fiber, threads sliding out from skin and muscle like she’s pulling the seam of herself loose. A thin strand snakes along the floor, fast and quiet, slipping around Makima’s ankle.

When the light returns, Jolyne yanks.

Makima’s foot should slide. Her balance should break. Instead, she remains perfectly upright, like gravity has agreed to behave for her.

Makima looks down at the string around her ankle and then back up, delighted. “So it’s true.”

Jolyne’s teeth bare. “Don’t act impressed. Act threatened.”

Makima lifts her heel and steps forward. The string snaps—not from force, but from something like permission being revoked. Jolyne feels it break in her hands with the wrong kind of sensation, like a thought being interrupted.

Jolyne stiffens. That wasn’t normal. Stone Free’s string doesn’t just *snap* unless something stronger cuts it.

Makima’s voice stays gentle. “Please don’t struggle. I’d rather not hurt you.”

“Lady, you’re already pissing me off,” Jolyne says, and Stone Free blooms behind her—humanoid, sleek, its body threaded like woven muscle. It raises a fist.

The guards gasp, but they don’t run. Their faces don’t change.

Makima watches Stone Free with a kind of calm hunger. “So beautiful,” she murmurs. “A Stand. A soul made visible.”

Jolyne swings.

Stone Free’s fist rockets toward Makima’s face. It should smash bone. It should make that smile go away.

Makima doesn’t dodge. She doesn’t flinch.

At the last instant, one of the collared people in the grated room jerks forward as if yanked. The fist connects with *them*—not physically, but in a way Jolyne feels through Stone Free’s knuckles. The impact lands somewhere else.

A wet crack sounds from behind the grate. Blood spatters the inside of the window.

The leashed person collapses out of view.

Stone Free’s fist stops, still inches from Makima’s cheek. Makima’s hair doesn’t even move.

Jolyne’s breath catches. Her stomach twists.

Makima smiles, almost apologetic. “I’m sorry. Sometimes things happen to my dogs when I’m in danger.”

Jolyne’s eyes lock on the grate. “You… redirected it.”

Makima tilts her head. “You’re quick.”

The corridor flickers again. Jolyne’s mind races in the darkness. *If I hit her, someone else takes it. If I attack, she spends other people like coins.* That’s not defense—that’s ownership.

The light returns. Jolyne’s hands shake once, not from fear but from fury she can’t spend on a punch.

Makima steps closer, close enough that Jolyne can see the concentric rings in her eyes like chains.

“I don’t want to be your enemy,” Makima says softly. “You’re special. If you cooperate, you could be comfortable here.”

“Comfortable,” Jolyne repeats, tasting the lie. “In a place like this?”

Makima’s smile doesn’t change, but the warmth in it is a mask with no face behind it. “Comfort is relative. Freedom is relative. Most people don’t even notice their leashes.”

Jolyne’s throat tightens. She thinks of her father, of Jotaro’s silence and weight, of promises that aren’t spoken. She thinks of how this place has a different kind of cage, one you don’t see until you try to move.

Makima extends a hand, palm up, like she’s offering Jolyne a dance.

“Come,” she says. “Let’s talk privately.”

Jolyne looks at the hand. Then at the leashes. Then at the guards standing like furniture.

Stone Free’s fingers twitch. Jolyne’s body wants to lunge. To spit. To tear the corridor apart into string and climb out through the vents. But she’s not just fighting walls. She’s fighting a person who turns people into walls.

Jolyne lets out a short, sharp laugh. “You know what?” she says. “If this is your idea of Disney, there’s one problem.”

Makima’s eyes glint. “Oh?”

Jolyne’s grin turns mean. “If there’s no Mickey, this shit ain’t Disney.”

She unravels.

Stone Free pulls Jolyne’s torso into thread, skin and muscle turning into a whip of string that slides out of the cuffs like smoke through bars. The metal clatters to the floor, empty.

In the same motion, her string lashes upward, sewing itself into the flickering light fixture, the grating, the seams between concrete slabs—anchoring points, escape routes, traps.

Makima watches, captivated. The guards finally move, but Makima lifts one finger and they stop mid-step, their bodies obeying like a command to sit.

Makima’s voice remains soft. “Running is pointless.”

Jolyne’s string gathers, twisting into a tight braid that hums with tension. She doesn’t run yet. She stares at Makima from a dozen angles at once, her presence distributed across the corridor like a web.

“Listen to me,” Jolyne says, voice coming from the wall, the ceiling, the air itself. “You’re not gonna touch my head. You’re not gonna put a leash on me.”

Makima’s smile thins for the first time, not with anger but with something like appraisal. “You believe you’re equal to me.”

“I believe you’re full of shit,” Jolyne spits.

The overhead light flickers again. In the darkness, Makima speaks, and her words land with quiet certainty.

“Jolyne Cujoh,” she says, and the way she says the name feels like fingers closing around a throat. “Come here.”

For a heartbeat, Jolyne’s strings twitch toward Makima without permission.

Jolyne’s eyes widen—wherever her eyes are right now. Her whole body tries to obey, dragged by a command that bypasses muscle and goes straight for the part of her that decides.

She digs in.

She anchors more string into the concrete, into the bolts, into the doorframe, pulling against the invisible pull with everything she is. The corridor groans. Hairline cracks spider across the wall.

Makima watches, fascinated, as if Jolyne is an animal showing unexpected bite.

“You can resist,” Makima murmurs. “How rare.”

Jolyne snarls, voice shaking with effort. “How about this? Go to hell.”

Makima steps forward anyway, and the leashed figures behind the grate shift, ready to be spent.

Jolyne realizes, with a cold clarity that cuts through her anger, that this isn’t just a fight. It’s a negotiation with someone who doesn’t recognize consent as real. Someone who will keep smiling while she empties a room of bodies to make a point.

The light flickers. The corridor becomes a strobe-lit battleground of string and control, of concrete cracking under unseen pressure.

And in the on-off rhythm of that failing bulb, Jolyne understands the danger perfectly:

Makima isn’t trying to kill her.

Makima is trying to *own* her.
