Caleb found the watch in the lost-and-found bin like it had been waiting for him.

It wasn’t supposed to be there—he knew the bin. It was usually full of lonely mittens and half-smashed lunchboxes, with the occasional hoodie that smelled like gym class. But this thing sat on top like a prize: a black smart watch with a glass face so smooth it didn’t even show fingerprints.

“Hey,” Caleb said, because talking to objects was normal when you were alone in the hallway outside the main office.

He slipped it on.

The band tightened by itself. Not painfully—just snug, like it had measured him. The screen flickered to life.

**WELCOME BACK, CALEB M.**

Caleb blinked. “Back? I’ve never—”

A little icon spun in the corner. Under it, tiny words crawled across the bottom:

**REVISION MODE: READY**

He turned his wrist. The words stayed there, as if the glass was a window into something deeper than electronics.

“Caleb?” Mrs. Darnell’s voice floated from inside the office. “Are you still out there? You said you lost your planner.”

Caleb snapped his arm down, suddenly guilty. “Uh—yeah.”

He took one step toward the door and smacked his shin on the metal leg of a chair someone had left in the hall.

Pain exploded up his leg. Caleb yelped and hopped in place, grabbing at the chair like it had attacked him.

The watch buzzed. A single word appeared:

**REWIND?**

Two buttons glowed beneath it. **YES** and **NO.**

Caleb stared.

The pain throbbed. His eyes watered. And in the office, Mrs. Darnell’s footsteps were getting closer.

His thumb hit **YES**.

The hallway lurched.

Not like he was falling—like everything else was falling backwards. The fluorescent lights blurred into stripes. The air became thick, like walking through a pool.

And then—

Caleb stood in the exact spot he’d been in before, the chair leg safely a foot away from his shin.

His leg didn’t hurt.

His mouth was open, but no sound came out.

The office door opened. Mrs. Darnell poked her head out. “Caleb? You still there?”

Caleb swallowed. “Yes,” he said, voice weirdly thin. “Sorry. I just—uh—forgot what I was doing.”

Mrs. Darnell gave him a look that said *middle schoolers* and retreated.

Caleb lifted his wrist slowly.

The screen showed a tiny clock icon with a number beside it.

**REVISIONS: 1**

Below that:

**COST: 00:03:12**

“What cost?” Caleb whispered.

The words faded. The watch face returned to normal, showing the time and a heart-rate meter that read a little higher than usual.

Caleb didn’t take it off. He should have. He knew he should have. But it was like his skin had decided the watch belonged there.

By lunch, Caleb had used the watch three more times.

Once when he dropped his milk and it splashed all over Amelia Tran’s white sneakers (rewind, catch carton, act casual). Once when Coach Harper called on him in gym to demonstrate a layup and he threw the basketball straight into the bleachers (rewind, fake an ankle cramp). Once when he accidentally sent a meme to the class group chat that was definitely supposed to go to Ben only (rewind, delete, breathe).

Each time, the world snapped back like a rubber band.

Each time, it felt easier.

The watch kept count. **REVISIONS: 4.**

And each time, there was that little line:

**COST: 00:—**

The numbers changed too fast for him to read.

On his way to last period, Caleb cut across the side of the building where the dumpsters were. The teachers said not to, but it was faster, and you didn’t have to pass the eighth graders who liked to shoulder-check people for no reason.

A cat sat on the cracked sidewalk near the chain-link fence.

Not a school cat—Caleb had seen the usual orange tom before. This one was gray, scruffy, with one ear nicked like it had survived something sharp.

It watched him like it had been waiting.

Caleb slowed. “Hey.”

The cat’s eyes were yellow-green and too steady. It didn’t blink. It tilted its head, just slightly, like it was trying to fit Caleb into a memory.

Caleb took another step, and the cat’s tail flicked once—impatient.

“You want something?” Caleb asked. “I don’t have food.”

The watch buzzed softly against his wrist, like it was listening too.

The cat’s gaze dropped to the watch.

Then back to Caleb’s face.

Caleb felt a chill crawl up his arms.

He hurried past.

In the bathroom after school, Caleb finally noticed something he couldn’t rewind away.

He washed his hands, leaned toward the mirror, and froze.

His face looked… off.

Not like he’d woken up with a pimple or gotten a bad haircut.

Older.

His chin looked sharper. His cheeks less round. His eyebrows seemed thicker, darker. There was a shadow above his lip that hadn’t been there this morning.

Caleb lifted his wrist to his mouth and rubbed. The shadow didn’t smear. It was *him.*

He stared at his reflection until the fluorescent lights hummed too loudly.

“Okay,” he whispered. “Okay, puberty. Great timing.”

He said it like a joke, but his stomach didn’t laugh.

When he got home, Mom paused at the front door like she’d walked into the wrong house.

“Caleb?” she said slowly. “Did you… did you do something to your hair?”

Caleb’s hand went to his head. “No.”

Mom leaned closer, squinting. “You look… taller.”

Caleb tried to smile. “Maybe I’m growing. Like you said.”

Mom’s eyes flicked to his wrist. “Where did you get that watch? I told you—no expensive stuff from other kids.”

“It was lost and found,” Caleb said too fast. “It’s probably cheap.”

Mom didn’t look convinced, but she let it go in that way adults sometimes did when they were tired and you were almost old enough to be a problem.

At dinner, Dad stared at him over his plate.

“What?” Caleb said, mouth full.

Dad frowned. “Your voice cracked.”

“It did not.”

It did. Even as he said it, the words came out lower, rougher.

Caleb went to his room and slammed the door a little harder than he meant to.

The watch screen lit up on its own.

**REVISIONS: 4**

**TOTAL COST: 00:17:46**

Seventeen minutes didn’t explain a mustache shadow and a voice drop.

Unless the watch wasn’t taking minutes.

Unless it was taking something else.

Caleb swallowed. He pulled up his sleeve and stared at his forearm.

Fine hairs stood thicker than usual. His wrist looked less like a kid’s wrist.

He imagined using the watch again, and suddenly the numbers weren’t cool anymore.

They were teeth.

The next morning, Caleb stood in front of his locker and tried to act normal.

Normal meant ignoring the fact that his jeans felt tighter in the thighs and his shirt pulled across his shoulders like he’d put it in the dryer too long.

Normal meant not noticing the way people looked at him like they weren’t sure he belonged in the seventh-grade hallway.

A pair of sixth graders walked past and one of them whispered, “Is that an eighth grader?”

Caleb flinched. He turned, but they were already gone.

Ben jogged up, panting. “Dude. Where were you? I texted you like ten times.”

Caleb pulled out his phone. Ten notifications.

“I didn’t—” Caleb started.

Ben stopped mid-sentence. His eyes went wide. “Whoa. Why do you look like… like my cousin Ethan?”

“What?” Caleb snapped, too sharp.

Ben took a step back. “Okay, sorry. Just—your face. And your voice. Are you sick?”

Caleb’s fingers curled around his locker handle until it hurt.

“I’m fine,” he said, and hated how adult it sounded.

The first disaster happened in science.

Mr. Kline was doing a lab demo with a Bunsen burner. The kind of flame that was pretty until it wasn’t. Caleb sat in the third row, trying not to itch at the hair on his arms that felt wrong.

Mr. Kline held up a beaker. “Remember: we never—*never*—leave open flame unattended.”

Of course, someone laughed.

Of course, it was Mason Greer, who treated rules like they were optional suggestions.

Mr. Kline turned to write something on the board, and Mason leaned forward like he couldn’t help himself.

He flicked a paperclip toward the flame.

Caleb saw it in slow motion. The metal flashed. It hit the beaker. The beaker tipped.

The flame whooshed up, licking the sleeve of Mr. Kline’s lab coat.

For a second, no one moved. It was like the whole class had forgotten what fire was.

Mr. Kline shouted, swatting at his arm. The lab coat caught. The safety shower was across the room.

Caleb’s heart slammed. His watch buzzed hard, almost painful.

**REWIND?**

Caleb didn’t think. He slapped **YES**.

The classroom twisted. Sound turned inside out. The flame folded backward like a ribbon. Mason’s hand snapped back. The paperclip leapt off the floor and into his fingers.

Caleb blinked.

Mr. Kline was holding up the beaker again. “Remember: we never—*never*—leave open flame unattended.”

Caleb’s breath came fast.

He had a chance. A real chance.

He kicked Mason’s chair as hard as he could.

Mason yelped. “What the—”

The paperclip flew—but not toward the beaker.

It pinged off the metal gas valve on the lab table.

There was a click.

A hiss.

Caleb’s stomach dropped.

The smell hit him—sharp and rotten, like eggs left in the sun. Gas.

Mr. Kline turned, frowning. “Who touched the—”

The hiss became a roar.

A tongue of flame shot sideways, not up, not neat.

It crawled along the table like it was looking for something.

Like it was hungry.

Screams erupted. Chairs scraped. Someone knocked over a stool.

Caleb stood frozen while panic broke the room into pieces.

The flame caught the edge of a worksheet, then a sleeve, then—

The overhead lights flickered.

The fire alarm didn’t sound.

The sprinklers didn’t go off.

The smoke curled up, darkening into something thicker, and Caleb realized with a sick twist that this wasn’t just an accident.

It was different now. Worse.

The watch buzzed again. The screen flashed.

**REVISION MODE: UNSTABLE**

**TEMPORAL DRIFT DETECTED**

Caleb’s fingers shook.

At the back of the room, through the small window in the door, he saw something that didn’t belong there.

A gray cat.

Sitting in the hallway like a guard.

Its nicked ear was backlit by the emergency exit sign, its eyes reflecting the firelight.

It stared straight at Caleb.

Not scared.

Not confused.

Recognizing.

Like it had seen this version already.

Caleb raised his wrist, coughing. His vision blurred at the edges.

The watch face showed the number again, but it had changed.

**REVISIONS: 5**

**TOTAL COST: 03:11:04**

Three hours?

Caleb’s skin prickled. He felt it then—his body, suddenly too heavy, his bones aching like they’d been stretched. His throat tightened, and his voice, when it came, sounded like it belonged to someone older than seventh grade.

“Everyone out!” he croaked.

A few kids looked at him, and for a terrifying second, they actually listened. Like he was a teacher.

Smoke rolled along the ceiling. Mr. Kline fumbled with the fire extinguisher, but his hands were shaking. The flame leapt again, greedy.

Caleb backed toward the door, eyes locked on the cat.

The cat blinked once, slow.

Then it looked at Caleb’s watch and made a sound that wasn’t quite a meow.

More like a warning.

Caleb’s thumb hovered over the watch.

If he rewound again, he could undo the paperclip. He could stop himself from kicking Mason’s chair. He could stop the whole thing.

But what would it take?

A day? A year?

Would he come back looking like Dad?

Would he come back not at all?

And if he *didn’t*… the smoke was getting thicker, and the room was filling with a timeline he had made wrong.

Caleb swallowed ash and fear.

The watch buzzed like a heartbeat.

**REWIND?**

Outside the door, the gray cat sat perfectly still, eyes unblinking, as if it was waiting to see what Caleb would choose.

Caleb’s thumb pressed down.

And the world began to fall backward again.
