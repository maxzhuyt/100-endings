The Court of the Dreaming convened as it always did: in a room that looked like a courtroom because everyone expected a courtroom to look like that, and because expectations were a kind of mortar in this place.

The vaulted ceiling disappeared into dimness. Pillars rose like frozen smoke. The air smelled faintly of old paper and storm-wet earth. The benches were filled with the usual gallery of witnesses who did not, strictly speaking, have lungs: a cluster of half-remembered nursery rhymes; a trio of recurring teeth-falling-out dreams in ill-fitting suits; one anxious office hallway that kept stretching itself longer whenever anyone looked away.

At the center sat Dream.

He wore his impenetrability like a robe. Today it sat a little crooked.

Before him, a lectern of polished black wood waited to be struck by a gavel. Dream did not use a gavel. The gavel was for people who needed wood to feel authoritative.

A quill hovered above a ledger, scratching by itself. The ledger’s title read: TERRITORIAL DISPUTES, DREAMSCAPES, MINOR HUMANS. Underneath it, someone—no one—had doodled a small skull with a heart in its eye socket.

Dream’s gaze moved, and the room quieted as if it had been told not to dream too loudly.

“Bring the parties,” Dream said, and somehow it sounded like a sigh that had been given legal standing.

First Love stepped forward first, cheeks rosy, hands clasped as if he had been asked to recite a poem in front of the class. He looked like he belonged in the bright edge of a memory: soft hair, hopeful eyes, the faint scent of wildflowers and embarrassment.

He bowed too deeply. Straightened. Bowed again, as if unsure which one had been the real bow.

On the other side, Mora drifted into place like a bruise forming. She did not so much walk as settle, as if gravity were an opinion she was willing to humor for now. A shawl of shadow hung from her shoulders, stitched through with something that looked uncomfortably like dried frost.

When she smiled, the room grew a degree colder and a degree more intimate.

A faint cackle escaped her—small, delighted, and wrong—and it bounced along the vaulted ceiling, echoing in places where no sound should ever go.

Dream watched that echo with a look that suggested he was writing its behavior down for later use.

“Case,” Dream said, “of the contested dreamscape of one Max—human, schoolboy, age irrelevant in this Court—filed by First Love against Mora, nightmare spirit, for invasion and subversion of a recurring dream.”

A pause.

“In plain terms,” Dream added, because the Court sometimes benefited from plain terms, “someone has been tampering with a boy’s meadow.”

First Love raised a hand halfway, then realized he was not in school, then raised it again anyway with a little tremor of habit.

“Yes, Your—” He hesitated on the appropriate title and chose, with desperation, “Your Dream.”

“That will do,” Dream said, with a faint, suffering patience. “Present your claim.”

First Love cleared his throat. The sound was surprisingly brave for something so gentle.

“Max’s recurring dream,” he said, “was a carefully cultivated narrative of formative affection. A field of wildflowers. Warm sun. A girl he likes. The setting repeats to create safety. The emotional crescendo always culminates in a muddled almost-kiss—”

From the gallery, one of the teeth-falling-out dreams coughed politely, as if to say, *classic*.

“—and then he wakes,” First Love continued, gaining momentum, “pleasantly confused. Heart racing, but in a way that makes him write her name in the margins of his notebooks.”

Dream’s eyes flicked down to his ledger. The quill wrote *MARGINS: APPROVED*.

First Love gestured, and the air to Dream’s right shimmered. A window opened into the dreamscape in question: a meadow of wildflowers, bright as a promise; a boy on his back, blinking into a sun that did not burn; a girl leaning close, laughing softly, her hair falling like an answer.

Then the scene stuttered, as if someone had thumbed the page too hard.

The girl climbed astride the boy with playful confidence. The boy’s hands lifted, unsure where to go, his face reddening.

And then her face shifted.

Not entirely. Enough. Her eyes deepened into something old. Her smile sharpened. The laugh became a faint cackle that echoed even here, as if the meadow had grown a vaulted ceiling of its own.

She sat down on his chest with a weight that turned flirtation into paralysis. The boy’s breath caught. His eyes widened. He tried to inhale and found the air had suddenly been made reluctant.

Mora’s shadow-self leaned close and drank, slow as a kiss and hungry as a grave.

The window snapped shut like a mouth.

First Love stood with his hands clenched, as if trying not to cry in court was a matter of legal decorum.

“This,” he said, voice tight, “is not merely an edit. It is a hostile takeover.”

Mora tilted her head, listening to him the way a cat listens to a mouse explain property law.

“A hostile takeover,” she repeated, tasting the phrase. “So dramatic. I call it… diversification.”

Dream’s gaze shifted to Mora. “Your response.”

Mora took a step forward, and the light in the Court forgot how to behave. Her faint cackles continued, small punctuations in the silence, echoing uncomfortably through the vaults as if the room itself were trying to laugh and failing.

“Your Dream,” she said, with a voice like lullabies sung too low, “I did not *invade.* I visited. The boy is already at an age where his dreams are… complicated. His body is learning new fears. New hungers. New confusions.”

First Love made a small strangled noise of outrage.

Mora spread her hands. “And frankly? A girl’s gotta eat too, you know?”

That line landed in the Court with the soft thud of something indecent being made practical.

From the gallery, a recurring dream of being late to an exam muttered, “We’re all just trying to get by.”

Dream did not look at the gallery. Dream did not have to. The gallery quieted anyway, as if chastened by an invisible glare.

Dream’s fingers steepled. “You claim necessity.”

Mora shrugged, a ripple of darkness. “I claim reality. Children do not stay children. The dream was… sweet. Too sweet. Like a pastry left out. I merely added a pinch of salt.”

“A pinch?” First Love snapped, then visibly tried to soften his tone. “She turned it into—into—”

“A rite,” Mora offered.

“A violation,” First Love corrected, and his cheeks went even pinker, though whether from anger or humiliation was unclear. “That dream was mine by craft. By investment. By repetition. It was stable. He trusted it.”

Mora’s smile held a kind of fondness that made it worse. “Trust is such a delicate thing. I admire it. I break it. We all have hobbies.”

Dream leaned back, and the Court leaned with him.

“Ownership,” Dream said, “in the Dreaming, is not decided by claim alone. It is decided by function. By the shape it makes in the mortal mind.”

He looked down, and the ledger’s pages turned themselves, landing on a section labeled: PRECEDENTS (UNPLEASANT).

“First Love,” Dream said, “your dream offered anticipation. Uncertainty. Desire withheld. It served the human’s development.”

First Love nodded urgently, like someone agreeing with the teacher in hopes of being spared further questions.

Dream turned to Mora. “Your alteration introduces paralysis, terror, the intimate confusion of attraction mixed with fear. It also… feeds you.”

Mora’s eyes glittered. Her faint cackle climbed the pillars and came back down sounding like a verdict trying on different tones.

“Yes,” she admitted. “It is efficient.”

“And,” Dream continued, with the smallest hint of irritation, “you chose the moment when the girl mounts him.”

Mora’s expression was innocent enough to be obscene. “Where else would I sit?”

First Love spluttered.

Dream’s eyes narrowed. “Do not answer that.”

The quill wrote *DO NOT ANSWER THAT*.

A long silence settled, heavy as sleep.

Dream’s voice, when it came, was level. “This Court recognizes that nightmares have their place. But it also recognizes that recurring dreams are territorial. Their repetition creates a boundary.”

Mora’s cackle softened, as if she were laughing into a pillow on someone else’s face.

“However,” Dream said, and something in that single word made the room pay closer attention, “the boundary in question is… porous. The boy is at the cusp of adolescence. His mind is building new rooms. New locks. New doors.”

First Love’s hands tightened. “So she’s allowed to just—just—walk in?”

“She did not *walk,*” Mora murmured. “I floated. It’s a talent.”

Dream looked very briefly like someone who wanted to banish both of them to a nightmare about paperwork.

Instead, he tapped one finger against the arm of his chair. No gavel. Just a finger. The sound was quiet.

The Court held its breath on Max’s behalf.

“I will not award this dreamscape exclusively,” Dream said. “Not to you, First Love, and not to you, Mora.”

First Love stared, stricken. Mora’s smile widened, hungry.

Dream continued, “The dream will be partitioned.”

“Partitioned,” Mora repeated, amused. “How bureaucratic.”

“You will each be given jurisdiction,” Dream said, voice dry, “at different phases of the dream.”

First Love’s eyes brightened with fragile hope. “I— I get the meadow.”

“You get the meadow,” Dream agreed. “The flowers. The warmth. The approach. The laughter. The almost-kiss.”

Mora’s gaze sharpened. “And I get—”

Dream looked at her, unimpressed.

“You get,” Dream said, “the waking.”

The Court blinked.

Mora’s smile faltered for the first time. “The—”

“The moment after,” Dream said. “When he sits up in bed. When his heart pounds. When his breath returns. When his mind tries to name what he felt and cannot.”

Mora opened her mouth.

Dream lifted a hand, and her protest stopped as if it had hit glass.

“You will be permitted,” Dream went on, “to haunt the edges. A chill in the room. A shadow at the corner. A brief pressure—brief—on the chest, no longer than a mortal blink. Enough to remind him that desire has teeth, but not enough to feed you a meal.”

Mora’s faint cackles became thin, irritated little sparks that echoed uncomfortably, like laughter trapped in a coffin.

“And,” Dream added, as if he were reading from a clause he had just invented, “you will not use the girl’s face.”

Mora’s eyes narrowed. “But it’s such a good face.”

“Irrelevant,” Dream said. “Choose something else. A shape less… litigious.”

First Love let out a breath he had apparently been holding since the beginning of puberty.

Mora crossed her arms. “So I starve.”

Dream’s expression returned to its usual mask. Only the edges of his voice betrayed that he was enjoying this a little more than he should.

“You will not starve,” Dream said. “Humans produce plenty of fear without your assistance. And if you must feed, you may do so—elsewhere. Not in this particular meadow.”

He paused, and the pause felt like a page turning.

“And,” Dream said, “to satisfy the Court’s interest in balance… there will be an addition.”

First Love froze. Mora leaned forward, intrigued despite herself.

Dream’s eyes glinted like ink.

“In the meadow,” Dream said, “there will now be a witness.”

“A witness?” First Love repeated.

“A small one,” Dream said. “A harmless one. A creature of the Dreaming tasked with observing compliance.”

Mora’s cackle returned, suspicious. “What kind of creature?”

Dream’s gaze drifted upward, as if choosing from a shelf of petty mischief.

“A goose,” Dream said.

The Court went silent in the way only absurdity can command.

“A goose,” Mora repeated, slowly.

“Yes,” Dream said. “A goose in the wildflowers. Watching. Judging. Honking, if necessary.”

First Love looked as if he did not know whether to laugh or weep. “Your Dream… why?”

Dream’s irritation slipped through, thin and sharp. “Because,” he said, “I am tired of adjudicating the romantic lives of schoolboys.”

Mora’s faint cackles echoed high in the vaulted ceiling, but there was something offended in them now, as if even she recognized the indignity of being policed by a goose.

Dream leaned forward.

“This Court is adjourned,” he said. “First Love, tend your meadow. Mora, mind your blink. And if either of you attempts to circumvent this judgment—”

His eyes, dark as an unlit room, moved from one to the other.

“—I will assign you both,” Dream finished, “to an eternity of dreams where you are perpetually about to speak, and someone keeps raising their hand to ask an unrelated question.”

The gallery shuddered. The dream of being late to an exam crossed itself.

First Love bowed, deeply, grateful. Mora smiled, thinly, already plotting.

As they turned to leave, Mora’s cackle followed them, faint and echoing, threading through the pillars like smoke.

Dream watched them go. For a moment, his expression softened into something almost like satisfaction.

Then, very quietly, he leaned toward the quill and murmured a final note into the ledger.

In Max’s meadow, the goose would not only observe.

It would be wearing a tiny powdered wig.
