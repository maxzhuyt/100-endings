The first thing I clock in Studio C is the silence between counts.

Not real silence—there’s bass rumbling through the sprung floor, air vents hissing—but the kind that makes every mistake loud. It sits in the space like a second instructor, watching.

Across the far wall, above the mirrors, a digital clock burns red: **02:57:41**. The colon blinks like a pulse. The numbers don’t care who you are. They just eat minutes.

I stand on a strip of gray tape that’s been laid on the floor like a grid: A, B, C, D across; 1, 2, 3, 4 down. I’m on **B2**, second row from the mirror, one square left of center. They’re already arranged in a clean rectangle: twelve trainees, three rows of four, each person exactly an arm’s length apart. Their sneakers line up on the tape edges like they were measured with a ruler.

Me, I’m still learning how to land inside the lines.

My reflection looks like I got dropped into somebody else’s life—baggy sweats in a room full of fitted practice gear, hair tied back tight, no jewelry, no loose ends. Everyone’s got that academy posture: neck long, chin level, shoulders down like they’ve been ironed.

Instructor Han stands at the front, between us and the mirror, so we see him twice—his back in real life, his face in the glass. He doesn’t need to raise his voice. When he speaks, it snaps right into our bones.

“From the top. Eight counts intro. Then chorus. Clean. Again.”

He taps the remote. The track starts with a metronome click buried under synth. It’s not a beat you melt into. It’s a beat you obey.

We’re facing the mirror—north. My right side is east, left is west. The formation is set so each of us can slide without colliding. I’ve been drilled on spacing all week, but my body still wants to cheat into circles.

“Five, six, seven, eight—”

On **1**, we hit a sharp prep: feet parallel under hips, knees soft, fists at hip bones, elbows back like hinges. I do it half a hair late because my shoulders want to roll through it, add sauce, make it mine. Han’s eyes catch the delay in the mirror like a hook.

On **2**, we step out wide to second position—right foot to the east edge of B2, left foot stays planted on the west edge—so my stance spans the whole tape square. Arms shoot to a low V, wrists straight. The girl at **C2** matches me perfectly; we look like reflections. It makes my chest tighten, like I’m being erased into symmetry.

On **3-and**, we hit a chest pop forward then recoil. Commercial pop—clean isolation, no extra ripple. My chest does the pop, but my ribs want to keep talking, roll down into a wave. Muscle memory from battles: if the music gives you space, you fill it. Here, space is a crime.

On **4**, we pivot a quarter turn to face east, weight over the right foot, left heel lifted. Both arms cross at the forearms in front of the chest, like closing a door. My left heel lifts too high—habit from top rock, where you show the heel, flirt with the floor. Han’s head tilts a millimeter.

“Again,” he says, but he doesn’t stop the music. He makes the correction land on top of the counts, like punishment layered in rhythm.

We keep going.

On **5**, a sharp step-together: left foot snaps in to meet right. Hands open, palms down, flick out at shoulder height. I flick with my fingers first, then wrist, then elbow—too fluid. The others flick like a switch: on/off.

On **6**, we drop into a low plié—knees push over toes, back straight, pelvis tucked. Street would let you sit into it, let the groove settle. This is a controlled descent, like an elevator stopping exactly at the right floor. My thighs burn because I’m holding the shape, not bouncing through it.

On **7-and-8**, we travel: two quick chassé steps to the right—east—landing me on **C2** by the end of **8**. That means the trainee who was at C2 has to travel too; she slides to **D2**. It’s a clean lateral shift across the grid, no drifting forward. My sneakers squeak because I push off the floor like I’m chasing a circle, not cutting a line.

The clock blinks: **02:56:58**.

Chorus hits. This is where the academy choreography turns into machinery. Every move is built to be seen from ten rows back, to look identical in twelve bodies, to read as a single creature.

On **1**, we all face front again—north—by snapping a half turn left from facing east. Feet land shoulder-width. Right arm punches diagonally up to the northeast corner, left hand clamps at the left hip. No wobble.

My arm punches, but my shoulder follows through, wants to swing. In my neighborhood, you punch a diagonal and let it ride into a groove, let your torso twist. Here the twist is measured: exactly forty-five degrees, then stop. My stop is late by a fraction. It feels like tripping on invisible curb.

On **2**, right arm retracts to the chest, elbow tight, like reloading. Left arm shoots out straight to the west, palm down. Head snaps to follow the left hand. Snap, not turn.

My head snaps, but my neck tries to roll to the beat, that sweet lag that makes things look effortless. Han hates effortless. He wants exact.

On **3**, we do a heel-toe swivel: right heel in, left toe in, making the knees knock inward, then pop out on **3-and**. It’s a classic K-pop texture—tight, readable footwork. My feet know different games: I can do a six-step blindfolded, thread my legs through space like rope. But heel-toe swivels? My ankles argue. I over-rotate, my right foot slides off the tape line by two inches into the gap between C2 and C3.

That two inches is a canyon. The trainee behind me at **C3** is supposed to step forward on **4** into the space my heel just invaded. I see her in the mirror, eyes flicking down. She adjusts mid-count—tiny—but I’ve broken the grid.

Han’s hand lifts. Not to stop the music. To mark me.

On **4**, we all step forward one square—north—right foot first. From C2 to C1. Hands slice down like chopping air at hip level, elbows locked.

I correct fast, pulling my right foot back onto line before stepping forward. But the correction is its own movement, and it shows. Like a stutter.

On **5**, we hit a body roll—but not the kind I love. It’s segmented: chest forward, ribs down, hips tuck. Three stops inside one wave. A wave with teeth.

My body tries to pour it smooth. It’s in me, that liquid spine. My chest goes, ribs go, hips go—but I smear the stops. It looks good in a cypher. Here it reads as sloppy.

On **6-and-7**, we do a canon in place: right shoulder hits up, then left shoulder, then both drop. The timing is surgical: **6** right, **and** left, **7** both. I hit right on 6, left late on the and, and then my both-drop lands on **7-and**. One half-beat off. In the mirror, it’s like my body is subtitles out of sync.

Han finally kills the music on the downbeat, slicing it off mid-air. The sudden quiet makes my breathing sound huge.

The clock: **02:55:44**.

“B2—no, C2,” he says, eyes on me, not asking, correcting my existence. “You are dragging. You are decorating. This is not a battle.”

My stomach clenches at the word. Battle is the first place I ever mattered. Battle is where nobody cares if your shoes are cheap as long as your rhythm is rich.

“I’m trying,” I say, and it comes out rough, like I’m still on my block and not in this polished box.

Han walks toward us, steps measured, stopping exactly one tape square in front of me, on **C1**. He’s close enough that in the mirror his face and mine overlap, like a double exposure: his calm, my heat.

He points at my chest, not touching. “Your talent is here. Raw. But your attitude is louder than your counts.”

Behind me, twelve bodies stand frozen in formation, hands at sides, eyes forward. Nobody looks at me directly. In the mirror, I see their peripheral vision pretending not to see.

Class anxiety hits like a cramp. Back home, being loud keeps you safe. Here, loud gets you cut.

Han turns, walks back to the front, and claps once. “We drill the chorus eight times. No music first. Only counts. You will be early, not late.”

He starts counting. “One.”

On **1**, punch diagonal. I punch. I lock my elbow. I freeze the shoulder. It feels like holding my breath.

“Two.”

Retract, shoot left, head snap. I snap. My neck hates it, but it obeys. The movement loses warmth, gains clarity. It feels like trading blood for glass.

“Three-and.”

Heel-toe swivel. I slow it down in my head: right heel in, left toe in, then push out. I keep my feet on the tape. Ankles shake, but I keep them. I hear my sneakers whisper against the floor like an apology.

“Four.”

Step forward into C1. Chop hands. I land exactly in the square. The girl behind steps into C2 without having to dodge me. The grid holds.

We repeat.

By the fourth round, my muscles start to memorize the prison. It’s scary how fast the body learns cages. My old instincts begin to fade under repetition, like chalk being rubbed off a sidewalk.

On the sixth round, Han finally turns the music back on. The beat floods the room, and my heart leaps—music is home, even foreign music. The synth line opens a pocket on the **and** of **2**, begging for a groove, a little bounce, a small shoulder roll to make it breathe.

My body answers before my brain can stop it. On **2-and**, instead of holding the clean retract, my chest sinks a fraction and rebounds—just a hint of groove, a ghost of my cypher self. It feels so good it hurts. Like saying my own name after being called a number.

In the mirror, the formation stays perfect—except me. That tiny bounce makes my punch on **3** land a hair late. Hair late becomes visible in a room trained to see hairlines.

Han’s count cuts through the track. “Stop.”

The music dies again. The silence slams.

He walks toward me, slower this time. Not angry. Interested, like a mechanic looking at an engine that keeps trying to become an animal.

“You can make it feel,” he says quietly. “That is why you are here.” His eyes lift to the clock—**02:53:12**—then back to me. “But if you cannot submit to precision, you will never debut. You will be a person with talent and no place.”

No place. That word lands like my neighborhood did when rent went up and people started leaving—like the ground moving under your feet.

I look at my square on the floor, at the tape lines that say where I’m allowed to exist. I look at the other trainees, their faces unreadable, their bodies ready, already remade.

My lungs pull in air that smells like clean wood and effort. My palms itch to freestyle, to throw a top rock into this chorus and prove I’m not a robot. To rebel and keep my voice even if it costs me everything.

But I also see my mom’s hands holding the scholarship letter like it was fragile glass. I see the number on the clock, red and relentless, counting down a future that doesn’t wait for pride.

Han steps back to the front without another word. He raises his hand.

“From the chorus. Music. This time: same shapes. Same timing. You may add feeling only inside the shape.”

Only inside the shape.

The track starts. The metronome click is a chain.

We hit **1**—punch diagonal. I lock it.

On **2**, retract and shoot. I snap.

On **2-and**, the pocket opens again. I make a choice so small nobody would call it rebellion: I don’t bounce out of time. I don’t smear the count. I put the feeling in my breath instead—exhale sharp on **and**, let my eyes burn into the mirror like the movement means something, even if it’s clean.

On **3-and**, heel-toe, in-out. Feet stay on tape.

On **4**, step forward. Hands chop. Formation holds.

I can feel my old self pressing against the choreography like water behind a dam. Maybe I can learn to be precise without becoming empty. Maybe I can hide a river inside a straight line.

The clock keeps eating minutes: **02:52:47**.

Han’s voice rides over the beat. “Again.”

And my body, caught between the block and this bright machine, moves—trying to fit without disappearing.
