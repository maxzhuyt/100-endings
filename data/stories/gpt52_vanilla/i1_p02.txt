**Letter — 3 March 1911**  
Rubha Bàn Lighthouse, Sound of Jura  
My dear Ma,

I’m here safe, after a rough crossing from Oban that had the skipper swearing and me turning the colour of porridge water. The lighthouse sits on a low point of rock with the sea on three sides and the wind on the fourth. They call the headland Rubha Bàn—White Point—though there’s not much white in it but the foam. To the west you can see the Paps of Jura when the cloud lifts, three lumps like knuckles on a fist.

The quarters are clean enough, all stone and lime and echo. The lamp room smells of oil and brass. Mr. Haldane, the principal keeper, is a man that speaks only when it saves him labour. He has a fine beard and a habit of looking past you as if you’re a gull and not worth feeding.

I’ve been set to polishing until I’ve seen my own face in the fittings and not liked it. There’s a bell buoy out in the Sound that moans all night and makes you think someone’s crying in their sleep.

Write back, Ma. It’s queer to have no street sounds, no folk, only the sea making a mouth at the rocks.

Your son,  
**Euan**

---

**Letter — 16 March 1911**  
48 Saltmarket, Glasgow  
My Euan,

Your letter came with salt on it, or else I imagined it. I’m glad you’re landed and not at the bottom like your father’s cousin, God rest him. I mind the Paps from a picture on a biscuit tin. Don’t you be staring at them like a lass at a window. You’ve work to do.

I’m still at McNair’s, mending shirts for men that never wash. Mrs. McNair says my stitches are steady. I told her I learned it keeping you in trousers when you grew like a weed.

Eat what they give you and don’t take to the drink out of boredom. Your father did and it took his good temper first and the rest after. When you feel the loneliness, say the psalm I taught you, even if you don’t believe half of it.

I’ll write again when I’ve put aside a bit for your boots.

Your Ma,  
**Isobel**

**Letter — 2 April 1911**  
Rubha Bàn Lighthouse  
Ma,

The days are lengthening and the light comes in early, so you wake up thinking you’ve slept in and it’s only the sun making a fool of you. Haldane keeps a book where we note the weather, and he makes me do it in a neat hand. “If the sea takes us, the book will float,” he said, like it was a joke.

I’ve taken to walking the path down to the little shingle bay when I’m off watch. There’s a cleft in the rock with fresh water pooling, and in it tiny shrimp that flash like bits of copper. Yesterday I saw an otter, slick as an eel, and it stared at me like I was the odd creature.

The hard part isn’t the work. It’s the talking. There’s no one to say nonsense to. You don’t realise how much of a day is filled with small talk until it’s all gone. Haldane reads old papers and mutters at them. I tried to tell him about the otter and he said, “Aye,” as if I’d told him the sky was up.

Do you ever feel like that, Ma? Like your thoughts have no place to land?

Your son,  
Euan

---

**Letter — 19 April 1911**  
Glasgow  
Euan,

Don’t you go making a poem out of it. Thoughts land where you put them. If you’ve no one to speak to, you speak to God or you speak to your hands and keep them busy.

I mind being a girl on Barra, before Glasgow and before your father. There was a rock out beyond the house we called The Minister because it sat alone and looked stern. I used to climb it and shout my secrets at the sea. The sea never told anyone. That was its one virtue.

You’ve always had too much inside you. It’s not a sin, but it will make you restless. Write it down. Put it in your letters if you must. I’ll read it.

And Euan—do not be taking risks on those rocks. We’ve had wind like knives here, and if it can cut through these streets it can cut you clean in half.

Your Ma

**Letter — 10 May 1911**  
Rubha Bàn Lighthouse  
Ma,

I sent the telegram so you wouldn’t hear it from someone else and fret. A gale came down the Sound and made the whole tower tremble like a horse with flies. You could feel it through your boots. The lantern glass sang. Haldane was calm, I’ll give him that. He strapped the vents, checked the wicks, and told me to keep my fingers away from the gears.

But after, when the worst passed, I went down to the rocks and saw a sheep from some croft across the water smashed against the kelp line. Its wool was full of shells. I stood there longer than I should. It’s a hard thing, seeing what the sea does when it’s in a temper.

There’s a steamer went through last week, close enough to see men on deck. They waved. I waved back like an idiot, my heart running after them.

I’ve been dreaming of our close in Glasgow, of the smell of coal and frying. I used to complain about it. I’d take it now like medicine.

Your son,  
Euan

---

**Letter — 3 June 1911**  
Glasgow  
Euan,

You waved because you’ve manners. Don’t be ashamed of it. A man that won’t wave is a man that thinks himself above folk, and I’ve no time for that.

There’s a boy in the next stair, Hamish, taken on at the shipyard. He came home with his hands raw and his chest up like he’d swallowed a trumpet. Working men are all the same; pain makes them proud because it’s all they’ve got to show.

I’ve been thinking on what you wrote about the sheep. Don’t let the sea make you hard, but don’t let it make you soft either. It doesn’t care what you are.

I bought a small tin of tea with the pennies I saved. I’ll send it if the post will take parcels to the ends of the earth.

Keep writing. Your letters make the room feel less empty.

Your Ma

---

**Letter — 28 July 1911**  
Rubha Bàn Lighthouse  
Ma,

The tea came. I swear I could smell Glasgow in it—smoke and shop counters and you. Haldane took a cup and said, “That’s decent,” which is as near as he comes to praising anything.

There’s something I’ve not told you because I didn’t want you worrying. The isolation’s gotten into me. Not in a grand way, just in the small daily way. Some mornings I wake and my chest feels packed with wool, like I can’t draw a full breath. I do the tasks, I speak when I must, but there’s a heaviness. Even the birds seem to mock you, always in company.

Last week I heard a voice calling my name out over the water. Clear as if someone was on the path. I ran out like a fool and there was no one. Haldane said it was the wind in the guy wires, that it makes words if your head is full of them. He looked at me sharper than usual and asked if I was sleeping.

I am sleeping, mostly. I just—Ma, I don’t want to go strange here. I’m not my father. I don’t want drink in me or anger. But sometimes the quiet feels like a weight on the tongue.

Write back quick.

Euan

---

**Letter — 12 August 1911**  
Glasgow  
My dear boy,

Listen to me. You are not going strange. You are lonely. There’s a difference, and it matters.

Your father’s trouble wasn’t only drink. It was that he would not speak. He kept his worries like coins in a pocket until they tore a hole. You are speaking. You are writing. That’s you saving yourself, do you hear?

When you feel the heaviness, do a thing with your body. Scrub the step. Run up and down the stairs if you’ve to. The mind follows the muscles more than folk admit.

And Euan—if you hear your name, answer it out loud. Not because there’s someone, but because you are here. Say, “Aye, I’m here.” It’s a plain thing but plain things hold you.

I went to the kirk and lit a candle though the minister says we’ve no need of such. I needed it.

If it gets worse, you tell Haldane and you tell the Board. Pride is dear but your life is dearer.

Your Ma,  
Isobel

**Letter — 5 October 1911**  
Rubha Bàn Lighthouse  
Ma,

Autumn’s in, and the rain comes sideways. The Paps are hidden most days. The Sound looks like hammered lead. Haldane has been kinder, in his way. He leaves the paper on my bunk when he’s done, as if lending me his thoughts. I think he’s noticed the strain.

Something happened two nights ago. A fishing boat from Craighouse got caught in a squall. We saw her lantern low and erratic, and then nothing. Haldane had me keep the beam steady on the channel while he worked the horn. We could hear shouting, faint, then a crack like a tree splitting. The boat hit the skerries south of the point—there’s a line of teeth-rocks there the charts mark but men forget.

We launched the dinghy. I was terrified, Ma. Not the clean fear of a sudden thing, but the sick fear that makes you want to sit down. Haldane shoved me and said, “Move.” So I moved.

We got two men out of the water and dragged them into the store room, wrapped in blankets. One kept saying his wee brother was still out. Haldane held his shoulders and told him, “He’s gone,” flat as stone. Later, when the men were sent off with the relief boat, I went up to the lantern and I cried where no one could see.

After, Haldane poured me a dram—just one—and said, “You did fine.” It near broke me, those three words. I didn’t know I’d been starving to hear them.

I’m writing this with my hands still smelling of tar and seaweed. I keep thinking of you telling me to answer my name. That night, in the dark, when Haldane shouted “Euan!” I answered. Loud. And I was there.

Your son,  
Euan

---

**Letter — 20 October 1911**  
Glasgow  
Euan,

I read your letter twice and then I sat a long while. I’m sorry for the one that was lost, and I’m proud of you for the ones you pulled in. Proud, and frightened, and thankful, all in the one breath.

You’ll carry it for a time. That’s the way of it. Don’t let anyone tell you to shrug it off. Men that shrug off death become cruel. You’ve a tender heart and it will hurt. Let it. It means you’re alive.

I’ve spoken to Mrs. McNair and she says if you can get leave at Christmas she’ll give me a day off and we’ll have you home proper. I’ll make broth and you can sit with your feet to the fire like you used to when you were small.

And Euan—your father would have been proud too, if he’d been able. He was not all bad. He just didn’t know how to be scared and still stand up.

Keep standing.

Your Ma

**Letter — 6 January 1912**  
Rubha Bàn Lighthouse  
Ma,

Christmas leave didn’t come. The Board sent word: shortage of men, extra watch through winter, no relief until spring. I took the news badly at first—threw my cap at the wall like a child—then I felt ashamed.

But I did something I wouldn’t have done in March. I went to Haldane and said plain I was struggling. He looked at me a long moment, then nodded once and said, “Aye. Me too, sometimes.”

That’s the most human sentence I’ve heard out of him.

Since then we’ve made a sort of routine that isn’t only work. After the evening check we drink tea—your tea, when it lasts—and he tells me about his sister in Dundee and the time he served on Skerryvore where the waves hit so hard they’d shake plates off shelves. I told him about you and the Saltmarket and how you mend other men’s shirts like it’s nothing. He laughed, just a puff through the beard, and said, “That’s real work.”

I still get the heaviness, but it doesn’t scare me the same. When the wind makes a sound like my name, I say, “Aye, I’m here.” Sometimes Haldane answers from the next room without knowing why, “Aye.” And it helps.

There’s talk I may be moved to a shore station come summer if my reports stay good. Part of me wants it like hunger. Part of me looks out at the Sound and thinks—this place nearly broke me, and then it held me up.

I’ll come home when they let me. Until then, know this: your letters have been a rope. I’ve held on.

With all my love,  
**Euan**

**Letter — 14 June 1912**  
Glasgow  
My dear Euan,

A letter from the Northern Lighthouse Board came this morning, with your name typed neat. I read it slow, to make it last.

They’re posting you to Cloch Point on the Clyde, shore station, reliefs regular. You’ll be nearer, near enough that if I save the tram fare I can come and see the tower with my own eyes. I sat down and laughed, and then I cried, and then I wiped my face because there was shirts on the line and no one will do it for me.

I’m glad you spoke to Haldane. Men think silence is strength, but it’s only hiding. You did the brave thing, and I hope you know it. I hope you forgive yourself for the cap against the wall. If you can’t throw your cap now and then, you’ll throw something worse later.

When you come home, we’ll walk by the river and you can tell me about the Sound and the Paps and the bell buoy that cried all night. I want to hear it in your voice, not on paper. We’ll sit at the window and watch ordinary folk going past and you’ll see there’s a comfort in it, even if it smells of coal.

Bring your sea-stiff clothes. I’ll wash them soft again.

My son—Euan—you went away a boy that thought he had to be made of stone. You’re coming back a man that knows he’s made of flesh, and that’s better.

With love,  
**Ma**
