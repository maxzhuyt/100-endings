A crimson harvest moon hangs low over the thatched roofs, staining the deserted village streets as if the sky itself has begun to bleed. Wind worries at loose shutters. A sign creaks on one hinge, its painted saint flaking away into black wood beneath. The air smells of cold ash and old fear—fear that has soaked into stone and straw until it becomes part of the place.

Yennefer of Vengerberg walks through it as if she owns it.

Her boots make no sound on the packed earth. Her cloak is dark, but the night still finds her—finds the pale line of her throat, the shimmer of raven hair, the unnatural violet of her eyes when she lifts her gaze to the moon. Lilac and gooseberries cut through the stink of abandonment, an insult to rot.

She stops at the village well. The rope is snapped. A bucket lies cracked like a skull. She draws a small circle in the air with two fingers, casual as an aristocrat gesturing for wine. A thin thread of magic—purple and sharp—slides from her hand and sinks into the stones.

The well answers.

Not with water, but with echo. A memory of screams trapped in mortar. A throb of something predatory that has fed here and moved on, leaving emptiness behind like a shell.

“Sloppy,” she murmurs, and the word fogs slightly in the cold. “Or arrogant.”

A laugh answers from nowhere and everywhere, warm as spilled blood.

“You say that like they’re separate things.”

A figure stands at the mouth of the alley that wasn’t empty a blink ago. Tall. Black silhouette and red coat, the color too vivid to belong in a world of mud and plague. A wide-brimmed hat shadows his face; orange lenses catch the moon and give it back as a predatory gleam. His smile is all teeth and invitation.

Yennefer’s hand does not move toward a dagger—there’s no point—but the air around her tightens. Magic coils like a serpent under her skin. She studies him the way she studies a court: quickly, brutally, for weaknesses.

The man’s presence is wrong. Not merely dangerous—wrong, like a wound that refuses to close.

“Another monster,” she says, voice bored on purpose. “How original.”

He steps forward with the unhurried grace of something that knows it is the apex. Mud should claim his boots; it doesn’t. The village should creak and protest; it holds its breath.

“Monster.” He tastes the word, amused. “Such an ugly label for something so honest.”

Yennefer’s lip curls before she can stop it. Repulsion rises—ancient, instinctive. Dead things should stay dead. Predators that wear a man’s shape are abominations no perfume can mask.

And yet—fascination follows close behind. Power does that. Power makes rules feel optional.

“What are you?” she asks, and lets a fraction of her aura show, a controlled flare of authority meant for kings.

His smile widens as if she has offered him a game.

“The bird of Hermes is my name,” he says softly, and the cadence makes the village seem smaller, like a chapel before a god. “Eating my wings to make me tame.”

The phrase is nonsense and poetry and threat all at once. He inclines his head, mock-gallant. “You smell like springtime. That’s almost unfair.”

“Don’t,” Yennefer says, because she refuses to be reduced to scent and softness. “Flatter someone else. Or better—leave.”

His laughter is quiet. “And miss this?” He gestures with one gloved hand at the moon, the empty houses, the well that has confessed to her. “You’re here because something kills your peasants, sorceress. I’m here because something kills my boredom.”

He is close enough now that she can see the pallor beneath the shadow of his hat, the suggestion of eyes behind orange glass. She expects cold. Instead there is heat—contained, pressurized, the way a storm contains lightning before it chooses a tree.

Yennefer’s fingers twitch. A simple spell would be enough to tear a human apart. She could do it without looking.

But he is not human. And the part of her that has survived courts and battlefields and lovers who become liabilities recognizes the danger of assuming.

“Back away,” she says, and her voice hardens into command. The air answers her: a ripple, a tremor in the red moonlight. Invisible force presses toward him like a wall.

He doesn’t move.

The force hits him and… nothing. It’s not that it fails. It’s that it meets something deeper and is swallowed, like a wave breaking on an abyss.

His head tilts. “That’s cute.”

Yennefer’s smile is sharp as a blade. “So is your hat.”

For an instant, his grin turns feral. Then he lifts one hand and, with almost lazy elegance, draws a handgun from beneath his coat. It is grotesquely large, made for a hand that wants the world to understand its intentions. Moonlight glints on metal. The barrel points at her heart with the intimacy of a kiss.

Yennefer does not flinch. She has faced crossbow bolts and curses and the hunger of djinn. A weapon is only a weapon.

“You are either very brave,” she says, “or very stupid.”

“Neither.” His voice lowers, velvet over steel. “I’m just… curious.”

The gun cracks.

The sound slams into the village, ricochets off empty walls. The bullet is silver—she feels it in the way her magic recoils from it, like skin from fire. It should rip through her.

It doesn’t.

The bullet stops midair, hanging an inch from her sternum, held by a net of violet light so fine it looks like spider silk. Yennefer’s eyes narrow. The spell costs more than she wants to admit; silver carries an old hostility to sorcery. She hides the strain behind disdain.

She plucks the bullet from the air as if taking a grape from a plate. It is warm from the shot.

“Is this supposed to impress me?” she asks.

Alucard’s chuckle is delighted, genuine in its cruelty. “Maybe. Or maybe I want to see if you bleed.”

Yennefer flicks her wrist. The bullet whips back toward him like an insult thrown with perfect aim.

He doesn’t dodge.

It hits him in the forehead. For a heartbeat, his head snaps back, hat brim tilting, orange lenses catching the moon. Blood—thick and black in this light—spatters the air.

Then the wound closes.

The bullet falls to the dirt with a soft, obscene little sound.

Yennefer’s pulse steadies. Her face doesn’t change, but something inside her recalibrates. Immortal. Regeneration beyond the usual curses. Not a common vampiric strain—if “common” can be applied to such things at all.

“You’re difficult,” she says, and means it as both complaint and compliment.

He steps closer until her perfume and his blood-scent mingle, until the space between them is all tension and choice.

“And you’re wasting your talent on villages,” Alucard murmurs. “You should be in a palace. Pulling strings. Breaking kings.”

Yennefer’s gaze flicks to his mouth and away. She hates that he sees her so easily. She hates more that he’s not wrong.

“I do,” she says. “When it suits me.”

A shadow shifts at the edge of the street—a movement too quick for human eyes. Yennefer senses it first through the well’s lingering echo: something wounded, feral, starving. A lesser vampire, perhaps, or a ghoul drawn by gunfire and magic like flies to meat.

Alucard’s head turns toward it with predatory interest. Yennefer feels, abruptly, like prey has entered the hunting ground—except she can’t tell who the hunter is.

From behind a collapsed fence, a pale thing crawls into the open. Its jaw is too wide. Its fingers are claws. Its eyes are milky, mindless with hunger.

It hisses when it sees them, then lunges at Yennefer, drawn to the warmth of her living blood.

Alucard doesn’t shoot. He simply watches, as if waiting to see what she will do.

Yennefer’s disgust flares. “Pathetic,” she spits, and lifts her hand.

Chains of violet energy lash out, snaring the creature mid-leap. The magic bites into dead flesh; the ghoul convulses, limbs jerking like a puppet on burning strings. Yennefer tightens her fingers. Bones crack. The creature’s scream is thin and hungry.

Alucard’s laughter is low, appreciative. “That’s more like it.”

The ghoul’s head twists toward him, sensing a predator greater than itself, and its fear is almost comical.

Alucard steps forward. His shadow stretches wrong under the blood moon, widening, splitting—tendrils of darkness that ripple as if alive. For a moment, he is not a man in a red coat but something vast wearing one, something with too many mouths and too much patience.

He grabs the creature by the throat.

It claws at him, but its nails break on his skin. He brings it close to his face, inhaling as if savoring wine.

“You’re one of the weak ones,” he says conversationally. “The kind that hides in villages. The kind that makes humans tell stories so they don’t have to admit they were food.”

He squeezes.

The ghoul’s neck collapses. Its body goes limp. Alucard releases it, and it drops into the mud like discarded clothing.

Yennefer watches without blinking. She has seen cruelty in courts, cruelty in war. This is different. This is cruelty with a smile, cruelty that considers itself art.

She should be repulsed.

She is. And yet her mind, traitorous and sharp, catalogs the efficiency, the certainty. There is a purity to it—monstrous, yes, but honest.

Alucard turns back to her. Blood dots his glove; he wipes it on his coat as if it were nothing.

“You hate me,” he says, as if stating the weather.

“I hate what you are,” Yennefer corrects. “There’s a difference.”

He leans in slightly, and she feels the pressure of him like gravity. “Is there?”

Yennefer’s smile is sudden and cold. “I’m not sweet,” she says, each word placed like a chess piece, “and I’m not a maiden in distress in need of saving.”

Alucard’s grin sharpens. “Good. I hate damsels. They break too easily.”

For a moment the air between them is a blade’s edge. He could tear her apart. She could try to unmake him. Both of them know neither would be simple. Both of them refuse to step back first.

The harvest moon watches, crimson and indifferent, casting ominous shadows on the deserted village streets as two monsters—one born, one made—circle each other in silence.

Yennefer lifts the silver bullet from the mud with a flick of magic and holds it up between them, a small gleaming coin of threat.

“Tell me,” she says softly, “vampire… can you be killed?”

Alucard’s laughter is quiet, delighted, and it sends a shiver through the night like a promise.

“Try,” he whispers. “And I’ll show you what worthy looks like.”
