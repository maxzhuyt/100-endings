The bell above the door gave a thin, tired jingle, quickly swallowed by the hush of paper and carpet.

“Sorry—” a man said, already half inside, half looking back over his shoulder. “Just—two seconds.”

Nia looked up from behind the counter, pen paused over an invoice. The day had been damp in that specifically Welsh way that made the air feel older than it ought to be. Outside, someone’s camera shutter snapped like a thrown pebble.

The man turned fully, pulled the door shut with his shoulder, and leaned his back against it as if he could hold the whole world out with the strength of his spine. He pushed his sunglasses up into his hair, and the shop’s warm light caught a face Nia had seen, annoyingly, on billboards that made the A487 look more glamorous than it had any right to.

“Right,” he breathed, laughing once like he couldn’t help it. “That was… dramatic.”

Nia stared at him for a beat, then let her eyes drop to the rain on his coat. “You’re dripping on the welcome mat.”

He blinked, like he’d expected a different opening line. “It’s a very hospitable mat.”

“It was,” Nia said. “Before you.”

A flash of teeth—easy, practiced. “I can buy it dinner later by way of apology.”

Nia put her pen down carefully. “This is a bookshop.”

“I know.” He patted his pockets as if checking for contraband. “I saw books in the window. That’s what tipped me off. Books have always been a safe haven for fugitives.”

“Fugitives,” she repeated.

“Paparazzi,” he said, with the faint indignation of someone who’d been forced to notice their own fame again today. “They’re out there. Like… seagulls. If seagulls had lenses and no shame.”

Nia tilted her head. “This is Abercelyn. We mostly get actual seagulls. They steal pasties.”

“I’d rather negotiate with birds,” he said, then winced at his own melodrama and softened it into a grin. “I’m Finn Marlowe, by the way.”

“I know.” It came out flat.

His grin faltered, the tiniest stutter in the machinery. “You do?”

“You’re on a poster in the pharmacy window,” Nia said. “For that… film where you’re brooding on a cliff.”

“That could be any of my films.”

“The one where you look like you’ve been wronged by a cloud.”

He barked a laugh, genuine enough that it surprised her. “God. Yes. That’s the one. I did three hours in hair and makeup for that cloud to wrong me properly.”

Nia lifted her chin toward the shop. “Well, Mr Marlowe, are you buying a book or hiding?”

“Hiding, ideally,” he said promptly. Then, as if sensing that sounded less charming than he preferred, he added, “And possibly buying. I’m multifaceted.”

“We’re closed in twenty minutes,” Nia said.

Finn’s eyes flicked up to the clock behind her, then back to her. “Twenty minutes. Plenty of time for a life-changing purchase.”

“It’s a small-town bookshop,” Nia said. “Most purchases are… mug-changing at best.”

“I could be your first life-changing customer.” He straightened from the door and took a step in, gaze sweeping the shelves with a kind of theatrical reverence. “This place is—”

“Dusty,” Nia offered.

“—perfect,” he finished, as if she hadn’t spoken. He ran his fingers along a spine without pulling it out. “It smells like—like my grandfather’s study. Leather and old paper and secrets.”

Nia’s throat tightened, just a fraction. “We don’t keep secrets. We keep stock.”

Finn turned, studying her properly now. Not just the shopkeeper, but the person. His eyes—too bright for a rainy day—caught on the name tag pinned to her jumper.

“Nia,” he read aloud, like tasting it. “That’s Welsh, isn’t it?”

“It’s… my name,” she said.

“And you own this?” He gestured at the shop as if it were a small kingdom.

“I do,” Nia said, then corrected herself because truth mattered, even to strangers with famous faces. “Well. The bank owns half of it and the damp owns the other half. I just unlock the door.”

Finn’s mouth quirked. “That’s a very bleak way of saying you’re a business owner.”

“It’s a very accurate way.”

He moved closer to the counter, not in a looming way—more like he was drawn by magnetism and didn’t want to admit it. He glanced past her at the kettle and two mismatched mugs. “Are you going to offer a desperate fugitive tea?”

“I don’t give tea to people who call themselves fugitives,” Nia said. “It encourages them.”

“Coffee?” he tried.

“Water,” she said.

Finn put a hand to his chest like she’d stabbed him. “Water. In Wales. I’m being punished.”

“I’m not punishing you,” Nia said. “I don’t know you.”

He looked at her as if that was a personal affront. “Everyone knows me.”

Nia didn’t blink. “That sounds exhausting.”

For a moment the charm slipped; something raw edged into his expression, gone almost immediately, but it was there—like seeing a crack in a glossy magazine page.

“It is,” he said, quieter.

Nia felt the impulse to soften, to soothe, and resented it on principle. She reached under the counter and slid a small laminated sign forward. *NO PHOTOGRAPHY. NO FILMING. NO VLOGGING.*

Finn followed her motion and read it. “You have a sign.”

“We had a local lad try to make ‘cosy bookshop content’ and he nearly knocked over the poetry section,” Nia said. “So yes.”

Finn raised both hands. “I’m harmless. Also, I’m not filming. I’m merely… existing.”

“Try existing away from the door,” Nia said. “If anyone looks in and sees you pressed against the glass like a fish, I’ll have to deal with it.”

He grinned again. “You’re good at boundaries.”

“Practice,” she said. “Pick a shelf. Pretend you’re normal.”

Finn’s eyes widened with mock indignation. “Normal? Nia, I was born in a hotel and raised by stylists.”

“That explains the hair,” she muttered before she could stop herself.

His hand went instinctively to his hair, ego flaring, then he laughed, but there was a little too much hunger in it. “Is my hair… a compliment in Wales?”

“It’s… present,” Nia said.

Finn leaned an elbow on the counter, closer now. She could smell rain and something clean underneath, like soap that cost more than her electric bill.

“You’re really not impressed,” he said.

“I sell books,” Nia replied. “I see worse faces on dust jackets every day.”

“That’s brutal.”

“It’s true.”

Finn’s gaze lingered on her, like he was trying to find the trick. “Are you doing it on purpose?”

“Doing what?”

“Not liking me.”

Nia snorted. “I didn’t say I didn’t like you.”

“You didn’t say you did.” He watched her the way he might watch a scene partner, waiting for the line that would make him look good.

Nia felt heat crawl up her neck—annoyance, yes, and something else she refused to name. “Why do you need strangers to like you?”

Finn’s smile held for a beat too long. “Because—” He stopped, glanced toward the window as another shutter click snapped outside. His voice lowered. “Because if they don’t, what’s the point?”

Nia didn’t answer immediately. She looked at him, really looked, and saw not a rogue but a man who’d built a whole persona out of being wanted. She hated how quickly her chest went soft.

She busied her hands with the invoice. “The point of what? Acting?”

“Of me,” he said, and the honesty of it made her stomach flip.

Nia set the paper down. “That’s… dramatic.”

He let out a breath, laughing at himself again, but it sounded thinner this time. “I warned you.”

She surprised herself by asking, “Why are you here? In Abercelyn. Of all places.”

Finn’s eyes flicked to the shelves again, as if the books might give him cover. “Location scouting. There’s a project—small thing. Indie. I’m supposed to play a man who returns to his childhood village and—” He waved a hand, dismissive. “It doesn’t matter.”

“It matters if you’re hiding in my shop,” Nia said.

He gave her a look, earnest now. “Fine. It’s about grief.”

Nia’s fingers curled around her pen. “Whose?”

He hesitated. The rogue had vanished, replaced by a man who looked briefly younger. “His. Mine. Everyone’s, probably.”

Nia waited. Silence in the shop felt different—like the books themselves were listening.

Finn swallowed. “When I was fifteen, I tried to audition for the school play. I wanted to be—God, I wanted to be someone else so badly. I went up on stage and I forgot every line. Every single one. Just… blank.” He snapped his fingers once, softly. “My brain shut down and everyone stared, and I could hear them breathing, and I thought, That’s it. I’m done. I’ll never show my face again.”

Nia’s mouth parted slightly before she remembered to close it.

Finn shrugged like it didn’t matter. “Afterward, my dad said, ‘At least you made them look at you.’ Like that was the goal. So I learned how to make people look. I got good at it.” He lifted his eyes to hers. “But sometimes I still get that feeling. Like I’m about to go blank and everyone will see I’m—”

“Human?” Nia said, before she could stop herself.

His lips twitched. “Don’t say it like it’s comforting.”

“It is,” she said, then immediately wished she hadn’t.

Finn’s gaze sharpened, catching on that slip like a hand finding purchase. “There it is.”

Nia frowned. “There what is?”

“You,” he said softly. “Not made of stone.”

“I’m not stone,” Nia said, too quickly. “I’m—busy.”

“Busy being unimpressed.”

“Busy running a shop,” Nia corrected, voice tighter than she intended. “Busy making sure people don’t knock over poetry.”

Finn smiled again, but it wasn’t the glossy one. It was smaller, private, like it belonged in a book more than a magazine. “Tell me what to read, then. If I’m going to hide, I might as well hide with purpose.”

Nia should have pointed him to the celebrity memoirs out of spite. Instead, her eyes drifted to the fiction table, to a slim paperback with a worn spine.

“Here,” she said, stepping out from behind the counter. She handed it to him, their fingers brushing for the briefest moment. It was nothing. It was—everything.

Finn looked down at the cover. “*The Mabinogion?*”

“It’s Welsh,” Nia said, as if that explained everything.

Finn held it like it might bite. “Is it… difficult?”

“It’s stories,” Nia said. “Heroes. Tricksters. People making terrible choices because they want to be loved.”

Finn glanced up at her. “Sounds familiar.”

Nia felt her face warm and turned away, fussing with the stack of bookmarks on the counter. “If you’re going to flirt, do it quietly. There are books.”

“I’m not flirting,” Finn said, offended, which was almost convincing.

Nia lifted her eyebrows.

He huffed a laugh. “Fine. I am. But you’re not giving me anything to work with.”

“That’s because I’m not working,” Nia said.

Finn stepped a fraction closer, lowering his voice as if the shelves might report him. “Nia. Do you always talk to customers like this?”

“Yes,” she lied.

He watched her, eyes bright. “Then your customers must be in love with you.”

Nia’s heart did something inconvenient. “They’re in love with the discount shelf.”

Finn tipped his head. “What are you in love with?”

Nia’s mouth went dry. She could have said books. She could have said quiet. She could have said the sea, the mountains, the way Wales held you whether you wanted it to or not.

Instead she said, because she was a fool, “Not being chased.”

Finn’s gaze flicked to the window again. The shutters had gone silent for the moment, like the outside world had paused to breathe.

He held up the book. “Can I stay,” he asked, and the question had an edge of something real under it, “until they get bored?”

Nia should have said no. She should have remembered the bank and the damp and the sensible boundaries.

She heard herself say, “If you knock over poetry, I’ll throw you out.”

Finn’s smile was sudden and bright, relief and triumph tangled together. “I would never.”

Nia met his eyes, steadying herself. “And if anyone asks, you’re just a man buying a book.”

Finn’s voice softened. “Just a man.”

“Yes,” Nia said, though she wasn’t sure she believed it. “Now—go. Sit in the corner by the travel guides. You’ll blend in.”

Finn turned, then paused, looking back over his shoulder. “Thank you,” he said, like it mattered.

Nia lifted her pen again, pretending it didn’t. “Don’t thank me yet. You haven’t paid.”

He laughed under his breath and disappeared between the shelves, the bell silent now, the shop holding its breath with her.

Outside, the rain kept falling, patient as a story that knew exactly where it was going.
