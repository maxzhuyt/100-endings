The underworld has a surprising number of job titles, most of them involving the words *eternal* and *torment* and *why did I major in philosophy*. Sisyphus’s official designation was still, technically, “That One With The Rock.”

But recently—after a minor clerical error involving a pallet of unused papyrus, two quills, and a crow who’d discovered that letters had *seals* and seals had *wax*—there was a second title, handwritten in a neat column beneath the first:

**AGONY AUNT (PROVISIONAL).**

The mountain was much the same as it had always been: grey, steep, and designed by someone with a personal grudge against geometry. The boulder was much the same too, right down to the faint scrape marks that looked like the world’s oldest tally chart.

The novelty sat on the boulder’s crown: a small wooden board, balanced with practiced ease, upon which lay a stack of letters and a crow with ink on its beak.

“Crow,” said Sisyphus, leaning his shoulder into the rock and pushing. “Do we have any new ones?”

Crow ruffled his feathers importantly, which shook out three wax seals, a bit of string, and something that might once have been a biscuit.

“Caw,” he said, which in context meant: *Four. One smells like goat. One is crying. One is unwise.*

Sisyphus smiled in the way of a man who had learned that smiling did not, in fact, cause lightning. “Let’s start with the crying one. You can open it—no, not with your—Crow, the letter is not a worm.”

Crow jabbed. The seal surrendered. He placed the letter on the board and made a theatrical show of dipping the quill, as if he hadn’t been stealing ink from the accounts office for a week.

Sisyphus read aloud as he pushed. His voice had the calm steadiness of someone who had no expectation of the universe being fair, but every expectation that people could be.

---

**Dear Sisyphus,**

**My partner says I’m “emotionally unavailable.” I say I’m “tired.” We both say we’re “fine.” We are not fine. I love them, but whenever we talk about feelings I feel like I’m being asked to assemble a chair with no instructions, and also I’ve lost the screws. How do I stop shutting down?**

**—Numb in Naxos**

Crow wrote *NUMB IN NAXOS* in the margin and then added, in smaller script: *Also on Naxos: overpriced olives.*

Sisyphus paused long enough to shift the boulder around a rut. The rock made the same old complaining sound, like an ancient accountant.

“Tell them this,” he said.

---

**Dear Numb in Naxos,**

**First: being tired is not a personality flaw. It’s information. If you’re carrying too much, your mind will protect you by going quiet. It thinks it’s saving you.**

**Second: don’t start with “feelings.” Start with facts. When your partner says you’re emotionally unavailable, ask: “When do you feel that most?” Not to argue, but to understand the specific moments. People can handle moments. “Always” is too big to hold.**

**Third: you don’t have to be brilliant at talking. You only have to be honest about where you get stuck. Try saying, exactly: “I’m here, and I care, and I’m getting overwhelmed. I need a minute.” Then actually take the minute. Come back. The coming back is the important part.**

**Fourth: pick a small, repeatable habit that signals connection even on tired days. A touch on the shoulder when you pass. Sitting together for ten minutes without solving anything. Ask one question you genuinely want the answer to.**

**If you treat closeness like a test, you will freeze. If you treat it like practice, you’ll get warmer.**

**Yours,**

**Sisyphus**

---

Crow, still writing, made a sound that was half cough and half judgement.

“Something to add?” Sisyphus asked.

Crow wrote under the signature: *P.S. “I need a minute” is not the same as “I need a week.”* Then he added, as if remembering himself, *Not legal advice.*

Sisyphus chuckled, and the boulder moved another foot. The top of the hill was closer now; it always was, right up until it wasn’t.

“Next,” he said.

Crow selected a letter with a seal shaped like a tiny hammer. The handwriting marched across the page like an army that had lost the war but refused to stop drilling.

---

**Esteemed Sisyphus,**

**I work at a smithy. My master says I’m “lucky to have work” and expects me to stay late, come early, and also be grateful about it. I’m so exhausted I can barely lift my cup, and my partner says I’m turning into a ghost. I’m afraid if I set boundaries I’ll lose my apprenticeship. What do I do?**

**—Hammered in Hades (No, Not That Hades)**

Crow wrote *HAMMERED* and underlined it twice. Then: *Wordplay detected. Sentence reduced for good behaviour.*

Sisyphus cleared his throat. “This one is common.”

Crow wrote: *Like fleas.*

---

**Dear Hammered,**

**It’s true you’re lucky to have work. It’s also true your master is lucky to have you. You are not a candle that burns better when it’s shorter.**

**Make a plan, not a speech. Choose one boundary you can keep. For example: “I can stay late two nights a week, not five.” Or: “I can come early, but I leave on time.” Then tell your master in practical terms—what you can do, what you cannot, and what you will do instead.**

**Say it once, calmly. Don’t argue about whether you’re “grateful.” Gratitude doesn’t hammer horseshoes. If your master tries to make it about your character, bring it back to the work: “I want to do good work. I can do that if I’m rested.”**

**Also: talk to other apprentices. Quietly. Not to gossip—just to learn what’s normal and what’s abuse wearing an apron. If everyone is collapsing, it isn’t discipline; it’s mismanagement.**

**Finally: have a small exit plan. Update your skills. Save what you can. Knowing you could leave makes it easier to speak without shaking.**

**You deserve a life that includes sleep. Even smiths. Especially smiths.**

**Yours,**

**Sisyphus**

---

Crow appended: *If your master says “In my day we worked until our bones sang,” respond: “Then perhaps your day was badly scheduled.”*

Sisyphus’s eyes creased. “Crow. Be kind.”

Crow scratched out *badly* and wrote *inefficiently*.

They reached the top.

The boulder, as always, paused like a cat deciding whether affection was beneath it. For a moment it sat there, pretending it might stay.

Sisyphus rested his hands on the warm stone and looked out over the flat grey distance where the dead went about their endless, petty errands: queueing, filing, complaining about queueing and filing. A line of shades shuffled past, each holding a form.

One of them glanced up at Sisyphus and then quickly looked away, as if caught seeing something human.

Sisyphus exhaled, and gave the boulder the final small nudge.

It rolled.

Crow watched it go, head tilted. “Caw,” he said thoughtfully, which meant: *See you in a minute.*

“Yes,” said Sisyphus, and started down after it at a steady walking pace. There was no hurry. The boulder would be waiting.

By the time he reached it, three more letters had landed on the board, courtesy of Crow’s tireless, thieving postal service.

Crow pecked one open immediately. The seal was pink. There was glitter. In the underworld. This was, somehow, worse than the punishment.

Sisyphus picked it up carefully, as if glitter might be contagious.

---

**Sisyphus,**

**My neighbour is impossible. They borrow things and return them “eventually.” They comment on my laundry. They have opinions about my sandals. They tell me I’m “doing grief wrong” because I didn’t cry loudly at the market. If I say anything, they act wounded. If I say nothing, they get worse. How do I deal with someone who weaponizes hurt feelings?**

**—Quietly Seething**

Crow wrote in the margin: *Also known as: “A neighbour.”* Then: *May the gods invent fences.*

Sisyphus adjusted his grip and began pushing again, as if the motion made thinking easier. (It did; some minds, like some bread, rose better with steady warmth.)

---

**Dear Quietly Seething,**

**People like this thrive on two things: access and confusion. They want you close enough to poke, and uncertain enough to apologise for bleeding.**

**Start small and specific. Don’t accuse them of being “impossible.” Say: “I’m not lending that.” “Please don’t comment on my laundry.” “I’m not discussing my grief.” Short sentences are your friend. They are difficult to twist.**

**When they act wounded, don’t rush to rescue them. You can acknowledge without surrendering: “I hear you don’t like that. I’m still not lending it.”**

**If they bring it to an audience (market, doorway, any place with ears), keep your voice calm and your words boring. Drama needs fuel. Give them damp wood.**

**And if you can, build distance—literal or social. Spend time with people who don’t make your peace feel like a provocation.**

**You are allowed to be private. You are allowed to have boundaries. You are allowed to grieve in your own shape.**

**Yours,**

**Sisyphus**

---

Crow wrote: *“Damp wood” is good. I like damp wood. Damp wood makes smoke. Smoke makes them cough.*

Sisyphus glanced at him. “Crow.”

Crow added: *Metaphorically.* After a beat: *Mostly.*

The next letter was heavier, the parchment thicker, as though the writer believed seriousness could be increased by weight. The seal was plain.

Sisyphus opened it and his face softened.

---

**Dear Sisyphus,**

**Everyone else seems to have a purpose. My sister has a cause. My friend has a craft. Even my dog has a mission (digging). I wake up and do my chores and go to sleep and nothing feels like it means anything. I’m not in despair exactly. I just… don’t know what I’m for. Is there something wrong with me?**

**—Unassigned**

Crow’s marginal note this time was only: *…* which for Crow was practically a hymn.

Sisyphus pushed in silence for a while, boots slipping on gravel, the boulder leaning into him with familiar weight. When he spoke, it wasn’t grand. It didn’t need to be.

---

**Dear Unassigned,**

**There’s nothing wrong with you. Not knowing is a very common condition. Most people with “purpose” have simply found a place to put their care where it can be used.**

**Try this: instead of asking “What am I for?” ask “What do I care about, even a little?” Not what you admire in theory—what you actually notice. Stray animals. Stories. Fixing broken stools. Making soup. Keeping your promises.**

**Then make your world slightly more like that, on purpose. One small action. Not a vow. Not a new identity. Just a deliberate choice. If you care about people feeling welcome, invite someone for tea. If you care about beauty, put something clean on the table. If you care about fairness, speak up once when it’s easy.**

**Meaning often arrives the way bread does: quietly, while you’re doing other things.**

**Also: if your life feels like only chores, check whether you’re resting. When a person is exhausted, even joy feels like another task.**

**Yours,**

**Sisyphus**

---

Crow wrote at the bottom: *Dog’s mission is valid.* Then, after a moment, added: *Also: digging is a craft.*

They were halfway up again when Crow produced the last letter with a flourish that suggested either drama or indigestion.

The seal was… well. It was a seal, but it had been pressed with something that was definitely not intended for sealing. It had an unmistakable spiral pattern.

Sisyphus held it between thumb and forefinger. “Oh dear.”

Crow preened. *This is why we have hands*, his posture said.

Sisyphus opened it.

---

**Sisyphus,**

**Is it normal to think about my spouse’s best friend during intimate moments? Nothing has happened. I love my spouse. But my brain keeps… visiting. I feel guilty, and then I avoid intimacy, and then my spouse thinks I don’t want them. Help.**

**—Ashamed and Overactive**

Crow, without being asked, wrote: *“visiting”* and then underlined it, because crows enjoy words that can mean two things and also neither.

Sisyphus’s expression remained gentle, as if he were being asked about the weather.

---

**Dear Ashamed and Overactive,**

**Thoughts are not actions. Many minds are like dogs: they run off after interesting smells. The important part is what you do with the leash.**

**Guilt makes the thought stronger, because it turns it into a forbidden room your mind keeps checking. Instead, acknowledge it plainly: “That’s a thought.” Then return to what you’re actually doing. Not by force—by attention.**

**If you’re avoiding intimacy because you’re afraid of your own brain, tell your spouse something true without the unnecessary details: “I’ve been anxious during sex and it’s made me pull away. I want to be close. Can we go slower and talk about what helps me stay present?”**

**Also consider what the thought is really about. Sometimes it isn’t the person—it’s the feeling. Do you feel admired by the friend? Do you feel playful? Do you feel seen? If there’s something missing, you can bring that into your marriage without bringing in another body.**

**If the guilt is consuming you or you feel out of control, speak to someone wise in your town—a counsellor, a priest who isn’t obsessed with punishment, an aunt who knows how bodies work. You don’t need to carry this alone.**

**Yours,**

**Sisyphus**

---

Crow’s note: *“Priest who isn’t obsessed with punishment” narrows it down to three.* Then, more quietly, as if reluctant to admit sincerity could exist: *Good answer.*

Sisyphus reached the steep part again. His breath deepened, not strained, just present. Crow shifted on the board, balancing automatically.

“Crow,” Sisyphus said, after a while. “Do we have enough ink?”

Crow looked at the inkwell, then at Sisyphus, then at the distant administrative building where ink was kept.

“Caw,” he said brightly.

Sisyphus nodded, as if this answered a question no one else could hear.

The boulder scraped onward.

A shade on the path below stopped and stared up at them.

“Is it true,” the shade called, “that you answer letters now?”

Sisyphus didn’t stop pushing. “Yes.”

The shade hesitated, then cupped his hands. “Can I ask—how do you keep going?”

Sisyphus considered. He could feel the rock’s steady insistence, the mountain’s patient resistance, Crow’s weight like a warm, judgemental hat.

He said, simply, “Write it down. Crow will fetch it.”

Crow flapped once, offended by the implication that he fetched on request, and then immediately looked pleased at being indispensable.

The shade blinked, as if this hadn’t occurred to him. “Oh. Right.”

Sisyphus smiled, and put his shoulder back into the work.

Crow dipped the quill, ready for the next person’s messy handwriting, the next small human emergency, the next chance to make the underworld—if not lighter—at least a little more legible.

Somewhere behind them, far below, the boulder waited to be pushed again.

And for once in eternity, Sisyphus had mail.
