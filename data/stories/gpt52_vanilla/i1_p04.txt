Midas and Chewy held council over a breakfast of boiled nettles and something that might once have been an oat.

Chewy, being an animo-tronic stuffed koala from some long-forgotten Before Time, held council by blinking twice, whirring once, and producing a small recorded sigh that had probably been designed to melt the heart of a shopping-mall queue in the Year of Our Lord Two Thousand And Something.

It now mostly melted Midas’s patience.

“Right,” Midas said, leaning over the map with the air of a man leaning over his own life choices. The map was not, strictly speaking, a map. It was the back of a cereal box with “FUN MAZE!” on one side and Midas’s desperate pencil scratches on the other. “We have a predicament.”

Chewy’s glassy eyes stared at him with the serene expression of a creature that had never, in its entire existence, had to decide between dehydration and being eaten by feral goats.

Midas tapped the cereal cardboard. “Home base—” he nodded at the derelict garden shed they’d christened Fort Mostly Standing “—has three assets and nine liabilities. Assets: one solar panel that works when it’s in the mood, one water barrel that sometimes doesn’t taste like regret, and you.”

Chewy’s speaker crackled. “I love you,” it said warmly, with a tiny rising inflection. Then, because the universe enjoyed timing, it added, “Please insert fresh batteries.”

“Liabilities,” Midas went on, “include: the barrel. The shed. The goats. The fact that the sun is currently doing that thing where it tries to kill you for free. And,” he added, wagging the pencil as though it might shame reality into behaving, “our ongoing lack of anything resembling civilisation.”

Chewy blinked once, then twice. Its head twitched a fraction to the left, a movement that suggested affection, concern, or an internal gear deciding to die later rather than now.

Midas had, by necessity, become the sort of man who could read deep meaning into a stuffed koala’s defective servo.

“Today’s mission,” he announced, “is simple. We go into town, we find batteries and food that was packaged back when people still believed in best-before dates. We return. We do not get chased, trapped, shot at, bitten, or emotionally devastated. If we see any signs of—”

Chewy’s speaker chose that moment to chirp, “Let’s go on an adventure!”

“—yes, any signs of adventure,” Midas finished, “we walk the other way.”

The world, being the world, listened carefully, took notes, and prepared to do the opposite.

They left Fort Mostly Standing at midmorning, with Midas wearing a backpack assembled from three smaller backpacks and the sort of expression you see on a man about to argue with a vending machine. Chewy rode in the top like a furry periscope, its stitched paws fixed in a permanent hug around the straps.

The road into town was a museum of the Age of Convenience, where everything had been engineered to save time and had therefore died in an especially time-consuming manner. Cars sat like beached metal whales. Billboards flaked away to reveal older billboards beneath, as if advertising had sedimentary layers. A sign that once read WELCOME TO LARCHTON now read WELC ME TO AR HTO, which felt accurate.

They reached the first houses and moved carefully, because “quiet” had become a luxury you only got by not making noise.

Midas eased open the door of what had been a corner shop. It smelled of dust, mould, and the lingering ghost of chocolate.

“Alright,” he whispered, as though the building might wake up. “Batteries. Tins. Anything with a cartoon mascot smiling too hard.”

Chewy’s eyes glinted. Somewhere inside it, a motion sensor decided that “dark shop” counted as “presence of child” and triggered a cheerful jingle.

“Chewy,” Midas hissed, “please don’t—”

“I’m Chewy the Koala!” Chewy announced at full pre-collapse volume. “Let’s be friends forever!”

From outside came the sudden, unmistakable sound of something choosing this exact moment to investigate.

Midas froze. Chewy blinked. The shelf behind them, which had been held in place by optimism and rust, gave a soft sigh and leaned forward.

Midas grabbed the nearest thing—an entire display rack of potato crisps turned to powder—and tried to catch the shelf before it finished its slow-motion decision to collapse. The crisps went up in a dust cloud that made the air smell briefly of salt and long-lost parties.

There are, broadly speaking, two categories of apocalypse survivor: those who move silently through ruined buildings like ghosts, and those who appear to be personally reenacting slapstick for the benefit of an absent studio audience.

Midas belonged to the second category in a way that suggested commitment.

The shelf hit the ground with a crash that echoed out into the street like an invitation.

Chewy’s speaker chirped, “Uh-oh!”

From the doorway came a figure, followed by another: scavengers, by their patched jackets and the particular look people get when they’ve decided you own their belongings but haven’t had the courtesy to hand them over yet.

“Morning,” Midas said, because politeness had not technically died and he liked to keep it limping along. He raised his hands slowly. “We’re just—uh—window shopping.”

The taller scavenger stared at Chewy. “Is that… a toy?”

Chewy, sensing attention like a sunflower senses light, rotated its head. “Hug me!” it said.

The shorter scavenger snorted. “It talked.”

Midas, with the instinct of a man about to be relieved of his teeth, did the only sensible thing. He ran.

Chewy bounced in the backpack like an eager child in a collapsing amusement park.

They shot through the back door, across a yard full of waist-high weeds, and into the alley behind. Midas had one of those minds that, in moments of panic, became very good at noticing unnecessary details. He noted that the weeds had little purple flowers. He noted that his shoelace was untied. He noted, with bleak clarity, that he had run directly into a dead end.

Ahead was a brick wall. To the left, a dumpster. To the right, a fence with a sign that read PRIVATE PROPERTY as though anyone was still paying attention.

Behind them, footsteps.

Midas looked up at the wall, then at Chewy, as though the koala might have ideas. Chewy blinked.

“Please insert fresh batteries,” it said helpfully.

Midas seized the dumpster lid and yanked. It screamed in protest. Inside was a treasure hoard of the post-collapse: broken glass, damp cardboard, and the lingering sense that someone had once thrown away something perfectly usable.

On the inside of the lid, scratched into the metal with something sharp, were words, crude but deliberate:

THE BEAR SLEEPS WHERE THE RIVER FORGETS.
FOLLOW THE THREE WHITE STONES.
KNOCK TWICE. DON’T KNOCK THRICE.

Midas stared at it. The scavengers’ footsteps drew closer.

“This,” he whispered, “is either a clue to a hidden refuge, or the world’s least helpful poetry.”

Chewy leaned forward as far as its harness allowed and, by pure mechanical coincidence, bonked its head against the lid.

*KNOCK.*

Midas snatched it back. “Not now!”

Chewy’s speaker purred, “Again!”

“KNOCK TWICE,” Midas muttered, brain performing arithmetic at gunpoint. “Right. Twice.”

He lifted the lid and knocked twice, hard, because if you’re going to obey cryptic graffiti you might as well be thorough.

Nothing happened.

“Excellent,” Midas said. “The mystery is—”

The dumpster shifted.

Not much. Just a lurch, as though the ground beneath it had changed its mind about being solid. The base slid sideways with a grinding scrape, and a rectangle of darkness appeared in the brickwork behind, previously hidden by the dumpster’s bulk. A narrow service door, painted the same colour as the wall, with a rusted handle and, in faded letters, MAINTENANCE.

From behind, the scavengers rounded the corner and stopped, surprised to find their quarry apparently conversing with rubbish.

Midas did not waste time on disbelief. He yanked the maintenance door open. It stuck for a heartbeat, then gave way with an unhappy groan.

He shoved Chewy through first—because if the world had rules, it would surely insist on him saving the koala—and dove after it.

The door slammed behind them with a clang that sounded, at least briefly, like safety.

They landed in darkness and stale air. Midas lay on his back, trying to convince his lungs that breathing was still a good idea.

Chewy’s eyes glowed faintly with reflected light. Somewhere in its chest, a cheerful mechanism ticked.

“I’m having fun!” it announced.

Midas sat up. “We are,” he said, “going to have a conversation later about your definition of fun.”

A thin beam of light appeared—Chewy’s built-in “nightlight mode,” presumably intended for children afraid of the dark and not for adults trapped in mysterious service corridors beneath dead towns.

The passage was narrow, concrete-lined, and sloped down. Old pipes ran along the ceiling like metal vines. The air smelled of damp and the kind of old secrets that had been stored without proper ventilation.

They moved forward because going back meant scavengers, and going forward meant only whatever the universe had decided to keep under the town.

After a few minutes, the passage opened into a larger chamber. It might once have been part of some municipal utility system—waterworks, drainage, or the place where they kept spare apocalypse.

Against one wall sat a row of shelves. On the shelves were boxes. On the boxes were words. Beautiful, miraculous words.

BATTERIES.

Midas felt his heart do something that was either joy or a minor arrhythmia.

He snatched a box down. The packaging was dusty but intact. He opened it and held up a battery like a jeweller appraising a diamond.

Chewy watched, entranced.

“Fresh batteries,” Midas breathed. “Chewy, you’re going to be insufferable.”

Chewy’s speaker crackled, as though offended by the implication it wasn’t already. “I love you.”

Midas stuffed batteries into his pack with the fervour of a man who has made peace with the fact that he will never again see a supermarket. He grabbed tins, too—beans, peaches, a can labelled MEAT PRODUCT in a font that suggested legal compromise.

Then he heard it.

A low rumble, distant but approaching, like a giant clearing its throat.

Water.

Midas froze. The chamber had a faint line of grime on the walls, a tide mark. The kind you get when something fills up periodically.

“The river forgets,” he whispered, suddenly recalling the graffiti. “Oh, that’s not— that’s not poetic. That’s *hydrological*.”

Chewy tilted its head, as though considering the concept of hydrology. “Splash!” it said.

The rumble deepened. Somewhere down the passage, water began to roar.

“Run,” Midas told Chewy, which was ridiculous because Chewy had never run in its life, but it felt good to say things that had clear solutions.

He bolted back the way they’d come, pack heavier now with hope and canned ambiguity. The slope that had been friendly on the way down was now a long incline designed by someone who hated legs.

Behind them, water surged into the chamber with the enthusiasm of a thing that had been waiting all day to make an entrance.

Midas pumped his arms. Chewy bounced. The nightlight beam wobbled wildly, painting frantic shapes on the walls.

They reached the maintenance door. Midas threw himself at it, shoved, and it did not move.

Because of course it did not.

He slammed a shoulder into it. Rust complained. The door gave a fraction. Behind them, water hissed closer, licking along the floor.

Midas pushed again. The door opened with a sucking sound, and daylight spilled in like an answer to a prayer filed in triplicate.

He tumbled out into the alley, dragging Chewy, just as water began to gush from beneath the door and spread across the cracked pavement like an angry puddle.

Outside, the scavengers were gone—either scared off by the noise or smart enough not to argue with unexpected flood events. Midas lay panting, drenched from the knees down, and watched the water run down the alley toward the street.

Chewy, in the backpack, was dripping. Its fur sagged. Its eyes blinked slowly, as though it had just experienced something profound.

“I’m wet,” it announced sadly.

“Yes,” Midas said, hauling himself up. “We all are.”

The trip back to Fort Mostly Standing was a catalogue of indignities. Halfway there, Midas stepped on a loose slab of pavement and went down hard, saving the batteries but sacrificing his dignity, which had been on life support anyway. Chewy’s speaker, perhaps shorting slightly from the water, began cycling through its entire library of comforting phrases.

“You can do it!”
“Try again!”
“Let’s sing together!”
“Please insert fresh batteries!”

By the time they reached home, the sun was low and Midas was limping, soaked, dusty, and carrying enough canned food to survive the week if the week was not ambitious.

He kicked the shed door open and dropped the pack.

Chewy slumped forward like a weary explorer. Midas fished out two of the precious batteries and, with the tenderness of a man defusing a bomb that also happened to be his only friend, replaced the old ones inside Chewy’s back panel.

Chewy’s eyes brightened. Its servos whirred with renewed purpose. It straightened, shook off a few droplets, and beamed at him.

“Adventure complete!” it declared.

Midas sank onto the crate they used as a chair, then winced and rearranged himself into a less painful version of sitting.

“We went out for batteries,” he said. “We were chased. We found a message. We discovered a secret door by knocking on a dumpster. We nearly drowned in an underground flood. We came home with food, bruises, and a wet koala.”

Chewy blinked and, for once, did not speak immediately, as though even its chip had to process that.

Finally, it said, softly and perfectly timed, “Let’s do it again tomorrow.”

Midas looked at the sagging shed, the fading light, and the barrel of water that still tasted faintly of regret.

He looked at Chewy, who stared back with the unblinking devotion of a toy designed for a world that had ended.

“No,” Midas said.

Then he opened a can of peaches with a knife that had once been a letter opener, handed half the contents to Chewy in a tin lid (because you had to humour him; he liked to feel included), and added, “Probably.”

Outside, somewhere beyond the dead town and the whispering wilds, a river forgot and remembered and forgot again.

And somewhere out there, if graffiti was to be believed, a bear slept.

Which was, Midas decided, either a comforting thought or an ominous one.

In the apocalypse, it was often hard to tell the difference.
