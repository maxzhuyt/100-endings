The first thing I notice every time is the diagram.

It’s bolted to the wall over the treatment table like a witness that can’t be bribed: a flayed human figure with its cleanly labeled muscle groups, reds and pinks and tidy Latin names. Nothing about it looks like my body feels. Nothing about it looks like the mess I became the day my knee gave up on me and took everything with it.

I sit on the edge of the vinyl table anyway, paper crinkling under my thighs. My left leg hangs off, foot swinging an inch above the floor. My right leg—my *bad* leg—stays planted, sole flat, knee bent, because letting it dangle makes the joint bark in a way that lights up my whole nervous system.

The door clicks and I can tell who it is without looking.

There are people whose footsteps you learn like a cadence drill. His are controlled, even now, even in soft-soled clinic shoes: heel, roll, toe, like he’s still counting reps.

“Hey,” he says, like he didn’t use to shout my name over the roar of a stadium. Like he didn’t use to slap my shoulders after a win, sweaty and laughing, like our bodies were just tools and not this—this charged thing I can’t stop tracking with the corner of my eye.

I make myself look at the musculature diagram instead of him. Trapezius. Latissimus dorsi. Gluteus medius. A map of everyone I used to be.

“Morning,” I say, which is what you say to a man who is absurdly, unfairly good-looking in navy scrubs, hair pushed back, forearms roped with muscle that came from work instead of vanity. I resent that too: his body still makes sense. He found a new use for it. Mine feels like a betrayal I’m forced to negotiate with three times a week.

He rolls the stool into place at my right side, close enough that I can feel the warmth of him before he even touches me. The stool’s wheels squeak once, then stop. He washes his hands at the sink—water running, soap, the brisk rub of palms—then dries them, and the clinic air carries the clean sting of sanitizer.

“Any swelling after last session?” he asks.

I look down at my knee. The scar is pale now, the kind of line that fades only because the body is tired of remembering. “A little.”

“Pain?” His voice stays professional, but I remember the old version of him, the one who’d look at me across a weight room and grin like we were about to do something stupid and glorious.

“Always,” I say.

He nods once, like that’s data instead of a mood. “Okay. We’ll start with range and tissue, then we’ll load it lightly. You tell me before you hit sharp.”

There’s a rule in here: pain is allowed, panic is not. You ride the line. You don’t leap off it.

He steps between my hanging left leg and my planted right, squares himself with my injured knee. “Scoot back a bit,” he says.

I shift my hips backward on the table, hands braced behind me. The paper under me crackles. The wall diagram stares.

He reaches for my right shin with both hands—one just above my ankle, the other cradling the back of my heel. His thumbs avoid the surgical hardware landmarks the way he’s been trained to. When he touches me, my skin does that stupid, immediate thing: it wakes up. A flare of awareness that has nothing to do with ligaments and everything to do with having someone’s hands on you with intention.

“Relax your quad,” he says.

I laugh once, humorless. “Sure.”

His fingers tighten slightly, not hard, just enough to communicate: *I’m holding you; you can let go*. He slides my foot forward a few inches so my heel rests on the table, leg extended. My knee straightens gradually, guided by his hands. The joint resists at the last ten degrees, where the capsule feels like it’s made of dried rope.

He supports my heel with his left palm and uses his right hand to press gently down on my thigh just above the kneecap, encouraging extension without shoving the patella. “Breathe,” he says.

I inhale. The front of my knee stretches in a slow, tight burn, like a band being pulled. Not sharp—yet—but insistent. My toes flex on their own.

“There,” he murmurs. “Hold. Five seconds.”

I stare at the diagram’s vastus medialis label while my own VMO quivers under his hand. The humiliation of it—that a muscle that used to explode me into motion now shakes like a frightened animal—mixes with the heat of his palm through the thin fabric of my shorts.

“Okay,” he says. “Ease.”

He lifts pressure and the tension slackens. Relief floods, too quick to be satisfying. He shifts his grip, sliding his right hand to the lateral side of my knee, fingers splayed along the joint line, and with his left hand he bends my knee by lifting my heel and guiding it toward my butt.

Flexion is worse. It’s always worse. The kneecap feels like it’s dragging over gravel as the joint angle closes.

“Tell me when you hit six out of ten,” he says.

He moves slowly, controlling the arc: heel gliding on the table, calf approaching the back of my thigh. The paper crinkles under my heel. The stretch builds behind my knee, then around the front as the scar tissue tightens. My breath turns shallow without permission.

“Six,” I say, voice clipped.

He stops immediately, holding my lower leg in that bent position. His forearm is close to my inner knee, but he keeps his body angled so he isn’t crowding me—professional distance even while his hands do intimate work. I hate how grateful my body is for that restraint.

“Good. Stay. Don’t fight it.” His thumb presses lightly into the soft tissue just above my kneecap, not on bone, applying a small inferior glide to help the patella track as the knee bends. It’s precise, targeted—he’s not just moving me, he’s negotiating with my anatomy.

Pain flares, hot and clean. My hands curl around the edge of the table. The urge to jerk away is immediate, animal. Instead I clench my jaw and force air out through my nose.

“You’re guarding,” he says, calm. “Let the hamstring soften.”

Guarding. Like my body is a soldier refusing orders. I feel my hamstring tendons stand out at the back of my knee, rigid cables. He shifts, bringing his right hand to the back of my ankle to keep the joint steady, and uses his left to palpate along the medial hamstring—semitendinosus—just above where it hooks near the knee.

“Here?” he asks.

His fingers sink into the muscle belly with a firm, slow pressure. The tenderness is immediate and sharp, a spark that shoots up my thigh. My whole leg tries to recoil.

“Yeah,” I manage, teeth tight.

He holds the pressure, not digging deeper, just waiting for the tissue to stop fighting. His touch is clinical, but my body interprets touch as touch, and the proximity—his torso close enough that I can smell him, soap and something warm underneath—turns the whole moment electric in a way that makes me furious at myself.

“You can swear,” he says, almost wry.

“I’m saving it,” I say.

His mouth twitches, then smooths. He’s good at that: letting the human flicker show and then locking it back down.

After a few breaths, the muscle gives a fraction. The pain changes character—less sharp, more spreading, like heat in a bruise. He eases the pressure and returns his hand to my shin.

“Now we’ll do a heel slide,” he says. “Active. You control it.”

He lowers my foot so my heel rests on the table again, knee still bent. “Dig your heel gently into the table and slide it toward you. Stop before sharp.”

I plant my heel. The hamstring engages, tentative. I slide my heel an inch, two. The knee bends, friction whispering under my shoe. It’s work just to move through that small range. My quad tries to seize in protest.

His hand hovers over my knee without touching, ready to stabilize but giving me ownership. “Good. Keep the kneecap pointing up. Don’t let it roll in.”

I watch my patella like it’s a compass. The urge is for the knee to collapse inward; my hip wants to cheat. I correct, engaging glute medius the way the diagram says it should. The movement is slow, ugly. My breath catches.

“That’s it,” he says quietly. “That’s the line. Right there.”

I slide a fraction farther and the pain spikes, sharp at the front of the joint, a warning flare.

“Stop,” he says immediately.

I freeze. My whole leg trembles. Sweat beads at my hairline. The room is too bright.

He places two fingers lightly on the top of my knee, barely any force, just contact. The gentleness of it hits harder than the pressure did. My skin registers the pads of his fingers; my nervous system, starved and angry, lights up.

“Let it settle,” he says, voice low. “You’re safe.”

Safe. The word makes something inside me twist. There’s nothing safe about losing who you were. There’s nothing safe about being reduced to inches of motion on a table under a diagram.

I swallow. “Don’t—” I start, then stop because I don’t know what I’m asking him not to do. Don’t be kind. Don’t be competent. Don’t be the version of our old life that made it out.

His gaze meets mine for a second—steady, unreadable. Then he looks back to my knee like it’s the only thing in the room.

“Okay,” he says, and the professionalism is a mercy. “We’ll add a stretch for your hip flexors. It’ll help take strain off the knee.”

He steps to the side of the table. “Lie back.”

I lower myself onto my elbows, then onto my back, shoulders sinking into the vinyl. The diagram is now above my face, the figure’s abdominal muscles perfectly defined in a way that feels like mockery.

He lifts my injured leg by the calf and heel, keeping the knee slightly bent to avoid joint compression, and slides me so my hips are closer to the table’s edge. My left leg stays bent with the foot flat on the table for stability.

He positions my right thigh so it hangs just off the edge of the table—Thomas stretch setup, I know it, I’ve done it a thousand times on locker room floors. But this time I’m not doing it myself. This time he controls the weight of my limb.

His left hand supports under my right knee, preventing it from snapping into extension; his right hand stabilizes my pelvis at the front of my hip, fingers pressing into the bony ridge of my iliac crest to keep my lower back from arching.

“Pull your left knee toward your chest,” he instructs.

I hook my hands behind my left thigh and draw it in. My low back flattens against the table under his guidance. My right thigh, hanging, lengthens at the front. The stretch blooms deep in the hip flexor—iliopsoas—an ache that feels like reaching into a place I haven’t been able to access since the injury.

“Good,” he says. “Now let this leg be heavy.”

He applies a gentle downward pressure just above my right knee, not forcing the knee joint, just increasing the hip extension. The sensation is intense, a long, clean pull that makes my breath hitch. My pelvis wants to tilt. His hand at my hip keeps it honest.

The contact is everywhere: his palm anchoring my bones, his other hand guiding my thigh, his body close enough that if I turned my head, my cheek would be inches from his forearm. I don’t turn my head. I stare at the diagram’s iliopsoas label as if naming it can make it behave.

“How’s that?” he asks.

“Tolerable,” I lie.

He adjusts a hair’s breadth—backing off, then reapplying pressure in a way that finds the exact threshold where the tissue yields without triggering my protective spasm. It’s a skill. It’s also, infuriatingly, intimate: he is learning my limits by touch.

My right hand grips the table edge. My nails press into vinyl. My throat tightens around something that isn’t pain.

He holds the stretch, steady. “You’re doing it,” he says, not encouragement, just fact.

I want to tell him I don’t want to be doing it. I want to tell him recovery feels like admitting defeat in slow motion. Instead I keep my left knee hugged in, keep my right thigh heavy, and let him keep his hands where they have to be.

His fingers shift minutely on my hip, ensuring my pelvis doesn’t rotate. The pressure is firm, controlled. My body responds the way bodies do: a tremor through the muscle as it lengthens, a flush of heat where his skin meets mine, an involuntary swallow when the stretch crosses into that sharp-sweet edge that makes you feel too much of yourself.

In another life, we would’ve turned this closeness into something else—laughing, reckless, sweat-slick and unthinking. In this life, he counts seconds.

“Ten more,” he says.

I exhale, long and shaky, and the diagram above me blurs for a moment. For those ten seconds, my world narrows to contact points and thresholds: his hand on bone, the pull in my hip, the angry whisper of my knee, the humiliating tremor of muscle being taught to work again.

When he releases, the relief is so sudden it almost feels like grief.

He steps back, giving me space, and the air where he was is cold.

“Sit up slow,” he says.

I obey, because that’s what mandatory therapy buys you: compliance. I swing my right leg back onto the table, knee stiff, and sit, shoulders hunched.

He notes something on my chart, pen scratching. The sound is ordinary. The moment isn’t.

“Same time Thursday?” he asks, still not looking at me like a person.

I watch his hands as he writes—steady, capable, no tremor. The hands that used to chalk up beside mine for deadlifts. The hands that now hold my body together in small, precise increments.

“Yeah,” I say.

He finally looks up. Something flickers there—recognition, maybe, or the memory of who I was before I became this resentful, broken thing on a table. His gaze drops, professionally, to my knee again.

“Keep icing. Do the heel slides twice a day. Don’t push into sharp,” he says.

As if I’m not already living inside sharp, every hour I’m awake.

He opens the door for me, letting me leave first. I step down carefully, weight shifting through my good leg, then cautiously into the injured one, feeling the joint accept me with reluctant, aching compliance.

The musculature diagram stays behind, perfect and unmoving.

So does he.

And I walk out carrying the heat of his hands on my skin like a bruise I can’t decide whether I want to heal.
