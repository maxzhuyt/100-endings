They had briefed Aster all the way down the gravity well: the Colony of Sile, established two hundred and nine years prior by a splinter of the Equatorial Consortium, environmentally harsh, politically delicate. Its people had adapted—some by selection, some by design—to thin air and high ultraviolet. Distinct lineages had been kept apart “for health,” “for tradition,” “for stability.” There were tensions between the Highstone communes and the River-basin assemblies, between those called the Longbreathed and those called the Deepblooded. The briefing spoke of “subspecies,” and said the word as if it were a neutral classification, a technicality like the difference between basalt and granite.

Aster’s job was to be neutral, a diplomatic surface: smooth, reflective, giving back what the other wanted to see. Aster was nineteen standard years and had never been trusted with anything that mattered until now, which was either a mark of talent or of expendability. In the receiving hall of the Governor’s House—the House cut into the living rock, all cool shadow and pink lamplight—the formalities went on without end.

There were speeches with translations that lagged a second behind, there were strings of titles like beaded necklaces, and there were the carvings, everywhere the carvings: a stone frieze above the dais showing a woman with serpent-hair holding a mirror; a doorway lintel with a figure hunched under a sphere, its back bent as if under the weight of the world. Earth myths, the briefings had said, “used as a unifying cultural referent.”

Unifying. Aster watched the Longbreathed delegates—tall, narrow-chested, their skin clouded with a faint opalescence to protect against the sun—watch the Deepblooded—stockier, dark-eyed, with the capillary flush of those who lived close to water. They did not look unified. They looked like people trained to stand in a room without moving.

At some point Aster stopped being able to tell protocol from performance. The words slid by. The air tasted of stone dust and dried herbs. In that moment of fatigue, Aster did something simple and unforgivable: when the ceremonial exchange of gifts began and everyone’s attention fixed on a latticework of hammered copper presented in the name of the Governor, Aster let their assigned aide fall behind, turned into a side corridor, and kept walking.

There was no alarm. No hand clamped down on their shoulder. There was only the corridor, the hush of rock, the coolness that seeped into the bones. Doors opened onto smaller rooms with low tables, onto storage alcoves and passages that went down, down. Aster followed the smell of air that was not conditioned and perfumed, but alive. A service door stood ajar. Through it came light.

Outside, the city did not look like a colony in the briefings. It looked old. Not ancient, not ruined, but as if it had grown there for a long time, fitted to the stone the way lichen fits to a rock. Buildings rose in steps along the slope, blocks of pale stone cut tight and clean. Between them ran narrow streets and stairs worn shiny at the edges.

The sun was sharp. Above, the sky held that particular blue that means thin air; it made the distance between things feel deceitful. Aster blinked and walked downhill until the sounds of people found them: a child’s high shout, the clatter of ceramic, the low murmur of bargaining.

The market was under awnings of woven reed and synthetic cloth. Vendors sat behind slabs of stone and wood, their goods arranged in a kind of deliberate disorder—bundles of dried fungus, beads cut from river glass, small carved figures. The air smelled of salt and roasted grain. People moved with a watchfulness that was not fear, exactly, but attention: heads turning, eyes tracking.

Aster had been told that the lineages lived apart. Yet here, in the press of bodies, there was no clear separation. A Longbreathed woman with hair braided close to her skull leaned over a tub of fish beside a Deepblooded man in a sleeveless tunic, their shoulders nearly touching. Aster saw a third kind of person too—short, sinewy, pale as ash with wide pupils—who must have been from the high caves. No one in the briefings had mentioned them.

Aster stopped at a stall where stone tiles were laid out like playing cards. Each tile bore a carved glyph: an eye, a spiral, a hook, a hand. The seller, an older person with a scar that crossed their mouth, watched Aster with a neutral gaze.

“Offworld,” they said in Trade, the consonants softened by local accent. Not a question. A statement of fact, like naming the weather.

Aster nodded. “Yes.”

The seller turned over a tile. It showed a figure with wings and a man’s face, falling.

“Who is that?” Aster asked, though they knew, or thought they did.

“You know him,” the seller said. “He thought he could be the sun.”

“Icarus,” Aster said.

The seller’s scarred mouth moved, almost a smile. “We carve him because the stone remembers. The stone tells us: don’t fly too close. Don’t go too high. Don’t forget the ground.”

Aster picked up another tile. A woman holding a thread. “Ariadne.”

“She helps,” the seller said. “But only if you listen. Only if you take the thread and don’t cut it.”

Aster looked up. Across the market, on the wall of a public cistern, a long band of glyphs ran like writing. There was Medusa again, her snakes stylized into a pattern of rivers. There was Atlas, but instead of a sphere he held a dome with a map of stars. The myths were not merely references. They were arguments, warnings, instructions.

“You put Earth stories on your walls,” Aster said, more to test the seller than from curiosity.

“We put our stories on our walls,” the seller corrected. “They came from Earth, yes. So did you. But the stone is here. So the stories are here now.”

Aster felt a small, unreasonable relief at being corrected. It meant the world could still surprise them. It meant the briefings were not the whole truth.

A group of young men passed, laughing, pushing at each other. One wore the silver throat-ring of a Governor’s House attendant, another had the red cord that marked a River-basin assembly runner. They slapped hands and separated without looking back. Aster saw, in a gap between bodies, a woman with a basket of bread stop to speak to a boy with the pale high-cave eyes; she touched his cheek with her thumb as if he were her own child. The boy flinched, then leaned into it. Social structures contradicting briefings: not neatly segregated, not purely hostile. Complicated, and alive.

Aster bought one tile—Ariadne, because of the thread—and tucked it into their pocket. They walked until their lungs began to complain at the altitude, until the narrow streets opened onto a small plaza with a dry fountain. The fountain basin was carved with glyphs of sea creatures—dolphins and whales—on a planet with no sea. Aster sat on the rim, feeling the stone’s stored cold. They listened to the city breathing.

When they returned to the Governor’s House by way of a side stair, it was too late to pretend they had simply taken a wrong turn. Their aide found them in the corridor outside their assigned quarters, face pale with controlled panic.

“Envoy,” the aide said, using Aster’s title like a shield. “Command is waiting.”

The comm unit in the room was already live. The screen showed the angular, unblinking face of Command Liaison Sen Varr, half-lit by shiplight. Behind him, the flag of the Consortium hung as if it could impose gravity.

“You were absent,” Sen Varr said. Not raised voice, not anger—worse: certainty.

“I went for a walk,” Aster said.

“You went alone.”

“Yes.”

“You broke protocol. You exposed yourself. You exposed the mission.”

Aster’s throat tightened. In the market, offworld had been a fact. Here, it was an accusation.

“I needed to see,” Aster said. “The briefings—”

“The briefings are curated intelligence,” Sen Varr cut in. “Not a travel guide. Your function is to maintain the ceremonial sequence, present our goodwill, receive their declarations, and relay them accurately. You are not here to ‘see.’”

Aster looked down at their hands. Their fingers had stone dust under the nails. They had forgotten to be clean.

Sen Varr leaned forward. “You will not repeat this. You will attend the remaining functions. And,” he paused, as if savoring the word, “you will go speak with the Minister of Science.”

Aster blinked. “The Minister of Science is part of tomorrow’s panel.”

“You will speak tonight. In private. He has requested it, after hearing of your… excursion.”

Aster’s pulse hitched. “He requested me.”

“He requested to assess you,” Sen Varr said, as if correcting a naive child. “Minister Orm is a political appointment. He holds the portfolio that manages lineage policy. He is not a scientist; he is a gate. You will go to him and you will not improvise. Understood?”

Aster’s mouth was dry. “Understood.”

Sen Varr’s gaze sharpened. “One more thing. Do not mention what you observed in the streets. It is not relevant.”

The screen went dark.

Aster stood for a long moment, hearing the faint hum of the Governor’s House vents. In their pocket, Ariadne’s tile pressed against their thigh like a secret. They thought of the thread and the maze and the part the hero never told: that the thread is given, not taken.

Minister Orm’s rooms were in a wing that looked outward over the city. The corridor was lined with stone plaques carved in glyphs, each plaque lit from below. Aster recognized one: a figure with a mouth full of stars.

“Cassandra,” murmured the attendant who led them. “She tells and is not believed.”

The door opened.

Minister Orm was not old, but he had the kind of settled face that meant he had been in power long enough to be bored by it. His skin was the honey-brown of someone from the middle altitudes; his eyes were very dark. He rose as Aster entered, not to honor them but to control the room by moving first.

“Envoy Aster,” he said in careful Trade. “Welcome. Sit.”

There was no offer of drink. No small talk. The room itself was spare: stone walls, a table of polished basalt, shelves holding a few objects that looked ceremonial rather than personal. On the far wall a carving showed a man with a lion’s skin over his shoulders, club raised.

“Hercules,” Aster said before they could stop themselves.

Orm’s gaze flicked to the carving, then back to Aster. “Yes. Strength. Labor. A useful myth for officials.”

Aster sat, hands on their knees. “Command told me you wished to assess me.”

Orm’s mouth curved slightly. “Command told you? Good. We like clear channels. We have had trouble with channels here. With lineages, with assemblies, with the Highstone communes. With offworlders bringing us solutions we did not ask for.”

Aster kept their face still. “I am here to listen.”

“To listen,” Orm repeated, as if tasting the word and finding it thin. “And yet you walked the streets alone. That is not listening. That is—what do you call it?—fieldwork.”

Aster felt heat in their cheeks. “I wanted to understand the place I’m speaking to.”

“Understanding is dangerous,” Orm said calmly. “It complicates policy. Tell me, Envoy: what did you see in the market?”

Aster hesitated. Sen Varr’s order pressed like a hand on their spine. But Orm’s question was a hook; to refuse to answer would be to be caught, and to answer falsely would be to be owned.

“I saw people,” Aster said. “Living together more than our intelligence suggests.”

Orm’s eyes narrowed, satisfied, not surprised. “You saw what you were not supposed to see.”

“I saw that the lineages aren’t sealed compartments,” Aster said, finding their voice. “Not in ordinary life.”

Orm leaned back. “Ordinary life,” he said, as if it were a concept offworlders carried in their luggage. “Do you know why we created the Ministry of Science?”

“To oversee adaptation policy,” Aster said, reciting.

“To name things,” Orm corrected. “To make categories. ‘Longbreathed.’ ‘Deepblooded.’ ‘Subspecies.’ Words with sharp edges. If you can name, you can count. If you can count, you can ration. If you can ration, you can rule.”

Aster’s mouth went slightly open. They shut it. “So the Ministry is not about science.”

Orm smiled without warmth. “Science is a story we tell to make power sound inevitable. We carve Hercules on the wall to make labor sound noble. We carve Cassandra to make dissent sound doomed. We carve Medusa to make the enemy monstrous.” He gestured toward the shelves. On one sat a small stone head with snakes for hair, the eyes blank. “Do you know why that one is here?”

“To ward off evil,” Aster said, remembering a childhood lesson.

“To remind me,” Orm said softly. “That anyone can be turned into a monster by a story.”

Aster felt the thread in their pocket like a tug. “Then why request me tonight? Why test me?”

Orm’s gaze held theirs. “Because you are young. Because you walked alone. Because you might not be fully trained into your own story yet.” He leaned forward. “Offworlder: tell me what you will report.”

Aster’s heart thudded. The easy answer—what Command wanted—rose to their tongue: that the city was tense, the lineages volatile, the Ministry competent, the Governor cooperative. But that answer would be a cut thread. It would lead them deeper into a maze built by other people.

Aster looked at Orm’s face, at the calmness that could be kindness or cruelty depending on what it needed to be. “I will report what is useful,” Aster said carefully, “and what is true.”

Orm’s eyebrows rose. “Both? That is ambitious.”

Aster swallowed. “Truth is useful. Eventually.”

For the first time Orm laughed, a short sound. “Eventually,” he echoed. Then he sobered. “You will learn that ‘eventually’ is where governments bury inconvenient facts. Listen to me, Envoy. Your Command wants a clean narrative: subspecies in conflict, Ministry as mediator, Consortium as benevolent arbiter. My rivals want a different narrative: offworld interference, lineage pride, resistance. The streets you walked have their own narrative, which nobody in this room is paid to hear.”

Aster’s hands tightened on their knees. “But I heard it.”

Orm’s eyes sharpened again. “Did you? Or did you hear what pleased your conscience? The market can look like unity because people must eat. Hunger makes treaties that politics breaks.”

Aster thought of the woman touching the boy’s cheek. That hadn’t been hunger. That had been something else—something like kinship, or pity, or habit. Ordinary life. “It wasn’t only food,” Aster said.

Orm watched them a long moment. “Good,” he said finally, as if marking a point on an invisible tally. “You’re not entirely made of slogans.”

He stood, ending the conversation as he had begun it, by moving first. He walked to the window and looked out at the stepped stone city, the lights beginning to come on like low stars.

“You want to be a thread-holder, Envoy,” he said without turning. “Ariadne with her ball of string. But remember: the thread can also be a noose. If you report too much truth, you will hang yourself. If you report too little, you will hang us, slowly.”

Aster rose as well, compelled by the weight of his words. “What do you want from me, Minister?”

Orm turned then, and his face in the low light looked carved, as if he too were one of the glyphs on the wall—an emblem of something older than a job title.

“I want to know,” he said, “whether you will be a mouthpiece or a person. Whether you will fly like Icarus because you think you can touch the sun, or whether you will learn the shape of the ground.”

Aster felt again the stone under their fingers at the fountain rim, cold and patient. “I don’t know yet,” they admitted.

Orm nodded, as if that were the only honest answer he could respect. “Then you may be worth the trouble.” He gestured toward the door. “Go back to your quarters. Attend your functions. Smile the right smiles. And when you can, walk again—but not alone. Take someone from the streets with you. Learn their story from their mouth, not from my ministry’s categories.”

As Aster left, the corridor plaques seemed to watch. Cassandra with her star-filled mouth. Medusa with her river-snakes. Atlas bending under a dome of mapped stars. The myths were not unifying, Aster thought. They were warnings carved into stone: about power, about pride, about seeing what you are not supposed to see.

In their pocket, Ariadne’s tile warmed against their skin, as if the thread had been there all along, waiting for a hand willing to hold it without cutting.
