The iron throne had survived three apocalypses, a civil war, and an unfortunate fad in ceremonial flaming swords. It was being undone, leg by leg, by a hellhound named Pancake.

Lucifer Morningstar watched him work with the tired fascination one reserved for slow-moving disasters and reality television. Pancake had braced his paws against the basalt floor, jaw locked around the throne’s front leg as if it had personally insulted his lineage. He chewed with devotion. The throne protested in a small, metallic squeal.

“Stop that,” Lucifer said.

Pancake wagged, never releasing the iron. His tail thumped like a drumbeat for the damned.

Lucifer leaned back, the throne shifting under him in a way thrones should not. “I said stop that.”

The hellhound’s eyes met his—molten amber, innocent as a child with a stolen cookie. He chewed harder.

From the far side of the hall, a minor demon in a clerk’s collar cleared its throat. “My Lord?”

Lucifer kept his gaze on Pancake. “If this is about the requisition forms for the ninth circle’s lava—”

“It’s about the ninth circle’s lava,” the demon said, because the universe was rarely creative in its torments. “It’s coagulating. Again. The damned are complaining.”

“The damned are always complaining,” Lucifer said. “It’s their whole brand.”

“Yes, My Lord, but there’s a petition.”

Lucifer closed his eyes. He could hear, distantly, the papery rustle of bureaucratic suffering: quills scratching, stamps slamming, the sweet shriek of a misplaced semicolon. Hell ran on torment, true, but it ran on paperwork more.

“Tell them we’re pivoting to a more artisanal magma experience,” Lucifer said. “Hand-stirred. Limited batches. Put it on a chalkboard.”

“My Lord,” the demon ventured, “they’ve eaten the chalkboard.”

Lucifer opened his eyes. “Of course they have. Put it on a non-edible surface.”

Pancake’s chewing reached a new pitch of enthusiasm. There was a sound like a cathedral groaning.

Lucifer leaned forward and gently pried the hellhound’s jaw off the throne with the sort of careful patience one used on exploding artifacts. Pancake immediately latched onto Lucifer’s sleeve instead, teeth sinking into expensive black cloth.

“Wonderful,” Lucifer said, calm as a man noticing his house was on fire. “Now you’re eating my wardrobe.”

Pancake released him and sneezed a small puff of brimstone. The sleeve smoked.

A shadow moved at the edge of the great hall—subtle, but Hell’s light was always infernal and full of tricks. The air shifted. Perfume, old blood, night-blooming flowers that should not have grown.

Lucifer didn’t need to turn to know.

Lilith’s footsteps were unhurried, each one a statement. She crossed the basalt like she owned it, which was, inconveniently, a point she’d made with weapons in the past.

“You’re early,” Lucifer said.

“I’m not on a schedule,” Lilith replied. Her voice held the soft edge of a knife that had been sharpened lovingly. “That’s kind of the point.”

She looked him over, her gaze pausing on the singed sleeve and then on Pancake, who had sat down and begun licking his paws as if he’d achieved something spiritual.

“That thing is still alive,” Lilith said.

Lucifer’s mouth twitched. “He’s thriving.”

Lilith’s eyes flicked to the throne leg—now bearing a crescent of fresh tooth marks. “Your symbol of tyrannical authority is being eaten.”

“It’s more of a suggestion of authority these days,” Lucifer said. “If you’ve come to kill me, do it before lunch. I’ve got the quarterly torment metrics at two.”

Lilith’s expression suggested she had once considered killing him an excellent hobby and still had the equipment. She walked closer, trailing her fingers along the throne’s armrest as if assessing its structural integrity. It squealed faintly, like a guilty secret.

“I didn’t come to kill you,” she said, and it was almost disappointing how casually she said it. “I came because your imp sent me a message.”

Lucifer frowned. “Which imp?”

“The one with the nervous smile,” Lilith said. “So. All of them.”

Lucifer glanced at the clerk demon, who had begun to pretend it was part of the architecture.

“What did it say?” Lucifer asked.

Lilith tilted her head. “It said you were… lonely.”

Lucifer’s expression did not change. His pride was a well-maintained fortress, complete with boiling moats and a gift shop. “I’m not lonely.”

Lilith raised an eyebrow. “You’re talking to a dog that’s eating your throne.”

Lucifer looked down at Pancake. Pancake met his gaze and yawned, revealing teeth like gravestones.

“He listens,” Lucifer said. “And he doesn’t bring petitions.”

Lilith’s smile was small, sharp, and a little sad in a way she would deny under oath. “Is that what this is? A cry for help?”

“I don’t cry,” Lucifer said. “It ruins the aesthetic.”

Lilith stepped around the throne. Her dress was the color of old bruises. Her hair fell like a threat.

“You could replace the throne,” she mused, tapping the chewed leg. “You always loved symbolism. Get something new. Something softer.”

“Softness is not traditionally my brand,” Lucifer said. “Hell’s customers expect a certain… firmness.”

Lilith looked at him. “And what do you expect?”

Lucifer considered telling her to leave. He considered telling her to stay. Both options had consequences that tasted familiar.

Before he could choose, Pancake stood, stretched, and resumed gnawing on the throne leg with renewed purpose. The iron gave a little.

Lucifer’s patience, which was already a finite resource, frayed. “Pancake, no.”

Pancake wagged harder, as if encouraged.

Lilith watched this with a kind of delighted horror. “You named it Pancake.”

“He was flatter when I found him,” Lucifer said. “It seemed respectful.”

Lilith’s laugh was quiet, unexpected. It warmed the air for half a second and then Hell remembered itself.

“So,” she said, “what does the King of Hell do all day when he’s not busy being tragically misunderstood?”

Lucifer leaned back again, carefully shifting his weight so as not to encourage structural collapse. “I manage. I oversee torment allocation. I adjudicate disputes between demons who have discovered the concept of ‘boundaries’ and are now weaponizing it. I sign forms in triplicate because someone decided Hell should have checks and balances.”

Lilith’s gaze slid toward the clerk demon. “Does he always talk like that?”

“He’s a demon,” Lucifer said. “They talk in systems.”

“And you?” Lilith asked.

Lucifer’s eyes stayed on the horizon of his own kingdom, where distant fires painted the cavern roofs like sunsets. “I try not to think about the silence when the screams stop.”

Lilith’s expression shifted, almost imperceptibly. The irony of Hell was that it was loud enough to hide loneliness until it wasn’t.

She moved to stand beside the throne, close enough that Lucifer could smell her—danger and something domestic, like dust in sunlight.

“You could get rid of the dog,” Lilith said, as if discussing a stain.

Lucifer looked down at Pancake. “He’s loyal.”

Lilith’s mouth twisted. “Loyalty is overrated.”

“Is it?” Lucifer asked softly.

Lilith’s eyes flashed. “Don’t.”

Lucifer held her gaze, because pushing her away was a habit and a comfort. “It’s interesting,” he said, “how you show up when you’re not here to fight.”

Lilith’s fingers drummed once on the throne arm. The iron rang. “Maybe I’m bored.”

“Maybe you miss me,” Lucifer said.

Lilith scoffed with practiced ease. “Don’t flatter yourself.”

The clerk demon coughed again, desperate to return them to the comfort of procedure. “My Lord, about the petition—”

Lucifer’s stare could have unmade it. The demon shrank into itself.

Lilith’s eyes followed the demon. “You’ve turned Hell into an office.”

Lucifer sighed. “It’s either that or chaos.”

Lilith smiled, almost fond. “Chaos suited you.”

“It still does,” Lucifer said. “It’s just… scheduled now.”

Pancake suddenly growled, low and pleased, and the throne leg gave way with a loud crack. The throne lurched. Lucifer grabbed the armrest to steady himself; Lilith, reflexively, placed a hand on his shoulder.

For a moment, they were close enough that old wars felt like foreplay.

Lucifer looked up at her hand, then at her face. “Well,” he said dryly, “at least someone in this relationship is finally supporting me.”

Lilith’s eyes narrowed. “You’re insufferable.”

“And you’re here,” Lucifer replied.

She removed her hand as if it had burned her, then hesitated, as if not entirely sure what to do with her own fingers. “I didn’t come to make this… whatever this is.”

Lucifer gestured broadly at the hall, the flames, the bureaucracy, the throne being eaten. “Welcome to my life.”

Lilith looked at Pancake, who had triumphantly dragged the broken chunk of iron away like a trophy. “You could train him,” she said.

Lucifer stared at the ruined throne leg. “I’m the Devil,” he said. “I’m not good at training.”

Lilith’s lips curved. “I could train him.”

Lucifer arched an eyebrow. “You?”

Lilith’s smile turned wicked. “I have experience with beasts.”

Pancake trotted over to Lilith, tail wagging, iron chunk clanging against the floor. He dropped it at her feet and sat, looking up at her with adoration that was frankly unearned.

Lilith blinked. “Traitor.”

Pancake sneezed, coating her shoes in ash.

Lilith stared down at her shoes, then up at Lucifer. “Your pet is disgusting.”

Lucifer’s grin was real, despite himself. “He takes after his owner.”

Lilith’s gaze held his for a beat too long. Something softened, infinitesimal and dangerous.

“Fine,” she said, with the air of someone making a concession in a war she intended to win. “I’ll stay for lunch.”

Lucifer’s loneliness shifted, rearranging itself like furniture moved slightly to accommodate company. “Hell’s cafeteria is serving despair casserole,” he said. “And—” he glanced at the clerk demon, who was still holding the petition like a life raft, “—apparently, chalk.”

Lilith’s nose wrinkled. “Do you ever eat?”

Lucifer stood carefully, the throne wobbling behind him like an embarrassed relic. “Sometimes,” he said. “It depends who I’m with.”

Lilith turned away first, because she always did, leading the way as if this was her kingdom too. Pancake bounded after her, chewing on his iron prize.

Lucifer followed, letting the clerk demon trail behind with its petition, because Hell would keep running whether or not anyone was happy.

As they walked, Lilith glanced back once. “Don’t think this means anything,” she said.

Lucifer’s smile was understated, the sort of irony that kept the universe from taking itself too seriously. “Of course not,” he replied. “It’s just lunch.”

Behind them, the iron throne gave a final, mournful creak and toppled sideways with the slow dignity of a fallen empire.

Pancake barked, delighted.

“Add ‘throne replacement’ to the agenda,” Lucifer called over his shoulder.

The clerk demon scribbled furiously, soot smearing the parchment.

Lilith watched Lucifer watch the chaos, and for a fleeting moment her expression suggested she remembered why she’d ever loved him.

Hell, as it turned out, was other demons.

And occasionally, it was lunch.
