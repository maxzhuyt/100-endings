The rain had stopped sometime before dawn, but Canton still wore it—every tile and plank sweating last night’s water back into the air. From the factory balcony the Pearl River looked like tarnished metal, sluggish with sampans and the low, patient creak of oars. Somewhere below, a hawker called out breakfast in a sing-song that snagged on the corners of my skull.

“Lieutenant Merrick.”

I turned before my name finished echoing. Midshipman Hargreaves stood in the doorway, his cheeks already pink from humidity and earnestness, his collar too clean for this place.

“You’re wanted aboard the *Vigilant*, sir. Captain’s asking after the dispatches.”

My tongue felt thick, as if I’d slept with a wad of cotton between cheek and gum. I swallowed. The act had a sound.

“Tell the captain I’ve a matter ashore. The supercargo—” I began, then had to start again, slower. “The supercargo has a complaint. I’ll attend by noon.”

Hargreaves’ gaze flicked to my hands. I was already worrying the jade in my pocket, thumb grinding a polished curve that should have been a comfort and had become a tell. I curled my fingers.

“Yes, sir,” he said, and there it was—the faint pause that meant he had noticed something he wouldn’t name.

When he’d gone, I stood on the balcony and tried to be a man. The air smelt of wet wood and river mud and, faintly, the sweet rancid breath of boiled sugar from the godowns. Somewhere in my body, a clock began to tick louder: a quickening in the gut, a prickle under the skin, the first thin wire of dread drawing tight behind my eyes.

I descended the stairs into the narrow passage between the foreign factories, boots slipping on stone worn smooth by years of hurried commerce. British voices and American voices mingled with Cantonese—laughter, bargaining, someone swearing in a language that had no business being so jaunty. A coolie brushed past with a shoulder pole; the baskets at either end carried something that smelt like fish and iron.

At the corner by the Danish flag, I found Collins.

He leaned against a post as if he’d grown there, hat tipped low, his whiskers damp at the ends. Collins had once been a ship’s clerk; now he was an errand, a whisper, a man who could procure anything provided you didn’t insist on knowing how.

“You look ill,” he said without preamble.

“I haven’t slept,” I lied.

He showed his teeth. “You haven’t smoked.”

The jade bit into my palm. “What do you have for me?”

Collins spat into the gutter with careful aim. “Same as yesterday. Same as tomorrow. But the price shifts, James. The mandarins sniffing about like dogs. Lin’s men—” He jerked his chin toward the city wall, invisible beyond the press of roofs. “—making examples. Some of the smaller dens closed up. The big ones are dear.”

“My purse doesn’t grow because Commissioner Lin fancies himself virtuous,” I said, and heard the sharpness in my own voice.

Collins’ eyes did not soften. “Then you’ll need to make it grow.”

I took a breath through my nose, slow, measured like a gunnery exercise. The air filled me with rot and spice.

“What do you want?” I asked.

“What I want?” He laughed softly. “I want not to be caught between your Navy and their law. But I’ve a job. A small one. A letter.”

“A letter,” I repeated. My stomach tightened as if it understood before my brain.

“A letter carried from the factories to a certain godown, no questions. You do that, and I’ll see you into a place that still serves the good stuff. None of that cut rubbish the river boys sell.”

My thumb found the jade’s familiar ridge and rubbed until the skin warmed. “Whose letter?”

Collins’ smile thinned. “You’re particular today.”

I could feel the day already unraveling—noon, dispatches, the captain’s eyes. But the wire behind my eyes had turned into a saw.

“Fine,” I said. “Give it.”

He produced it from inside his coat as if conjuring—an envelope of thick paper, the seal unbroken. No name. Only a red ink mark like a careless thumbprint.

“You’ll deliver it to a man called Wu,” Collins said. “At the godown behind the fish market. You’ll say: *The tide turns at second watch.* He’ll know you’re mine.”

“I’m not yours,” I said.

Collins lifted his shoulders. “Today you are.”

The streets nearer the city were narrower, the foreign order giving way to Canton’s own crowded logic: stalls pressed against walls, awnings sagging with water, baskets of lychees and ducks hanging upside down. The air changed every ten steps—ginger, incense, sewage, frying oil, damp cloth.

A vendor held up a lacquered box. “Sahib! Good luck. Good box.”

“I’ve luck enough,” I muttered, and walked on.

Withdrawal made the world too loud. Each shout struck like a slap; each smell was a hand closing around my throat. Sweat gathered under my collar despite the relative cool of morning. My joints ached, not like pain but like a memory of pain, an insistence.

At the fish market, the river smell rose heavy and obscene. Slabs of carp lay open like books, their flesh gleaming. Flies made a black lace over everything. Men in blue jackets moved with quick economy, knives flashing. I kept my gaze forward, as if the act of not looking could make me invisible.

A boy no older than twelve sidled near, bare feet silent on the wet stone.

“You want find godown?” he asked in pidgin, eyes darting to my uniform coat—plain enough, but it marked me.

“No,” I said too fast.

He smiled, showing a gap where a tooth had been. “You want find *wu*,” he whispered, almost a hiss.

I stopped. My hand went to the jade without permission.

“Who sent you?” I demanded.

The boy shrugged. “Everybody see foreign man walking wrong place. Come. I show. You pay.”

“How much?”

He held up three fingers.

“Three cash?” I scoffed.

He shook his head solemnly. “Three dollars.”

I barked a laugh that sounded like it hurt. “You’ll take three cash and be grateful.”

He looked at me for a long moment, then nodded as if indulging an eccentric. “Three cash. Come.”

He led me through an alley that smelled of piss and star anise, past a doorway where an old woman sat folding paper offerings with fingers like bird claws. The city pressed close; above, laundry hung between buildings like flags of surrender.

At last we reached a plank door set into a wall blackened by smoke. No sign. No lantern. Only two men idling nearby as if they were always idle, their eyes too awake.

The boy pointed and vanished as if swallowed by the air.

I approached. One of the men stepped in my path. His hair was oiled back, his face expressionless.

“What?” he said in Cantonese, then in rough English: “You want buy fish?”

I took the envelope from inside my coat. My fingers trembled. I hated that.

“I have a message,” I said. “The tide turns at second watch.”

Something shifted in his gaze, not surprise but calculation. He held out his hand, palm up.

“No,” I said. “For Wu.”

The man’s mouth twitched. “Wu busy.”

“I’m busier,” I snapped, then regretted it immediately. Anger was a luxury for men whose nerves did not feel flayed.

The second man moved behind me. I could feel him there, a presence at my back like a blade.

The first man took the envelope anyway, smoothly, as if my refusal had been a formality. He weighed it, eyes narrowing at the red mark. Then, without looking away from me, he called something over his shoulder.

A woman appeared from the shadows behind the door. She wore plain clothes but her hair was pinned with a jade ornament that caught what little light there was. Her gaze travelled over me—uniform coat, boots, the sheen of sweat. It lingered at my mouth, as if she could taste my need.

“You are Merrick,” she said in careful English.

I stiffened. “Who are you?”

“Wu’s sister,” she replied. “He sends you thanks.” Her eyes flicked to the envelope. “He sends you also question. Why British officer carry letter like runner?”

I swallowed. The jade pressed into my palm hard enough to hurt. “Because it was asked of me.”

She nodded as if that answered everything. “You want something.”

“No,” I began automatically, then stopped. The lie felt foolish in that damp, fish-stinking alley.

“Yes,” I said. “I want—” The word opium felt too raw to put in the air. “Smoke.”

The woman’s expression did not change, but her eyes sharpened. “Money?”

“I have some,” I said. Not enough. Not for what I needed.

She looked past me, down the alley. “You have uniform. You have access. You have eyes in foreign factories.”

I felt cold despite the sweat. “I’m not an informer.”

She gave a small sound that might have been amusement. “Everyone informs. Even your body informs on you. Look.” She tapped her own wrist lightly, mimicking a pulse. “Too fast. Your hands—” She gestured subtly. “You shake.”

I forced my fingers still around the jade. My thumb rubbed its smooth belly until my skin threatened to split.

“What do you want?” I asked again, hearing Collins’ words echoed with different teeth.

She stepped closer. I could smell camphor on her breath, clean and medicinal against the market’s rot.

“There is a chest,” she said. “In the American godown. Marked tea. Not tea. It goes tonight to the river. We want it. Wu wants it. You tell when the watch changes. You tell which door they use.”

“That’s theft,” I said.

She tilted her head. “It is commerce.”

“It’s—” My voice broke, irritation and desperation tangling. “If I’m caught—”

“If you are caught,” she interrupted, “you are British. You go back to ship. Maybe you lose face. Maybe you lose job.” Her gaze pinned me. “If Chinese caught, he loses head.”

The sweat on my back turned to a chill.

She held out a small packet wrapped in oil paper, no larger than my palm. I could smell it even through the wrapping: that sweet, dark promise like burnt sugar and flowers gone wrong.

My mouth watered. My stomach clenched.

“You do this,” she said softly, “and you get more. You do not, you find smoke somewhere else. Maybe you find bad smoke. Maybe you find none.”

Time narrowed to that packet, to the aching hollowness under my ribs. The world outside the alley—ships, dispatches, captain—felt like a story I’d once read and half forgotten.

“I can’t be seen going into a den,” I said, the words scraped out of me. “Not in uniform.”

She glanced at my coat. “Then don’t wear it.”

I laughed once, bitter. “I’m a naval officer.”

“You are a man who shakes,” she corrected, and placed the packet into my hand.

The paper was warm from her fingers. My thumb found the jade again automatically, as if to ask its permission. It offered none.

“What is your name?” I asked, because names felt like anchors.

“Lan,” she said. “Go now. Before you change into something brave.”

I pocketed the packet and, for a moment, felt the thin relief of having the cure within reach without yet having taken it—the promise of silence to come.

As I turned to leave, Lan’s voice followed me, low and precise.

“Lieutenant,” she said, tasting the title. “Second watch. Which door.”

I did not look back. The alley spat me out into the market’s noise and stench. Somewhere beyond the roofs, a ship’s bell rang faintly over the river—metal on air, marking time I was no longer keeping.

My hand closed around the jade in my pocket until the edge left an imprint in my skin.

“Noon,” I murmured to myself, though it sounded like a prayer and not a plan. “By noon.”

But my feet were already carrying me away from the factories, away from duty, toward the place where smoke would soften the world’s sharp corners and, for a little while, make me forget the taste of my own name.
