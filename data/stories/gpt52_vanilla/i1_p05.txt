### Chapter Two: The Cabin That Wasn’t on the Map

By the time they got back to the lodge the light had changed, going flat and pewter-colored, the kind of afternoon that made you feel like the day was already half over and you hadn’t done anything with it. The sky sat low over the trees like a lid. The lodge’s windows reflected the woods instead of the other way around, and for a second JT had the stupid, unreasonable thought that if he walked up to the glass and pressed his face to it, he’d see the forest looking back from inside the room.

He told himself that was just nerves. Hunger. Cold.

Inside, everything smelled like it always had: wet wool steaming by the stove, old bacon grease, gun oil, the sharp pine-cleaner his mother used back home but which, here, always seemed to come from nowhere. His father unwrapped the rifles with the careful reverence of a priest opening a reliquary. Mike went straight for the beer cooler.

JT hung back, unlacing his boots, fingers clumsy. He kept seeing that moment in the clearing—the buck’s eyes, the way his father’s hand had lifted like he was about to pet a dog. He kept waiting for his father to say something about it, to crack a joke, to blame the wind, to curse his own hesitation. Anything that would make the whole thing fit back into the world.

But his father only said, “We’ll go out again at first light. They’re moving.”

Mike snorted. “Yeah. They’re moving all right.”

JT looked up. “What’s that mean?”

Mike’s mouth twitched like he was deciding whether to be nice. He was three years older, which in their family might as well have been thirty. “Means it’s gonna be a good season. They’re not skittish. Like they know what’s coming.” He took a swallow of beer. “Dumb as stumps.”

Their father’s gaze flicked over. Not quite a warning. Not quite approval. Something in between.

JT almost asked if either of them had noticed—if they remembered—what happened with the buck. Almost. But the question stuck in his throat like a fishbone. Saying it out loud would make it real. Or worse, it would make *him* the thing that didn’t fit.

So he said, “I’m gonna grab some wood.”

His father nodded without looking up from the rifles. “Don’t go far.”

That should’ve been normal. But the way he said it—flat, clipped—made JT’s stomach tighten.

Outside, the air had that early-winter bite that made each breath feel like it scraped the back of your throat. The sun was a pale coin behind cloud cover, not warming anything. JT crossed to the woodpile and started stacking split logs into his arms. The lodge sat at the edge of a clearing. Past that, the trees crowded in, black spruce and birch like a wall of bristling teeth.

He saw the tracks again near the edge of the snow—caribou prints, wide and neat. And something else. A drag mark, maybe. Or the impression of something heavy being pulled. It led toward the treeline and vanished under a scatter of needles.

JT told himself it was a fallen branch. A sled. Something from last season.

But he was holding still without meaning to, listening.

The forest had a way of making silence feel like a presence. Not quiet, exactly. More like…waiting. The lodge behind him creaked, settling, and he jumped so hard a log slipped and thumped his boot.

“Jesus,” he muttered, and laughed under his breath like that made it okay.

He started back with the wood, then stopped again because he realized he could smell smoke.

Not from the lodge stove—that smoke was clean and faint. This was different. Old, damp, like wet leaves burning. It came from the woods, thin as a thread, the kind of smell you only noticed when you weren’t supposed to.

He stood there with the armload of logs and stared at the treeline. The thought came uninvited: *someone’s out there*.

Then another thought, worse: *they’ve been there the whole time*.

He carried the wood inside quickly, almost jogging, and dumped it by the stove.

Mike was at the table cleaning his rifle with a rag. Their father had gone quiet, staring into the flames like he was reading something in them.

JT said, “You ever smell smoke out there? Like…not ours.”

Mike didn’t look up. “Probably some idiot camped too close to the river. Locals do it sometimes.”

Their father said, “No locals this far in.”

That should’ve been reassuring. It wasn’t. JT opened his mouth to ask what he meant, but his father stood abruptly and went to the pantry. He came out with a tin of coffee and set it on the counter with a hard little clank, as if the conversation had been nailed shut.

That night, after stew and whiskey for them and cocoa for JT (because he was “still a kid” even though he could shoot as straight as either of them), the wind picked up. It pushed at the lodge, making the old boards groan. Somewhere in the walls a mouse—or something bigger—scratched and then stopped, as if it realized it was being listened to.

JT lay in his bunk in the small room he shared with Mike, staring at the underside of the top bunk slats. Mike’s breathing had gone heavy, almost immediately. Their father slept in the room across the hall. JT could hear the faint creak of his bed when he turned over.

He tried not to think about the buck.

He tried not to think about the first caribou, the way it had stood and waited.

He tried not to think about the smell of smoke.

But thoughts were like that up here. They didn’t move the way they did back home. They got stuck. They circled. They came back to the same place no matter how far you tried to walk away from them.

At some point—he didn’t know when—JT heard a sound that didn’t belong to the lodge.

A knock.

Not on the door. Not on the wall. Something else. A soft, hollow tapping, as if someone was rapping their knuckles on a tree trunk.

Tap…tap…tap.

JT sat up, blankets sliding off his lap. He held his breath.

The tapping stopped.

He waited. Long enough that his eyes began to water.

Then it came again, farther away. Tap…tap…tap, like a signal being passed down the line.

He looked over at Mike. His brother’s face was turned to the wall, asleep and slack. JT watched for any sign he’d heard it too. Nothing.

JT swung his feet to the floor. The boards were icy under his socks. He moved to the window, careful not to wake Mike. The glass was cold and slightly wavy, old. He pulled the curtain back a crack.

Outside, the clearing was silvered by moonlight, the snow reflecting just enough to see shapes. The woodpile. The dark bulk of the truck. The treeline beyond.

And something else.

At first he thought it was a stump at the edge of the woods. Then it shifted, just a fraction, and he realized it was an animal standing perfectly still. A caribou, maybe. Or a moose calf. It was hard to tell in the dim.

It lifted its head.

JT’s heart stuttered. The animal’s eyes caught the moonlight and flashed pale.

*Looking at me,* he thought.

Then—because his brain was doing what it always did when it got scared—it supplied something even worse: *Waiting.*

He swallowed, throat clicking. He wanted to wake Mike. He wanted to go get his father. He wanted to hide under the blankets and pretend none of it was happening.

Instead, he pressed his forehead to the glass like an idiot and stared.

The animal didn’t move. It didn’t blink. It didn’t run. It simply held its place at the edge of the trees, as if the clearing was a stage and it had been told to hit its mark.

Tap…tap…tap.

This time it was closer, and JT realized with a small lurch that the tapping wasn’t on a tree at all.

It was on the side of the lodge.

From outside.

A gentle knocking, like a polite visitor.

JT jerked back from the window, breath coming too fast. He listened, every hair on his arms standing up.

Again: tap…tap…tap.

He took one shaky step toward the bedroom door, meaning to cross the hall and wake his father. But as he reached for the knob, he froze.

Because he heard his father’s voice, low and thick with sleep, coming from the other side of the hall.

Not calling out. Not startled.

Talking.

Like he was answering someone.

JT leaned close to the crack of his door. The hall was dim, lit by a thin stripe of moonlight from the living-room window.

His father said, “Not yet.”

A pause. Silence heavy enough to feel.

Then his father again, softer, almost pleading: “Not the boy.”

JT’s mouth went dry. His fingers tightened on the doorknob until it hurt.

He told himself his father was dreaming. Talking in his sleep. People did that.

But then came another voice.

Not loud. Not clear. More like a sound with the shape of words. A whispering scrape, like wind through dead grass.

JT couldn’t make out what it said.

His father did, apparently. He answered, and this time there was a tremor in it, the same tremor JT had seen in his hand reaching for the buck.

“No,” his father said. “We do it right. We always do it right.”

Then a long pause, and the whispering voice seemed to drift farther down the hall, away from his father’s door, away from the lodge itself.

JT realized he was shaking.

He backed away from the door and sat on his bunk again, hard. The blankets felt thin and useless.

Mike rolled over, half-awake. “What’re you doing?”

JT stared at him. For a second he considered telling him everything: the tapping, the animal, their father talking to *something*. All of it.

But Mike’s eyes were cloudy with sleep, impatient already.

JT heard his own voice come out small. “Nothing. Just…couldn’t sleep.”

Mike grunted and turned his face back to the wall. “Don’t start with that spooky shit. It’s just the wind.”

JT lay down again, staring at the ceiling. His heart wouldn’t slow.

He thought about something old Mr. Lasky from town used to say, years ago, when JT was little and the men would drink at the hardware store before heading up to the lodge.

*Don’t go near the abandoned cabin on South Ridge,* Lasky had said, wagging a yellowed finger like it was a joke. *That place ain’t empty, even when it is.*

JT had asked his dad about it once. His father had gone stiff, then laughed too loudly and said, “Just a story to scare kids.”

But now, in the lodge’s thin, creaking dark, JT saw that cabin in his mind as clearly as if it were drawn in ink: a squat little hunting shack with a collapsed porch, a stovepipe that leaned like a broken finger, and a door that never stayed shut even when you latched it.

Rumor said somebody had holed up there after a bad season. Rumor said you could see smoke some nights even though nobody ever claimed it. Rumor said if you went close, you’d hear tapping from inside, like a person trying to get your attention through the walls.

JT listened.

No more tapping now. Only the wind worrying the trees.

But he couldn’t shake the feeling that something had come to the lodge, had stood outside and knocked politely, and his father had answered it like it was an old friend.

And in the morning, when they went back out into the woods, JT knew—without knowing how he knew—that they’d be heading toward South Ridge.

Toward the cabin that wasn’t supposed to have anyone in it.

Toward whatever had been waiting in the clearing, calm as a priest, with dark eyes and no fear of the gun.
