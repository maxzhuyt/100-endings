Astrid said Útiseta was simple, if you didn’t make it complicated.

“You sit out,” she told me earlier that evening, as if explaining how to microwave a kåldolme. “Alone. Quiet. You don’t look back when you hear things. And you don’t talk first.”

We were in her room, door shut, the thin walls of her family’s house breathing in the sounds of television and her little brother’s laughter from downstairs. Her posters—Roxette, Nirvana, a faded moose from a tourist magazine—made her look like any other girl in any other place. But her desk drawer didn’t. She slid it open with her foot and showed me what she’d collected: a stub of tallow candle wrapped in paper, a small pouch stitched from worn leather, a bundle of dried something tied with red thread.

“It smells like… tea?” I said, and immediately regretted sounding like a city idiot.

“Rölleka,” Astrid said. Yarrow. She pronounced it the way her grandmother would have, like the word belonged to the land. “For keeping your thoughts from slipping. And salt. And this—” she tapped the leather pouch “—is just for the feeling.”

“What’s in it?”

She shrugged, the gesture half teenage indifference, half something else. “A bit of jord from the mound. I took it last time. Gran would slap my hands for that. But she’s not here, and we’re not doing it like she did.”

That was the thing with Astrid. She could talk about “gran” with a kind of affectionate contempt, as if the old woman had been equal parts saint and police officer. And she could talk about the old practices like they were dares. Like spinning a bottle at a party, only colder, deeper.

It had started with smaller games. Knock three times on the sauna door and whisper a name, see who dreamt of who. Leave a hair under the doorstep and watch someone trip over their words the next day in class. Astrid always had a reason, always a story about how it worked, and always a grin when it did. I told myself it was coincidence. That in a village this small, everyone’s words tripped eventually.

But she’d been right about things too often. About who had stolen the teacher’s chalk. About who was sleeping with whom. About the way our classmate Per looked at me when he thought I wasn’t watching.

“Tonight,” she said, leaning closer over the desk, “we do it for real. Not like the other stupid things. This one gives you knowledge. If you can sit.”

I should have said no. I can say that now without even tasting the lie. But back then, I’d been new and raw, my Stockholm arrogance bruised by the quiet. I hated how the village watched me, hated how the snow seemed to press in from all directions. I wanted something that felt like control. I wanted to be invited into something.

“What do I have to do?” I asked.

Astrid’s smile flickered, as if she was pleased and disappointed at the same time. “We’ll prep. No metal. No watch. No coins. You don’t bring your city crap. You bring bread.” She pulled a heel of dark bread from a plastic bag as if she’d been saving it. “Old bread, preferably. And you don’t eat it.”

“Why bread?”

“For the ones who come close. For… courtesy.” She said the last word like she’d heard it from an adult and was trying it on. “And you don’t pee on the mound. That’s important.”

I snorted, because it was easier than admitting my stomach had tightened. “That’s your grandmother talking.”

Astrid’s eyes slid toward the window, toward the black outside. “No. That’s just common sense.”

We left her house close to eleven, when her parents were asleep and the only light in the kitchen came from the refrigerator’s hum. We dressed in layers, wool and denim, and Astrid made me empty my pockets like we were going through airport security. She took my wrist and unclasped my cheap watch.

“No metal,” she reminded me.

“It’s not like it’s iron,” I whispered, but I let her slip it into her jacket.

Outside, the cold met us with a flat hand. Northern Sweden in winter had a way of making every sound sharp. Snow squeaked under our boots. The sky wasn’t fully dark; it had that faint, bruised blue the north keeps even at night, the moonlight caught in ice crystals. The village lay behind us—one road, a handful of houses, the dark shape of the school, the sleeping church.

Astrid led without hesitation. We cut across a field crusted with snow, past a stand of birches where the trunks looked like bones. She carried a small backpack. I carried the bread wrapped in paper, stupidly heavy for what it was, as if the weight was not in the loaf but in what it meant.

The burial mound sat at the edge of the forest, a low rise in the landscape that you wouldn’t notice if someone didn’t point it out. Summer kids had carved their names into the nearby pine. In the day, it looked like a hill. At night, with the snow making everything smooth, it looked like something trying to breathe under a sheet.

Astrid stopped at the base and crouched, brushing snow away with her mittened hands until dark earth showed through.

“Here,” she said. “This is where you sit. Not on top. Not at the edge. Here. It matters.”

“How do you know?”

She shrugged again, but this time her shoulders were tense. “Gran showed me. And before you ask—no, she didn’t say why. She just said, ‘Don’t be stupid, Astrid.’”

“So we’re doing the stupid version,” I said, and tried to laugh.

Astrid didn’t laugh back. She pulled the yarrow from her backpack and crumbled a little between her fingers, letting it fall onto the exposed earth.

“For your head,” she murmured. Then she took out the candle stub and a matchbox. She didn’t light it yet. She set it beside the spot, half buried in snow. “For your eyes. You can use it if you have to, but don’t stare into it. It makes… holes.”

“Holes in what?”

Astrid looked up at me, her face pale, her breath a cloud. “In you.”

I didn’t have a good reply. I sat down where she indicated, the cold seeping through my jeans immediately. The mound was harder than I expected, packed earth under snow, as if it had been pressed down by countless winters. I placed the bread in front of me, like an offering in a picture book.

Astrid knelt close, her voice dropping into that matter-of-fact tone she used when she wanted to sound like she knew everything.

“You sit with your hands like this,” she said, placing my palms flat on my thighs. “You keep your mouth shut. You don’t hum, you don’t pray, you don’t… Stockholm-think.”

“Stockholm-think?”

“Like you’re narrating everything. Like you’re trying to be funny.” She paused. “And if you hear someone say your name, you don’t answer. Even if it’s me. Especially if it’s me.”

My throat tightened. “So you’re leaving?”

“I’m going to the tree line.” She nodded toward the dark fringe of pines. “I’ll be close enough that you’re not dead, far enough that you’re alone.”

“You make it sound like—”

“Like what? Like it’s dangerous?” She leaned in, and for a second her breath warmed my cheek. “It is. But it’s also just sitting. That’s why it works. Because it’s so stupid you don’t guard yourself.”

Then she stood and stepped backward, careful, like she didn’t want to turn her back on the mound. At the edge of the trees she stopped, a darker shadow among shadows.

“Midnight,” she said softly. “When you hear the bell.”

“There’s no bell,” I whispered. “No one rings it.”

Astrid’s silhouette lifted one shoulder. “It rings anyway.”

I sat. I listened to my own breathing, loud in my ears. The cold crept from my legs into my hips, numbing, then aching. Somewhere in the forest, something moved—maybe a hare, maybe just snow sliding from a branch. The air smelled of pine resin and something older, like damp soil under ice.

I tried to do what Astrid said. I tried not to Stockholm-think. But thoughts were slippery. They skated toward the obvious: I was sitting on a grave, at night, because a girl I’d known for three months had told me to. I could picture my mother’s face if she knew. My father’s silence.

I stared at the bread. The paper around it had softened from the cold and my touch. I wondered what exactly it was for. Courtesy. For the ones who come close. That phrase had lodged in my head like a splinter.

Time stretched. Minutes felt like hours, then snapped back into the reality of my numb fingers and the ache in my teeth from clenching them.

At some point, the forest went very still. Not the normal stillness of night, but a held breath. Even my own breath seemed too loud, like a rule being broken. I wanted to turn, to look for Astrid, to make sure she was there. But the instruction—don’t look back—sat on my shoulders like a hand.

Then, exactly at midnight, the church bell chimed once.

It was distant, across the field, muffled by cold air and snow, but unmistakable. One clear note that didn’t belong to any schedule I knew. No service. No holiday. Just one measured strike, like a finger tapping a glass to get attention.

My skin prickled under my layers. The sound traveled through the ground more than the air, a vibration that seemed to find the mound and settle into it.

After the bell, the silence returned, deeper than before.

I waited for laughter—Astrid’s, someone else’s. For a flashlight beam to sweep the mound and reveal a prank. Nothing happened. I could not hear Astrid breathing. I could not hear anything.

Then I heard my name.

Not loud. Not the way someone calls across a field. It was close, intimate, as if someone had leaned down near my ear and spoken softly so only I could hear.

“Linnea.”

My entire body tensed, muscles locking as if I’d been struck. The voice was wrong in a way I couldn’t explain. It had the shape of a girl’s voice, maybe even Astrid’s, but it didn’t carry breath. It was too clean, too perfectly placed.

I swallowed. My tongue felt thick. Astrid had said not to answer. Especially if it was her.

I kept my mouth shut so hard my jaw hurt.

The cold intensified, or maybe I noticed it more. The exposed patch of earth in front of me seemed darker than it should have been, as if the snow around it was reflecting light that the earth refused. My eyes fixed on it, unblinking.

There was a faint sound then—scratching, or the soft crumble of soil. I couldn’t tell if it came from the mound beneath me or from inside my own head, like when you press your ear against a pillow and hear your blood.

A thought rose up, uninvited: What if the bread isn’t for them. What if it’s for you, so you remember you’re still a person.

I realized I was holding my breath. I forced myself to inhale slowly, quietly, the way Astrid had shown me when we’d smoked stolen cigarettes behind the sports hall and tried not to cough.

“Linnea,” the voice said again. Closer. Or maybe I was listening harder.

My eyes watered from cold and strain. In the blur, the bread looked less like bread and more like a piece of someone’s heel, pale and offered.

I wanted to run. I wanted to laugh. I wanted to turn and see Astrid’s face, to confirm that this was a game, that she’d thrown her voice somehow, that we were just two stupid girls in the snow.

But sitting there, feeling the mound’s hard curve beneath me, hearing the bell that shouldn’t have rung and the name that shouldn’t have been spoken that way, I understood something I hadn’t understood when Astrid first opened her desk drawer.

We weren’t pretending to be witches. We weren’t playing at being old.

We were calling into a place that didn’t care why we were calling.

From the trees, far enough away to be uncertain, I heard a faint crack—like a boot shifting on frozen ground. Or like a branch snapping under weight.

I didn’t look.

I stayed on the mound, hands flat on my thighs, bread in front of me, mouth shut, and waited for whatever came next to decide whether it was real.
