The transfer vessel's air filtration system hums with a persistent mechanical drone.

Arthur Neegan had long ago learned to measure a machine by its noises. A good pump had a reassuring cough. A bad compressor had a whine like a guilty conscience. The hum surrounding him now was neither; it was the indifferent sound of a device doing exactly what it had been built to do, and doing it for someone else's benefit.

It made him feel—absurdly—like a contaminant.

He sat in a narrow acceleration couch that tried to convince the human spine it belonged in a different century. A web of straps crossed his chest and thighs. The fabric had been scrubbed clean of dust, yet Arthur could still smell Mars in it: iron, peroxide cleanser, and the faint metallic tang that got into everything and never left. The cabin light was a steady amber, chosen, no doubt, to soften faces and hard facts alike.

Across from him, a man in a slate uniform watched him with professional patience. On the man's left breast was an insignia: a five-point star with a sweeping arc, stylized as though it were the trail of a ship. Antares Diplomatic Corps.

Diplomatic in name only.

Arthur glanced, not for the first time, at the weapon on the man's hip. It was not the crude, overbuilt pistol the corporate security men on Mars favored—those were designed to work in dust and panic. This was a compact, clean-lined thing that looked like it had been conceived on a drafting table, in a quiet office, by someone who hated surprises.

Arthur said, "Are you going to tell me where we're going?"

The man replied, "You were informed you are being taken to a meeting, Mister Neegan."

"Informed," Arthur echoed. He shifted against the restraints. "At gunpoint, in my kitchen. Very formal."

The man did not react, which itself was a reaction. "You were not harmed."

"My tea was," Arthur said. "I liked that cup."

A flicker—almost amusement—touched the man's eyes and vanished. "We will compensate you for any property damage."

Arthur let out a short laugh that had no humor in it. "You don't even know what you damaged."

The man tilted his head a fraction. "You refer to the Azra claim."

Arthur went still. It was always said so casually, as though it were a mineral like any other, something you could weigh in a balance and argue over in a surveyor's office. But Azra was not like any other.

Azra made radioactive atoms hurry toward their graves.

That was what they said in the popular pamphlets, anyway: *accelerates decay*. If you held a chunk of it close to a piece of spent thorium fuel, the thorium would shed its dangerous appetite for time as though ashamed of how long it meant to linger. That made Azra priceless. It turned years into minutes, waste into heat, heat into thrust. It made interstellar drives practical and weapons obscene.

Arthur said carefully, "I refer to my life. Until three days ago, my greatest worry was that the sump pumps on rig fourteen would seize because corporate sent me a shipment of bearings that were three microns off spec."

The man nodded, as if Arthur had confirmed a data point. "That was before the assay results were transmitted."

"Transmitted," Arthur repeated again, tasting the word. He could see it: a thin packet of numbers and chemical signatures and spectral lines, popping up on a corporate console in Olympus Station, then copied, sold, stolen, whispered, accelerated through the invisible arteries that bound Mars to Earth and the Belt and the far moons. It did not take long for a secret to become a commodity.

"Who sent you?" Arthur asked.

The man said, "The Antares Diplomatic Corps."

"And who sent the Antares Diplomatic Corps?"

A pause. A shift so slight it might have been the movement of the ship itself.

"The Corps acts in the interests of the Antares Combine," the man said at last.

Arthur watched his face. It had the calm, symmetrical look of someone who had been selected for this kind of work: noncommittal features, a voice trained not to rise, and eyes that did not give away the mind behind them.

"The Combine doesn't have jurisdiction on Mars," Arthur said.

"It has influence," the man replied. "And interests."

Arthur leaned his head back against the couch. The straps creaked. "Of course it does."

Mars, for all the romanticism in the recruitment posters, was not a world; it was an agreement. It existed because Earth governments had decided it was cheaper to let corporations build domes than to build them themselves, and because the corporations had decided it was cheaper to import bodies under indenture than to pay free labor. Mars was a ledger with red dust between its pages.

Azra had made the ledger catch fire.

Arthur said, "What does 'meeting' mean?"

"It means you will speak with a representative," the man said. "Off-world."

"Off-world" could mean a hundred places. Phobos, with its crowded docks and cheap air. Deimos, with its quiet men and quieter deals. A private station with no registry and a reactor that ran too hot.

Arthur said, "What if I refuse to speak?"

The man looked at him a moment longer than necessary. "Refusal is unlikely to be productive."

Arthur's jaw tightened. "Because you would shoot me?"

"Because others would," the man said.

There was the larger picture, then, slipping through the cracks of polite language. Arthur felt a chill that had nothing to do with the ship's recycled air.

"Others," he repeated.

"Corporate security," the man said, "does not appreciate complications. Nor do the Free Belters. Nor do the Martian Interim Authority. Nor—" He stopped himself with a sort of disciplined restraint. "Nor certain parties whose names you do not know."

Arthur stared. "You make it sound like my land claim is a war."

The man said, "It may become one."

For a moment the ship's hum seemed louder, as though it approved of that statement. Arthur imagined his little plot of regolith and basalt, the survey pins he'd hammered in himself, the drilling rig he'd coaxed to life with improvised gaskets and stubbornness. Beneath it, a vein of Azra thick enough to turn empires hungry.

He said softly, "I didn't ask for this."

"No," the man agreed. "You discovered it."

The ship shifted—an almost imperceptible change in the vibration through the couch as the transfer vessel matched vectors with something larger. A muted clang sounded through the hull. Docking.

A panel in the bulkhead flashed from amber to white. The air hummed. Somewhere, a lock cycled with a sound like a sigh.

The man unstrapped himself with practiced efficiency and stood. "This way, Mister Neegan."

Arthur remained seated a moment longer. "If I stand up, will that be taken as consent?"

"It will be taken as cooperation," the man said. "Consent is not a term we will be using."

Arthur rose. His legs were unsteady, not from acceleration but from the surreal normality of it all. He followed the man through a narrow corridor whose walls were the color of old bone. There were no windows. There were no signs. A ship without identity, or a ship that did not care to advertise it.

At the end of the corridor, the man keyed a hatch. It opened soundlessly.

The room beyond was larger than Arthur expected and arranged with a deliberate austerity: a table bolted to the deck, three chairs, a wall screen currently blank. The air smelled faintly of ozone and expensive sterilization. A single overhead light cast no shadow; it made everyone equally visible.

A woman stood at the far side of the table.

She was tall, with dark hair pulled back in a severe knot and a uniform that looked like Antares at first glance, until Arthur noticed the difference. Her insignia had the same star and arc, but beneath it was a thin bar of iridescent metal—a strip that caught the light and broke it into colors.

Azra, Arthur realized, or a clever imitation.

She smiled, and it was not a warm expression so much as a calculated one. "Mister Neegan."

Arthur stopped just inside the hatch. "Who are you?"

She gestured to the chair opposite hers. "Director Salin Voss. Antares Diplomatic Corps."

"You people keep saying that like it explains things."

"It explains enough," Voss said. "Please sit."

Arthur sat. The chair was comfortable in a way that made him suspicious. The guard remained by the hatch, hands folded, weapon visible.

Voss took her own seat with measured grace. She placed a small folder on the table. It was paper.

Arthur blinked. "Paper?"

Voss watched him notice. "It is harder to intercept."

He said, "So is not kidnapping engineers."

"Engineers are often intercepted," Voss replied. "It is one of your occupational hazards. You build the tools that move history."

Arthur's fingers twitched toward the folder but did not touch it. "What do you want?"

Voss leaned back slightly. "You have something on your claim, Mister Neegan. Something you do not yet fully understand."

"I understand it's Azra."

"You understand the label," Voss said. "Not the implications."

Arthur said, "It accelerates decay. That's the implication."

Voss's eyes sharpened. "It accelerates decay in a way that violates several comfortable assumptions about how matter behaves. It is not merely a catalyst. It appears to—" She stopped, as if considering what to reveal. "It appears to persuade the nucleus that time is optional."

Arthur felt his throat go dry. "That's poetry."

"It's policy," Voss said. "The Antares Combine cannot allow a substance like that to become an unregulated commodity."

Arthur snorted. "You mean you can't allow anyone else to have it."

Voss's smile returned, thinner. "You are a practical man, Mister Neegan. I respect that. Yes. We prefer to control the supply chain."

Arthur leaned forward. "My lease is with HelioDyne. Their contract—"

"Is a corporate fiction upheld by a Martian authority that survives on tariffs and fear," Voss said. "Contracts are honored until they are inconvenient. You know this. You have repaired too many machines with parts that were promised and never delivered."

Arthur's hands curled on the table. "So what is this? A buyout? A threat?"

"An offer," Voss said, and tapped the folder once. "A generous one."

Arthur did not open it. "Generous to me, or generous to your conscience?"

Voss's gaze held his. "To your survival."

There was silence then, filled only by the ship's steady breathing through its filters. Arthur became abruptly aware that this room, this ship, this moment—was a sealed system. He could say yes, no, scream, laugh. The air would keep humming. The Combine would keep moving.

Voss spoke again, softer. "Mister Neegan, you are currently the axis of a tug-of-war you did not volunteer for. HelioDyne will claim your find under the indenture statutes. The Martian Interim Authority will claim it under emergency resource provisions. Certain Belter factions will claim it under the oldest law in the Belt: take what you can hold. And there are Earthside committees already debating whether your claim constitutes a strategic threat."

Arthur swallowed. "And Antares?"

Voss spread her hands, palms up, as if she were offering something benign. "Antares offers you a way to step out of the line of fire. We will relocate you. We will pay you. We will give you a position. You will help us extract the Azra properly, with safeguards. You will be protected."

Arthur said, "Protected by the people who brought a gun to my kitchen."

Voss's eyes did not flicker. "Protected by the people who can keep larger guns away."

Arthur looked down at the folder. It sat there like a small, flat door. On the edge of it, he could see a watermark—again that iridescent strip, again the suggestion of Azra.

He asked, "And if I refuse?"

Voss's expression did not change, but the room seemed to tighten around the question. "Then you return to Mars," she said. "And you will discover how quickly a man becomes a footnote when he stands between powers."

Arthur let out a slow breath. "You said I would speak with a representative."

"I am speaking," Voss said.

"You're not the one who wants this," Arthur said quietly. "You're the one sent to get it."

Voss regarded him for a long moment. Then she nodded once, as if acknowledging a competent diagnosis. "There are those above me who do not enjoy meetings. They enjoy results."

Arthur's mouth tasted of recycled air and old fear. "So what's the catch?"

Voss slid the folder an inch toward him. "You sign. You agree to exclusive technical consultancy under Antares charter. You relinquish your claim in exchange for compensation and relocation. And you tell us everything you know about the vein—its depth, its spread, the assay method, the way your instruments behaved when you first hit it."

Arthur stared. "My instruments behaved?"

Voss's eyes glinted. "You did not think we only intercepted your assay numbers, did you? We intercepted your rig telemetry. Your drill head temperature dropped by twelve degrees when it touched the Azra seam. Your radiation counters spiked and then—impossibly—flattened. That should not happen."

Arthur felt a prickle along his arms. He remembered that moment: the drill biting into something that did not feel like rock, the sudden hush in the Geiger chatter, like a crowd holding its breath.

He whispered, "It ate the radiation."

Voss said, "Or redirected it. Or converted it. We do not know. That is why you are here."

Arthur looked up. "And if I help you, you'll keep it from becoming a weapon."

Voss's smile returned, and this time it was almost pitying. "Mister Neegan," she said, "it is already a weapon. The only question is who holds it first."

The hum of the air filters continued, patient and mechanical, as though time itself were merely another system to be regulated—unless Azra decided otherwise.
