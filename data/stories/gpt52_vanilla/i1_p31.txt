The first thing New Orleans does is hit me in the face.

Not, like, literally. More like the air. It’s thick and wet and smells like hot concrete, fried something, and—underneath it all—old river water that’s been keeping secrets longer than any person I’ve ever met. My shirt sticks to my back the second I step away from the curb like I’m already sweating out my entire life.

The guy who gave me a ride from somewhere outside Biloxi (I think? I wasn’t exactly tracking) doesn’t even look at me when he says, “This is as far as I go.”

We’re downtown. Cars and people and noise and colors I don’t have words for. A tourist couple is arguing over a map. A kid in a Saints jersey is eating something powdered sugar is actively falling off of. Everyone looks like they belong here, even the ones who obviously don’t.

I stand there with my backpack—too light because I couldn’t take anything that would make Mom notice faster—and I watch his tail lights vanish into traffic.

And for one gorgeous, terrifying second, I feel… free.

Then my stomach drops through my shoes.

Okay. New plan, Taylor. Because the old plan was “go literally anywhere else,” and you’ve achieved that. Congrats. Gold star. Now what?

I start walking because standing still makes me feel like a target. My phone’s battery is at 12% and I’m not turning it on. If I turn it on, it pings. If it pings, somebody finds me. If somebody finds me, I’m back in that kitchen with my mother’s face like stone and my dad looking at the table like he could bore a hole through it and disappear.

“Taylor?” Mom had said, like she was trying it on. Like it was a coat she didn’t want to buy. “No. That’s not— Don’t do this.”

Don’t do this.

As if I’m a tantrum.

The street is crowded and loud and the buildings are old in this proud, leaning way. Iron balconies drip plants like the city is growing over itself. A saxophone threads through all the noise, turning it into something almost… cinematic. Like if I look over my shoulder I’ll see a camera crew, and someone will yell cut, and we’ll reset and do it again with different parents.

The sax is coming from up ahead. It’s not cheerful, exactly. It’s the kind of sound that makes your ribs ache, like it knows what your chest has been holding.

I follow it without thinking.

There’s a guy on the sidewalk with a battered case open at his feet, wearing a white shirt that used to be crisp. He’s older, maybe late forties, hair pulled back, eyes closed while he plays like the street itself is his stage and everyone else is just passing through his story. A couple people throw bills into the case without stopping. A little kid sways and grins until his mom tugs him along.

I stop at the edge of the crowd, the music catching me in a way I don’t want. It makes the past two days feel like they’re pressing their hands against my back.

I’m not crying. I’m not. My eyes are just—whatever.

Someone bumps my shoulder.

“Watch it,” a voice says, sharp and low.

I turn, ready to snap back, and instead I find a person about my age glaring at me like I just stepped on their foot on purpose.

They’re maybe seventeen. Dark hair cut in a messy blunt bob under a black cap. Their eyeliner looks like it was applied in a moving vehicle, which I respect. They’ve got a long sleeve shirt even though it’s approximately a million degrees, and they’re holding a paper cup like it’s the last thing tethering them to Earth.

They look me up and down in one second, clocking the backpack, the too-fast breathing, the way I keep scanning like I’m expecting someone to jump out from behind a lamppost and drag me home.

“Tourist?” they ask, like it’s an insult.

“No,” I say automatically. Which is true, because tourists have plans. Tourists have hotels. Tourists have people expecting them to come back with souvenirs and stories, not… whatever this is.

They snort. “Sure.”

I should walk away. Stranger danger. Tennessee health class actually did cover something useful, once. But my brain is stuck on the fact that they didn’t say sir or ma’am, didn’t gender me at all, and my body unclenches by a microscopic amount.

“What’s your deal?” I ask, because apparently I’ve decided to become the kind of person who starts conversations with random hostile teenagers.

They take a sip of whatever’s in their cup and make a face. “My deal is it’s hot and I’m broke. Your deal is you look like you’re about to bolt.”

“Maybe I like bolting.”

“Mm.” They tilt their head. “You got somewhere to be?”

I almost laugh. It comes out more like a cough. “Do I look like I have somewhere to be?”

They glance past me, toward the sax player. “You got cash?”

“Like… five dollars.”

“Good,” they say, immediately. “You can buy water. Or you can buy a beignet and die happy in a sugar coma.”

“I’m not dying today,” I say, and the words land heavier than they should. Like they have history.

Their eyes narrow a fraction, like they heard it too. “Okay. Not today.”

The saxophone slides into a long note that makes my spine tingle. The musician opens his eyes and looks straight at me like he knows I’m not supposed to be here. Like he can see the invisible label that says RUNAWAY in sharpie across my forehead.

I shift, uneasy.

“Come on,” the teenager says, jerking their chin. “If you stand there looking lost, somebody will try to sell you something or steal your bag.”

“Are you going to steal my bag?” I ask.

They grin, not friendly. “If I wanted your bag, I’d already have it.”

I hate that they’re right.

“What’s your name?” I ask, as I fall into step beside them.

They hesitate. Just a beat too long.

“Jules,” they say finally.

It feels like a lie. Or like a name they put on when the real one doesn’t fit. Which, okay. Pot, meet kettle.

“I’m—” My throat tightens around it. Saying my name out loud still feels like stepping onto thin ice. Like if I say it, it becomes real, and then I have to deal with all the consequences of being real.

Jules looks at me, waiting, but not pushing. It’s weirdly… considerate.

“Taylor,” I say. “I’m Taylor.”

Something in me relaxes. Just a notch. It’s not joy. It’s not peace. It’s just… not lying.

Jules nods like they’re filing it away. “Taylor. Alright. You hungry?”

I should say no. I should conserve everything. But my stomach chooses that moment to growl loud enough to be heard over the city.

Jules laughs. “Yeah. That’s what I thought.”

We weave through the crowd, past souvenir shops and bars that aren’t even pretending to be subtle. The street smells like spilled beer and perfume and something sweet baking somewhere close.

My heart won’t stop doing this jittery thing, like it’s trying to sprint out of my chest. Every time someone brushes too close, I flinch. Every time a man’s voice gets too loud behind us, my mind flashes to my dad’s, low and dangerous, saying, “You are not—” and then cutting himself off because the words were too ugly to finish.

Jules leads me down a side street where it’s quieter. Not quiet, because New Orleans doesn’t do quiet. But quieter. The buildings here feel more lived-in. Less like a postcard.

They stop at a corner store and push the door open with their shoulder. Cold air hits me like a blessing.

Inside, Jules grabs two bottles of water without asking. I hover, panicking, because what if they expect me to pay for both? What if this is the part where the trap snaps shut?

At the counter, Jules slides one bottle toward me and then digs in their pocket. Coins. A crumpled bill. Not enough, I can tell. They glance at me.

“You got that five?” they ask, casual.

Relief floods me so hard it’s almost embarrassing. “Yeah.”

I hand it over, pretending my fingers aren’t shaking. We pay. We leave. No alarms. No cops. No concerned adults materializing out of the humidity to send me back.

Outside, Jules twists their cap around and drinks half the bottle like they’ve been wandering the desert. I drink too, slower, trying to make it last.

“Thanks,” I say.

“Don’t thank me,” Jules replies. “You paid.”

“Still.”

They look at me, eyes sharp. “So where you sleeping tonight, Taylor?”

My mouth opens and closes. The answer is nowhere. The answer is I didn’t get that far. The answer is my escape plan was basically a middle finger and a prayer.

Jules watches my face and their expression changes—just a flicker of something like recognition, like seeing a reflection in dirty glass.

“Yeah,” they say softly, like they’ve just confirmed something. “Thought so.”

My throat tightens again, but this time it’s not the name. It’s everything else. The fear. The anger. The stupid, traitorous part of me that misses my little brother even though he didn’t say a word when I told them. The part of me that wants to go home and also wants to burn it down.

“I can’t go back,” I say, and I don’t mean to say it out loud. It just slips out, raw.

Jules doesn’t ask why. They don’t do the pity face. They just nod like, yeah, no kidding.

“Me neither,” they say.

And there it is. The crack in their armor.

We stand there on the sidewalk with the city moving around us, the saxophone still faint in the distance like a heartbeat, and for a second I can almost believe in the idea of not being alone.

“What happened?” I ask, immediately regretting it because it’s none of my business.

Jules’s jaw tightens. Their fingers tap the side of their water bottle too fast.

“Long story,” they say. “Not telling it to someone I met five minutes ago.”

Fair.

“I’m not telling mine either,” I say, and I’m trying to sound tough, but it comes out small.

Jules’s mouth twitches. “Good. Don’t. People hear your story, they think they own it.”

I stare at them. “You talk like you’ve been here awhile.”

“I have.” They glance down the street, like they’re checking for someone. “This place… it’ll swallow you if you let it. But you can survive if you’re not stupid.”

“I’m not stupid,” I say automatically.

Jules raises an eyebrow.

I sigh. “Okay, I might be a little stupid.”

“Honesty,” Jules says. “We love to see it.”

They take another sip, then nod toward the shaded side of the street. “Come on. There’s a park a few blocks over. You can sit, think, freak out privately. Figure out a plan.”

“A plan,” I repeat, like it’s a foreign language.

Jules starts walking, and I hesitate. Every instinct screams not to follow. Don’t trust. Don’t attach. Don’t make yourself dependent on a stranger with a name that might not be real.

But then the alternative appears in my mind: me alone, wandering until it’s dark, until someone decides I look easy.

So I follow.

As we walk, the music fades behind us, but it doesn’t leave. It lingers in my head, curling around my thoughts. The city presses in, vibrant and indifferent.

I watch Jules’s shoulders, the way they move like they’re always ready to pivot and run. Like they’ve learned the geography of danger.

And I think: maybe we’re the same kind of broken.

Which is either the worst possible sign—

—or the first good one I’ve had in two days.
