The bell over the airlock door doesn’t jingle here—no point, not with the pressure seals and the hiss of cycling atmosphere—but the Outpost has its own tells. A certain change in the room, like the asteroid belt itself shifts and everyone feels it in their bones.

Tonight it’s the usual low-gravity lull. Miners fresh off a rock run crowd the long tables, boots still dusted with gray grit. Merchants sit in their careful little islands of quiet, drinks untouched while they calculate margins in their heads. Someone’s got a hand of cards going near the heater vent, the slap of polymer chips punctuating a slow ballad drifting from the wall speakers.

I’m polishing glasses that don’t need polishing because it gives my hands something honest to do.

“Three more Rimshots, Nets,” Jori calls from booth six, waving a credit chit like a flag. His beard’s still braided with cable ties from his shift. “Put it on my tab, and don’t you make that face like I won’t pay.”

“I don’t make faces,” I say. “I curate expressions.”

“Same thing,” he says, grinning.

I pour the Rimshots—half citrus synth, half spark liquor, topped with a fizz that tastes like a dare—and slide them down the bar to the runner drone. The drone zips off, its little stabilizers whining. The Outpost isn’t fancy, but it’s efficient in the ways that matter.

Kesh leans an elbow on the bar, her jacket half-unzipped, hair shaved on one side and braided on the other. She watches me like she’s watching a moonrise.

“You’re quiet,” she says.

“I’m always quiet,” I lie.

Kesh hums. “You’re quiet in a way that says you’re listening for something.”

I keep my eyes on the bottles lined up in their brackets. Vega whisky. Drifter gin. Fermented kelp brandy that tastes like regret and ocean storms. “Maybe I just like the music.”

“Mm-hm.” She taps the bar once, an old habit from when she used to run comms and thought everything could be signaled in code. “You hear from your… friend?”

My throat tightens. “No.”

She doesn’t push. That’s why Kesh lasts longer than most people in a place like this. “If they show up,” she says instead, “I’ll be right here.”

“Yeah,” I say, and force my hands to keep moving. “Where else would you be?”

The airlock hisses.

I feel it before I see it: a hush in the room’s attention, a subtle recalibration. The Outpost is a lazy hub, sure, but lazy doesn’t mean blind. People survive out here by noticing the difference between routine and trouble.

The stranger steps in like they’ve forgotten how to be inside. Tall—taller than most humans, though the stoop makes it hard to tell. Skin the color of deep tea under station lights. Their hair isn’t hair; it’s tentacled dreads, thick cords that curl and knot and look like they’ve been slept on for three days. Their coat is too thin for the rim, patched at the elbows, a heat strip hanging dead along the collar.

They scan the room once, quick, and then they choose my bar.

They don’t swagger. They don’t preen. They just… arrive, and sit, and exhale like they’ve been holding their breath for several light-years.

“Vega whisky,” they say, voice low and scraped raw. “Neat. Better make that a double.”

I reach for the Vega bottle without asking questions. The label is faded from being touched too often. “You got credit,” I say, “or are we doing favors tonight?”

The stranger’s mouth twitches, not quite a smile. “Credit.”

A coin-chip lands on the bar—real metal, old style, the kind that makes merchants sit up straighter. It spins once, then falls flat with a dull clack.

Interesting.

I pour. Two fingers becomes four. The whisky hits the glass with a warm amber glug and the scent of smoke and star-anise.

They take it like they’ve been thirsty for weeks. One swallow, then a second slower one, like they’re remembering what it is to taste something that isn’t recycled air.

“What’s your name?” I ask, because names matter and because if I don’t ask, my brain will invent worse possibilities.

The stranger’s eyes flick up. Dark. Reflective in a way that makes me think of deep water. “Do I need one?”

“Here?” I nod toward the room. “Yeah. People get nervous around blanks.”

A beat. “Call me Sable.”

“Sure,” I say. “Sable.”

They cradle the glass, hands steady now that the drink has something to do. Their fingers are long, with faint suction-cup ridges at the pads that catch the light when they shift.

Kesh makes a quiet sound beside me, not quite a warning, not quite a greeting.

Sable’s gaze flicks to her and back to me. “You run this place?”

“I pour drinks,” I say. “The place runs itself. Mostly.”

Sable’s mouth opens, like they’re about to say something else, but the words don’t come. Instead they listen, head angled slightly.

I listen too, because whatever they’re listening for, it makes the hair on my arms lift.

At first I hear nothing but the normal: laughter at the card table, the rattle of ice, the slow thrum of the Outpost’s aging life-support vents. Then—beneath it—something else.

A soft, irregular ping, like metal being tapped with a nail.

Kesh’s eyes narrow. “Nets,” she murmurs, too low for anyone else, “did you order a delivery?”

“No,” I whisper back. “Did you?”

She shakes her head once.

Sable swallows another mouthful of whisky. “You have a back door?” they ask.

“There’s an access corridor,” I say carefully. “For supplies. Why?”

Sable sets the glass down. “Because someone’s tracing me.”

The words land like a weight. Not loud, not dramatic—worse. Matter-of-fact. The kind of truth you can’t argue with.

I force my face into calm. “People trace lots of people,” I say. “Miners with debts. Merchants with cargo. Lovers with bad boundaries.”

Sable’s gaze doesn’t blink. “Not like this.”

Across the room, the merchant at table three—Linn, the one with the gold tooth—stops mid-sentence. His eyes lock on the airlock door, then slide away too fast.

I know that move. I’ve done it myself.

“Who?” I ask, keeping my hands busy, wiping a nonexistent spill. “Out here, it could be anyone.”

Sable’s tentacled dreads shift as they tilt their head, a subtle ripple like sea grass. “If I say it, do you throw me out?”

“That depends,” I say. “On whether you bleed on my floor.”

A sound cracks through the bar—sharp, sudden. A glass shatters somewhere behind me, near the dish station. Everyone flinches. For a heartbeat, the Outpost’s cozy noise collapses into a tight silence.

“Sorry!” calls Miko, my runner, from the back. “Dropped one—my bad!”

Laughter resumes in a delayed wave, too loud, too quick. People are eager to pretend nothing happened.

But Sable’s shoulders go rigid.

“That wasn’t an accident,” they say quietly.

I glance over my shoulder. Miko is crouched, sweeping glass into a magnetic pan. His hands are shaking. He looks up and meets my eyes—just for a second—and the panic there is raw.

Kesh’s fingers curl on the bar. “Nets,” she says, “do you want me to—”

“Not yet,” I murmur.

Sable leans forward, lowering their voice until it’s just for me. “I was supposed to meet someone here,” they say. “A contact. They didn’t show.”

My pulse ticks in my throat. “A contact from where?”

Sable’s eyes flick toward the ceiling, toward the vents, toward the places where sound hides. “From the inner lanes,” they say. “From the kind of work you don’t want to know about.”

I bark a soft laugh that isn’t funny. “You’re in a bar on the Andromeda rim. I know about work I don’t want to know about.”

Sable’s expression tightens. “Then you understand why I came here.”

“I understand why you came,” I say. “I don’t understand why you’re telling me.”

Sable’s gaze sharpens, like a blade finally unsheathed. “Because they’ll ask you,” they say. “And if you answer wrong, this place stops being your home.”

My fingers still on the rag. For a second, the Outpost feels too small, the air too thin.

“Who is ‘they’?” I ask again, and this time the question has teeth.

Sable’s lips part. A name is right there, hanging on the edge of breath—

The airlock hisses again.

Every head turns, not because they want to, but because something in their bodies knows. The way prey knows when the grass parts wrong.

Three figures step inside. Not miners. Not merchants. Clean boots. Matching jackets with a faint sheen that says corporate composite. Their faces are ordinary in a way that feels engineered. The one in front carries a small case in one hand like it’s nothing.

They don’t look around like they’re new.

They look around like they own the air.

The leader’s eyes land on me first, then slide to Sable, and the smallest smile touches their mouth.

“Evening,” the leader says, voice pleasant as poured syrup. “We’re looking for someone.”

Sable’s hand closes around their glass hard enough that I hear the faint protest of strained crystal.

Kesh leans closer to me. “Nets,” she whispers, “tell me what you want me to do.”

I wet my lips, tasting citrus and smoke. My mind runs through exits, hiding places, people I can trust, people I can’t. Past lives I thought I’d outrun.

I meet the leader’s gaze and force my voice steady.

“Outpost policy,” I say, “is you tell me what you’re drinking before you tell me what you’re hunting.”

The leader’s smile widens just a fraction. “How charming,” they say. “In that case—two waters. And”—their eyes flick back to Sable—“we’ll take our fugitive on the side.”

Sable’s breath stutters.

And behind the bar, where my hands are supposed to be steady, my fingers tighten around the rag like it’s a lifeline.
