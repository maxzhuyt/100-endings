I woke before the first trumpet, when the air in the ludus still held the night’s cool like water cupped in a hand. The men around me slept in uneasy heaps—arms flung across chests scored by old cuts, feet twitching as if they ran in dreams. Somewhere down the corridor a chain rasped softly as a man turned over, and the sound slid through the dark like a reminder: even in sleep we belonged to iron.

I lay on my back and listened to Rome waking beyond the high walls. A cart rattled over the stones outside, the wooden wheels striking the paving with a steady clack-clack-clack. A mule brayed. Farther off, faint as breath, rose the first murmurs of the Subura: vendors shouting, a child crying, a woman laughing sharply and then cutting it off. The city was never silent; it only changed its rhythm.

My throat tasted of yesterday’s barley and cheap wine. The lanista liked us fed and heavy, “to bleed less,” he said, as if the gods had written that rule in the stars. I sat up and the straw pricked through my tunic. My shoulder ached where the scar tightened, and I rolled it slowly, feeling the stiffness give way. I had learned to measure myself by pains: what had eased, what had worsened, what threatened to become permanent.

On the wall by my pallet, scratched into the plaster with a nail, was a small owl. Its eyes were two dots, nothing more, but I touched it with two fingers anyway—my private salute. Minerva had been mine since I was a boy in northern Italia, when my mother told me the goddess watched over those who learned and endured. A strange patron for a man trained to kill for amusement, some would say. But I had clung to her all the same, not for war, but for craft: for the thinking that kept a man alive when strength failed. When the editor of games raised his hand and the crowd roared, it was not Mars who steadied my breathing. It was Minerva—clear-eyed, unsentimental, the goddess who knew that a sharp mind could be sharper than a blade.

A cough barked from the next cell. Dama, a murmillo from Africa with shoulders like an ox, pushed himself upright, blinking. “You’re awake early,” he muttered in Latin thickened by another tongue.

“Dreams were loud,” I said.

He snorted. “Better loud dreams than loud spectators.”

We both fell quiet. There was a day coming; there was always a day coming. Some days ended with sweat and bruises and the hope of a hot bath if the lanista was in a generous mood. Some ended with a body carried out through the back gate to be burned quickly, without names, before the smell of death could seep into the training yard and sour the next day’s work.

The corridor brightened as a slave passed with a lamp. The oil’s bitter scent followed him, and for a moment the walls looked like they were sweating. He stopped at each door, unlocking and relocking, counting us as if we were sacks of grain. A man in the corner started humming a tune from his homeland—something with odd, falling notes that made my skin prickle. Another voice joined, then stopped when the overseer’s sandals approached.

When the doors were opened, the yard greeted us with its familiar odors: sand warmed by yesterday’s sun, old leather, metal rubbed clean and then dulled again by hands. The armory door stood ajar, and inside, helmets hung like sleeping beasts. The sun had not yet climbed high enough to burn the shadows away, but already the air promised heat.

We lined up for breakfast. The cook, a sour-faced Greek slave, ladled barley porridge into bowls. The steam rose with the smell of wet grain, and someone had stirred in garlic—sharp enough to make my eyes water. I ate because it was required, because my body was the only coin I possessed and I could not afford to spend it carelessly. Across from me, a young Thraex named Corvus chewed with the desperate speed of the newly bought.

“You heard?” he said, mouth half full.

“Heard what?”

He leaned closer, lowering his voice as if the stones could carry it back to the lanista. “The games will be for the emperor’s cousin. They say the grain dole will be announced. Free grain. And more races.”

Dama barked a laugh. “Bread and circuses,” he said, in the tone of a man repeating a proverb he despised.

Corvus’s eyes shone with a kind of hunger that had nothing to do with porridge. “If the people are fed, they are kinder,” he said. “They might shout for mercy.”

I swallowed, feeling the porridge sit heavy. “Or they might be fed enough to be cruel for sport.”

His face fell. He was young enough to think the crowd had a single mood. I had stood in the arena long enough to know Rome’s temper was like the Tiber—calm on the surface, muddy and dangerous underneath, always moving toward whatever power pulled at it.

After breakfast we trained. Not combat—nothing so exciting for those who paid to watch later—but the endless work that made combat possible: footwork until the calves burned, shield drills until the arms trembled, the wooden sword striking the post again and again with a dull thud that matched the beat of my heart. The lanista watched from the shade, his ringed fingers tapping against his thigh. He spoke of discipline as if it were a virtue and not a chain.

Between drills, I caught glimpses of the city beyond the ludus wall. A strip of sky, white with rising heat. The distant curve of a temple roof. The top of a statue that might have been a god or an emperor; in Rome the difference was sometimes only a matter of time and decree.

Near midday, we were allowed a brief rest. I went to the small shrine tucked into a corner of the yard, where someone—perhaps a fighter long dead—had placed a rough clay figure of Minerva. The paint had faded, but the outline of her helmet remained. I poured a few drops of watered wine onto the packed earth as an offering. The smell rose faintly, sharp and sour, swallowed almost at once by the heat.

“Lady,” I whispered, feeling foolish and sincere in the same breath, “grant me clarity. Not victory. Clarity.”

Behind me, footsteps crunched in the sand. I did not turn until I heard the familiar jingle of keys.

The lanista’s steward, Marcus, stood with a wax tablet under his arm. He was a freedman, proud of it, and he carried himself with the stiff dignity of a man determined never to be mistaken for a slave again.

“Felix,” he said, using my arena name. I had not been born Felix. I had been given the name when I first fought, as if good fortune could be assigned like a helmet. “You are to be paraded this afternoon.”

My stomach tightened. “Paraded?”

He nodded toward the gate. “The sponsor wants the people to see their favorites. A procession through the Forum. The editor believes it will please the crowd. And the crowd must be pleased.”

There it was again—the word that ruled everything. The crowd. Not law, not virtue, not the Senate’s thin speeches. The crowd, hungry and loud, could make a man into a hero or a corpse with the tilt of their voices. Emperors pretended they controlled it, but I had seen even powerful men glance nervously at the stands when the murmurs turned sour.

I wiped my hands on my tunic. “Am I a favorite?”

Marcus’s mouth twitched, almost a smile. “You have survived. That is enough for them to make stories.”

Stories. I had heard them shouted from the cheap seats: that I was blessed by Minerva, that I was cursed, that I was a runaway noble, that I had killed my own brother. Rome loved myths because myths were easier than truth. The truth was simpler: I wanted to live, and I was good at what I had been forced to do.

We were washed and oiled like animals for market. Slaves scraped the sweat from my skin with a strigil, the curved metal biting gently, leaving behind the smell of rancid olive oil and the faint tang of old blood where the scars opened slightly. My hair was combed back. A fresh tunic was pulled over my head, coarse but clean. When they strapped a decorative sword at my side—blunted, for show—I felt its weight and had the strange thought that even our pride was regulated. We were allowed to look dangerous, but not to be dangerous unless someone paid.

The gate opened, and the city struck me like heat. Rome at midday was a living furnace. The stones radiated warmth through my sandals. The air tasted of dust and smoke and fish left too long in the sun. From a bakery came the rich scent of bread, so comforting it hurt. Incense drifted from a nearby altar where a priest chanted in a sing-song voice. Over everything lay the stink of humanity: sweat, urine, perfume too sweet, the sharp bite of vinegar from a street-seller’s jug.

People noticed us at once. Heads turned. Fingers pointed. A boy ran alongside, chanting my arena name, laughing as if we were part of a festival. A woman with dyed hair and gold earrings leaned out from a doorway and called something obscene that made Corvus blush crimson. An old man spat near my feet and muttered about morals, then craned his neck eagerly to get a better look.

We passed beneath triumphal arches carved with victories won in lands I had never seen. The reliefs showed barbarians kneeling, their hands bound, their faces twisted in fear. I wondered if those men had looked like me when they were herded into Rome, dazed by the noise and the scale of everything. The Empire ate worlds and then decorated its walls with the bones.

In the Forum, the noise thickened. Senators in white-edged togas moved like swans among the crowd, pretending not to see the beggars who clustered near the steps of temples. Clients trailed after patrons, murmuring praise, hoping for coins or favors. A herald shouted announcements about grain distribution, and the crowd surged as if pulled by an invisible rope. I saw anger there, too, under the excitement—a tension that felt like a rope stretched too tight. Too many mouths, too little bread, too many promises made by men who would never go hungry.

A man standing on a raised platform was speaking—one of the politicians, his voice trained to carry. I caught only fragments: “the generosity of our divine house,” “the peace of the provinces,” “the traitors who would stir unrest.” His words floated over us like smoke. The people cheered when he lifted his arms, but the cheers sounded practiced, like a habit.

As we passed, the speaker’s gaze flicked to us. For a heartbeat his eyes met mine. There was calculation there, and something else: relief. We were useful to him. Our blood and spectacle could turn the crowd’s hunger away from politics and toward the sand of the arena. If the people shouted themselves hoarse for a gladiator’s death, they had less breath left to shout at the emperor.

I felt suddenly tired—not in my muscles, but in the place behind my ribs where anger lived. I thought of Minerva again, of her calm. Wisdom did not mean softness. It meant seeing clearly. And I saw, as we walked through Rome like ornaments, that I was both prisoner and tool, and that the city loved me only as long as I remained both.

A little girl stood on the edge of the crowd, holding a small wooden doll. Her eyes were wide, not cruel. She lifted her hand shyly, as if in greeting. For an instant, I imagined another life—one where I might have been someone’s father, someone’s husband, someone who bought bread and complained about the price of olives. The thought was so sharp it almost made me stumble.

I raised my fingers to my forehead in the smallest salute I could manage, not to the girl, but to the goddess I carried inside my skull like a lamp. Minerva, I thought, give me clarity. Let me see the threads. Let me know where to place my feet.

The procession moved on, swallowed by the Forum’s noise. Behind us, the politician’s voice rose again, promising peace with one hand and hinting at punishment with the other. Ahead, the road curved back toward the ludus, toward the familiar walls and the smell of oil and sand. The day was not yet half done. Somewhere, in a villa on a hill, a sponsor was deciding which men would live and which would die for his generosity. Somewhere, a baker was counting coins and wondering if the grain dole would ruin him. Somewhere, an emperor listened to advisers and pretended he did not fear the crowd.

And I—Felix, favorite of strangers—walked through Rome’s heat with a blunt sword at my side and an owl scratched into plaster waiting for me back home, trying to hold onto the only freedom I could claim: the freedom to think, to remember, to choose what I offered to the gods when everything else was taken.
