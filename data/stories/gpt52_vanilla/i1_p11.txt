The projector squeaks every time Mr. Donnelly clicks to the next slide, like a little rubber shoe skidding on a gym floor. Squeak. New bullet point. Squeak. Diagram of the water cycle. Squeak. I can feel the sound in my teeth, which is probably a dramatic way to describe it, but my body loves drama when there’s literally nothing else happening except sitting in a lab stool and trying not to implode.

“Groups of two,” Mr. Donnelly says, and my brain immediately starts listing every person in the room in order of likelihood of pairing with me, like an algorithm I didn’t mean to run. Best case: someone I can be normal around. Worst case: someone I want to be normal around.

And then my eyes do the thing they do even when I tell them not to. They flick to Owen Patel.

Owen is not even doing anything. That’s the irritating part. He’s just sitting there with his pencil behind his ear like he has a commercial deal with the Pencil Behind Ear Association. He’s looking at the slide like it’s interesting, which makes him either very engaged or very good at pretending. He laughs sometimes, but not in the loud way that makes the teacher stare. In the normal-person way.

Please don’t pair me with him. Please pair me with him. Please don’t—

“Owen and Maya,” Mr. Donnelly says, like he’s announcing the weather.

My stomach drops so fast I swear it bounces.

Okay. Okay. This is fine. I am fine. I am a human girl in a classroom and I can participate in a science project without dying. People do this every day. People get married. People go on talk shows. People jump out of planes. I just have to say words about evaporation.

Owen turns toward me, and there’s a tiny second where I don’t move because I’m still deciding what my face should be doing. Friendly smile? Casual nod? Neutral so I don’t look desperate? If I smile too much, it’s like I’m auditioning. If I don’t smile enough, I look mean. There is no correct setting. My face is a malfunctioning thermostat.

I land on what I hope is “pleasantly normal,” which is probably “confused hostage.”

He smiles first. That helps. It also makes my brain completely blank, which is less helpful.

“Hey,” he says.

Say, “Hey.” Say it back. Easy. One syllable. Babies can do it.

“Hi.” My voice comes out thin, like I left it at home on the charger.

Perfect. Great. I sound like a ghost.

We move our chairs closer. I move mine too slowly, like I’m in a stealth game. I don’t want to scrape the floor. Scraping the floor is loud, and loud is attention, and attention is death. But moving too carefully makes me look weird. Normal people scrape chairs. They are not afraid of chair noises. My hands grip the seat and I try to do it in one smooth motion. The chair squeals anyway, an awful protest, and I feel heat rush up my neck.

Don’t look up. If you look up, people might look back. Just pretend it didn’t happen. Chairs squeal. The world squeals. The projector squeaks. Everything is noise and no one cares except me.

Owen doesn’t react, which means either he didn’t notice or he’s polite or he’s too busy being normal.

Mr. Donnelly is talking about creating a mini ecosystem in a sealed container. We have to choose variables and predict what will happen over a week. Like a tiny terrarium with science-y paperwork.

Owen pulls the handout closer between us. “So… we need to pick a plant and—” He taps the page with his pencil. “—decide what we’re testing.”

My brain supplies twelve possible replies instantly, like I’m a customer service chatbot.

Option A: Be smart. Say something insightful, show competence, impress him.
Option B: Be funny. Make a joke, show personality, seem effortless.
Option C: Be agreeable. Let him lead, avoid mistakes, avoid attention.
Option D: Be flirty. (Absolutely not. Illegal.)

If I try to be smart and I mess up, I’ll look like a try-hard. If I try to be funny and it’s not funny, I’ll look like I eat glue. If I just agree, I’ll look boring. If I say nothing, I’ll look like I’m malfunctioning, which is unfortunately true.

Just say a normal sentence. A normal sentence about plants. “We could test how much water affects growth.” Normal. Safe. Boring. Good.

“We could do… um… like… water?” I say, and I hate myself immediately.

Why did I add “um” and “like”? Why did I phrase it like I’m not sure water exists? I sounded like I just discovered water five minutes ago. “We could do, um, like, oxygen?” No. Stop.

Owen nods, like I said something completely acceptable. “Yeah. Like amount of water? Or sunlight?”

He says it so easily. The words just fall out of him in the correct order. He doesn’t pause to check his tone. He doesn’t analyze whether “sunlight” is a desperate word. He just speaks.

I have an entire library inside my head, and aloud I’m a pamphlet.

“Sunlight,” I repeat, because repeating is safer than creating. “We could… change, like, where we put it?”

Stop saying “like.” It’s not even a bad word. Everyone says it. But when I say it, it feels like evidence.

Owen leans over the handout. He smells like laundry detergent, the kind my mom buys in giant plastic jugs. “We could do one near the window and one farther away,” he says, then glances at me. “Unless that’s too hard with one container.”

My brain: Here is where you say something clever. Suggest using foil. Suggest artificial light. Suggest measuring using a ruler. Suggest making two containers. Show that you are capable and interesting and not a trembling deer.

My mouth: “Yeah.”

That’s it. Just “yeah.”

I can feel my soul doing that thing where it presses its face against the glass of my skull like, Hello? Are you going to let me out?

He doesn’t seem disappointed. He just keeps going, scribbling something on the paper. “Maybe we test water instead, since we can actually control that. Like, different amounts.”

Say: “Yes, and we can measure soil moisture.” Or: “We can predict that too much water will cause mold.” Or: “We could also track condensation and link it to the water cycle.” I literally know this. I read three articles about terrariums last night because I couldn’t sleep and my brain decided tiny ecosystems were the solution to existential dread.

Aloud, my brain hands my mouth a sentence, and my mouth drops it.

“Different amounts sounds good,” I manage, and it comes out too fast, like I’m trying to outrun my own awkwardness.

Owen looks up again. “Cool. You wanna be the one who writes the hypothesis? Or should I?”

This is a trap, not from him but from my brain. If I say I’ll write it, I might seem bossy. If I say he should, I might seem lazy. If I say “either is fine,” I might seem like I don’t care. But I do care. I care so much my ribs hurt.

I want to say: “I can write it, I’m good at wording things, and also I’m silently panicking, but in a productive way.” That would be honest. Nobody wants honest.

“I can,” I say, then immediately worry it sounded too eager. Did I say it too quickly? Did my eyes light up? Did my voice go up at the end like a question? Did I look desperate to be useful? Useful is good. Desperate is bad. How can the same thing be two different things depending on eyebrow angle?

Owen hands me the pencil without thinking, like it’s normal for someone else to take over writing. Like he trusts me. That’s a heavy feeling, trust. It sits on my chest and makes breathing complicated.

I stare at the blank line labeled HYPOTHESIS: and my mind starts writing a dissertation.

If the amount of water in a sealed container is increased, then the plant will initially grow faster due to greater availability of water for photosynthesis and transport, but excessive water may reduce oxygen in the soil leading to root stress and mold growth, resulting in decreased overall health.

That’s what I want to write. That is what is in my head, in full sentences with commas that know what they’re doing.

What I write: If we add more water, then the plant will grow more, but too much water might make mold.

Simple. Fine. Middle-school appropriate. Not trying too hard. Not trying at all. Trying exactly the right amount, which is exhausting.

Owen watches me write for half a second and then looks away, which is polite. My brain interprets it as: He is bored. He regrets being paired with you. He can tell you rehearsed this. He can see your sweat glands.

The projector squeaks again. Squeak. New slide: PROCEDURE.

I am hyper-aware of everything I’m doing. The angle of my shoulders. The way my hair falls forward when I bend. The fact that my knee is bouncing and I need to stop because bouncing looks nervous. But if I stop suddenly, it will look like I realized I was bouncing, which is also nervous. So I slow it down gradually, like I’m easing a car to a stop so no one spills coffee. No one is even looking at my knee. Owen is not studying my leg movement. Owen is not a knee detective. But my brain is convinced the entire room is a panel of judges.

Owen says, “We should decide how much water each one gets. Like, 10 milliliters and 30?”

Here comes a chance to sound competent. I can do measurement. I can do controlled variables. I can say “milliliters” without fainting.

“Maybe… 10 and 25?” I say. Why did I phrase it like a question? Commit, Maya. Numbers deserve confidence.

Owen tilts his head. “Yeah, that works. We should keep everything else the same.”

“Yes,” I say, and then, because my brain hates peace, I add, “Obviously.”

Why. Why. Why did I say “obviously”? Now I sound like I’m correcting him, like I think he’s stupid. I don’t think he’s stupid. I think he’s— I think he’s—

Stop. Stop thinking.

He doesn’t seem offended. He just nods and writes “keep variables same” on the paper. Maybe “obviously” didn’t sound rude. Maybe it sounded like agreement. Maybe it sounded like I’m engaged. Maybe it sounded like I have a personality. Or maybe he now thinks I’m the kind of person who says “obviously” and that’s a dealbreaker in eighth grade, where everything is a dealbreaker.

I can’t tell. I can never tell. That’s the worst part. My internal voice is clear, like a narrator with perfect diction. Out loud, I’m guessing in the dark.

There’s a moment where our elbows almost touch over the handout. My entire nervous system lights up like a warning sign. Don’t move away too fast. That looks weird. Don’t stay too close. That looks weird. Just exist normally in space, like you have done for fourteen years.

I adjust my arm one centimeter. It feels like moving a boulder with my mind.

Owen asks, “Do you wanna present? Or should we split it?”

My brain screams: Presenting means standing up in front of people and speaking, which is basically my personal horror movie. Also: If he presents, he might think I’m useless. Also: If I present, he might see me panic and think I’m pathetic. Also: If I don’t present, I’ll regret it for the rest of my life because I’ll imagine an alternate version of me who can talk.

I could say, “We can split it. I can explain the hypothesis and variables, you can do procedure and prediction.” That is logical and fair and prevents me from being alone at the front.

I open my mouth and I can feel the words lined up behind my teeth like they’re waiting for permission.

“I can… do part,” I say.

Good enough. Not a full plan, but a start. He smiles—small, quick, like he’s relieved too.

“Okay. We’ll split it,” he says. “You can do the hypothesis and variables?”

“Yes,” I say, and this time it comes out steady. One clean syllable. No “um.” No “like.” Just yes.

The projector squeaks again and Mr. Donnelly is still talking, and the classroom smells like dry erase markers and pencils and somebody’s grape gum, and my heart is still doing that annoying thing where it acts like we’re in danger when we’re literally talking about plants.

But for a second—just a second—my brain quiets enough for me to notice something else.

Owen is still leaning toward me, not away. He’s looking at the paper, at the project, at our shared little plan. He’s not making a face like I’m weird. He’s not laughing. He’s not scanning the room for a better partner. He’s just… here.

And maybe the gap between what I can say in my head and what I can say out loud is huge, like an ocean, but oceans have bridges. They have boats. They have ways across. They have tides that change.

I press the pencil tip to the next blank line and try to focus on the science, on the tiny ecosystem we’re building, on something that isn’t my own heartbeat echoing in my ears.

Just write. Just breathe. Just be a person in a chair next to a boy who smells like laundry detergent, while the projector squeaks and squeaks and squeaks, and the world, somehow, doesn’t end.
