The boardwalk smelled of salt and frying batter, of hot metal from the rides and old wood soaked in summer. Somewhere behind her a calliope stuttered through a tune that had no right to sound cheerful with the sky going bruise-purple over the Atlantic. The sodium lamps along Surf Avenue had begun their nightly argument with the dark—buzzing, flaring, dimming—so that faces appeared and vanished in quick, untrustworthy slices.

She stopped before the shooting gallery, where tin ducks looped endlessly on a track and paper targets promised prizes too bright to be innocent. A row of rifles rested on their hooks like patient animals.

*His locks.*

The phrase slid through her again, archaic and intimate, as though it belonged to a novel she’d once hidden beneath her pillow. It had no business in her head. Neither did the memory of the stranger’s hair—obsidian curls that looked soft enough to slip between fingers and hard enough to hide a blade.

*I shouldn’t be here.* The thought had been with her since the subway, since the moment she’d stepped back onto Coney’s boards like returning to a crime scene she hadn’t yet committed. *I could turn around. I could go home, put my hat on its hook, wash the salt from my lips, pretend I never answered him.*

But her feet stayed.

A vendor with a tray of taffy barked, “Sweet as a kiss, miss!” and she flinched at the timing. The sodium lights flickered, and the vendor’s grin jumped from friendly to predatory for the length of a heartbeat.

“Not tonight,” she murmured, more to herself than to him.

From the shooting gallery booth, the barker leaned forward, elbows on the counter. “Two shots for a nickel, sweetheart. Knock down the whole row, you take the big bear.”

“I’m not here for bears.”

He laughed. “Everybody’s here for something.”

*And some of us pretend we’re not.*

She traced the rim of her clutch with a gloved thumb. The glove was kid leather, pale as cream, chosen because it made her feel like a woman with rules. She wore it like armor; it was as flimsy as a lie.

“Miss?”

The voice came from behind her, low enough to be mistaken for the ocean.

She turned—and for a fraction of a second, the lights dipped. His face was cut in half by shadow, then returned in harsh yellow, then softened again. It made him look like several different men taking turns wearing the same expression.

He was younger than she wanted him to be—late twenties, perhaps—clean-shaven, dressed plainly but with a precision that suggested he’d been taught how to disappear in a crowd. The curls were real. The eyes were darker than the curls.

“You came,” he said.

“I said I might.”

“And you did.”

She lifted her chin, because a woman should always pretend she isn’t nervous. “I don’t know your name.”

He glanced toward the gallery rifles, as if considering which one might answer for him. “Does it matter?”

“It matters to me.”

A pause. The lights buzzed and flared; a stranger passing behind him looked briefly like a corpse, then like a saint.

He said, “Call me Daniel.”

“Is that the truth?” She hated how quickly the question came out, how intimate it sounded.

“It’s a name,” he said, and his mouth tilted as though he’d made a private joke. “We can start there.”

“We can start where?” Her voice came out steadier than she felt. “On a boardwalk full of noise and cheap perfume?”

“Noise is useful.” He angled his body so the crowd flowed between them like a curtain. “It keeps people from hearing what they shouldn’t.”

She should have stepped away. She did not.

“Why did you ask me to meet you?” she said.

“You noticed me first,” he replied.

“That’s not an answer.”

“It is,” he said. “You were watching. I was watching. It’s a kind of introduction.”

Her cheeks warmed under the powder. “You were watching me?”

“Of course.” He let the word hang as if it were nothing, as if it didn’t carry the weight of a hand on a doorknob. “You stood by the Cyclone, pretending you were waiting for someone. Your gaze kept returning to the men in the suits near the ticket booth.”

Her stomach tightened. “I don’t know what you mean.”

Daniel’s eyes moved over her face with the same calm he might have given a map. “You’re lying.”

The bluntness sparked something sharp in her. “And you’re rude.”

“I’m honest,” he said. “There’s a difference.”

*Is there?* The thought came like a whisper from some darker corner of herself. *Or is it only the difference between what a gentleman says and what he does?*

She gestured toward the shooting gallery, because it was safer to point at tin ducks than at the question between them. “Is this your idea of romance, Daniel?”

“I didn’t say romance.”

“You implied—”

“I implied nothing,” he said softly. “You supplied it.”

The barker interrupted with a shout. “Step right up! Show the lady how a man shoots!”

Daniel didn’t look at him. He looked at her. “Do you want to?”

“I’ve never held a rifle,” she said.

“That’s a shame,” he replied, and his voice made it sound like a promise.

She should have bristled. Instead she heard herself ask, “Why a shooting gallery?”

He reached past her, not touching, but close enough that she felt the heat of him through the thin summer air. He lifted one of the rifles from its hook and set it on the counter.

“Because here,” he said, “no one questions why you’re aiming at something.”

The flickering lights made the gunmetal wink, then dull, then wink again.

Her throat dried. “Are you asking me to shoot?”

“I’m asking you to choose,” Daniel said. “It’s different.”

The barker leaned in, delighted. “That’s right, miss. Pick your piece.”

She forced a laugh that sounded like someone else’s. “My piece.”

Daniel’s gaze held hers. “Take it.”

The words were simple, but they landed with a weight that had nothing to do with the rifle. Her gloved fingers hovered, then closed around the stock. The wood was warmer than she expected, polished by other hands. Men’s hands. Boys’ hands. Hands that had wanted prizes or wanted to feel powerful for two shots and a nickel.

Her pulse kicked, traitorous.

“See?” Daniel murmured near her ear, still not touching. “You can hold a weapon and still look like a lady.”

The barker slapped coins into his palm. “Two shots, miss? Or you want the whole run?”

“How much for the whole run?” Daniel asked, casually, as if buying a row of bullets was no different from buying popcorn.

“Quarter gets you ten,” the barker said. “A dollar gets you the works.”

Daniel produced a bill without looking down. “The works.”

The barker whistled. “All right then. Lady’s got herself a sponsor.”

*A sponsor.* The word landed wrong. Like *patron.* Like *owner.* She felt the urge to refuse on principle—and the equally strong urge to accept because refusing would mean stepping out of the charged air between her and Daniel.

“I didn’t ask you to—” she began.

“You didn’t have to,” Daniel said. “Consider it an investment.”

“In what?”

“In your steadiness.”

She swallowed. “My steadiness is fine.”

“Is it?” he asked, and his tone made the question sound like he knew precisely how her hands had trembled on the subway pole all the way here. “Aim.”

The targets were paper silhouettes pinned in neat lines. She realized, with a slow creep of unease, that they were men—little black cutouts with hats, like the men she’d seen near the ticket booth that day. Like the men Daniel had mentioned.

She tightened her grip. “That’s in poor taste.”

“Is it?” he echoed. “Or is it instructive?”

The sodium lamp above them flickered hard, and for an instant Daniel’s face was all shadow except his eyes, which seemed to hold their own light. When the lamp steadied, he looked almost ordinary again. That was worse.

“What do you want from me?” she asked, voice low.

Daniel’s answer came after a beat too long. “To see whether you can do what you say you can.”

“I haven’t said I can do anything.”

“You said—” He leaned closer, and the edge in him sharpened, controlled but unmistakable. “You said you were tired of being good for nothing but waiting.”

Her breath caught. She had said it. Not aloud. Not exactly. In a letter she hadn’t signed, slid beneath a door she hadn’t wanted to admit she’d found.

“You read my—” she started.

“I listened,” he corrected. “You can tell a lot from what a woman chooses to write when she thinks no one will answer.”

Her fingers went cold around the rifle. *This is madness. This is exactly how respectable women become headlines.* Yet the part of her that had been suffocating for years—under duty, under polite conversation, under the soft prison of being looked after—leaned toward him as if toward a heat source.

Daniel nodded toward the paper men. “Take the first shot.”

“I don’t want to hurt anyone,” she said, and hated how weak it sounded.

“Then don’t,” he replied. “It’s paper.”

“It doesn’t feel like paper.”

“That,” he said, “is the point.”

The barker slid cartridges into a tray. “Whenever you’re ready, miss.”

She lifted the rifle, surprised by its weight, by the way it demanded her shoulder and her cheek. The world narrowed to a sight line. The boardwalk noise fell away until she could hear her own breath.

Daniel spoke quietly, right behind her now. “Breathe in.”

She did, and smelled his cologne—something clean under something dangerous, like soap over smoke.

“Hold,” he said.

Her finger found the trigger. The flickering lights made the target jerk in and out of clarity, as if it were trying to dodge her.

“Do you trust me?” Daniel asked, almost conversational.

The question wasn’t fair. It wasn’t even about the gun.

“No,” she whispered.

His laugh was soft. “Good.”

“Then why are you here?” she asked, still aiming.

“Because you came,” he said. “Because you’re standing here with a rifle in your hands and you haven’t run.”

The barker called, “Take him out, sweetheart!”

Her finger tightened. The trigger resisted, then gave—just slightly.

Daniel’s voice slid in, velvet over steel. “Now decide what kind of woman you are.”

The shot cracked through the dusk, sharp as breaking glass. A tinny bell rang somewhere on the track. The paper silhouette jerked, a neat hole punched through its chest.

For a moment she couldn’t breathe. Not from fear of the sound, but from the shock of satisfaction that flared in her like a match.

Daniel watched her profile, his expression unreadable in the flicker of sodium light.

“You see?” he said, so close she felt the words against her skin. “You can hit what you aim at.”

She lowered the rifle, hands suddenly unsteady. “That doesn’t mean anything.”

“It means everything,” he replied. “It means you’re not as innocent as you’ve been pretending.”

She met his eyes, heart pounding, and heard herself say the most dangerous thing of all:

“What do you want me to aim at next?”
