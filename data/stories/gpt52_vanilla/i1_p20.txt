The bar squats between a pawn shop and a shuttered noodle joint, its windows filmed with grease and cigarette tar until the neon outside turns into bruised halos. Inside, the air is warm with stale beer and old violence. A neon jukebox in the corner keeps playing the same scratchy rock track on repeat—guitar riff, drum kick, the singer’s voice warping like it’s trapped in a tin can—over and over until it stops sounding like music and starts sounding like teeth grinding.

Spike Spiegel slips in like smoke finding a crack. He’s tall, loose-limbed, a dark suit that never quite looks clean, hair falling in stubborn waves. A cigarette hangs off his mouth, unlit. His left eye tracks the room a fraction slower than his right, that tiny hitch in depth that only shows when he reaches for the barstool and touches wood a heartbeat late.

He doesn’t look like a man chasing money. He looks like a man killing time until time kills him back.

The bartender clocks him and decides Spike is either trouble or broke. Probably both.

Spike sits anyway. “Whiskey. Cheap.”

The bartender pours without comment. The scratchy track restarts. Again.

Spike lets the glass sit there for a second, watching the amber wobble. He’s hungry in the familiar way, hollowed out by weeks of stale ramen and bad leads. This bounty—some accountant-turned-runner with a habit of selling data to syndicates—smells like enough credits to buy food, fuel, and maybe a few days of not worrying.

The door opens and the bar’s temperature shifts.

Revy walks in like the room owes her something. Short shorts. Black crop top. Dragon tattoo coiling down her right arm like it’s alive under the skin. Two pistols riding low at her hips, not hidden, not even pretending to be. Her boots hit the floor with purpose. Her eyes scan faces like targets, not people.

She catches Spike’s profile at the bar and stops just long enough for the jukebox to scrape through the chorus again.

Spike doesn’t turn right away. He feels her presence like a gun aimed at the back of his neck. When he does glance over his shoulder, he meets a stare that doesn’t blink.

Revy’s mouth curls, not a smile. “You’ve got that ‘I’m too cool to die’ look.”

Spike taps the unlit cigarette against his lower lip. “I’ve already died once.”

Revy snorts and walks to the bar, taking the stool one space away like personal distance is a myth. “Then you won’t mind if I put holes in you.”

“Depends,” Spike says. “Are you buying the next round?”

She signals the bartender. “Rum. No ice.”

The bartender pours like he’s defusing a bomb.

Spike finally lights his cigarette. The flame briefly paints his face—tired eyes, a shadow of something old behind them. He exhales slowly, watching smoke braid with the bar’s haze. “You here for the numbers guy?”

Revy’s laugh is short and sharp. “Yeah. And I’m here to tell you to get lost.”

Spike takes a sip of whiskey. Cheap, burning. “Small universe.”

“Or you’ve got a bad habit of showing up where you’re not wanted.”

“I can relate,” Spike says, and it comes out softer than he intends.

Revy downs half her rum and wipes her mouth with the back of her hand. “Don’t get cute with me, suit.”

Spike’s gaze dips—quick, assessing: the pistols, the stance, the tension in her shoulders like coiled wire. “You’re packing heavy for a conversation.”

“I don’t do conversations,” she says. “I do outcomes.”

The jukebox restarts the same scratchy track. Again.

Spike’s eyes flick toward it. “Someone ought to kill that thing.”

“Add it to your bounty list.”

The tension between them doesn’t settle; it leans in. Revy’s knee bounces once, impatient. Spike sits too still, like a man who’s learned that stillness can be a weapon.

A trio of locals at a back table—leather jackets, cheap cybernetic implants, bad confidence—notice Revy’s guns and decide it’s funny. One of them says something crude loud enough for her to hear.

Revy’s head turns, slow, predatory.

Spike sighs like it’s all mildly inconvenient. “Don’t.”

“Don’t what?” Revy asks, already sliding off the stool.

“Make a mess,” Spike says. “I just sat down.”

Revy’s eyes narrow, then she grins—wolfish, delighted by the excuse. “Then finish your drink.”

The first guy stands, swaggering. “Hey, sweetheart—”

Revy’s pistol is in her hand before the insult finishes forming. The bar exhales as one body. Spike’s hand drifts toward his own gun, not hurried. The bartender ducks automatically.

Revy fires once. Not at the man—at the bottle on his table. Glass explodes, liquor raining down, the sound like a small bomb. The man flinches back, cursing.

“Sit,” Revy says, voice flat.

He reaches inside his jacket. Bad move.

Spike’s gun clears leather in a smooth arc. He fires a single round that takes the guy’s gun out of his hand—metal snapping, fingers spared by a whisper. The pistol clatters across the floor.

For a second, the entire bar is held in the thin thread of what almost happened.

Revy looks at Spike like he’s a problem she hasn’t decided whether to solve or keep. “Show-off.”

Spike blows smoke from the corner of his mouth. “You were about to paint the wall.”

“Yeah,” Revy says. “It’d look better.”

The other two locals scramble, dragging their wounded pride toward the exit. Nobody laughs now. The jukebox keeps grinding the same track, indifferent.

Revy holsters one pistol, keeps the other loose in her hand like it’s part of her anatomy. “So. Numbers guy. How do you want to do this?”

Spike’s eyes half-lid, casual. “Split?”

Revy’s bark of laughter is pure disbelief. “Split. Listen to you. Like we’re partners.”

Spike shrugs. “I’m not big on attachments.”

Revy’s expression flickers—something sharp behind the sarcasm. “Good. Saves me the trouble of watching you bleed out and feeling something about it.”

The words hang between them, ugly and honest.

Spike finishes his whiskey. “He’s staying at a motel off the highway. Room twelve. Pays in cash, keeps a cheap drone for lookout.”

Revy’s gaze snaps back. “How the hell do you know that?”

Spike taps his temple with two fingers. “I ask questions.”

“Or you’re working for someone,” Revy says, suspicion sliding into place like a blade.

Spike stands, stretching like a cat. “Whatever happens, happens.”

Revy drains her rum and tosses bills on the bar. “Yeah. We’ll see.”

They leave together, not side by side so much as orbiting the same target. Outside, neon bleeds onto wet pavement. The night tastes like gasoline and rain.

The motel is worse than the bar—flickering sign, curtains like dirty bandages, the kind of place where secrets go to rot. A dim light buzzes over the walkway. Room twelve sits at the end, door shut, no sound.

Revy moves first, silent despite her boots. Spike follows, hands loose, eyes scanning angles. His depth is a little off; he compensates by reading shadows and reflections, by trusting the part of him that survived worse than bad eyesight.

Revy crouches by the window, peeks through a gap in the curtain. “One guy. Bed. Laptop. He’s got a gun on the nightstand.”

Spike leans in, close enough that his breath brushes her ear. Revy stiffens, not from fear—something else, something that pisses her off because she can’t shoot it.

“Got a plan, Two Hands?” Spike murmurs.

Revy glances at him, eyes bright in the motel’s sick light. “Yeah. Kick the door in.”

Spike’s mouth twitches. “Subtle.”

Revy’s grin returns. “Effective.”

She kicks. The door splinters inward. The runner jolts upright, reaching for the gun—

Spike moves like a whip crack. He steps in, gun up, muzzle steady. “Hands where I can see them.”

Revy is already inside, pistols leveled, her stance wide and certain. The runner freezes between them, eyes darting like a trapped animal.

“Please,” the runner babbles. “I can pay—”

“Shut up,” Revy says.

Spike’s gaze flicks to the laptop, the cash on the dresser, the cheap drone controller. “We can take him alive. More money.”

Revy’s jaw tightens. “Or we can take him dead and not babysit.”

Spike’s voice stays mild, but his eyes harden. “Not your call.”

Revy’s laugh is humorless. “Not your call either, pretty boy.”

The runner’s fear spikes; he makes a desperate grab for the nightstand—

Revy fires. The round tears the lamp in half, plunging the room into stuttering darkness and flicker from the motel sign outside. The runner yelps and drops, hands over his head.

Spike doesn’t flinch. He shifts, kicks the gun away. “Enough.”

Revy’s pistols stay up. “You always this calm?”

“I’m always hungry,” Spike says. “Different thing.”

She steps closer, and the space between their guns and their bodies shrinks. The air is thick with cordite and adrenaline, the kind that tastes like wanting. Spike can feel it, the pull—dangerous, stupid, alive. Revy’s eyes rake over him like she’s deciding where to shoot and where to touch, and she hates herself for even having the second thought.

“You think you can trust me?” Revy asks, voice low.

Spike’s cigarette is gone—lost somewhere between bar and motel—but he still looks like smoke. “No.”

Revy’s lips part, almost a smile. “Good.”

The runner whimpers from the floor. Neither of them looks at him now.

Spike’s gun angles slightly—not at Revy’s face, but not away either. “We take him in. We split the bounty. Then we walk.”

Revy’s pistols don’t lower. “You walk. I don’t.”

Spike takes a step closer. The muzzle of his gun is inches from her collarbone. Her pistols are inches from his ribs. Their breathing syncs without permission.

Outside, the neon sign flickers: MOT—EL—MOT—EL.

The jukebox song from the bar isn’t here, but it’s in Spike’s head anyway, that same loop of scratchy defiance. Revy’s eyes are dark and bright at once, like a storm deciding where to break.

“You’re not scared,” she says, almost accusing.

Spike’s voice drops. “I’m scared of different things.”

Revy’s throat works. For a heartbeat, her guns dip—barely, not surrender, more like curiosity. Spike sees it and doesn’t move. Trust is a trap; he knows that. So does she.

Her pistols rise again just as quickly. “Say we do split,” Revy says. “How do I know you won’t screw me?”

Spike’s gaze flicks to her mouth and back to her eyes, a tiny betrayal of interest. “You don’t.”

The runner sobs quietly, forgotten.

Revy steps in until her chest nearly touches his. The guns between them make it intimate in the worst way. Her voice is a rasp. “You want something, Spike?”

Spike’s prosthetic eye catches the motel sign’s flicker, making distance deceptive. He compensates by not reaching, by letting the want sit there like a loaded chamber. “I want breakfast.”

Revy’s laugh is sudden, surprised, and it cracks something open before she slams it shut. “Liar.”

Spike’s lips tilt. “Whatever happens, happens.”

Revy’s eyes narrow, and for a second it looks like she might kiss him just to see if it makes him flinch—or shoot him for making her consider it. The unresolved tension coils tighter, hotter, until the room feels too small for it.

Neither of them lowers their weapon.

Neither of them steps away.

The motel’s bad light flickers, and the night holds its breath, waiting to see which of them pulls the trigger first—or which of them makes a different kind of mistake.
