The door yielded with a sigh like a bellows giving up its last breath.

Aya went first, her gloved hand on the iron latch, her other hand hovering near the concealed slit in her sleeve where a sliver of salt-etched bone waited—an old comfort, a heresy, a mother’s inheritance ground down to something she could hide beneath lace. The air beyond was colder than the corridor above, damp and mineral, laden with the faint, sweet rot of bilgewater and old blood. The underhold of the Morska Guild had always smelled like the city’s underside: tallow, river-silt, rust, and secrets.

Behind her, Katra moved like a cat that had learned to hate cages. Simeon brought up the rear with the careful tread of a man who trusted weight and leverage more than luck. Their lantern threw a narrow cone of light that caught on rivets and pipes, on brickwork slick with condensation, on a web of chains hung for reasons no one asked aloud.

It was too quiet.

No gamblers whispering over dice. No clerk scratching accounts. No sentries feigning disinterest. Even the rats were absent, as though the stones themselves held their breath.

Aya’s pulse tapped in her throat. *It’s a trap,* her mind supplied at once, sensible and useless. *Or else the whole guild has fled. Or else—*

A distant foghorn moaned from the harbour beyond the guild walls.

Once.

The sound rolled through the brick and iron like a warning through bone. Aya paused at the threshold, listening for the usual answering chorus of coughs, curses, boots. Nothing.

Katra leaned close enough that Aya felt her breath, peppermint and cheap gin. “You feel that?” she murmured. “Like a grave waiting to be filled.”

Simeon’s voice came low, practical. “Door’s open because someone wanted it open.”

Aya swallowed. “Then we won’t keep them waiting.”

They stepped in.

The underhold chamber widened into a cavernous space where the guild’s heart beat—if one could call it a heart. Pipes ran along the walls like ribs. Brass gauges stared down with blind glass eyes. A skeletal gantry crossed overhead, its chains and hooks hanging in stillness. In the centre stood the vault-mouth: an arched door of scythan make, dark metal chased with patterns that hurt the eye if you looked too long, as if the design refused to be understood by sane minds. Around it, the guild had built their own protections: steam valves, pressure locks, and a ring of gas lamps whose light trembled sickly green.

And in front of the vault-mouth, in that jaundiced light, stood a woman in a long coat of oiled black leather, her hair pinned up with a severity that made her cheekbones look sharper. A cigarette burned between two fingers, ash poised and unfallen, as though time itself had been made to mind its manners.

Mara Veldt.

Guild-leader. Supposed prisoner. Supposedly dragged from her bed in the small hours by constables with warrants and righteous enthusiasm.

Decidedly un-incarcerated.

She did not bother with surprise. She regarded Aya’s lantern, then Aya’s mask, then the two figures at her flanks, with the calm interest of someone appraising meat.

Around Mara, half a dozen bodies arranged themselves in the shadows with professional ease. Men in boiled coats and leather aprons. A woman with a shock of white hair and a brass monocle that clicked as it adjusted. A hulking dock-hand type with hands like mallets and a chain wrapped twice around his forearm. Weapons were present without being waved: pistols, cudgels, a narrow bayonet whose edge caught the gaslight like a sliver of moon.

A second foghorn sounded.

Twice.

Mara took a slow drag, exhaled smoke that curled like a question mark. “Well,” she said, voice rough velvet. “My little wraith has come down from the drawing rooms.”

Katra’s posture shifted, a fraction. Simeon’s hand hovered near his coat. Aya forced herself to stay still. *No fight,* she told herself, though the word *fight* had never been the danger. The danger was what came after, when men with brass and oil decided you were a problem to be solved permanently.

Aya tried for coolness. “You’re alive,” she said.

Mara’s mouth twitched. “Disappointed?”

“I was… informed,” Aya said carefully, “that you were taken.”

Mara’s eyes flicked to Katra, then Simeon. “You were informed because I wanted you informed.” She stepped forward, boots clicking on damp stone. “Did you think a raid happens on Morska without Morska’s permission?”

Katra spat, the sound small in the vast chamber. “So you sold out half your people to make a point.”

“To make a correction,” Mara replied, unbothered. “A guild is a machine. When a gear starts believing it can become the hand that turns the key, you remove the gear before it grinds the whole assembly into scrap.”

Aya felt her stomach tighten. She could feel the immateria like a bruise behind her ribs—an ache that answered fear with hunger. She pictured her mother on the gallows platform, the way the crowd had leaned forward as if attending the opera. The way law and superstition had held hands and called it justice.

Mara’s gaze fixed on Aya’s mask. “Take it off.”

Aya didn’t move.

The woman with the brass monocle—one of Mara’s bodyguards—made a small motion. Two men stepped from the shadows as if they had been poured out of them. One seized Aya’s wrist, fingers digging through glove and skin. The other hooked a thumb under the edge of her mask.

Katra surged forward a step. Simeon started to raise his hands as if to argue with an invisible judge.

Mara’s voice cut in. “Stay.”

It wasn’t shouted. It didn’t need to be. The room complied.

Aya’s breath shortened. She could have called something, she knew—could have opened the thin seam between worlds with a word she had learned in whispers and nightmares. But there were gas lamps here, steel valves, pressure locks. Men with pistols who would fire on reflex. And if she called, if she let the immateria seep into this place, it wouldn’t only take her enemies. It would take anyone it could find purchase in.

The bodyguard pulled.

The mask came away. Cold air kissed Aya’s face like an accusation. Her hair, pinned to hide beneath the disguise, loosened slightly. The lantern light caught on her features—on a mouth used to measured smiles, on eyes too curious for their own safety.

Mara’s brows lifted, slow as a curtain. “Ah.”

Aya held Mara’s stare and felt the old aristocratic lessons rise like a shield: chin level, eyes steady, do not flinch. *If they see fear, they own you.*

Katra’s voice came out raw. “Aya…?”

Simeon went very still. His jaw worked once, as though he’d bitten down on a thought.

Mara tasted the moment like fine liquor. “Lady Aya Rurik,” she said, pronouncing the name with deliberate care. “The harbour’s darling. The Academy’s bright little curiosity. The sort the newspapers write about when they want to pretend this city has a soul.”

Aya’s throat tightened. Her true name sounded obscene down here, among pipes and chains. “I don’t know what you mean.”

Mara flicked ash to the stones. “Don’t insult me, girl.” Her eyes narrowed with something that might have been amusement, might have been pity. “Did you imagine your perfume didn’t cling to you? Did you think your hands, no matter how often you dirtied them, would ever quite forget silk?”

The third foghorn sounded, long and mournful.

Three.

It felt like the harbour itself tolling a bell for the dead.

Mara made a small gesture. The men holding Aya shifted their grip. Another pair moved in on Katra and Simeon with the efficiency of workers securing cargo. Simeon raised his hands higher, palms out, choosing survival over pride.

Katra fought only with her eyes. “Touch me again and I’ll bite your throat out,” she hissed.

“Romantic,” Mara said. “Bind them.”

Rope rasped. Leather straps clicked. Aya’s wrists were pulled behind her, tied tight enough that her fingers tingled. The knot was sailor’s work—quick, brutal, final.

Aya forced her voice to stay level. “You staged your arrest to draw out… what? Disloyalty?”

Mara’s smile showed no warmth. “Ambition. Treachery. Curiosity. All the little worms that wriggle in the dark when they think the lantern’s gone.” She stepped close enough that Aya smelled tobacco and oil on her breath. “You, for instance. I wondered when you’d stop playing thief and start reaching for crowns.”

Aya’s mind raced, a frantic ledger of consequences. If Mara spoke her name aboveground—if word reached the constables, the papers, the Academy—her father’s house would be ruined in a week. And if anyone remembered her mother beyond the scandalous headline… *immateria.* The rope around Aya’s wrists felt suddenly like a noose rehearsing.

Katra snapped, “If you’re going to kill us, get on with it.”

Simeon’s eyes flicked to Aya, then away, as if he could not decide whether to look at her was betrayal or loyalty. “Mara,” he said carefully, “there’s no need for blood. We were—misinformed.”

Mara turned her head just enough to regard him. “Were you?” Her gaze sharpened. “Or did you smell a vacuum and think you could become air?”

Aya drew in a breath that tasted of rust and damp. *Talk. Keep talking. Don’t let the silence turn into a gunshot.*

She lifted her chin. “I am valuable to you.”

Mara’s eyes returned to her, bright with interest. “Go on.”

Aya heard her own voice as if from a distance: calm, aristocratic, practiced. “If you kill me, you lose leverage. You lose access. My family’s name opens doors your men cannot even knock on without being shot. There are bankers who will answer my letters. Customs clerks who will misplace manifests for me. Officers who will look the other way because my father once hosted their superiors for supper.”

Katra made a strangled sound, half disbelief, half anger. Simeon stared at Aya as though seeing her for the first time—not in a mask, not in the underhold’s grime, but as what she had always been beneath it.

Mara watched Aya with the focus of a surgeon. “And if I keep you,” she said softly, “I gain all that. Is that your argument?”

“Yes,” Aya said, and felt sick with the clarity of it. “I am more use to you breathing than dead.”

Mara tapped the cigarette against her lip, considering. “Useful people are usually the most dangerous kind.”

Aya’s pulse thundered. She tasted metal. “I can be made less dangerous.”

“How?” Mara asked, almost conversational.

Aya’s mind flashed with images: her mother’s wrists bound; the hood over her head; the crowd’s murmur like surf. *Do not beg,* she told herself, but her body wanted to. “You can keep me as… insurance. As a hostage. Quietly. No one need know where I am. My disappearance will be noticed, yes. But it will be noticed in a way that can be guided. Rumour can be steered.”

Mara leaned in, close enough that Aya saw the fine lines at the corners of her eyes—lines earned, not inherited. “And your friends?” Mara asked, glancing toward Katra and Simeon, bound and held between guards.

Aya’s chest tightened. *Friends.* The word felt precious and damning. “They’re loyal,” she said quickly. “They came for the artifact because they believed the guild needed a hand on the wheel.”

“Loyal,” Mara repeated, as if tasting the word for poison. “To whom?”

Aya opened her mouth, and for the first time since the door had sighed open, she did not know what to say.

Katra’s voice cut through, sharp as broken glass. “Don’t you dare sell us, Aya.”

Simeon swallowed. “Aya,” he said, quieter, not accusing—afraid. “What are you doing?”

Aya’s stomach twisted. She had meant to bargain with her own worth, not theirs. But bargaining was a hungry thing. It ate whatever was closest.

Mara straightened, cigarette ember glowing in the greenish light. “Here is what you are going to do,” she said, and the room seemed to constrict around her voice. “You are going to tell me why an aristocrat with a dead witch for a mother thinks she can wear my guild like a costume. You are going to tell me what you’ve touched in the immateria, and what it’s touched back.” Her gaze hardened. “And then we will discuss whether you are leverage… or contagion.”

At the word *witch*, the old bruise behind Aya’s ribs flared, a dark warmth, as if something on the other side of the world’s skin had turned its head.

The gas lamps hissed. The scythan vault-door loomed behind Mara like a sealed mouth.

Aya forced herself to meet Mara’s eyes, even as dread crawled up her spine with cold, familiar fingers. *This is it,* she thought. *The moment the mask comes off and the world decides what it will do with what it sees.*

Aloud, she said, “If you kill me, Mara, you will have the law at your throat within days. If you keep me, you may yet have a city.”

Mara smiled, slow and terrible. “A city,” she echoed. Then she flicked her cigarette to the stones and ground it out beneath her boot like a spell. “Bring them in,” she told her people. “The underhold has been too quiet. Let’s see what screams sound like in it.”
