The abandoned temple had lost its name long before it lost its roof.

Wind worried at broken rafters, and vines threaded through cracked stone like sutures that couldn’t hold. At the center of the nave, where an altar had once faced a congregation, a square of bare flagstones had been swept clean—an island of fight-space amid rubble. A single torch, jammed into a split pillar on the north side, hissed and guttered in its own grease. Its light skated across fallen beams and chewed-up carvings, carving the room into sharp blacks and moving amber.

They kept the crowd back with a rough ring—men and women pressed shoulder to shoulder, lanterns forbidden, coins already changing hands in quick whispers. Jigen-ryū supporters clustered along the east wall beneath a collapsed gallery, their posture stiff, chins lifted as if even the dust should bow. Across from them, along the west where the temple’s outer wall had split open to the night, Piguaquan adherents stood looser, hands in pockets, grins like blades. Between the factions ran a narrow corridor of empty air—an agreement enforced not by law but by the threat of what would happen if it broke.

In the center, two young men faced each other with three paces of stone between them.

Sakon of Jigen-ryū stood nearer the torch, slightly north of center, the flame behind his left shoulder. He wore a short jacket tied tight at the waist; his sleeves were rolled high, forearms corded. His feet were set in a forward stance, left foot leading, weight coiled and ready to spring. His hands were open but tense, fingers flexing as if gripping an invisible hilt. In his head lived the old arrogance of his line—an art meant for men who owned land and bloodlines and the right to look down.

Wei Jun of Piguaquan stood south of him, closer to the broken wall where cold air poured in. He angled his body sideways, right shoulder forward, knees soft. His arms hung almost carelessly, but the slack was deceptive—wrists loose, elbows ready to swing like hinged gates. He had the hard compact build of someone who’d hauled and shoved and fought for space in alleys. The disdain in his eyes was for pretension itself.

No masters watched. No sanction hung over this. Only the torch and the murmuring circle.

Sakon inhaled through his nose, lifted his chin a fraction, and stepped forward with his left foot, closing the gap from three paces to two. It was not a probing step; it was a claim. His right heel hovered, ready to drive.

Wei Jun didn’t retreat straight back. He slid his rear foot (left) half a step out to his left, turning his torso so he was more side-on, giving Sakon less to hit. The movement widened the angle between them: Sakon remained north-center; Wei Jun drifted slightly toward the southwest quadrant of the flagstones, closer to the broken wall.

Sakon exploded.

He drove off his right foot and surged forward in a straight line, his left foot stamping down hard on the stone to anchor, hips snapping. His right hand shot out like the first cut of a sword—an open-handed strike aimed at Wei Jun’s face, the kind of decisive, overwhelming entry Jigen-ryū prized.

Wei Jun read the line and refused it. He shifted his weight onto his right leg and pivoted clockwise, letting Sakon’s strike pass just outside his left cheek. At the same moment, his left arm whipped up from low to high in a wide arc—Piguaquan’s signature: a long, circular backhand meant to clip the neck or jaw as the opponent committed.

The arc would have landed if Sakon had remained in the lane. He didn’t. As soon as his right hand passed empty air, Sakon’s left hand snapped up, forearm angled, catching Wei Jun’s whipping arm on the inside near the wrist. The block was sharp and short, stopping the swing’s last third. Sakon’s feet were now inside Wei Jun’s space—Sakon’s left foot planted near the center; his right foot had slid up behind it, narrowing the distance to less than a pace.

With contact made, Sakon tried to end it. He drove his right shoulder forward and threw a straight palm strike toward Wei Jun’s sternum, aiming to knock him back into the rubble and finish with a follow-up. The torchlight flashed on his knuckles; his face held contempt, as if the outcome were owed.

Wei Jun yielded—not backward, but sideways. He let his torso fold and turn, absorbing the palm on his right pectoral rather than center mass, and used the push to spin counterclockwise. His feet skittered: right foot pivoting, left foot stepping around to the outside. The palm didn’t stop him; it powered him into a new angle.

Now Wei Jun was no longer in front of Sakon. He was at Sakon’s right flank, slightly behind him, occupying the southeast of the square. Sakon, momentum forward, had his chest facing south where Wei Jun had been. For half a heartbeat, Sakon’s back quarter was exposed.

The Piguaquan crowd hissed approval—soft, like a knife drawn.

Wei Jun’s right arm snapped out in a whipping forearm strike toward the back of Sakon’s ribs. The motion started from his waist, not his shoulder, and it came around like a rope cracking. He targeted the lower floating ribs—painful, destabilizing.

Sakon sensed it more than saw it. He dropped his weight by bending both knees and rotated sharply to his right, bringing his elbow down and back. Wei Jun’s forearm collided with Sakon’s braced elbow instead of flesh, the impact jolting both. The sound was a dull crack in the torchlit air.

Sakon completed the turn and squared up again, now facing southeast, bringing them back to front-on with barely a pace between them. His eyes were hot. He didn’t waste the reset.

He stamped forward with his right foot and drove a headlong attack—left hand feinting high, right hand punching straight at Wei Jun’s nose. It was the Jigen-ryū philosophy embodied: overwhelm, don’t negotiate.

Wei Jun’s head tilted off-line as his feet slid. He stepped his right foot back and to the right, creating a diagonal retreat toward the south edge of the flagstones, keeping his torso angled. The punch skimmed past his left ear, close enough to stir hair.

But Wei Jun didn’t just evade. As Sakon’s arm extended, Wei Jun’s left hand snapped down to slap at Sakon’s wrist, redirecting the line. His right arm followed immediately—another circular strike, this time a horizontal whip aimed at Sakon’s temple from the outside.

Sakon could not block both with rigid stops without losing ground. He chose to crash in. He stepped in again—left foot forward, chest pressing close—and jammed Wei Jun’s swinging arm by catching it at the bicep with his left forearm. Their bodies collided shoulder to shoulder.

For the first time, they were chest-close, each trying to claim the center.

Wei Jun’s loose structure became dangerous up close. He dipped his weight and snapped his right knee up toward Sakon’s thigh, aiming the inner seam—where pain steals stance. Sakon reacted by turning his right hip outward and dropping his right elbow to smother the knee, absorbing it on the forearm and hip rather than letting it bite into muscle.

Sakon answered immediately with a short, brutal strike: a right forearm smash, rising from low to high, aimed under Wei Jun’s chin. It was not elegant; it was meant to break posture and end the argument.

Wei Jun’s spine arched back, not by stepping away but by folding at the hips and letting his head tilt. The forearm grazed his jawline instead of catching clean. He let the graze carry him into a retreating pivot, and as he turned, his left hand latched briefly to Sakon’s sleeve near the elbow—just a moment of tether.

Then he yanked.

The pull wasn’t meant to drag Sakon far; it was meant to make him step where Wei Jun wanted. Sakon’s weight was forward—of course it was—and his right foot had to step to recover. It landed near a loose tile at the edge of the cleaned square, north of a pile of broken roof slats.

The tile shifted.

Sakon’s ankle rolled a fraction. His posture dipped—not a fall, but a stumble. The circle of onlookers tightened with a collective intake of breath.

Wei Jun capitalized in the instant. He slid in from Sakon’s left, moving into the space the stumble opened, and swung a wide right backfist in a descending arc toward Sakon’s collarbone. The strike came like a flail, powered by a step that carried Wei Jun northward back toward the torch.

Sakon recovered with ferocity. He slammed his left foot down to re-anchor, turned his shoulders into the strike, and raised his right forearm to meet it. The impact landed on bone, jarring. But Sakon was now stable again—and close enough.

He surged forward off that block, turning defense into entry. His left hand shot out to seize Wei Jun’s right wrist as the arm rebounded. His grip locked, not delicate. With the wrist controlled, Sakon stepped diagonally to his left (toward the temple’s east side), pulling Wei Jun off his line and twisting the captured arm across Wei Jun’s body.

Wei Jun’s feet scrambled to avoid being toppled. He stepped with his left foot across his own centerline, turning his torso with the twist rather than resisting it straight. The motion kept his shoulder from torquing too far. He bent his elbow, slackening the lever Sakon wanted.

But Sakon didn’t try for a joint lock. He used the moment of control to launch the strike he’d been saving: a straight right palm to the face, driving from his hips with a sharp exhale, the classic decisive blow.

The torch flared as if startled.

Wei Jun’s head snapped back—this one landed, heel of the palm catching his cheekbone and jaw. His body recoiled toward the south, heels skidding on dust. He didn’t go down, but he lost a full step of space, forced back near the broken wall where night air made the flame’s shadow dance wildly.

Sakon advanced, scenting finish. He crossed the distance in two quick stamps, each footfall loud on stone. His hands rose, ready to strike again, eyes fixed like spearpoints.

Wei Jun’s face tightened; blood shone at the corner of his mouth in the torchlight. He didn’t retreat farther—there wasn’t room without stepping into rubble. Instead, he changed levels. As Sakon came in, Wei Jun dropped his weight low and to his left, slipping under Sakon’s line of attack, and swung both arms in a broad, scooping motion—left hand hooking behind Sakon’s lead knee, right forearm crashing into Sakon’s hip.

It was a commoner’s answer to samurai speed: take the base.

Sakon’s left knee was hooked; his hip was struck. His center of gravity tipped. He tried to sprawl back, throwing his right leg behind him, but the temple floor betrayed him again—dust and grit underfoot, the earlier shifted tile. His foot slipped a handspan.

Wei Jun drove through, not straight forward but diagonally across Sakon’s front, pushing him toward the torch-side pillar. Sakon’s shoulder hit stone with a thud, stopping his fall but pinning him for a blink—his back against the cracked pillar north of center, torchlight flaring behind him, his shadow huge on the wall.

Wei Jun rose with him, staying tight. His right arm whipped up from low to high—an uppercutting backfist aimed at Sakon’s throat.

Sakon’s response was pure Jigen-ryū: end it even from disadvantage. He shoved off the pillar with his left shoulder, stepping his right foot forward to re-center, and drove a straight left spearhand toward Wei Jun’s eyes.

For a frozen instant, both strikes were committed, both bodies moving into harm.

Wei Jun’s head snapped to the outside, the spearhand grazing his brow instead of blinding him. His backfist clipped Sakon’s throat just off-center, a glancing but ugly hit that made Sakon cough air.

They separated by force rather than choice: Sakon staggered one step east, hand at his neck; Wei Jun stumbled one step south, blinking, blood on his lips and now a smear above his eye. The distance between them reopened to two paces, the flagstones between them empty again.

Around them the crowd surged but did not breach the ring. Coins paused mid-wager. Pride tightened faces into masks.

Sakon’s breathing was harsh, and his stance lowered again, anger burning through the cough. Wei Jun rolled his shoulders, loose despite the pain, feet already circling to his left to search for a new angle—always the angle.

Under the single torch, their shadows tangled on the ruined wall like fighting spirits.

Neither looked toward the exits. Neither thought of their masters’ prohibitions or the generations of bitterness that had led them here. In that crumbling temple, in that illicit square of stone, the rivalry had become something simpler and more dangerous: two hot hearts insisting the world be arranged to fit their pride.

Sakon stepped first, cutting the distance with a stamp that promised finality.

Wei Jun shifted off-line, grin gone now, eyes sharp as broken glass—and the duel, unsanctioned and inevitable, surged into its next collision.
