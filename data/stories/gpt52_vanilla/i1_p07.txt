Den Den Town on a Saturday sounded like everything at once: arcade jingles bleeding into shop demos, the clack of gachapon capsules, someone yelling about a sale on used PCs. Under it all, a brighter thread—an electric guitar line cutting clean through the noise.

She followed it like a cursor snapping to a waveform.

Near the entrance of a crowded arcade, a girl stood on a folded amp case with a battered Strat-style guitar. Her hair was pulled up messy, like she’d decided five minutes ago it was in the way. A tip jar sat on the ground with a handwritten sign: *“Thank you! (No requests unless you smile)”*.

The guitar theme hit a familiar melody—one of those classic anime openings you couldn’t un-hear once it lodged in your head. Only this version was faster, sharper, like the song had grown up and learned how to bite.

The shy girl stopped at the edge of the small crowd. Her oversized headphones slid down a little as she leaned forward without meaning to.

“—okay, okay,” the busker said between phrases, grinning as she played, as if the guitar and her mouth were part of the same performance. “You’re gonna pretend you don’t know this one?”

The crowd laughed. Coins clinked.

The shy girl’s fingers tightened around the strap of her tote. Inside: a small synth module in bubble wrap she’d bought secondhand, and the receipt she’d already checked four times to make sure it was real.

The busker’s left hand climbed the neck. A run of notes spilled like glass marbles down stairs—clean, bright, impossibly controlled.

The shy girl forgot to breathe until the chorus landed. She nodded—once, quick—and her headphones slipped. She jerked them up with both hands, cheeks warm.

The busker’s eyes flicked toward her mid-solo. Just a glance. But it felt like a spotlight.

When the song ended, applause rose. Someone shouted, “Encore!”

The busker bowed exaggeratedly, hair falling into her face. “Thank you, thank you, I’ll be here all week—actually, no, I’ll be here until security remembers I exist.”

More laughter. She crouched, tweaked something on her pedal board, then looked up directly at the shy girl.

“You,” she called, voice bright. “Headphones. You’re gonna break your neck nodding like that.”

The shy girl blinked. She pointed at herself, as if there were another person with giant headphones behind her.

“Yes, you.” The busker pushed a strand of hair back with the side of her hand. “Come here. You look like you know what a compressor does.”

A couple people in the crowd turned to see who she meant. The shy girl’s stomach flipped. She took one cautious step forward, then another, like approaching a stray cat.

“I—I’m just listening,” she said. It came out smaller than she meant.

“Listening is a crime in Den Den Town,” the busker said. “We only shop and regret.”

The shy girl’s lips twitched. It almost counted as a smile.

The busker nodded toward the headphones. “Those are cute. But they’re… like, too big cute.”

“They’re—” The shy girl swallowed. “They’re… studio ones. I got them secondhand. The clamp is loose.”

“Clamp,” the busker repeated, as if testing the word. “Okay, so you *do* know words.”

The shy girl looked at the guitar, then at the pedal board, then at the amp. Her gaze stayed carefully off the busker’s face, like it was too bright to look at directly.

“Your tone is…” She hesitated, searching for safe ground. “It’s very… present.”

The busker’s eyebrows jumped. “Present. That’s a dangerous compliment. Present like a gift, or present like a math teacher?”

“Like—” The shy girl’s hands moved a little, as if shaping sound in the air. “Like it sits in front of the crowd. Not… buried.”

“Mmm.” The busker leaned down and turned a knob a fraction. “That’s because I’m fighting the arcade. It’s basically a boss battle.”

The shy girl nodded again—too fast. The headphones slipped. She grabbed them before they fell.

The busker pointed, laughing softly. “See? There it goes again. Your headphones are trying to escape.”

“I’m sorry,” the shy girl said, automatically, even though she wasn’t sure what she was apologizing for.

“Don’t apologize. I like it. Makes you look like a serious researcher in a documentary.” The busker set her pick between her teeth and crouched by the tip jar. “You wanna come closer? I don’t bite. Unless you’re a bad metronome.”

The shy girl stepped into the space near the amp. Up close, she could see the busker’s fingers—calluses, a small blister taped over, a faint ink mark on the side of her hand like she’d written a set list and washed it off.

The busker pulled the pick out, then extended her hand. “Name.”

The shy girl stared at the hand a beat too long. Then she took it. The busker’s grip was warm and confident, like a chord change.

“Rin,” she said.

“Rin.” The busker nodded like she’d filed it away somewhere important. “I’m Mei.”

“Mei,” Rin repeated softly.

Mei hooked her guitar strap higher and glanced at Rin’s tote. “What’d you buy? Den Den Town doesn’t let anyone leave without a bag full of something questionable.”

Rin’s shoulders tensed. “It’s… um. A sound module. Used. A small one.”

Mei’s eyes sharpened, interested. “Synth stuff?”

Rin nodded, then remembered the headphones. They slid again. She pushed them up with the heel of her palm. “I like… subtractive synthesis. And FM. But FM is… kind of—”

“Mean?” Mei supplied, grinning.

Rin’s eyes widened a little. “Yes. Mean. Like… it doesn’t forgive you. If your ratios are wrong, it just—” She stopped, realizing she was talking too much. “Sorry.”

Mei waved a hand. “No. Don’t stop. Everyone else talks at me like I’m a jukebox. You’re talking like you actually *hear* things.”

Rin looked down, embarrassed, but something loosened in her chest.

Mei nudged her pedal board lightly with the toe of her shoe. “So tell me, Professor Rin. My tone. What would you change?”

Rin froze. “I—I wouldn’t—”

“Come on,” Mei coaxed. “Be honest. I can take it. I’m very emotionally stable.”

Rin’s mouth opened, then closed. She tried again. “Your high end is… kind of—”

“Kind of what?” Mei leaned in.

“Sharp,” Rin said, quick, before she could soften it. “But… it matches the song. It cuts through the arcade. If you rolled it off, you’d disappear.”

Mei stared at her, then laughed—delighted, not mocking. “Okay, that’s actually a good answer. You’re dangerous.”

Rin blinked. “Dangerous?”

“Yeah. Quiet people with opinions.” Mei strummed a chord, then muted it with her palm. “You play anything?”

Rin shook her head. “No. I… program. Mostly. I make patches and—” She swallowed. “And I record little loops.”

“Loops.” Mei nodded, as if that made perfect sense. “So you’re a musician. Just… the kind that hides.”

Rin’s fingers touched the edge of her tote. “It’s not… hiding.”

“It’s okay,” Mei said, softer. Then she brightened again like a switch flipped. “Wait. You recognized the theme, right?”

Rin nodded, almost imperceptible.

Mei’s eyes lit up. “Which version is your favorite?”

Rin hesitated. “The original… but the second opening has better arrangement. The bass line is… more active.”

Mei pointed at her like she’d won something. “Yes! Finally. Someone with taste.” She leaned back, addressing the crowd still lingering. “Everybody heard that? This kid just saved the second opening from being slandered!”

Someone laughed and tossed a coin into the jar. Mei caught it without looking and made a show of biting it like a pirate.

Rin’s shoulders drew in, but she also—surprisingly—smiled. Small, quick, like a note that didn’t want to ring too long.

Mei saw it. “There. That. You smiled. Now I’m allowed to accept requests.”

“I didn’t—” Rin started, then stopped herself. Her smile threatened to come back. She looked away, flustered.

Mei strummed another chord, testing. “Wanna hear something else? Or are you on a mission?”

Rin glanced toward the street, where neon signs promised used consoles and model kits and bargain cables. “I was… going to look at MIDI interfaces.”

Mei made a face of exaggerated seriousness. “MIDI interfaces. The most romantic sentence in the Japanese language.”

Rin let out a quiet sound that might have been a laugh if it had more confidence.

Mei’s grin widened. “Aha. You *do* laugh.”

Rin’s headphones slipped as she nodded. She caught them, sighing under her breath. “I’m sorry. They really—”

Mei stepped closer without hesitation and, with a gentle practicality, adjusted the band on Rin’s head like she’d done it a hundred times. “There. Less escape.”

Rin went still, surprised by the closeness. She could smell a faint citrus shampoo and the warm metallic scent of guitar strings.

Mei stepped back like it was nothing. “Okay. After this next song, I’m taking a break.”

Rin blinked. “Oh. Okay.”

Mei tilted her head. “Do you have plans?”

Rin’s brain scrambled. Plans meant risk. Plans meant saying yes or no with consequences. “I… not really.”

Mei pointed down the street with her pick. “There’s a kaitenzushi place around the corner. Cheap. Fast. Loud enough that you won’t feel weird being quiet.”

Rin stared. “Sushi?”

“Conveyor belt,” Mei said. “You can sit there and overthink salmon in peace.”

Rin’s throat tightened. She wanted to say yes. She also wanted to disappear into a store full of cables where nobody talked to her.

Mei watched her, not impatient—just attentive, like she could hear Rin’s hesitation the way Rin heard a frequency peak.

“You don’t have to,” Mei said, lightly. “I’m inviting you because you look like you haven’t eaten today, and because you just roasted my EQ with scientific precision.”

Rin’s face warmed. “I didn’t roast you.”

“Mm-hm. Whatever you call it.” Mei shouldered her guitar again. “Come on. One plate on me. If you hate it, you can escape and I’ll never emotionally recover.”

Rin’s hands tightened on her tote. She nodded, careful. The headphones slid.

Mei pointed, laughing quietly. “There it goes again. That’s your tell. You’re agreeing.”

Rin pushed them up. “I… I guess so.”

Mei strummed a quick bright chord, like a celebratory jingle. “Yes! Okay, wait here. I’ll finish one more. Don’t run away.”

“I won’t,” Rin said, then realized she’d promised too quickly. She added, softer, “Probably.”

Mei laughed again, genuinely. “Probably is good. Probably is honest.”

She turned back to the crowd, fingers finding the fretboard. “Alright! One more song, then I’m going to commit the ultimate crime—taking a break.”

The opening notes rang out—another anime theme, different era, equally impossible to ignore.

Rin stood by the amp, feeling the guitar vibrate through her shoes, the street noise turning into a backdrop instead of a threat. She adjusted her headphones and, for once, they stayed.

Mei glanced over mid-phrase, eyes bright, and mouthed around the melody, *“Don’t run.”*

Rin swallowed, then nodded—slow this time, like she was learning a new tempo.

“I won’t,” she said, though no one could hear her over the guitar.

But Mei looked anyway, and smiled like she had.
