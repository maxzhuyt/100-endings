The darkroom door sticks the way it always does, like it wants to make you prove you belong here. I shoulder it open anyway, hip first, because that’s how I do most things—decisive contact, no hesitation—then let it thunk shut behind us.

Red light swells in the small space, turning Elliot’s face into something carved from wine-dark wax. The safelight makes everyone look guilty, like they’ve been caught thinking too hard.

He lingers by the sink, hands in the sleeves of his oversized black sweater like he’s trying to disappear into it. His thrift-store nu-goth thing is always immaculate in a deliberate, careless way—mesh underlayer peeking at the collarbone, a chain that might be a necklace or might be a leash if you squint, pants that hang just right off his hips. Tonight his hair’s damp at the ends, like he came straight from the rain.

I set my tote on the counter and start lining up trays, developer to stop bath to fixer, like muscle memory is a spell. “Okay,” I say, because silence in here can turn into pressure. “We’ve got… what, twelve more prints to get through?”

Elliot nods. His voice is soft, always like he’s afraid of taking up too much space in the air. “If we want the series to feel consistent, yeah.”

I like that he says “feel.” Most people in this class talk about concept like it’s a thesis statement. Elliot talks like he’s listening to the photos themselves.

He steps closer to the enlarger. We’re supposed to be a team, but the truth is I’ve been a satellite around him all semester: watching the way he composes without swagger, the way he critiques without cruelty. Fascinated, quietly, because fascination is safer than wanting.

Wanting makes you reckless. Wanting makes you obvious.

Still, here we are. Alone. Late. The building emptied out hours ago except for janitorial carts rolling down distant hallways like ghosts with keys.

I adjust the enlarger head, check the negative carrier. “Your frames are good,” I say, meaning it. “You got that… in-between thing. Like your subject is about to say something but doesn’t.”

His mouth tugs, not quite a smile. “That’s kind of how I feel most of the time.”

I look at him over my shoulder. In the red light his eyes are darker, his lashes a shadowed fringe. “Yeah?” I keep my tone casual on purpose, like I’m not suddenly very aware of my own pulse. “How do you feel most of the time?”

He shifts, weight rocking heel-to-toe. “Like I’m… performing. Even when I’m not trying to.”

That lands somewhere tender in my chest, and my instinct is to grab it, name it, make it manageable. That’s what I do. I make things manageable. I take charge. I solve.

But Elliot isn’t a problem to solve.

I turn back to the enlarger, focusing on the small square of projected light. “We’re all performing,” I say, then regret it immediately because it sounds too neat, too dismissive. I add, quieter, “But I know what you mean.”

He’s silent for a beat. The only sound is the faint hum of the ventilation fan and, beyond that, the distant drip of a faucet that never turns off all the way.

Then he says it, not like a grand announcement, but like he’s placing something fragile on the counter between us. “I’ve been… questioning my gender. Like, for a while.”

My hand pauses on the timer button. The room feels smaller, not in a claustrophobic way but in a focused way, like the red light tightens its grip around the moment.

I’ve slept with enough men and women to know how I’m supposed to respond to vulnerability: hold eye contact, speak softly, don’t make it about you. I’m good at that. People assume “assertive” means “insensitive,” but that’s lazy. Assertive just means I don’t pretend I don’t want things.

What I don’t know is what I want right now.

I turn toward him fully. “Thank you for telling me,” I say. It comes out firm, sincere. “Do you want to talk about what that means for you? Or do you want to just… say it out loud and let it exist?”

His shoulders drop a fraction, like he didn’t realize he was holding them up. “Both? Maybe?” He huffs a tiny laugh, embarrassed. “Sorry. I don’t know how to do this.”

“Don’t apologize.” I step closer, keeping a respectful distance. In the red light, the edges of him blur, but the reality of him doesn’t. “You don’t have to have a script.”

He looks down at his hands. “I don’t think I’m… a man in the way people expect.” He swallows. “But I also don’t know if I’m not. It’s like—sometimes when someone calls me ‘he’ it feels fine, and sometimes it feels like wearing the wrong jacket all day.”

That metaphor hits me because he really does look like a person made of jackets: layers chosen with intent, armor that’s also fashion.

I nod slowly. “So it’s not that everything feels wrong. It’s that some days it’s right, and some days it’s not.”

“Yeah.” His voice is thinner now, stretched tight with honesty. “And I’ve been messing around with how I look because it’s the only part I can control without having to… announce anything.”

Something warm and electric runs under my skin at the thought: him experimenting, claiming space in small ways. It’s attractive in a way I wasn’t prepared for. Not because he looks like a boy playing dress-up, but because he looks like a person in motion.

My brain tries to categorize him—boy, man, not-man, something else—and stalls. I’ve always liked my attractions clean, like bold lines in a poster design: masculine women, feminine men, defined edges. Elliot is all gradation and shadow, like a print that only reveals itself if you stand still long enough.

Maybe that’s why I can’t stop looking.

I clear my throat, buying myself time. “What… do you want me to do with that information?” I ask. My voice is steady, but inside I’m hovering between impulses: take his hand, tell him I’ve got him, tell him he can be whatever he is. Or shut up and give him room.

He lifts his gaze to mine, and for a second I can’t tell if he’s bracing for rejection or hoping for something. “Nothing,” he says quickly, then corrects himself. “I mean—maybe just… don’t make jokes. Don’t make it a thing.”

“I won’t,” I say immediately. “And Elliot?”

“Yeah?”

“If you ever want different words—different pronouns, whatever—you can tell me. No pressure. I’ll follow your lead.”

His expression shifts, subtle but real. Relief, maybe. Or the weird kind of gratitude that comes when someone doesn’t try to steer you.

I’m proud of myself for saying the right thing. That’s a dangerous feeling. Pride can slide into control without you noticing.

To keep my hands busy, I slide a sheet of photo paper out of the box, careful to keep it angled away from any stray light. “Okay,” I say, voice lighter. “Let’s make some art before we spiral into existentialism.”

He snorts softly, a sound that feels like a reward.

We work in a rhythm: I focus the image, he adjusts the contrast filter, I set the timer, he lays the paper on the easel. Our fingers get close without touching, the kind of almost-contact that’s louder than actual touch. Every time our hands brush accidentally, I feel it in my stomach, like my body is taking notes even if my head is confused.

Between prints, we talk—carefully, haltingly. He tells me about trying on his sister’s eyeliner once and not hating his face for the first time in a week. I tell him about the first time I shaved my head at nineteen and how my mother cried like I’d joined a cult. He laughs, then asks, “Did you feel… powerful?”

“I felt like myself,” I say, and it’s true. “And also like people were going to project whatever they wanted onto me anyway, so I might as well give them something to work with.”

He considers that. “That’s kind of… genius.”

“It’s kind of exhausting,” I admit, and the confession surprises me. I’m not used to admitting exhaustion. I’m used to being the person other people lean on, not the one who says, hey, I’m heavy too.

Elliot’s gaze softens. “You don’t seem exhausted.”

“That’s because I’m good at my brand,” I say, and his smile is real this time.

We’re in the middle of setting up the next exposure when it happens.

Elliot reaches past me for the contrast filters, and his sleeve catches the edge of the paper box. The lid flips up. In the same second, my elbow bumps the darkroom door latch—because of course it does, because of course the universe chooses this moment—and the door cracks open with a thin wedge of hallway light spilling in, bright and brutal.

“Shit,” I say, too loud.

Elliot freezes, eyes wide. For half a heartbeat, no one moves, like movement itself might make the damage worse.

The white light hits the stack of photo paper like a slap.

I lunge for the door, slam it shut, and press my palm against it as if I can press time backward. My chest is tight. My brain is already doing the math: how many sheets, how much money, how much time.

Elliot’s voice comes small. “I’m sorry. I didn’t—”

“Hey.” I turn, and the sharpness in my own voice startles me. I force it down, because I can see the way his shoulders climb toward his ears, the way his whole body prepares to be blamed. “No. Don’t. It’s paper.”

He looks at the box like it’s a crime scene. “But it’s… the whole stack.”

I walk over and peer inside. The top sheets are definitely ruined—gray-fogged already, a faint bruise of exposure. Maybe the bottom ones are salvageable, maybe not. It feels like a metaphor I didn’t ask for: a careless moment, light where there shouldn’t be light, something fragile made unusable.

I exhale slowly. “Okay,” I say, because okay is my way of making space around panic. “We can problem-solve. We’ve got some paper in my locker. And worst case, we adjust. We print smaller. We change the sequence.”

Elliot’s eyes flick up to me. “You’re not mad?”

“I’m annoyed at the universe,” I say. “Not at you.”

He swallows. The red light makes his throat look like a pulse. “I just… I feel like I ruin things.”

The words hang there, heavier than the ruined paper.

My instinct is to step in and contradict him, to tell him he’s wrong, to wrap him in certainty like a blanket. But certainty isn’t always kindness. Sometimes it’s just noise.

So instead, I ask, “Do you want me to fix this, or do you want to fix it together?”

He blinks, like he didn’t expect options. Like he didn’t expect his preference to matter.

“Together,” he says, and his voice has a little more shape to it now. “If that’s okay.”

“It’s more than okay.” I reach out, then stop myself half an inch from touching his wrist. I’m suddenly aware of how touch can be a kind of claim. “Can I—?”

He looks at my hand, then back at my face. “Yeah,” he says, barely audible. “You can.”

I let my fingers settle lightly around his wrist, not pulling, just anchoring. His skin is cool. He doesn’t flinch. He doesn’t melt either. He just stays, present, letting me be there without taking him over.

The contact sends a quiet thrill through me, but it’s braided with something else: a tenderness that makes my usual confidence feel too loud, like heels on a library floor.

We stand like that for a second, in the red light, two people with too many questions and not enough language. Then I let go and start opening cabinets, moving with purpose.

Elliot moves with me, grabbing trays, checking the timer, taking direction when I give it and offering his own when he has it. It’s not me leading and him following. It’s us making a new rhythm in the dark, adjusting to the fact that the rules we thought we understood—about paper, about light, about boys, about desire—can change in a single careless second.

While we work, my mind keeps circling the thing he told me. Questioning. Not knowing. Trying things on like jackets.

I realize my confusion isn’t just about him. It’s about me, too—about how much of my attraction has been tied to certainty, to labels that make my own desire feel legible. Elliot’s uncertainty should make him feel distant. Instead it pulls me closer, because there’s something in his openness that makes me want to be braver.

Not braver in the way I’m used to—bolder lipstick, sharper words, taking the first move.

Braver in the way that requires patience. Listening. Letting someone else set the pace.

I glance at him as he slides a fresh sheet onto the easel—one of my stash, saved from my locker like a backup life. He bites his lip in concentration, and the red light catches the curve of it.

“What?” he asks, sensing my stare.

“Nothing,” I say, then decide to be honest in the smallest safe way. “Just… I’m glad you’re here.”

His ears go a shade darker under the safelight. “I’m glad you’re here too.”

And in that moment, with our hands stained by chemistry and our project half-ruined and half-rescued, the darkroom feels less like a place where images are forced to appear and more like a place where you’re allowed to become something slowly.

Not in a flash of white light.

In red, soft, and careful.
