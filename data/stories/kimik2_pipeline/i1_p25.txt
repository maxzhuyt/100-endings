Arthur Neegan pressed his forehead against the transfer vessel’s viewport and watched Phobos skate across the elliptical arc of Mars. The moon’s cratered hide looked like a rusted hubcap that had rolled too far from its wheel. He told himself that if he could keep his eyes on that dead satellite until it vanished he would not think about the four silent marines who shared the cabin with him, nor about the Azra seam under his homestead, nor about the silver-and-black Antares sigil that had been stenciled—without his permission—on every crate of ore he had ever shipped.

The air-filtration system hummed with a persistent mechanical drone, the same B-flat he remembered from the old colonial ferries of his childhood. In those days the sound had meant evacuation drills and ration lines; now it meant diplomatic courtesy. Everything changes, he thought, and nothing does.

A soft chime. The marines rose in perfect unison, rifles slung muzzle-down so that the gravitic clamps would not rattle. The squad leader—her face hidden behind a gold-visored helmet—gestured toward the forward hatch. “Mr. Neegan. Transition in thirty. Please secure personal effects.”

Arthur had no personal effects except the wrist-slate that still bore his late wife’s initials. He tucked it into the breast pocket of his company jacket, feeling the paper-thin weight of the marriage license folded behind the screen. Paper, on Mars, was a declaration of faith in the future; paper, in orbit, felt like a confession of the past.

The hatch irised. Beyond it lay a docking ring—an old-Earth term the Belters still used—half carousel, half torus, spinning at one g for the comfort of diplomats whose bones had never tasted Martian gravity. The marines marched him through a corridor paneled in walnut laminate so perfect it had to be printed. Brass handrails gleamed like fresh pennies. Somewhere aft, a pneumatic tube whispered the way pneumatic tubes always had, carrying sealed capsules of conversation to destinations the speakers would never see.

They stopped at a door marked only by a single etched star. The squad leader palmed the panel; the door slid aside with the reluctant sigh of old hydraulics. Inside, a man waited at a round table set for two. He wore the Antares diplomatic uniform—midnight blue with a mercury-thread crest—yet his collar bore no rank. Arthur, who had spent twenty years decoding the insignia of every bureaucrat who ever signed a supply docket, recognized the absence as a rank in itself.

“Mr. Neegan,” the man said, “I am Counselor Voss. Thank you for accepting our invitation.”

The marines withdrew. The door sealed, and the room’s spin-induced gravity settled on Arthur’s shoulders like a lead scarf. He sat. A carafe of actual water—condensed from Terran clouds, unless the label lied—stood between them. Voss poured two glasses, though he drank none of his.

Arthur decided on bluntness; it was the only currency he hadn’t already lost. “Counselor, I’ve been polite to every lease officer, assay clerk, and safety inspector from Elysium to the Pole. Your people hijacked my shuttle, pointed rifles at my crew, and bundled me into a tin can with a phobos-view window. Let’s skip the diplomatic foreplay.”

Voss smiled the way a calculator smiles: all digits, no warmth. “Very well. The Confederation requires your vein.”

“Requires?”

“Requires,” Voss repeated, tasting the word. “Azra is the first matter we have found that violates the half-life curve without violating thermodynamics. A speck the size of your thumbnail could shorten plutonium decay by four orders of magnitude. Imagine a reactor that burns its waste before the waste exists. Imagine a warhead that leaves no fallout because the fallout has already fallen out.”

Arthur sipped the water. It tasted of Terran thunderstorms, a memory he hadn’t earned. “I’m a mining engineer, not an arms merchant.”

“Which is why we trust you,” Voss said. “You understand containment, shielding, extraction ratios. You also understand—though you have never said so aloud—that Azra cannot be quarried like iron. It must be seduced.”

The phrase hung between them, absurd and obscene. Arthur felt the deck plates vibrate with the torus’s rotation, a heartbeat measured in radians per second. “Seduced how?”

Voss touched the table. A hologram blossomed: Arthur’s homestead, the canyon walls, the buried seam glowing arterial red. “Azra migrates. Under pressure it creeps toward alpha emitters like a moth to candlelight. Your borehole happens to sit above a natural thorium lens. That combination is a coincidence the universe rarely serves twice.”

Arthur stared at the map. The vein resembled a tree root drinking from a hidden lake. He remembered his wife’s botany texts: how roots find water by tasting the soil. “You want me to keep the thorium in place. You want me to persuade the vein to stay put while you siphon it off.”

“Precisely. In exchange, the Confederation will purchase your lease at thirty times market value, guarantee your family’s transport to any system within nine light-years, and deposit a pension indexed to the Terran inflation basket.”

Arthur almost laughed. “I have no family. Mars inflation is a different species.”

Voss’s fingers tapped once, twice. “Then name your currency.”

The silence that followed was old-Earth heavy, Martian thin. Arthur heard the air recyclers cycle, the same B-flat. He thought of the marines, of the sigil, of the paper license in his pocket. “Suppose I decline.”

Voss did not threaten. He simply rotated the hologram. New overlays appeared: corporate fleets converging on Martian orbit, each ship flagged by a different power bloc. “Within six months your seam will be strip-mined by consortia who do not care about thorium lenses. They will fracture the crust, irradiate the aquifers, and leave a crater no atmosphere can refill. You will be compensated, of course—after litigation that outlives you. Azra will reach the black markets within a year. Warheads will follow within two. The Confederation offers stewardship instead of scramble.”

Arthur studied the trajectories. The vectors looked like the spider diagrams he once drew for blast patterns, back when he still believed rock could be coaxed rather than shattered. “Stewardship,” he echoed. “A polite word for monopoly.”

“A polite word for survival,” Voss corrected. “The mathematics of deterrence require scarcity. Azra is too useful to be plentiful.”

Arthur pushed his glass away. Droplets slid along the curvature, obeying Coriolis more than gravity. “You need my consent because the Outer Lease forbids eminent domain on first-generation plots. You also need my silence afterward. That silence has a price you haven’t named.”

Voss inclined his head, acknowledging the calculation. “State it.”

Arthur felt the paper license crackle as he breathed. He thought of his wife’s initials, of the walnut paneling, of Phobos rolling like a lost coin. “I keep the homestead. Full mineral rights revert to me after fifty years or upon my death, whichever comes second. You install containment systems that meet my specifications, not yours. And you fund a colonial scholarship in my wife’s name—enough tuition for ten Martian kids every year to study off-world engineering. They learn how to build what Azra might one day destroy.”

Voss’s fingers hovered above the table, as if the numbers themselves might bite. “Negotiable?”

“Non-negotiable,” Arthur said, surprised by the steadiness of his own voice. “And I publish the containment protocols—open access. If Azra is destiny, let the equation be public. Secrets age badly in vacuum.”

For the first time the Counselor’s composure flickered, a capacitor overloading. Then the calculator smile returned. “I will convey your terms.”

The door opened behind Arthur. The marines reappeared, rifles still muzzle-down, yet the gravitic clamps now seemed louder. Arthur stood, straightening a jacket that had never fit properly on Earth and never would. As he stepped into the corridor he glanced back. Voss was already pouring the second glass of water into a plant pot printed to resemble Terran loam. The roots of whatever grew there would never know thunderstorm, only recycled light.

Arthur followed the brass handrails toward the docking port. The B-flat drone followed him, unchanged since childhood, yet suddenly it sounded less like evacuation and more like countdown. He touched the breast pocket, feeling paper crinkle against slate. Somewhere beneath his feet, Azra crept toward thorium like a root toward water. Somewhere above, fleets converged on vectors he had helped redraw with a single refusal. The torus turned, and the stars outside the viewport held their positions, polite as calculators, patient as glaciers.
