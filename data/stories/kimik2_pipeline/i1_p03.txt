The bell above theoor gave a startled tinkle, like a wineglass tottering on the edge of a shelf. Outside, the camera shutters crackled like hail on slate; inside, dust floated slow as snow through the single shaft of Caernarfon light. Rhys yanked the hood tighter, pressed his back to the closed door, and listened to the hush settle over Cwpwrdd Cwl, a bookshop the width of a train carriage.

“Peace at last,” he announced to the silence. “What a perfect little hide-out you’ve got.”

From behind the counter a woman rose—small, ink-smudged, unimpressed. “This is not Waterstones,” she said. “We do quiet, not hide-outs.”

“Even better. Utopias should be hard to find.” He spread his hands as if unveiling the cramped aisles, the leaning towers of second-hand hymns and out-of-print maps. “Look—oak beams, dragon in the corner, even the smell of moth-eaten myth. You’ve curated Wales herself.”

“I’ve curated what people donate and what nobody buys,” Eira corrected. “You’re dripping rain on the flagstones.”

Rhys crouched to swipe at the puddle of his footprints and spotted a familiar spine of red on the bottom shelf. He tugged it free: Cewri’r Cymylau, the Welsh fairy-tales his grandmother once read aloud while he sucked a lamb-bone for supper. The spine was cracked like an old plate.

“Still here,” he whispered.

“Top shelf on the left, childhood section,” Eira said. “Though it’s effectively a museum. Print run died years ago—no one wants local lore when there’s Netflix.”

He opened the book; the letters looked like musical notes he could no longer play. “I loved this. Can’t read it now. Lost the tongue with the teeth.” The sentence escaped before he could catch it, and his smile faltered, a bulb blowing in the middle of the take.

Eira studied him, curiosity sharpening to sympathy but stopping just short of pity. “So what brings the great Rhys Morgan to a museum?”

He straightened, sparkle rebooted. “I’ll sign your copy if you hide me. Autograph’s going for sixty quid on eBay.”

“We don’t stock celebrity bios.”

“One sentence on the title page, then you can auction it for roof repairs.”

“I like the roof as it is.” She lifted the book from him. “What are you running from?”

He exhaled through teeth that had once grinned beneath Cannes spotlights. “A critic said I couldn’t act my way out of a carrier bag. Tweeted it. Memed it. Now half of Wales is forwarding the gif where I cry in a spaceship.”

She quoted without mercy: “‘Morgan’s emotional register ranges from pout to more pout.’ I read it over breakfast.”

Outside, a paparazzo shouted his name as if hailing a taxi. Rhys flinched. “Back door?” he asked. “Alley? No cameras?”

She jingled a key. “Could be.” Her fingers wrapped the ring, but the door stayed shut. Rain drummed the corrugated roof, sounding like an audience growing restless. “And tomorrow they’ll camp by the castle waiting for your next premiere. You’ll dash from car to carpet, sign fifteen phones, forget this place.”

“I’m forgetting everything already,” he said. The book lay open between them, a cracked map of somewhere he used to live. “Teach me how to say thank you the proper way—like I meant it.”

She considered, key still idle in her hand. “Diolch. But the ‘ch’ is a whisper from the back of the throat, not a cough. Say it right and I might let you try again tomorrow.”

He shaped his mouth around the sound; the syllable emerged half-born, a candle he couldn’t quite cup. Story ended on the soft choke of that consonant, the bell above the door waiting, unrun, the dust still drifting, neither of them moving to close the book.
