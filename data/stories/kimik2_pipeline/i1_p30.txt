1  
Astrid’s fingers tugged through the tangles at Linnea’s nape the way Mum used to when she was little, only firmer, threading strand over strand until the city ponytail became something tighter, something that arched Linnea’s head back just enough to feel the kitchen bulb stinging her eyes. Mum swayed to her old Aqua cassette, half-singing “Barbie Girl,” pretending not to watch. The braid grew like a rope down Linnea’s spine; Astrid’s thumbnail scratched skin once, twice, apology-laugh, then kept weaving. “Suits you,” she whispered. “Like the girls in the mounds wore it.” Linnea snorted, but the mirror showed someone she almost didn’t recognise, temples shining, scalp tingling as if the hair had suddenly decided to stay that way forever.

2  
Tuesday they stole the long matches from Dad’s sauna tin, the ones that strike on anything. Wednesday, Astrid scooped salt from the school mash-potatoes into her palm like a secret handshake. Thursday, Linnea climbed the cemetery fence with a spoon filed sharp, scraping orange dust from the iron spikes into a gum wrapper. They met behind the recycling bins and compared loot like kids trading stickers. “Rust for binding,” Astrid recited, “salt for waking, fire for will.” She crossed them off a list drawn on the back of a geography flyer. “One more thing,” she said, folding the paper into a tiny square and tucking it inside Linnea’s bra strap. “Tomorrow night. Then we’re almost ready.”

3  
They crouched beneath the bike shed, breath fogging. Astrid produced a safety pin, pricked her own thumb first, then Linnea’s, quick as detention. “Spit,” she ordered. They spat into the bleeding crescents, pressed palms together. “Repeat: no chickening out.” Linnea mumbled it; the words tasted metallic. Astrid grinned, smeared the mess on her jeans. “Perfect. Now, do you think Anton uses tongue?” She launched into a saga of last summer’s camping trip, Anton’s allegedly amazing shoulders, while Linnea’s pulse still thumped in her pierced thumb, mixing threat and pop-candy until she couldn’t separate which was hotter, the blood or the gossip.

4  
They slipped out when the last TV light behind the lilac hedge blinked off. Linnea’s trainers crunched gravel once, then grass swallowed them. The security lamp at the corner of the yard stretched their shadows across the potato patch, across the neighbour’s tin fence, across the county line—long, elastic girls nobody bothered to yank back. No dogs barked. Mum’s window stayed dark. Astrid didn’t speak; she simply walked, braid thumping like a heartbeat against her jacket. Linnea followed the rhythm, matches rattling in her pocket like spare bones.

5  
The burial mound rose moon-silvered, a pimple on the flat pasture. Astrid mounted it in three deliberate steps—knee, foot, knee—then stood upright, arms loose at her sides, suddenly taller than the church steeple poking above the spruce. She looked back. Linnea’s turn. The climb felt shorter than childhood, longer than forgiveness. Moss soaked through her jeans until the damp reached skin. Astrid offered her hand; the palm scab was already healing. They knelt, back to back, braid against braid, the way she’d shown in the library book about Viking seers. Wind combed the grass; something underground shifted, making room.

6  
Midnight pressed them together like pages in a book. Astrid draped the stolen gym towel over their joined heads—one breath, two breaths—until darkness tasted of detergent and shared heartbeat. Linnea felt Astrid’s ribs expand, contract, expand, a second set of lungs learning the cadence. The church clock gave one lazy toll, though the sky felt nowhere near one. Wind answered from inside the mound, a sigh that smelled of rust and salt. Neither girl could see the other’s face; Linnea wondered if eyes were open or already turned inward. She tried to speak; Astrid’s braid tightened across her throat like a favour. The sigh became a hum, became a name neither had ever taught the other.

7  
They stood on the road somehow, gravel sharp under socks. Astrid’s watch glowed 01:17, its minute hand twitching like a trapped insect. One bell, she said, confused. Linnea’s thighs were wet—cold, sticky. She smelled smoke in her own hair. They walked home in silence, shadows now short and obedient. Mum found her at dawn, standing by the washing machine in yesterday’s sweater. “Why do the sheets stink of bonfire?” Linnea opened her fist; the match heads were gone, only white dust remained. Somewhere inside, beneath the unopened geography of morning, something knocked twice, waiting for its name to be braided in.
