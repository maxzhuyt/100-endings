The pocket metronome ticks at 108 BPM—sterile, glassy—while Den Den Town roars like an overclocked game cabinet. I tweak the filter on my secret synth patch, the one I call “river-horse,” and keep my eyes on the cracked pavement; people are bosses I can skip. Headphones, big as melon halves, clamp the world to a safe hush.

Then a guitar slices straight through the foam—bright, sixteenth-note pride. It’s the opening riff of *Ahiru no Sora*, episode three, composer Yuki Hayashi. My fingers freeze mid-air-keytar. Nobody ever busks anime here.

Across the lane she’s cross-legged on a scarf, Strat across her lap, pick sparkling like a dropped arcade token. Shoppers flow around her, but she’s looking at the one unmoving sprite—me. Shoulders folding like a closed laptop, I bow halfway; the headphones slide forward, snag my bangs, thump my collarbone.

She grins, speaks through the intro of the second verse. “You clocked that, huh? Come here often, synth-otaku?”

I manage, “Yeah… four-bar loop, key change at bar nine.” The words leak before I can trap them.

She laughs. “Prove it—what show’s this?” A new riff: *My Hero*, season two.

“Episode twenty-three, composer Hayashi again, 140 BPM.” My voice cracks on the zero, but a passing guy in a Deku hoodie shoots us a thumbs-up, and the sky doesn’t fall.

She pats the tatami mat beside her. I step; the cable snags my sneaker. Headphones hit concrete—clang—and Osaka rushes in: pachinko jingle, takoyaki sizzle, cicada drill. She picks them up, offers them grip-first. I hug them to my chest instead, a plastic shield with no battery left.

“Collaborative experiment,” she says, winding a low E. “I need a synth pad under that river-horse thing you were tapping. Let’s talk over tuna—only if you’re done busking.”

I glance at my phone: 17:02. Cram-school starts at 18:30. “I’m done,” I say, surprised the sentence leaves the dock.

We walk one block to the sushi chain in silence. The traffic light beeps in triplet cicada time; I notice the headphone jack dangles unplugged, silver tongue flashing. Before the automatic door, I slide the set into my pocket—consent.

Inside, plates orbit like lazy satellites. The same item—bright-orange salmon with a radish ribbon—passes three times before we sit. She takes the inner seat, leaves me the aisle, retreat corridor clear.

“So,” she starts, “name?”

“Hina,” I say, then sip iced water.

She unspools: “Mika. Dropped out of Senri to busk full-time. Dream: compose an original anime score, something about runaway satellites.”

I nod, swallow, ask the smallest possible question. “Why here?”

“Osaka’s rhythm section never sleeps.” She drums chopsticks on soy dish. “Your turn—why machines over people?”

Words bunch like stuck MIDI notes, then burst. “I’m… better at machines than people. They loop when you ask.” I stare at the wasabi blob, pulse in my ears louder than any drummer.

The salmon plate glides past for the fourth time. Mika snags it, sets it before me. “Prize for admitting that.”

I bite; wasabi detonates. Air punches out of my nose—my laugh, raw as unmastered track. The restaurant doesn’t collapse; it sounds like someone else’s applause sample.

Cram-school clock looms. We sync calendars: she plays weekends outside Joshin; I’m free after 19:00 except Wednesdays. She opens LINE, types, hands back the phone. My thumb hovers over “Add Friend.” I press it—small click, big door.

Outside, neon signs already layer like overdubs. I slip the headphones on, but the cable swings loose, kissing my thigh—open circuit. I leave it dangling.

Mika straps on her Strat, shouts over the arcade din, “Next week, bring a patch that makes that river-horse sound!”

I grin, metronome silenced, and melt into the mix.
