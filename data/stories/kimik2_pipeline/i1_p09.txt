Just another late-night lab, I tell myself, hip-checking the art-building side door until the lock snicks free. The box of Grade-4 RC paper tucked under my chin makes a papery breastplate; the security guard, Ron, sweeps his flashlight beam over me like I’m a midnight exhibit.

“Forgot your key again, Dani?”

“Keys are for people who don’t look this charming carrying contraband,” I answer, batting my lashes hard enough to generate wind. He laughs, waves me through. Flirting is muscle memory, easier than saying thanks.

Inside the hallway smells of acrylic dust and stale pizza. I own every corridor I walk, but the darkroom corridor still swallows sound. When I push through its light-trap blackout, the red safelight clicks alive, a darkroom dawn. Color drains instantly; we’re all just silhouettes with pulses. Developer trays wait on the bench like little baptismal fonts, and the wall clock’s tick syncs with my heart. Elliot’s denim jacket hangs from the hook, sleeves still holding the shape of his arms. He’s here already, half a ghost.

He emerges from behind the drying cabinet, hair messy from static electricity. “Paper came?”

“Fort sheets of future masterpieces.” I toss the unopened box onto the counter, casual as a murder weapon.

We pin contact sheets to the line, clothes-pinning glossy rectangles like prayer flags. Sharpie squeaks across the margins: +5 sec, grade 3, burn bottom left. Professor Rao’s commandments echo in flat administrator monotone: ten finished prints, split-grade, no digital sleight-of-hand. The rules have always been my accomplice; I use them to linger, brushing Elliot’s knuckles when I pass the tongs. Tonight the rules handcuff us together until 3 a.m., no chaperone but the red bulb.

Elliot lifts one of his negatives to the lamp, frowning. “Mind if I re-shoot this self-portrait?”

I peer: he’s shirtless, shoulders folded forward as if apologizing for occupying the frame. “You look like you’re negotiating with your own body,” I tease.

He almost whispers. “That’s… kind of the problem.”

The safeelight hums. I default to flattery—reflex, like breathing. “You photograph great, promise.” His mouth tightens; my compliment has pinned him back inside the outline he was trying to crawl out of.

We enlarge the problematic negative anyway. I focus the enlarger—click, click—until the grain snaps into ridges of moonlit skin. Under the blur of contrast, binder lines appear, faint scars like chalk marks on a road. The picture chooses me, demands witness; attraction tilts, becomes something I can’t pocket or kiss better.

Paper stacked, Elliot hesitates, fingers drumming the timer. Acrid stop-bath stings the air. “Dani,” he says, voice thin, “I don’t know if I want to be the guy in these pictures.”

My swallow echoes. Words feel dangerous, so I move my body instead. I step sideways, lift my hands from the developer tray, offering him the tongs. He can push the paper, decide the plunge. Agency smells like potassium metabisulfite.

He’s still deciding when my elbow nudges the paper safe. The overhead fluorescents blink alive—two seconds of surgical white—before I slap them off. Gasps overlap. The whole stack of RC paper sits quietly fogged, future prints veiled gray before birth. Our careful masks—his good-student obedience, my take-charge swagger—overexposed, useless.

We collapse on the chemical-slick floor, laughter hiccupping into something wetter. Knees touch. Ruin tastes metallic but mutual; we’re suddenly same-height, same-loss.

Elliot’s whisper surfaces first. “Would you still… whatever-this-is… if I wasn’t a guy?”

Stones pelt the inside of my ribs: fear of losing the butch-boy script that turns me on, fear of fumbling his vulnerability, fear of my own unlabeled wants. I breathe through the bruise. No manifesto arrives. Instead I lift his hand, lay it over my sprinting heart, then place my palm against the soft cotton covering his chest—waiting for the beat that will let me stay.

The timer dings, automatic ventilation exhales. Dawn slips under the door, ordinary color flooding our red church. I start to speak, to promise, to name. “We’ll buy new paper tomorrow and—”

The sentence hangs, undeveloped, while we stand in sudden white light holding a truth that hasn’t dried.
