The flitter’s door dilated and Envoy Kail’s boots met stone that had waited three centuries for Terran weight again. Gravity clutched her ankles like a half-forgotten grief, heavier than ship-board, heavier than memory. Light the color of Earth’s Mediterranean at noon—too yellow—glanced off walls built to human scale but pressed lower, lintels bowed as if the stone had grown tired. The air tasted first of salt, then of something metallic, like blood breathed out through kelp.

She walked. The market spiral uncoiled beneath her, webbed fingers flashing silver as they weighed saffron and dried sea-moss in perfect silence. A child with goat-slit pupils traded an ammonite whistle for a twist of glass; elders breathed behind translucent ribs, each inhale a slow tide. No one stared. Difference had become domestic, accumulated like barnacles on a hull still recognizably human.

At the spiral’s navel she stopped. Basalt blocks carried the Labyrinth of Knossos, but the Minotaur had been planed away, only a bull-shaped absence left. Around that hollow, tiny human figures—Pelagians, Terrans, impossible to tell—pointed outward, away from the center. Kail’s fingers hovered over the stone; she felt the myth inhale and exhale at once, story revised by erosion and intention.

“Envoy. Unscheduled walk. Report.”

Commander Sol’s voice crackled in her collarbone, flattened to a bandwidth that could not carry salt or grief. Kail thumbed the channel shut without reply.

They summoned her at dusk. Three anterooms, each lower, narrower, colder. The final chairs were carved from single slabs; their high backs forced her chin up until she met Minister Arden’s gaze—tall, shoulders curved like a question mark, a single trilobite fossil pinned where a collar should be. Between them, a meter of stone air.

Arden spoke first. “What did you smell in the market?”

“Cumin and kelp,” Kail answered, before caution could edit her.

“You list cargo,” Arden said. “What did the air tell you?”

The silence that followed smelled of wet iron and distant storms. She had no vocabulary for that, not yet.

Arden flicked a slate awake: spirals of drift, Terran baseline thinning to Pelagian divergence. Then—casual, almost loving—she flipped the display. Kail saw her own chromosomes lit like city grids, a skin mote magnified into prophecy. Surveillance, sequenced and polite.

“We archive stories you haven’t written,” Arden murmured. “Omit the allele counts from your report and you may read them. Truth traded for truth.”

Kail felt the fossil trilobite watching, calcified witness. She inhaled the room’s cold, exhaled her answer in Arden’s borrowed cadence.

“A report is a breath; once exhaled, it belongs to the air.”

Arden’s smile was thin as shale. “Then let the wind decide.”

Night. Kail stood on the compound balcony, lungs full of unfiled data. Below, children chalked a maze directly onto the plaza stones—lines doubling back, forking, but never meeting at any center. They laughed, scattering dust like constellations. Somewhere inside, her recorder blinked red, hungry for closure. She left it blinking, the silence outside still uncatalogued, the maze below widening without end.
