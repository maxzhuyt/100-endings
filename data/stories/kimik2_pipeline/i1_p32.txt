The iron door groaned open on hinges that had not known oil in decades, revealing a staircase that spiraled downward into darkness so complete it seemed to swallow their lantern-light whole. Aya's gloved hand tightened around the copper handle as she descended first, her boots echoing against stone worn smooth by centuries of illicit traffic. Behind her, she heard Katra's breathing—quick, excited—and Simeon's heavier tread, each step measured with the patience of a man who'd spent too many years in service to gentlemen who preferred their crimes conducted in whispers.

"Seventeen steps," Aya murmured, counting by feel as much as sight. "Mother's notes mentioned—"

"Your mother's notes won't save us if this goes sideways," Simeon muttered, his voice carrying the particular strain of a former soldier navigating unfamiliar territory. "Should have brought proper explosives. Men trust what they can see burn."

"Men trust what they can see burn," Aya echoed, "and women trust what men cannot see at all. Now quiet—listen."

The silence below was unnatural. Guild headquarters should have hummed with activity even at this hour—fences cataloging goods, cutters sharpening blades, the perpetual chess game of information brokers trading secrets like currency. Instead, the underhold stretched before them in sepulchral quiet, its vaulted ceilings disappearing into shadows that their single lantern refused to penetrate.

Aya's skin prickled with the familiar sensation that preceded her magicka's awakening—not the comfortable warmth of natural fire or the mechanical certainty of clockwork, but the cold, slick feeling of something *other* pressing against the veil of reality. The sensation her mother had described as "reaching through silk to grasp a razor."

"Something's wrong," Katra whispered, her hand moving instinctively toward the weighted sap at her belt. "This place feels—"

"Dead," Simeon finished, his military instincts clearly warring with his professional thief's training. "Or waiting to be. Like a trap with the cheese already sprung."

They emerged into the main vault: a cathedral of crime, its walls lined with iron cages that once held goods worth more than entire neighborhoods. The central dais stood empty save for a single chair—a throne, really, carved from what appeared to be whale-bone and inlaid with brass fittings that caught their lantern-light like cat's eyes.

"Looking for this, were we?"

The voice emerged from the shadows between the cages, cultured and amused and utterly devoid of mercy. Madame Vex stepped into their light's circumference as if she'd been painted there by some artist with a taste for theatrical menace. The Guild's supposed leader wore her arrest like a poor joke, her scarlet gown unmarked by constable's hands, her silver hair arranged with the casual elegance of a woman who'd spent her evening at the opera rather than in a cell.

"I must confess disappointment," Vex continued, circling them with the predatory grace of a woman who'd built an empire on other people's necessities. "When the rumors reached me of a conspiracy within my own house, I'd expected something more... sophisticated than three children playing at revolution."

The shadows resolved themselves into movement—six guards, maybe seven, emerging from behind iron pillars with the mechanical precision of men whose loyalty had been purchased at rates that ensured no second thoughts. Their rifles gleamed with the particular brass-and-oil sheen that men found comforting, each barrel trained with the casual competence of professionals who'd never needed to wonder about the morality of their employment.

"Though I suppose I should congratulate you on making it this far," Vex mused, settling into her throne with the air of a woman claiming her rightful place. "Most who attempt to claim my position don't manage to find the door, much less stumble through it. Tell me, girl—what exactly did you imagine would happen when you reached my vault?"

Aya felt her mask suddenly heavy, suddenly foolish—a child's costume in a woman's game. When the guard's hand reached for her face, she didn't resist as the porcelain came away, revealing features that would have been aristocratic if they weren't currently arranged in an expression of such perfect terror.

"Well, well." Vex's smile sharpened. "Lady Ayamaria Corvina, if I'm not mistaken. The late Duchess Corvina's daughter, unless family resemblance lies more heavily than blood these days. How perfectly... poetic."

"Madame," Aya began, her voice steady despite the way her heart hammered against her ribs like a bird testing cage-bars. "I can explain—"

"Can you? Can you explain why the daughter of a woman hanged for witchcraft has been skulking through my halls like a rat with delusions of grandeur? Can you explain what possible value you imagine your titled name might carry in a place where titles are sold by the inch and purchased with blood?"

The foghorn sounded—three long, mournful notes that seemed to echo from another world entirely. Aya felt the question forming in her throat, bitter as medicine: *What happens to those who walk away from Omelas?* But this wasn't Omelas, and there was no walking away from Madame Vex.

"Information," Aya said instead, her mother's voice echoing in her memory—*when they see what you are, make them see what you can do for them*. "I know things. About the constabulary's movements, about which of your people have been whispering to which captains, about the particular weakness in your accounting that suggests someone's been skimming from the protection money in the Drowned Quarter."

Vex's expression didn't change, but something shifted in the quality of her attention. "Continue."

"And I know about the Scythan artifact," Aya added, tasting the words like poison she was choosing to drink. "The one that sits in your private vault, whispering to itself in languages that make men mad. I know what it wants, and I know why it's been... waiting."

The silence stretched between them like a held breath. Outside, the foghorn sounded twice more—a ship finding harbor, or perhaps losing it.

"How fascinating," Madame Vex murmured. "And what exactly does the daughter of a hanged witch imagine such knowledge might purchase?"

Aya met her gaze across the darkness, thinking of her mother's final words on the gallows—*they fear what they cannot understand, my love, and understanding is the only currency that matters*.

"Time," she said simply. "And conversation. The kind that might convince you that some tools are sharp enough to cut in both directions."
