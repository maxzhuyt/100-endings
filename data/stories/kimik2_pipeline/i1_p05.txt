# Caribou Season at the Lodge

JT's boots crunched across the gravel like they had every November since he was twelve, but the sound came back wrong—too sharp, too loud, like the driveway had grown teeth. The lodge looked exactly the same, weathered logs and green tin roof, but the air felt thicker, pressing against his skin with the weight of wet wool. He stopped at the threshold, fingers tightening around his rifle case.

"You coming in or just gonna stand there looking pretty?" His brother Marcus called from inside, voice muffled through walls that had never muffled anything before.

JT stepped across the threshold. The familiar scent of woodsmoke and gun oil hit him, but underneath lurked something else—something organic and watchful that made his shoulders crawl toward his ears.

"Dad's already planning the morning route," Marcus continued, unpacking ammunition with practiced efficiency. "Same as last year—head up past the old ridge trail, work our way down toward the clearing."

Their father grunted acknowledgment from his chair, cleaning a rifle that looked exactly like JT's memory of it, but when the old man looked up, his eyes seemed darker. Deeper. Like someone had drilled holes straight through to the forest floor.

That night, JT lay in his childhood bunk listening to the woods breathe. The lodge had always been quiet—respectfully, deliberately quiet, the way hunters kept things—but tonight the silence felt different. Pressed in against the windows. Leaked through the cracks around the door. He dreamed of caribou tracks that circled back on themselves, each hoofprint deeper than the last, until they became holes that went down forever.

Morning came gray and still. They walked the ridge in single file, their footsteps muffled now by fresh snow that hadn't been there the night before. JT's father moved with the fluid certainty of a ritual, following game trails that seemed to appear beneath his boots. Marcus followed close behind, rifle ready but somehow forgotten in his hands.

Then they found the caribou.

It stood in a small clearing, maybe fifty yards out—a bull, antlers spreading wide enough to catch the weak morning light. But it didn't flee. Didn't even startle. The animal simply watched them approach with dark eyes that held something JT couldn't name, something that made his finger hesitate on the safety of his rifle.

"What are you waiting for?" His father's voice carried the impatience of twenty seasons, but when JT glanced over, the old man wasn't looking at the caribou. He was staring at Marcus, who had taken three steps forward and stopped, rifle hanging useless at his side.

"Marcus?" JT whispered, but his brother didn't respond. Marcus's eyes had gone glassy, reflective as pond ice, and his free hand reached toward the caribou—not in threat, but in supplication. Trembling. Needing.

The caribou remained perfectly still, accepting the gesture with quiet dignity. JT watched his brother's fingers stretch across empty air and understood, with the certainty of nightmare, that the animal was waiting to die. Had been waiting. Would continue waiting until they completed whatever ritual they'd unconsciously begun.

JT raised his rifle, sighted in on the magnificent chest, and found his hands shaking too hard to hold steady. Not with excitement or buck fever, but with the visceral certainty that pulling this trigger would cost him something he couldn't name. The crosshairs danced across fur that seemed to absorb light, growing darker, deeper, more infinite with each passing second.

His father's hand settled on his shoulder—a weight that felt final as a headstone. "Not this one," the old man said quietly. "Not yet."

They backed out of the clearing slowly, leaving the caribou standing there like a monument to their failure. JT watched it watch them until trees blocked the view, and even after, he felt those dark eyes following their retreat. Marcus moved like a sleepwalker, stumbling over roots that shouldn't have been there, and when JT caught his arm to steady him, his brother's skin felt cold as river stone.

"Did you see—" JT started, but the words died in his throat. His father's face had gone hard as winter bark, and Marcus remained lost in whatever trance had claimed him. They walked back to the lodge in silence that felt more like ceremony than failure.

Lunch passed without conversation. Their father cooked venison chili from last year's freezer stores, moving through the kitchen with mechanical efficiency. Marcus sat staring out windows that reflected his face back distorted, stretched wide by the warped glass. JT picked at food that tasted of ashes and watched them both with growing dread.

"You remember the old LaChance place?" His father asked suddenly, not looking up from his bowl.

JT's spoon stopped halfway to his mouth. The LaChance cabin sat three miles north, abandoned since before his birth. Local kids told stories about it—hunters who'd gone missing, strange lights, animal behavior that broke every rule of wilderness logic. Everyone knew someone whose cousin's friend had seen something there, but nobody ever admitted to going themselves.

"Place is cursed," Marcus mumbled, some spark of awareness returning to his eyes. "Everybody knows that."

"Nothing cursed about it." Their father's voice carried the weight of confession. "Just hunters who couldn't pay the price."

JT felt the lodge's walls lean closer, heard the familiar creak of settling wood transform into something that sounded almost like language. "What price?"

But their father had already stood, carrying his bowl to the sink with finality that ended conversation. Through the window, afternoon light slanted across snow that bore tracks leading away from their lodge—tracks that hadn't been there an hour ago. Caribou tracks. They circled the building once, twice, three times before heading north.

Toward the LaChance place.

That afternoon, JT found himself walking those tracks alone. His feet followed them without conscious choice, each print perfect and deliberate as signposts. The woods had grown strange around him—trees leaning in at angles that defied their root systems, branches interlocking overhead until even weak daylight struggled through. But the caribou trail remained clear, pressed deep into snow that grew deeper with each mile.

He found others along the way. Old tracks crossing the fresh ones, patterns that spoke of animals walking impossible routes—circles within circles, spirals that tightened toward centers where snow remained undisturbed. Some prints looked fresh as morning. Others bore the softened edges of days, weeks, seasons. All heading generally north.

The LaChance cabin squatted in a clearing that felt older than the forest around it. Logs had gone silver-gray with age, roof collapsed inward like a mouth frozen mid-scream. Windows stared out black and empty, but JT felt watched as he approached. The caribou tracks he'd been following circled the building once, then led directly to the front door—which stood open, revealing darkness that seemed to swallow afternoon light.

"You're early."

JT spun, rifle rising automatically, but the speaker stood unarmed. A man in hunting gear that looked decades out of date, beard gone white as snow, eyes reflecting darkness that seemed to come from inside his skull. He held up empty hands—gesture of peace or surrender, impossible to read.

"LaChance?" JT asked, though he already knew the answer.

"They all come here eventually. The ones who see things clear." The old man gestured toward the cabin's interior. "Got a whole community of us now. Folks who couldn't pay the price, couldn't make the choice. We live between, watching the woods take what it's owed."

"What's it owed?"

LaChance smiled with terrible kindness. "Same thing it always takes. Pieces of what you think you are. Your daddy knows—knew the first time he looked down his sights and saw eyes looking back that understood him better than he understood himself."

Through the cabin's empty doorway, JT glimpsed movement. Other figures in outdated gear, faces turned toward him with expressions caught between welcome and warning. They moved wrong—too fluid or too jerky, like puppets operated by hands learning the rules of human motion.

"The caribou choose," LaChance continued. "Always have. They show themselves to hunters who carry the right weight—family history, tradition, the need to belong somewhere that doesn't want you whole. Your brother's got it bad. Your daddy worse. But you..." He studied JT with eyes like winter midnight. "You might walk away."

"Walk away from what?"

But answers died unspoken as sound carried through clearing—not the report of rifle fire, but something older. Hooves against frozen earth, too many to count, moving in patterns that made his bones ache with recognition. JT turned to see caribou emerging from tree line—not the solitary bull from morning, but dozens. Hundreds. They moved as one body, antlers weaving complex symbols against gray sky, and every set of eyes fixed on him specifically.

"Choice time," LaChance said softly. "Participate or disappear. Same bargain your daddy made, his daddy before. The woods remember every debt, and collection day always comes due."

The herd parted like curtains, revealing his father and brother walking among them. They moved easily, naturally, rifles forgotten as caribou pressed close enough to touch. Marcus looked up—his eyes held the same dark infinity JT had seen in the bull that morning, but now he understood it as recognition rather than loss. His brother had been chosen to see, same as him. Same as their father had been chosen, decades ago.

His father met his gaze across the clearing. No words necessary—the old man's expression held apology and explanation and terrible love, all the things that had gone unspoken through years of hunting trips and silences and traditions that passed father to son like genetic disease. He reached out slowly, hand extended not in greeting but in invitation. Around him, caribou pressed closer, waiting with the patience of stone.

JT felt the choice settling into his bones like winter cold. Not between hunting and not-hunting, but between different kinds of ending. Join the family tradition—whatever it truly meant, whatever it truly cost—or join the abandoned ones in their cabin liminal, watching from windows that reflected only what they'd lost.

The herd stood perfectly still, offering him all the time in the world to decide. Their eyes held depths that promised understanding, if only he would step forward and accept the weight of knowing. Behind him, LaChance and the others waited with equal patience—exiles from a system that allowed no neutral ground, no third option where tradition continued without cost.

Snow began falling again, thick flakes that blurred the distance between hunters and hunted, family and exile, choice and consequence. JT stood at the center of circles within circles, feeling the woods lean close to hear his answer, and understood that whatever he chose next would define him forever—but only because every choice made since the first human raised rifle against caribou had led inevitably to this moment, this clearing, this impossible decision that had never been a choice at all.

The silence stretched infinite as winter itself, waiting for him to speak the ending he'd always been walking toward.
