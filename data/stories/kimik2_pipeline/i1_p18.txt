The boulder reached the crest, sighed, and trundled back down as politely as a sheep that has mistaken a bedroom for a meadow.  
Sisyphus gave it a friendly wave. “Until tomorrow, you old sedimentary sofa.”  
The rock declined to answer, being a rock, but it did wobble in a way that suggested embarrassment at the attention.  

He brushed stone-dust from his toga, sat on the arrested lump, and produced a wax tablet from behind a thyme bush that had learned to grow sideways out of sheer hillside cussedness.  

“Ready, Crow?”  

A black comma detached itself from the sky, landed on the boulder, and became a bird wearing the expression of someone who has opened a tax demand instead of a love letter.  

“Satchel’s in the cave,” the crow muttered. “I’m not a beast of burden, I’m a conceptual omen.”  

“Concept this,” Sisy said kindly, and flipped the tablet open. “Take One: ‘Dear Rock-Wearied, Hill-Haunted, or Otherwise Geologically Inconvenienced.’ No, too long. Let’s begin with something sprightly.”  

The crow flap-dragged a leather bag across the dust. Sisyphus slit the thong with the same bronze knife he used for lunchtime cheese, releasing a smell of parchment, panic, and cheap incense.  

Letter the First:  
“Dear Sisyphus,  
My mother-in-law keeps reincarnating as my household utensils. Last week she was the soup ladle; tonight she’s the door key. How do I tell her to move out without hurting her feelings?  
Yours, Spooked in Sparta.”  

Sisyphus tapped the stylus against his teeth. “Advise substitution. Suggest mother-in-law be reincarnated as neighbour’s umbrella. Everyone stays dry, nobody gets stabbed with guilt. Next!”  

Letter the Second smelled of seaweed.  
“Great Sisy,  
I’m a minor sea-god with major envy. How can I be taken seriously when dolphins keep photobombing my storms?  
Respectfully, Tritoncé.”  

“Tell him,” Sisy dictated while massaging a calf muscle that had been in continuous employment since the Bronze Age, “to schedule tempest during dolphin siesta hour. Also, swap trident for something pointier—perhaps a motivational speech.”  

Crow scribbled, muttering, “Try not being immortal, mate,” which was not part of the official reply but certainly part of the official attitude.  

Letter the Third arrived with wedding confetti still stuck to the seal.  
“Oh Sage of the Eternal Hill,  
My marriage has lasted three centuries and the spark is dimmer than Hades’ night-light. Spice tips urgently required.  
Perplexxed in Thessaly.”  

Sisyphus leaned against the boulder, which cooled his back like a conscientious domesticated glacier.  

“Advice:  
1. Rotate the crockery. Nothing rekindles romance like discovering you own more than one plate.  
2. Schedule boredom. Put ‘Stare at wall’ in the calendar; you’ll rebel together and end up dancing.  
3. Tell the truth, but in accents. ‘I hate your sandals’ sounds adorable in Cretan.  
4. Once a century, walk to the top of something you can’t conquer. Watch the view refuse to change. Hold hands. Remember you’re both smaller than geography and larger than routine.  
Love, S.”  

Crow paused. “You’ve never been married.”  

“I’ve been dating this rock for eternity,” Sisyphus replied. “Same difference, fewer arguments about sandals.”  

A rumble like distant indigestion announced that the boulder had reached the bottom and was now politely requesting its owner.  

“Intermission,” Sisyphus announced, pocketing the stylus. He stood, stretched, and trotted downhill.  

Dictation continued off-stage. Crow, left alone, practiced autographs on abandoned clouds. The scrolls fluttered like over-educated butterflies.  

From halfway up the slope Sisyphus’s voice floated: “—regarding your haunted chariot, try sprinkling pomegranate seeds under the wheels; ghosts have union rules about squashing fruit—”  

He reappeared at the summit, cheeks pink, boulder in tow. The rock sat, grateful for the breather. Sisyphus produced a cloth, laid out bread, olives, and the cheese rind he was due to finish in 247 years.  

“Looking particularly gneiss today,” he told the stone.  

The boulder blushed, shedding a few polite grains.  

Crow flapped up, scrolls now neatly ribboned. “Post’s ready. You’ve got three marriage queries, one minor Titan complaining about joint pain, and a satyr who wants to know if rocks make good pets.”  

“Tell the satyr only if they fetch.”  

Crow launched, immediately lost three feet of altitude under the paperwork, cursed the aerodynamic defects of wax seals, and spirled downward toward mortal chaos like a black paper dart.  

Sisyphus watched until the bird was a punctuation mark in someone else’s sentence. Then he nudged the boulder. “Shall we?”  

The rock obliged by rolling. Sisyphus strolled after it, already rehearsing tomorrow’s opening: “Dear Descendant of whichever ancestor annoyed the gods this week—”  

In six minutes he would be back at the crest; in six hundred years he would still be back; the advice would still be good.  

He pocketed the cheese rind, smiled at the reliable hill, and started down to meet the next ascent, the next letter, the next laugh waiting inside the labour.
