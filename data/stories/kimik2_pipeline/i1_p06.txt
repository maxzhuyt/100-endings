I tell myself I am not waiting for anyone, yet the sea wind keeps rearranging my skirt so the slit shows. The silk stockings—mended twice, the left heel a spider’s web of thread—shiver against the salt. “His locks,” the words tick in my skull like a cracked record, though I’ve never read the line aloud. I walk the 1937 boardwalk at dusk, past the nickelodeon wheezing “Tiger Rag,” past the bathhouses already locked, their padlocks bruised with salt. I pretend I’m only passing through, but I’ve painted my mouth the color of shooting-gallery targets.

Between Madame Luba’s tent and a peanut wagon he appears, coat collar up, hair black enough to drink the light. “You came back to the scene of the crime,” he says. The sodium lamp above us hacks once and dies, leaving only his smile—small, competent, a coin spent after midnight.  
“Crime?” I ask, though my voice sounds like someone else’s.  
“Only the one we haven’t committed yet.”  
He steps aside; I follow, the way a nickel clatters toward the slot.

The shooting-gallery barker dozes. My companion lifts a .22 short as if presenting a corsage. “Five cents for five shots. Aim for the heart—it’s cheapest.”  
I don’t tell him I already know the weight of this wood stock; I simply sight. Tin rabbits jerk across the track. I squeeze—one, two, three, four—ears fly off like tiny moons. The fifth rabbit stutters; I let it live. He claps, slow metronome.  
“Mercy?” he wonders.  
“Bookkeeping,” I answer. The stuck phrase thrums louder: *his locks, his locks.*

Sarsaparilla, two straws, one paper cone. The carousel exhales a minor chord and the night splits open: I am twelve again, summer of ’27, watching Mother’s gentleman caller set bottles on a rail. Gun oil and burnt sugar braid the air; his obsidian curls bounce when the .32 cracks. Mother laughs, her laugh a red scarf waved at a bull. He let me fire once; the recoil kissed my shoulder purple. I never told her the bruise bloomed the shape of a heart.

He tilts the cone toward me, drinking where my lipstick stains. His hair brushes my pulse. “His locks,” I whisper, traitor to myself.  
He answers without looking up, “…are bushy and black as a raven. Song of Songs, chapter five, give or take.”  
Scripture tastes of licorice and gunpowder. The night leans closer.

We drift past the whip cars, the flea circus, the dark mouth of the funhouse. Paper lanterns twitch like eyelids trying to stay open. He speaks without turning. “Every July we reassemble, collect what was pledged. Your mother signed: one daughter, payable when the marksmanship shows. She traded the future for a ticket out of a bad marriage—standard contract, Depression rates.”  
I should laugh, but the air smells of hot iron, exactly like the day the fence bottles exploded. “And you?”  
“Barker, bondsman, night clerk.” He shrugs, tired as a three-shift accountant. “We keep the lights flickering to save on juice.”

I still carry the little rifle, barrel down, like a purse. We halt beneath the gallery’s canvas banner: a crimson heart pierced by five yellow darts. I lift the gun—not at him, not at me—and fire once. The slug chews rope; the heart folds down, a snowflake made of meat. I press my painted mouth to it, transferring proof. “I’ll honor her note,” I say, “but on my schedule. Compound interest payable in kisses or bullets, my choice.”  
He studies the candy-apple smear, nods approval. “Color suits the contract.”  
Behind us the carnival bulbs dim to a sigh. We walk toward the spinning carousel, contract unsealed, heart in my hand still beating paper.
