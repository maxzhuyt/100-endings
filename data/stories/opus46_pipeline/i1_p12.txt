# Rehearsing Rejection

The black shirt or the green one. The black one says I planned this, thought about it, wanted to look like I'd planned it. The green one says I didn't think about it, which says I don't care, which is worse. The black one. No. The green one, sleeves pushed up so it looks unconsidered, the kind of thing a person throws on when they're just meeting a friend and it's just a night and it's just dinner and none of this is the thing my hands think it is, because my hands are shaking, which is stupid.

My phone buzzes on the bathroom counter and I catch it before it can skitter into the sink.

**M.: hey! so excited for tonight. 7 still work? i grabbed us the corner table you like :)**

I read it four times.

*So excited.* Not *I'm so excited* — just *so excited*, which is either casual warmth or the performative kind you use when you're building a cushion of kindness around the thing you're actually going to say. The smiley face. M. doesn't always use smiley faces. When did they start? Was it after the conversation last Thursday, the one where I said I sometimes can't tell if people actually want to see me or are just being polite, and M. got quiet for a second too long? Are the smiley faces new data? Are they compensation?

*I grabbed us the corner table you like.*

That's — that's a kind thing. That's a specifically kind thing. They remembered I like the corner table because it has a wall on one side and I don't have to worry about what's behind me. They remembered that. They requested it. That is a person who thought about me before I arrived.

Unless it's the table you choose when you need to have a conversation you don't want other people to overhear.

I put the phone down. Pick it up. Read it again.

*— Hey. So listen, I wanted to talk to you about something. Don't freak out. It's not bad. Well, it's not — I just think we should be honest with each other, right? And honestly, I've been thinking, and I think maybe we—*

No. Too rehearsed. M. wouldn't lead with *don't freak out*. M. would come in soft, lateral, the way they do when they're working up to something. They'd start with a story about their week. They'd order first. They'd wait until I'd eaten something so I couldn't say I need to leave without it being obvious I was leaving. They'd—

*— I love you so much. You know that, right? You know how much I—*

That one. That's the one. That's the opening line of every exit speech I've ever heard. I love you so much, *but*. You know how much I care about you, *but*. You're one of the most important people in my life, *and that's exactly why* — exactly why I need to do this thing that will make you feel like you're dissolving, because if you were actually one of the most important people in my life, this wouldn't be happening. Important people don't get the speech. Important people get kept.

The phone says 34%.

I should charge it. The cord is right there, coiled on the nightstand like a — it's right there. I'll charge it in a minute. I need to finish getting ready.

The green shirt. I'm wearing the green shirt.

---

I'm doing the thing again.

I know I'm doing the thing. Dr. Lam and I have talked about this. We have a name for it. *Anticipatory scripting.* I wrote it in the notes app four months ago, in the list titled "Things to Remember When My Brain Lies," which is a document I have never once opened during an active spiral because opening it would mean admitting the spiral is happening, and admitting the spiral is happening means the spiral is real, and if the spiral is real then everything I'm feeling is just a symptom, which means the part of me that knows — *knows* — something is wrong between me and M. is just, what, misfiring? A glitch? A disorder in a green shirt going to dinner?

Point taken. Neural pathways. Emotional dysregulation. I know.

But.

Tuesday. Tuesday M. took four hours to respond to a text that took eleven seconds to read. I know it took eleven seconds because I timed it, reading at what I estimated to be M.'s pace, which is slightly slower than mine because M. subvocalizes, I've noticed this — their lips move faintly when they read, which is a thing I should not find as devastating as I do, but that's a different problem. Four hours. And when the reply came it was: **Haha totally. Anyway how's your day going?**

*Haha totally.*

Period instead of exclamation mark. *Anyway* — a pivot word, a word that means *I'm done with the subject you raised and I'm choosing a new one.* How's your day going: a question so generic it could be addressed to a coworker, a dental hygienist, a stranger in a waiting room who you're not sure you've met before. Four hours of silence and then *haha totally* from a person who once left me a three-minute voice memo just to describe a dog they saw on the way to work.

Dr. Lam would say: *What's the evidence? What's the alternative explanation?*

The alternative explanation is that M. was busy. That M. has a job and a life and a mother who calls during lunch and a coworker who talks too close and a Tuesday that didn't revolve around the eleven-second text I sent at 12:47 PM and then stared at my phone until 4:51 PM cataloguing the silence.

That's the reasonable explanation. I can feel it sitting there, reasonable, quiet, waiting to be picked up.

But reasonable feels like a trick. Reasonable feels like the thing you tell yourself so you're appropriately surprised when the blow comes, so the person leaving you gets to feel less guilty because look, you weren't even worried, you didn't even see it coming, which means they hid it well, which means they were *hiding it* —

34%. I should go. I'm going to be late and being late means M. sits alone and worries or, worse, doesn't worry, and either of those is an answer I'm not ready to have.

---

The 6 line is crowded enough that I have to stand, hand around the overhead bar, body swaying against strangers who don't look at me, and this is when it unfolds fully, the way it always does in transit, in motion, when my hands are occupied and my mind is not.

*— I think we need some space.*

M.'s voice. I know exactly how it would sound. That particular downshift M. does when being careful — half a register lower, slower cadence, the way you talk to something you don't want to spook. I've heard it before. Not directed at me, not yet, but I've heard it on M.'s phone when M. didn't know I was listening, talking to their sister about their sister's boyfriend, and the voice was: *I think you deserve to be with someone who doesn't make you feel like that.* Gentle as a hand on a bruise. That voice. M. would use that voice on me.

*— It's not that I don't want you in my life. I do. God, I do. It's just that sometimes I feel like I can't — like no matter what I do, you're always waiting for me to mess up, and I can't keep performing for someone who's keeping score of my failures.*

That's good. That sounds right. That sounds exactly like something M. would say, precise and even-toned and emotionally literate in a way that makes it impossible to argue with because they're not wrong, they're never wrong when they use that voice, and what would I say, I would say—

*— That's not fair.*

No I wouldn't. That's weak, that's the response of someone who's been caught doing the thing they were doing. Let me try again.

*— I understand. I appreciate you being honest with me.*

Dignified. Controlled. Adult. I would say that, and then I would leave, and I would walk home in the cold with the kind of posture that communicates to anyone watching that I am fine, I am handling this fine, I am a person who receives hard news with grace. And I would get home. And I would close the door. And then—

No. No, I wouldn't be dignified. I know myself. I know exactly what I'd do. The dignified version would last fifteen seconds. Maybe thirty, if the restaurant was loud enough to cover the shaking. And then:

*— So all of it was, what? What was last week? When you held my face and said—*

*— Don't do that. Don't weaponize—*

*— I'm not weaponizing anything, I'm asking you a direct question, you held my face in both hands on your kitchen floor and you told me you'd never felt—*

*— I know what I said. I meant it when I said it. That doesn't mean—*

*— So it expired? Feelings expire now? You felt it on Thursday and by Tuesday you're taking four hours to text me back—*

And there it is. I'd bring up Tuesday. I'd show my hand. M. would see the full architecture of my surveillance, every catalogued delay and decoded punctuation mark, and M.'s face would do the thing — not anger, worse than anger — *recognition*. The look of someone who has identified something clinical. And M. would say, gently, in the voice:

*— I think you should talk to Dr. Lam about this.*

And I would rather die.

The train shudders to a stop. Not my stop. Two more. I check my phone.

27%.

I could text M. right now. Something light. *On my way! Can't wait.* Exclamation mark. No, two exclamation marks signals anxiety. One exclamation mark. Or a period? No, a period from me would be hostile, M. knows my texting patterns, M. would notice a period where an exclamation mark should be and then M. would be the one in the spiral and I don't want — I don't want M. in the spiral. The spiral is mine. I've furnished it. I know where everything is.

I don't text.

Last week. On M.'s kitchen floor, because the couch was covered in laundry and we'd slid off it laughing and didn't bother getting up. M.'s hands on either side of my face, thumbs at my temples, and M. said — and this is exact, I remember exact, I record these things in a part of my brain that has no respect for context — M. said: *I'm so glad you exist. You know that, right? My life is so much better because you're in it.*

Guilt. That was guilt. M. already knew then. They were already building the goodbye and that was the advance apology, the kindness you pre-load so that later, when the blade drops, you can say *but I told you how much you meant to me, I made sure you knew.*

Or: M. had just come home from a long day and I was on their floor and they were happy to see me and they said a true thing.

Or: both. Both simultaneously. Which is worse, because how do I build a defense against something that's both true and a weapon?

The draft texts begin writing themselves in a place behind my eyes where I can't turn the screen off.

*I trusted you. That's the thing you don't understand. I trusted you and I don't do that. I don't DO that. And you knew, you knew what it cost me, and you held my face and you—*

Too raw. Delete.

*I hope you find someone who doesn't exhaust you. I mean that. I genuinely hope you find someone easy and uncomplicated and light and I hope you're very happy and I hope it only occurs to you occasionally, maybe at 3 AM on a Tuesday when the person next to you is sleeping and you're looking at the ceiling, that I would have burned the world down for you and you chose the comfortable thing instead.*

Better. Cruel enough to leave a mark but literary enough to deny the cruelty. I could send that. After the dinner, after the speech, I could wait exactly two hours — long enough to suggest I wasn't waiting by my phone, short enough that the silence itself communicates — and then I could—

22%.

There is a charging station on this train car. A woman beside me has her phone plugged into it, cord taut, her grip on the device like she's holding an IV line. There's a free port next to hers. I have the cable in my bag. I know I have the cable in my bag.

I don't reach for it.

Something in the not-reaching feels like armor. If the phone dies, I can't send the cruel text. If the phone dies, I can't check if M. texts to ask where I am. If the phone dies, I am unreachable, and being unreachable means being unable to be reached for, which means being unable to be dropped.

You can't hang up on someone who's already off the grid.

Two more stops.

---

M. is already there.

I see them through the window first — corner table, like they said, wall on one side. They're reading something on their phone, and they're doing the thing with their lips, the faint subvocalizing, and they haven't seen me yet, and for three, maybe four seconds, I watch them exist without me in the room and they are not steeling themselves. They are not rehearsing the speech. They are reading something, maybe a menu, maybe an article about peninsular architecture or the migration patterns of arctic terns, because M. reads things like that, M. is the kind of person who has seventeen tabs open about subjects that will never make them money, and their posture is easy, and their face is open, and they look like a person who is waiting for someone they're happy to see.

The door makes a sound when I open it. M. looks up.

And the face that breaks across them — I don't have a rehearsal for this. The face is fast and unguarded, a crack in the composure I'd built for them, because the M. on the bus was measured, careful, operating from a script, and this M., the real one, the one standing up now, is — their whole body moves. Not just standing. *Rising toward me.*

"Hi! Oh my god, come here."

M. hugs me. Their arms lock behind my back and they pull me in and hold, hold, hold — a beat longer than the rhythm I'd predicted, two beats, three, and I feel the specific heat of their jaw against my temple and the specific pressure of their hand on my spine, one vertebra above where a casual hug lands, and I can smell the soap they use, the cedar one, and the detergent their mother buys them in bulk because M. has never once purchased their own detergent, and underneath that something unnamed, something warm and hourly and *theirs*, and M. says, into my hair:

"I missed you. It's been like five days. That's too many days."

The rehearsals dissolve.

All of them — the speech, the dignified response, the cruel text, the Tuesday analysis, the four hours, the period instead of exclamation mark — all of it falls out of me like I've been tilted and emptied. What's left is only this: M.'s hand on my spine. The corner table with the wall. The amber overhead light making everything golden and soft-edged. The clink of someone else's glass across the room. M. pulling back to look at my face, both hands on my shoulders, scanning, the way they do, like they're checking to see if all the parts of me made it here intact.

"You look good," M. says. "I like the green."

The green. They like the green. I smile and my face means it. Every part of my face means it.

We sit. M. has already ordered the bread we share, the olive oil kind, and they're telling me about their week — the coworker, the sister, a thing that happened at the grocery store that I can tell they've been saving to tell me because they're leaning forward and their hands are sculpting the air the way they do when a story matters to them, and I am listening, I am here, I am in a room with a person who saved me a chair and remembered my bread and is telling me about a woman who argued with a cashier about the definition of "ripe" and M.'s eyes are wet from laughing at their own story and I think, clearly, without rehearsal:

*This. Stay in this. This is real and it is yours and you are allowed to have it.*

---

M. glances at their phone.

It's nothing. It's a reflex, the kind everyone does — a half-second check, the screen lights and darkens, M. doesn't even pick it up, their eyes return to me immediately, they're still mid-sentence, they haven't left, nothing has changed.

But the machinery is very fast and it was never fully off.
