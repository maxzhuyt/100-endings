# My Apocalypse Buddy

## I.

The argument, like most arguments between a man and a repurposed animatronic chipmunk, concerned breakfast.

"We're not eating the paste," Midas said.

Chewy's voice box, which had been salvaged from what was once a family-oriented pizza restaurant and had survived the collapse of civilization in considerably better shape than civilization itself, emitted a crackling burst of sound: *"It's your BIRTHDAY and you deserve a —zzkt— SPECIAL treat!"*

"It's not my birthday. It's no one's birthday. Birthdays require a functioning calendar and the will to live, and we're short on both." Midas held the dented can up to the light that fell through the gap in their ceiling — specifically, the gap where the second floor of the Greendale Public Library had decided, eight months ago, that it was tired of being a second floor. "This expired before the war. It expired before the *previous* war. This paste has seniority over most governments."

*"Every day's an ADVENTURE with—"* the voice box crackled, popped, and gave up.

"Exactly."

Midas ate the paste.

Their base, for the generous sort of person who used words like *base* to describe a ground-floor library corner shored up with filing cabinets and prayer, occupied what had been the Reference section. Midas had kept some of the books, initially for reading and subsequently for insulation. The complete works of the Encyclopaedia Britannica, volumes A through K, formed the eastern wall. Volumes L through Z had been traded to a passing merchant for antibiotics and a can of paste that Midas was now deeply regretting.

Chewy sat in his usual spot by the door, which was a barricade of desks, which was a door only in the philosophical sense that you could, with enough shoving, get through it. He was two and a half feet tall and built in the shape of an upright chipmunk, if the chipmunk had been designed by committee, manufactured cheaply, and then dragged through the end of the world by one arm. His left arm, specifically, which now attached to his torso at an angle God had not intended. His fur — synthetic, matted, the color of rust and originally the color of something cheerful — was worn to the plastic shell beneath in patches. His eyes were two black glass hemispheres that caught the light and did nothing with it.

Midas had found him in the ruins of a Chewy Charlie's Fun-Time Pizza Palace, standing upright in a corner, voice box still cycling through phrases on a dwindling battery, performing for an audience of rubble. That had been two years ago. Midas had replaced the battery. He had never fully interrogated why.

"Right," Midas said, wiping paste from his fingers onto pants that had given up on dignity long before him. "Today we go east. Past the interchange, past the medical center — don't look at me like that."

Chewy's glass eyes looked at him like nothing, because they were glass.

*"Safety FIRST! Always hold a grown-up's—"*

"I am a grown-up. I'm the only grown-up. That's the whole problem." Midas pulled on his pack — a hiking bag patched with duct tape and, in one optimistic corner, a square of tarp that he kept meaning to waterproof but that mostly served to funnel rain directly onto his spare socks. He lifted Chewy by the grab-handle he'd bolted onto the chipmunk's back, clipping him to the pack's external frame so that Chewy rode facing backward, surveying where they'd been with the blank contentment of something that had never once needed to worry about where it was going.

The morning was the color of a headache. It was usually the color of a headache. Midas could not remember the last time the sky had been definitively blue rather than the pale brownish-white of a recovering atmosphere trying its best. The sun was up there, probably. It was doing what it could.

## II.

He spotted the graffiti on the underpass wall three blocks east, where the old highway on-ramp curled over the surface road like a concrete question mark. Someone had sprayed it in yellow paint — actual paint, not ash-mixed substitutes — which alone was enough to make Midas stop walking.

The tag was a circle with a cross through it, and below that, in blocky letters:

**LINDEN HILL — STILL WARM — FOLLOW THE WIRE**

Below *that*, someone else had added, in different handwriting:

**BULLSHIT**

And below *that*, a third hand:

**maybe**

Midas stood in front of it for a long time. Long enough that a pigeon — the only species to have emerged from the apocalypse not merely intact but vindicated — landed on the railing above and regarded him with the proprietary disdain of something that had always known this was coming.

"Linden Hill," Midas said. "That's — what, twelve miles? Fifteen?"

*"The fun NEVER stops at Chewy Charlie's!"*

"You're not wrong." He chewed his lip. "'Still warm.' That could mean heated. Running generators. Could mean people. Community. Could mean a lot of things."

It could also mean a trap. It could mean the graffiti was six months old and whatever warmth had existed at Linden Hill was now the same temperature as everything else, which was cold, getting colder, and not interested in discussing it. It could mean someone had sprayed it to lure scavengers into an ambush. This was not a theoretical concern. Midas had a scar on his left shoulder from the last time he'd followed hopeful signage, and he'd gotten off easy because the ambushers had been, in his narrator's generous assessment, not very good at ambushing.

He looked at the three messages. The assertion. The rebuttal. The quiet, devastating *maybe*.

*"Who wants to go on a— on a— on a—"* Chewy's voice box stuck in a loop, the word *on a* repeating with the bright insistence of a machine that did not understand what giving up meant, because it had not been programmed with the concept.

"Adventure," Midas finished. "Yeah. Okay."

He pulled a crumbling road map from his jacket — actual paper, held together with tape at the folds, the highways and back roads of a county that no longer maintained either — and traced a line east with his finger. Past the interchange. Past the medical center that everyone avoided, for reasons involving the medical center and the fact that people had been very sick in it when the power failed and nobody wanted to think too carefully about the math of that. Past the burned-out commercial district where the Walgreens had become, briefly and absurdly, a feudal kingdom before its ruler was deposed by a woman with a crossbow and a legitimate grievance about prescription availability. Past all of that, into the stretch of outer suburbs where the houses thinned and the wilds began — the rewilding zones where nature, given five years and the sudden absence of landscaping services, had done what nature always does when you stop paying attention, which is everything, everywhere, all at once.

Linden Hill was at the edge of the map. He could make it by midafternoon if nothing went wrong.

Midas was, by constitution and repeated experience, not a man who planned for things going wrong. He planned for things going right and then treated the difference as a learning opportunity. This is an admirable quality in a teacher or a Little League coach. In a post-apocalyptic scavenger, it is what professionals would call a *non-optimal survival strategy*, if there were still professionals, or strategies, or anyone keeping score.

He turned east. Chewy, clipped to his back and facing west, watched the graffiti recede with the polite disinterest of a passenger on someone else's ride.

*"Here we GO!"*

## III.

The first four miles were fine. This was suspicious, but Midas was not the sort of person to be suspicious of things going well, a trait that separated him from the people who were still alive by statistical likelihood rather than by what could only be described as an unreasonable winning streak at a rigged casino.

The interchange was empty, the great concrete loops of highway overgrown with the determined vegetation that had spent decades waiting beneath the asphalt for its chance. Trees grew through medians. Grass erupted from expansion joints. A sedan sat in the middle of the westbound lane with a sapling growing through its sunroof, which was the sort of image that inspired poetry in certain people and inspired Midas to check if the trunk had already been looted. It had.

The medical center he gave wide berth, cutting south through a residential street where the houses had that deflated look of structures no longer inhabited — curtains like closed eyes, doors hanging open like mouths mid-sentence, the whole neighborhood wearing the expression of someone who'd stepped out for milk and slowly realized they were never coming back.

It was mile five that went wrong, which, by Midas's personal average, represented an improvement.

The road crossed a creek — or what had been a creek and was now, thanks to the collapse of a drainage system upstream and two seasons of unmanaged rainfall, something more ambitious. The bridge, a modest concrete span that had once supported two lanes of commuter traffic, had developed a philosophical objection to its own middle section. The gap was perhaps twelve feet across. The water below was not deep but it was fast and it was cold, and Midas had learned, through the kind of experience that leaves you shivering for two days, that falling into cold fast water in November with a pack on your back was the sort of mistake you only needed to make once.

"Huh," he said.

*"Uh oh! Looks like SOMEONE made a—zzkt—"*

"Thank you. Very helpful."

He surveyed the options. Upstream, the creek widened. Downstream, there were trees fallen across the water, but they were the kind of trees that looked stable from a distance and revealed themselves as rotted through the moment you put weight on them, which was a metaphor for several things and also just a genuine hazard.

Midas looked at the gap. He looked at his pack. He looked at the exposed rebar jutting from the broken edges of the bridge, bent outward like ribs.

"I can make that," he said, with the confidence of a man who had been wrong about what he could make before and had survived those errors through luck so consistent it was beginning to feel like a character flaw.

He backed up fifteen paces. He tightened the pack straps. He tightened the strap holding Chewy. He did not, because he could not, tighten anything about the situation itself, which was loose in every direction.

He ran.

The jump was, considered objectively, fine. He cleared the gap. His feet hit the far side of the bridge with room to spare. The landing, however, introduced a variable that Midas had not accounted for, which was that the far side of the bridge, while structurally present, had the surface integrity of a stale cracker. His right foot went through the concrete to the ankle. Momentum carried him forward, which freed the foot but sent him into a tumbling roll that ended with him on his back, staring at the pale sky, arms spread, with the sound of something clattering away from him that turned out to be the water bottle from his pack's side pocket, bouncing twice on the concrete before dropping neatly into the creek and sailing away with the current, the cap catching the light as it went like a small departing boat.

He lay there for a moment.

*"Whoopsie-DAISY!"* said Chewy, from somewhere behind and beneath him.

"Yep." Midas sat up. His ankle throbbed. His elbow had acquired a new scrape to add to its collection. The water bottle was gone, which meant their water supply was now whatever remained in the canteen on his belt, which was — he shook it — about a third full, which was a generous way of saying two-thirds empty, which was a pessimistic way of saying *this is going to be a problem in about four hours*.

He unclipped Chewy to inspect him. The chipmunk had taken the impact on his already-compromised left arm, which now dangled not at the previous wrong angle but at a new, more creative wrong angle, connected to the shoulder joint by a single bolt and what appeared to be determination. One of Chewy's glass eyes had a fresh crack running through it, a hairline fracture that split the reflected world in two.

"Sorry, pal."

*"No PROBLEM! That's what friends are—"* A burst of static. *"—for."*

Midas pulled a zip tie from his jacket pocket — he kept zip ties the way previous generations kept breath mints, always on hand, never enough — and secured the dangling arm to Chewy's torso in what he chose to think of as a dignified sling and what was, more accurately, a zip tie holding a plastic limb to a synthetic chipmunk in the middle of a broken bridge over an angry creek in a world that had stopped making sense half a decade ago.

He stood up, tested the ankle, called it functional, and clipped Chewy back into place.

"Nine miles to go," he said.

*"YAY!"*

An uninvolved narrator, surveying this scene from an appropriately cosmic distance, might observe that the remarkable thing about Midas was not his persistence, which was common enough among survivors, nor his optimism, which was rarer but not unheard of, but his apparently genuine inability to interpret events as *warnings*. The bridge had collapsed. His water was gone. His companion was deteriorating. A reasonable person would turn back. But then, a reasonable person would not have set out in the first place, and a truly reasonable person would probably not have survived the end of the world at all, because the end of the world had not been a reasonable event and did not reward reasonable responses.

Midas walked on.

## IV.

Linden Hill, when he finally reached it in the long amber light of mid-afternoon, had probably been a nice neighborhood once. The kind with homeowners' associations and arguments about fence heights and an annual block party where someone always brought store-bought potato salad and everyone pretended to be grateful. The houses were large, set back from the road, on lots that had been generous even before nature had expanded them into small wildernesses. The street curved uphill in a gentle arc, and at its crest, partially hidden by the overgrowth of five unchecked seasons, was a building larger than the rest — a community center or a church hall, something civic, with a parking lot and a flat roof and, mounted on its southern wall, a solar panel array that was, improbably, intact.

It was the wire that Midas saw first. Actual wire — electrical cable, strung from the solar panels along a series of poles (some utility, some improvised from metal pipes cemented into buckets) leading to the building's main entrance. The cable was intact. It was recent. Someone had *maintained* it.

"Holy shit," Midas whispered.

*"WOW! Isn't that NEAT?"*

His heart was doing something it hadn't done in months, which was the thing where it beat faster not from fear but from the idiot, irresponsible, beautiful cousin of fear that people used to call *excitement* and that Midas still allowed himself because he had never learned not to.

He approached. The front door was a heavy double-panel job, the kind with crash bars, propped open with a cinder block. Inside, he could see a hallway. A mural on one wall — faded, pre-collapse, children's handprints in paint forming a rainbow. And silence. The kind of silence that could mean *empty* or could mean *waiting*, and which provided no guidance as to which.

He stepped inside.

"Hello?"

His voice came back to him off linoleum floors and cinder-block walls. The hallway branched. Left led to what had been a large open room — a gymnasium or a cafeteria — and right led to a series of smaller rooms, classrooms or offices. The lights were off, but there were lights *to be* off, which was more than most buildings could claim. Fixtures in the ceiling. A light switch on the wall that Midas flipped with the gingerness of someone defusing a bomb.

Nothing happened.

He flipped it again. Nothing continued to happen, but now it was the specific nothing of a system that had power somewhere but not here, which was different from the general nothing of a dead building and, in its way, more frustrating.

The large room, when he reached it, told the story.

Someone had been here. Someone had been here *seriously*. There were cots — a dozen of them, arranged in rows. A table with supplies, though the supplies were gone. Shelves on the wall, empty. A woodstove, improvised from a metal drum, vented through a hole cut in the ceiling, cold. On the floor, scattered: a shoe, a paperback novel splayed open to a page no one would finish, a child's drawing on construction paper.
