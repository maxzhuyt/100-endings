# Carnival of Dangerous Intentions

She had promised herself she wouldn't come back.

The boardwalk at dusk was doing what it always did — becoming something more generous than itself. Sodium lights buzzed to life along the railing in stuttering successions, each one an argument the evening was losing against the dark. The smell reached her first: kettle corn going to sugar-smoke in a cast-iron drum, the salt flats beneath the pier exhaling their low mineral breath, and something else — gun oil and cork dust drifting from the shooting gallery forty feet ahead, where a man she did not know was waiting, or not waiting, or had simply never left.

She stopped walking.

Three days ago she had been nobody on this boardwalk. She had been a woman eating a pear on a bench, reading a water-stained copy of *Persuasion*, glancing up only because a sound from the gallery had startled her — a rapid trio of cracks, mechanical and clean, like a typewriter striking the same letter three times. She'd looked up. She'd seen the curls first. Dark and heavy against the nape of his neck, almost absurdly composed, the kind of hair that belonged on a marble bust or a daguerreotype. Then the rest of him assembled around that single detail — the lean economy of his body, the rolled cuffs of a shirt too good for the venue — and he had turned, as though she'd spoken, and looked directly at her across the crowd.

She had left immediately. She had dropped her pear. She had not stopped thinking about him since, and she was not a woman inclined to think about strangers.

*His hands on the rifle.*

She silenced it. She took a breath that tasted of char and brine and walked toward the gallery.

---

The lights along the façade were painted bulbs alternating red and white, and half of them had blown, so the effect was dental — a bright mouth with gaps. Wooden ducks tracked their mechanical circuit along a painted pond. A teenage barker with acne and an enormous voice called to no one in particular that everybody was a winner, everybody, step right up.

He was not at the counter.

For one vertiginous moment she felt the particular shame of a person who has dressed carefully for a room that turns out to be empty. She had not dressed carefully. She had changed three times and then put on exactly what she would have worn to edit galleys at her desk — a dark cotton dress, low-heeled shoes, no jewelry. This was the costume of a woman not trying, and the effort that had gone into constructing it appalled her.

"The pear was a nice touch."

She turned. He was behind her and slightly to the left, leaning against a support post with his arms folded, and she understood at once that he had been watching her approach for some time. The realization should have embarrassed her. It did something else.

"Excuse me?"

"Three days ago. The pear. Jane Austen and a pear on a bench. It was a very complete picture." He smiled. His mouth did something asymmetrical with it, the left side committing before the right, as though even his face couldn't fully agree on what it was offering. "I've been thinking about it."

"You've been thinking about a stranger eating fruit."

"I've been thinking about a woman who looked up, saw me looking, and left so quickly she forgot her book." He reached behind him and produced the water-stained *Persuasion*. The cover had buckled further in the humidity. "Chapter eleven. You dog-eared the page."

She did not take the book. She looked at it in his hand the way she might have looked at a card laid face-up on a fortune teller's table — something that revealed too much about the order of things.

"You kept it," she said.

"You came back."

This was the problem. Not his arrogance — she could have managed arrogance, could have dissected it and set it on a tray like a bad oyster — but his *certainty*. It presupposed something about her that she had not consented to being. She had come back for the book, she told herself. Austen was expensive to replace.

"People return for their possessions," she said. "It isn't prophecy."

"No," he agreed. "But you didn't ask the barker. You didn't post a notice. You came at the same hour, to the same place, and you stopped right where you could be seen." The flickering lights caught his eyes, which were not brown, she realized now, but some darker country past brown, the pupil indistinguishable from the iris. "So either you wanted the book, or you wanted to be where the book was last seen. One of those is about Austen. The other isn't."

She took the book. Their fingers did not touch. She had made sure of it.

"Thank you," she said. "Good evening."

She didn't leave.

---

His name, he told her eventually, was Julian. Or he told her to call him Julian — a distinction she noted and chose not to press. He asked for hers, and she gave it — "Margot, with a *t*" — and immediately wished she hadn't offered the correction, because it sounded like an intimacy, a small door opened.

"Margot with a *t*," he repeated, and the way he held the syllables in his mouth made her feel, absurdly, as though he had taken something from her pocket.

He suggested the gallery. "I'll win you a prize."

"I'm thirty-one years old."

"Then you've gone long enough without one."

She watched him pay the barker and lift the cork rifle. He didn't joke with it, didn't mime incompetence the way men did when they wanted a woman to find them harmless. He settled the stock against his shoulder with an economy that came from somewhere real, sighted along the barrel, and fired. The wooden duck flipped backward. He fired again. A second duck. A third. The barker, who had clearly expected the usual municipal spray of misses, looked at Julian with the first genuine expression he'd worn all evening: unease.

"Pick," Julian said, not glancing at the shelf.

The prizes were the standard Coney Island grotesquerie — plush animals with misaligned eyes, patriotic pinwheels, a ceramic dog painted so badly it appeared to be melting. She chose a small glass bird, blue, with a fracture running through one wing. It sat on her palm and caught the unsteady light.

"Why that one?" he asked.

"Because it's already broken. You can't disappoint it."

He laughed — a real one, surprised out of him — and for a half-second his entire architecture of composure fell away. She glimpsed something underneath: a face that had been young more recently than the rest of him pretended. Then it closed. She had the disorienting sense of having seen a room through a door that was already swinging shut.

"Walk with me," he said.

"Where?"

"East. The noise thins out."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got."

She walked.

---

The boardwalk stretched ahead of them in a narrowing corridor of salt-bleached planks. Behind them: the calliope wheeze of the carousel, children's screams from the Cyclone spiking and falling in Doppler arcs, the competitive baying of barkers. Ahead: the sound diminishing by degrees, as though someone were turning a dial. The ocean was black and restless to their left. The Wonder Wheel turned its vast slow circuit on their right, lifting its caged passengers into the sky and returning them with the blind patience of something liturgical.

The lights here were fewer and less reliable. They passed through pockets of full illumination — a functioning sodium bulb throwing its apricot wash across the planks — and then into stretches where the nearest light had died and the only glow came from the Wheel's distant articulation against the clouds. His face in these alternations became a different face. In the light: sharp, composed, beautiful in the way of men who know the word applies and have long since stopped being improved by it. In the dark: only the planes, the architecture without detail, and something about the set of his mouth she couldn't read.

"You're an editor," he said. Not a question.

"How did you know that?"

"You read the way editors read. Your eyes move ahead of the line. You're checking the next sentence before you've finished the one you're on."

"You watched me read."

"I watched you for about six minutes before you felt it. You're less careful than you think."

This landed with a precision that felt worrisomely intimate. She was careful. She had built a life on being careful — a tasteful apartment in Brooklyn Heights, a senior position at a press that published serious novels, friendships maintained with punctual correspondence. She had been loved twice, responsibly. She had ended both affairs when the math stopped working, and she had not contested the loneliness that followed because loneliness, at least, was honest. She had thought of this as discipline. Standing here in the flickering dark, being told by a stranger that her discipline had cracks, she could not locate the rebuttal.

"Everyone has a blind spot," she said.

"Yes. But most people's blind spots aren't standing next to them on a boardwalk."

"That's very confident."

"You're still walking."

She was. They had reached the stretch past the last food stand. An old man sat fishing from the railing, his transistor radio murmuring a ball game to no one. Beyond him, the boardwalk extended another hundred yards toward the jetty, unlit, uninhabited. The wooden railing here had begun to rot at the posts. She could smell the decay — the sweet mulch scent of wet wood returning to the earth it had been taken from.

Julian stopped. She stopped. The distance between them had closed to something she could have measured in the span of her hand, though she could not have said when it happened.

---

"Ask me something honest," he said. "And I'll answer it."

She was aware of how the offer was shaped — the deflection of initiative back onto her, the illusion of generosity that was actually a transfer of responsibility. Ask, and whatever comes next is something you chose to know. She recognized the mechanism. She had read enough manuscripts to know how people constructed traps from the materials of intimacy.

"Why were you at the gallery?" she asked. "Three days ago. What were you doing there?"

"Waiting."

"For what?"

"I don't know." The sodium light from the last surviving bulb pulsed and caught his face in a slow strobe. Bright. Dark. Bright. "I've been coming to this boardwalk for a month. Every evening. I can't explain it. Something needed to happen here. I was right, but I was right the way people are right about earthquakes — it doesn't help to know."

"That's a very elaborate way of saying you liked the look of me."

"No." He turned. In the light: his eyes, level and serious. In the dark: just his voice, close. "It's an honest way of saying I recognized you. Not your face. I'd never seen your face. Something else. The way you held the book. The way you weren't performing anything for anyone. You were just — there. Present in a way that cost you something. I thought: that woman knows what it is to be tired of being safe."

The glass bird pressed against her palm inside her coat pocket. She felt the fracture line along its wing like a ridge of scar tissue.

He was right. She hated that he was right, and she hated that the hatred itself felt like a relief, like a muscle releasing that had been clenched for years. The carefulness, the discipline, the punctual correspondence — all of it had been correct, and all of it had been slowly calcifying her into someone she could respect but not recognize. She had been editing her own life the way she edited manuscripts: trimming excess, correcting errors, removing anything that threatened the coherence of the thing. And what she'd been left with was a beautiful, airtight draft of a life that no one would actually want to read.

*You don't know this man. You know nothing about this man.*

But she knew something about herself, standing here, and it was the thing she had spent three days trying not to know: she hadn't come back for the book. She hadn't come back in spite of her judgment. She had come back because the moment she'd looked up from *Persuasion* and felt the shock of his gaze across the crowd, something in her — something older than sense, older than the whole careful apparatus of her adulthood — had sat up and said *yes*, and the three days since had been nothing but a long, elaborate performance of deliberation whose outcome had never been in question.

She was not being seduced. She was choosing this. And the choice terrified her, and she wanted it anyway, and the *wanting it anyway* was the truest thing she had felt in longer than she could admit.

"You're right," she said. "I'm tired of being safe."

"I know."

"That should worry me."

"It should."

---

The Wonder Wheel had slowed. Whether for mechanical reasons or the lateness of the hour, its revolution had become nearly imperceptible — the colored lights along its frame hanging in the sky like a constellation that had lost its myth. The ocean shushed and shushed against the pilings beneath them, the sound of something enormous trying to gentle itself.

Julian reached into his pocket and produced a cigarette, then seemed to think better of it and put it back. The gesture was so small, so human — a man deciding, in real time, to deny himself a thing — that it undid something in her chest.

"There's a place past the jetty," he said. "The old bath house. It's been closed for years, but you can get onto the roof if you know where the fence opens. You can see the whole park from up there. The Wheel. The water. Everything lit up and everything dark." He paused. "I'd like to show you."

"You'd like me to climb a fence with a stranger in the dark."

"I'd like you to see something beautiful with someone you haven't decided about yet."

The sodium light behind them gave a long, theatrical flicker — on, off, on — as if the evening itself were trying to make up its mind. In the last guttering flash, she saw his face clearly: not performing, not composing. Just looking at her with an expression that held both want and resignation, as though he understood that this moment was entirely hers and he could do nothing to close the distance she hadn't chosen to cross.

The old man's radio crackled with a pitch count. The ocean worked the pilings. The glass bird sat in her pocket with its fractured wing, and the book sat in her bag with its dog-eared page — chapter eleven, the chapter where Anne Elliot is persuaded against her own desire and spends eight years regretting the safety she chose.

He held out his hand. It was not a demand. It was an open palm in unreliable light, and it could have meant anything — *come with me, trust me, ruin yourself, begin* — and she stood on the rotting boardwalk with the smell of old wood and the sound of distant rifles cracking against their targets and the whole bright ruined carnival turning its slow circles behind her, and she thought: *This is the moment. Not what comes after. This is the one I will remember — the moment I was still free to choose, and had not yet chosen, and the choosing was the whole of it.*

The light went out. The light came back.

Her hand moved.
