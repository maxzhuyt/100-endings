# Blood Moon

The harvest moon bled over Möslingen like a wound that wouldn't clot.

Yennefer of Vengerberg walked the main thoroughfare with the unhurried precision of a woman cataloguing a crime scene. Doors nailed shut from outside, not within — a detail that mattered. Livestock pens empty, gates hanging open, yet no tracks leading away. The animals hadn't fled. They'd been *taken*. Drag marks scored the mud in long parallel furrows, too wide for human fingers, leading toward the well at the village center and then simply stopping, as though whatever made them had remembered it could fly.

She knelt beside the well. The smell rising from it was copper and offal, dense enough to taste. She'd come on contract — a letter bearing the seal of a burgomaster who, she was increasingly certain, now resided in pieces somewhere below the waterline. The payment promised had been exorbitant. That should have been the first warning.

The blood was hours old. The staging was centuries practiced.

This was not feeding. This was *décor*.

She stood, brushed mud from her knees, and sent a pulse of chaos energy outward — a diagnostic spell, reading the residue of violence the way a physician reads a rash. What came back made her fingers still. The death here wasn't magical. It was physical, exuberant, and so far beyond human capability that her spell couldn't categorize the perpetrator. Not cursed. Not possessed. Something older than her taxonomy had a word for.

"You pressed your thumb to the dead man's eyelid in the third house," said a voice from everywhere and nowhere, low and pleased and resonant with a darkness that had texture. "Checking pupil dilation to establish time of death. Wonderfully empirical. Most people just scream."

Yennefer did not turn quickly. She turned *slowly* — a choice, not a reaction — and watched the darkness between two collapsed market stalls resolve itself into a shape.

Orange lenses first, round and burning like secondary moons. Then the red, so much red — a coat that spilled from shadow like arterial spray given fabric and form, pooling around a figure that was too tall, too still, and smiling with too many teeth. He coalesced the way nightmares do: suddenly always having been there.

"You've been watching me since the gate," she said. Not a question.

"Since before the gate." His grin widened. "You stopped six hundred meters out and reapplied a glamour. Lilac. A lovely choice. It almost covers what you actually are."

"And what am I, actually?"

He inhaled — theatrical, unnecessary, a dead man pantomiming life for the pleasure of the gesture. "Not human. Haven't been for a long time. Rebuilt from the ground up, unless I miss the scent of deep magic stitched into the bones themselves." He tilted his head, the orange lenses catching moonlight. "Someone *made* you, sorceress. How delightful. Someone made me too."

"No one made you." Yennefer held his gaze through those absurd lenses and let her own vision shift, letting the chaos sight layer over the mundane. What she saw beneath his skin nearly cost her composure. Souls. Hundreds, thousands — roiling, screaming, compressed into a single vessel like a mass grave given legs and a tailor. "You ate your way to this. Every soul you've consumed is still in there, still aware. You're not a creature. You're a *oubliette*."

The grin didn't vanish. But it changed — the amusement thinning, the teeth less prominent, something almost startled surfacing in its place. He stepped closer. The shadows moved with him, not cast by him but *part* of him, extensions of a body that was only nominally solid.

"It has been," he said softly, "a very long time since someone looked at me and saw the prison instead of the prisoner."

"I've met monsters who mistake their cage for a crown. You're more honest about it than most." She paused. "The village was unnecessary."

"The village was an *invitation*."

"For me?"

"For anyone worth the conversation." Another step. He was close enough now that the shadows licking at the edges of his coat brushed against the hem of her skirt, cold as cellar air. "You haven't reached for a weapon. You haven't opened a portal. You aren't afraid."

"I'm not."

"Why?"

Yennefer smiled — not warmly. "Because you didn't kill the children. There are no small bodies here, no small drag marks. You moved them somewhere before you began. That tells me everything about you that you think your theatrics conceal."

Something happened to his face. The predatory architecture of it — the sharp angles, the impossible pallor, the mouth designed for tearing — shifted. For a fraction of a second he looked like a man receiving a knife he'd asked for without understanding it would actually cut.

Then the shadows *erupted*.

They fountained upward from his body, a column of shrieking dark that split the bloody moonlight and turned the village square into a maw. Eyes opened in the darkness, dozens, hundreds, the consumed souls pressing against the membrane of his control, and the sound they made was not a roar but a *chorus* — every voice he'd swallowed singing at once.

Yennefer didn't flinch.

She answered.

Chaos magic detonated from her hands — violet, coruscating, alive with an energy that was fundamentally *opposed* to what he was. It met his shadows and the collision was not an explosion but a *conversation* — her light reading his dark, his dark wrapping her light, the two forces circling each other with the same wary fascination as their casters. A market stall disintegrated. The well cracked. Above them, the blood moon seemed to descend.

Then silence.

They stood in the wreckage, closer than before — close enough to touch, though neither did. His shadows had retreated. Her magic had dimmed. The air between them smelled of ozone and old graves and lilac.

"Magnificent," he breathed.

"Don't." The word was quiet, and it carried a weight she hadn't intended — because she recognized in his eyes, behind those ridiculous lenses, the same thing she saw in her own mirror on the worst nights. The exhaustion of power without equal. The loneliness of a long, long life lived among things that broke too easily.

He saw her see it. She saw him see her see it.

Neither looked away. Violet eyes and red ones, holding the silence between them like a blade balanced on its point.

"I never got your name, sorceress."

"No," she agreed. "You didn't."

A portal opened at her back — she hadn't gestured, hadn't spoken, the magic responding to a thought she barely admitted to having. The instinct to leave before this became something she'd have to carry.

He dissolved at the same moment, his body unmaking itself from the feet up, the red coat the last thing to go, that terrible gentle smile lingering like light after a fire.

The village square stood empty.

The blood moon hung where it had always hung, indifferent and red, and the drag marks in the mud led nowhere, and two absences occupied the space where something had almost been named.

Neither of them turned their back.
