# Chapter Two: The Acheson Cabin

The evening settled over the lodge the way evenings always did—gradually, then all at once, the last copper light pulling back through the windows like a tide retreating from shore. JT sat on the bench by the woodstove with his boots off and his wool socks steaming faintly, watching his father work a brass brush through the barrel of the Remington 700 in slow, meditative strokes. The bore solvent smell was sharp and familiar, almost comforting—a smell that meant the day's hunting was done and what remained was warmth and routine and the small mechanical liturgies men perform when they've run out of things to say to each other.

Mike was at the kitchen counter, slicing an onion into rough circles for the elk sausage they'd brought up frozen from town. He was humming something—classic rock, probably Skynyrd, though he was flat enough it could have been anything. He cracked a beer with his knife hand, drank from it, and went back to slicing. The domesticity of it—the ordinariness—hit JT like a door swung open into a bright room after hours in the dark.

Because it was ordinary. That was the thing. Everything about this evening was *precisely* what it should have been—his father quiet and methodical, Mike easy and half-performing, the stove breathing its dry heat into the room, the smell of gun oil and onion and wood smoke braiding together into a scent that was indistinguishable from every October he'd ever lived. If someone had walked in right then, a stranger, they would have seen three men at rest after a day in the field. They would have seen nothing wrong.

JT couldn't decide if that made him feel safer or more afraid.

His father held the barrel up to the overhead bulb and squinted through it, checking the rifling for copper fouling. His hands were steady. JT watched them specifically for that—steadiness—because those same hands, four hours ago, had reached toward a caribou bull that should have bolted at their approach but hadn't, and the fingers had trembled so badly the tendons had been visible through the skin. He was sure of that. Almost sure. The afternoon already had the quality of something half-remembered from a fever: vivid in patches, vague at the seams, resistant to being held in sequence. The clearing. The bull standing broadside at forty yards, close enough to see the individual hairs along its flank shiver in the wind. His father stepping forward. Mike going still—not alert-still but vacant-still, slack-jawed, like a man watching television at three in the morning. And the sound. That low, resonant hum that hadn't seemed to come from anywhere, that had pressed against JT's eardrums from the inside.

None of it had been mentioned. Not during the walk back. Not when they'd stopped to fill canteens at the creek. Not when his father had hung the packs on the porch rail and said, simply, "Slow day." As though the whole encounter had been edited out of the shared record, snipped clean, the two ends of the afternoon spliced together so neatly you couldn't see the join.

JT had almost said something on the trail. He'd formed the words three separate times—*Dad, what was that? Mike, did you feel it?*—and each time the sentence had died somewhere between his lungs and his throat, extinguished by a force he couldn't name. Not fear exactly. Something older. A familial gravity, bone-deep, that governed what was sayable in this family and what would remain forever beneath the surface, unexamined, like the dark wood at the bottom of a lake that everyone agrees not to swim down to.

"Sausages or the canned stuff?" Mike called over his shoulder.

"Sausages," their father said. "Use the cast iron. And don't burn them this time."

"I didn't burn them. I *charred* them. There's a culinary distinction."

"There's a distinction between cooking and arson. You're on the wrong side of it."

Mike grinned. Their father almost smiled—JT saw the corner of his mouth twitch—and for a moment the scene was so achingly normal that JT wanted to surrender to it, to let the warmth and the banter and the amber light from the single overhead bulb just wash the afternoon clean. Families were good at this. *His* family was good at this. They had survived his mother leaving, his father's years-long retreat into silence, Mike's blown-out knee and lost scholarship and the drinking that followed—survived all of it not by confronting anything but by simply reconstituting the routine each morning, laying the table, cleaning the rifle, cutting the onion. The world could crack open beneath your feet and by dinner you'd be arguing about sausages.

He watched his father's hands—steady, steady, steady—and said nothing.

---

The sausages were good, actually. Mike had a talent for cooking he'd never admit to, seasoning by instinct, and the elk meat was rich and peppery and went well with the instant mashed potatoes and canned green beans that constituted the lodge's version of a side dish. They ate at the table with the overhead light humming its faint electrical whine and the stove popping behind them, and for fifteen minutes JT allowed himself to just be hungry and then full, which were simple mammalian states that required no interpretation.

It was over the last of the whiskey—their father poured three fingers each from the bottle of Evan Williams he brought on every trip—that the Acheson cabin came up.

Mike started it. He'd been talking about the creek crossing on the north trail, how the water was higher than last year, the beaver dam downstream backing everything up. "Probably flooding out that whole north bench," he said, swirling his whiskey. "All the way up to the old Acheson place, I'd bet."

JT looked at him.

"Acheson," their father repeated. He said it neutrally, but his fingers tightened around his glass—a small contraction, barely visible, the kind of thing you only notice when you've spent your whole life studying a man's hands for weather.

"You ever actually been inside that cabin?" Mike asked, directing the question at their father but leaning back in his chair with the loose, easy posture of a man making idle conversation, filling silence.

"Once. Years ago." Their father drank. "Before you boys were born."

"What's it like?"

"A cabin." A pause. "One room. River-stone chimney. Hank built it himself in—'68, I think. Lived out there alone for close to fifteen years."

"Christ," Mike said. "Fifteen years? Out here?"

"He liked his privacy."

"That's not privacy. That's feral."

Their father didn't smile. He looked at the surface of his whiskey the way he'd looked through the rifle barrel earlier—searching for something minute, some flaw or residue. "People in town talked," he said, slowly, as if selecting each word from a series of options and discarding the others. "Said he went strange near the end. Said he'd come into the store to buy supplies and wouldn't look anyone in the eye. Just recited his list. Flour, salt, ammunition, kerosene. Same list every time. Like a recording."

"Dementia," Mike said.

"Maybe."

"What else would it be?"

Their father was quiet for a long moment. The stove ticked. Outside, the wind moved through the spruce in a long exhale that JT felt against the back of his neck, though the windows were sealed and there was no draft.

"Frank Metcalf told me once," their father said, "that toward the end, the last year before Hank finally left, he'd started nailing things to the inside walls of the cabin. From the inside. Boards, scrap metal, pieces of hide. Frank went up there after Hank was gone and said you couldn't see the original walls anymore. Said it looked like the man had been trying to make the cabin smaller. Like he wanted less room between himself and the walls."

The way he said it—without inflection, without commentary, as a simple reporting of fact—made it worse than any ghost story told around a fire. It landed in JT's chest like a stone dropped into still water and sank without a ripple. Mike blinked, seemed to weigh a wisecrack, and then didn't make one. That in itself was notable. Mike always made the wisecrack. It was his function in the family architecture—defuse, deflect, perform ease so that no one else had to.

"What happened to him?" JT heard himself ask. "Acheson. After he left."

His father finished his whiskey. Set the glass down with the careful precision of a man who has decided the conversation is over.

"Nobody knows," he said. "He drove to town one morning, left his truck at the gas station with the keys in it, and that was the last anyone saw of him." He pushed his chair back. "I'm turning in. Mike, bank the stove before you go to bed."

He walked to the back bedroom and closed the door. Not hard, not soft—just closed, the way you close a book when you've reached the last page you're willing to read.

Mike let out a slow breath and poured himself another measure. He looked at JT across the table with an expression that could have been amusement or something more complex, something that had the structure of amusement but not its content—a mask shaped like a grin.

"Good old Dad," he said. "Campfire stories."

"Mike."

"Yeah?"

*Did you see it today? In the clearing? Do you remember?* JT held the question in his mouth like a coal, feeling it burn.

"Nothing," JT said. "Never mind."

Mike watched him a beat too long, then drank.

---

He tried once more. Later. Not directly—he'd learned. He came at it from the side.

Mike was on the porch smoking a cigarette, looking out at the clearing and the tree line beyond it. The sky was that late-October dark that wasn't quite black but more like the blue-black of deep bruise, and the stars were hard and close and too many, the way they always were this far from any town. JT leaned against the porch rail beside his brother and let the silence run for the length of a cigarette.

"You notice anything weird about the caribou today?" JT asked. He kept his voice easy, curious, the intonation of a man reviewing a day. Nothing more.

Mike tapped ash over the rail. "Weird how?"

"I don't know. The bull. How close it was."

"They get habituated sometimes. Herds that don't get hunted enough, they lose their—"

"It wasn't habituated, Mike. It didn't move. Dad walked right up to it."

Mike took a drag. Held it. Let it out in a thin stream that the wind tore apart.

"I don't remember it that way," he said.

The sentence was perfectly constructed—not a denial but a reclassification. *I don't remember it that way.* Not *that didn't happen* but *my version is different from yours,* which placed the aberration on JT's side of the ledger, made him the one whose perception was suspect. JT recognized the maneuver because it was the same one their father had used for decades—the half-pivot, the reframing—and he felt a flare of anger so acute it almost broke through the surface.

"You were standing right there," JT said. "You saw—"

"I saw Dad get excited about a bull and move too fast. Spooked it. It happens." Mike dropped the cigarette and ground it under his boot, and when he looked at JT his face was kind, patient, the face of the older brother who'd taught him to skate and picked him up off the ice every time he fell, and behind that kindness was a wall so seamless and so total that JT understood, with a clarity that felt like stepping off a ledge, that they would never talk about this. That Mike had already decided what had happened in the clearing, or—worse—that whatever had happened to Mike in the clearing had decided for him.

"You look wrecked, JT. Get some sleep."

Mike went inside. The screen door clapped shut behind him. JT stood alone on the porch, hands gripping the rail, staring at the dark.

---

The wrongness gathered through the rest of the evening the way weather gathers—not all at once but in accumulations, small pressures building toward something that had no name yet.

He noticed it first with the birds. Or the absence of them. The lodge clearing usually held a small population of boreal chickadees and gray jays that haunted the eaves and the woodpile, looking for scraps. They'd been there that morning—JT remembered hearing them while he laced his boots. Now there was nothing. No movement along the gutters, no flutter in the brush pile by the outhouse. The silence was specific, carved out, the kind of silence that happens not when life is absent but when it has *withdrawn*, pulled back to a perimeter JT couldn't see.

Then there was the creek. They could always hear it from the lodge—a faint, persistent murmur, audible in the gaps between wind. Tonight it was louder. Not dramatically, but enough that JT stood on the porch and listened, head cocked, trying to calibrate the change. The water sounded closer. Or deeper. Or both. As if the drainage had shifted, the creek finding a new channel in the night, bending toward the lodge the way a river bends around an obstruction.

And the light. The light was wrong in a way he couldn't articulate to himself, much less to anyone else. The twilight had lasted too long. He'd checked his watch—old habit, the digital Casio he'd worn since college—and the math hadn't worked. Sunset should have been 6:47. At 7:30 there'd still been a band of deep salmon along the western ridge, and by the time full dark came, his watch read 8:15, which meant nearly ninety minutes of lingering dusk in a season when dark came fast and hard. He thought about mentioning it. He ran the conversation forward in his head—*Hey, did the sunset seem long to you?*—and heard the response before it came: *You're overthinking. Have another drink. Go to bed.* So he didn't mention it.

He noticed the trees.

It wasn't what had happened earlier—the outright spatial wrongness of the treeline being *closer*, an impossibility he'd already sealed away in the part of his mind reserved for things he couldn't process. This was subtler. The trees at the clearing's edge looked different in their arrangement. Not moved, but *organized*—as if the natural randomness of their spacing had been gently corrected, trunks aligned into something that was almost a pattern, almost a repetition, the way a row of people standing at a distance might look like a fence. He stared at them from the kitchen window while rinsing his plate, running water over his hands, and the longer he looked the more the pattern seemed to assert itself and the more certain he became that if he looked away and looked back it would be gone, replaced by ordinary chaos, and his looking would become the problem, evidence of his own unraveling.

He looked away. He looked back. The trees were just trees.

But his hands were shaking.
