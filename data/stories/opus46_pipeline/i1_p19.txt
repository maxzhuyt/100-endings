# High Seas and Low Vices

---

## I.

The harbor at Canton smells of tar and ambition at six in the morning, though neither word does justice to the particular quality of the air—tar being the simplest note in a chord that includes teak oil, salt brine, the sulfurous breath of low tide, and the sweat of men already three hours into hauling crates of cotton and tin from the Company boats. I stood at the rail of the upper veranda outside the officers' quarters, in full dress because Lieutenant Endicott had called an inspection for reasons known only to God and his own petty aspirations, and I pressed my thumbnail into the groove between my index and middle fingers because the light was wrong. Not wrong in any way I could explain to Endicott. Wrong the way light is wrong when you have slept only in pieces, when the lining of your throat feels like sailcloth and the backs of your eyeballs ache with a pressure that has nothing to do with sight.

My left hand was in my pocket. My thumb moved across the belly of the jade figurine—a small seated figure, some deity or sage whose name I had never learned—and the motion was thoughtless, a metronome I did not remember starting.

"Merrick."

That was Endicott, already on the gravel below, his boots an offense to the senses at that hour. He was a man who wore starch the way other men wore armor: constantly, and with the evident belief that it made him larger.

"Lieutenant."

"You look like you've been eating the local cuisine again."

"Fish disagrees with me. I've said so."

"Fish disagrees with half the men in this factory. You don't see Pollard looking like he's been dragged behind a sampan."

"Pollard has a constitution like a frigate hull. I am built along the lines of a pleasure yacht."

Endicott almost smiled, which was as close as Endicott came to human warmth. He consulted a paper in his hand. "The manifest discrepancies from Tuesday's consignment—the Dowell and Gray shipment. Hodges needs your figures before noon. And there's a meeting with Dent's comprador at three regarding the coastal survey permissions."

"Noted."

"Are you noting it, or are you going to wander off again and come back at dusk smelling of something I'd rather not identify?"

My stomach turned over slowly, a deliberate revolution, as if the organ itself were examining its contents and finding them absent. I removed my hand from my pocket and placed it on the railing. Both hands visible. A gesture of transparency I had practiced until it no longer required conscious thought.

"The figures will be on Hodges's desk by eleven."

"By eleven. Good." He squinted at me. The Canton sun was behind my head, which helped. "You might also consider that the commission review is in six weeks, and that your record of late has been—how shall I say—impressionistic."

"I shall endeavor to be photographic."

"You shall endeavor to be present, Merrick. That would be a start."

He walked away across the gravel, each step crunching with the regularity of a well-wound clock. I watched him go and felt the sweat begin at my wrists, which was where it always started—the insides of the wrists, as though the veins themselves were leaking heat. Then the small muscles of my jaw clenched without instruction. Then the figurine was in my hand again and I could not recall the intervening motion.

I had ninety minutes before my body would make all of this academic.

The figures. Yes. I went inside, found the Dowell and Gray paperwork in the second drawer where I had left it three days ago, and transcribed the necessary columns in a hand that was, I noted with professional interest, not yet trembling. A fine distinction: the hand was *tight*, gripping the pen as if it might attempt escape, but it did not tremble. The trembling would begin in approximately one hour and would worsen on a curve I knew as well as I knew the tidal charts.

I left the figures on Hodges's desk at half past six, weighed down with a brass compass so the harbor draft would not take them. That was four and a half hours early, which Endicott would have considered exemplary had he been capable of crediting me with anything.

The veranda again. The harbor. A hundred junks and lorchas and sampans and, further out, the European merchantmen at anchor in the pearl-gray haze of the river, their masts a leafless forest. Beautiful, in the way that all working systems are beautiful when you are not currently inside their machinery.

My throat constricted. I swallowed twice, producing nothing. The jade figure's head was smooth against the pad of my thumb, worn to a feature-lessness that might have been serenity or might have been erosion.

I went to change my coat.

---

## II.

The foreign factories—the Thirteen Hongs as they were called, though their number had proved as fluid as any other fact in Canton—occupied a strip of riverfront that could, with generosity, be described as a quarter-mile of concentrated European anxiety. One walked past the Danish flag, the American, the Dutch, the façades of pale stucco turning green at their bases from the damp, and the cobblestones underfoot were almost correct, almost Bristol or Amsterdam, until you looked up and saw the roof tiles curved like the hulls of inverted boats and the sky that was never quite the right shade of gray for England.

Then the factories ended, and China began, though in truth China had never stopped—it merely tolerated the interruption.

I turned onto Thirteen Hong Street, which was not its Chinese name, and shed my officer's coat by the simple mechanism of folding it over my arm and then rolling it into a bundle that fit beneath the loose cotton jacket I had bought from a secondhand stall eight months ago. A Chinese jacket, or near enough—hemp-colored, collarless, commonplace. It did not make me Chinese. Nothing could make a man of my complexion and stature Chinese in Canton. But it made me less specifically a naval officer, which was the operative distinction.

The street narrowed. The buildings leaned toward each other as if conspiring. Bamboo poles jutted from upper windows, draped with laundry that formed a patchwork ceiling over the alley—trousers and tunics in faded indigo and black, a woman's red wedding jacket incongruous among them, dripping slowly. Below: a man mending rattan chairs, his fingers working without reference to his eyes, which were on me. A cook shop where the charcoal brazier threw a belt of heat across the passageway and the smell was rice porridge and scallion and something fried in sesame oil that made my stomach clench with a hunger I could not afford to feed. Two children running, one carrying a cricket cage, the bamboo slats blurring as he swung it. A barber's stool occupied by an old man whose face was half-lathered, the barber's razor catching light as it paused—both men watching me pass with the flat, measuring attention that Canton reserved for foreign bodies in its alimentary canal.

I was sweating through the cotton jacket. The humidity was a substance you moved through rather than breathed, and beneath it my skin had begun its secondary protest: a crawling sensation along the forearms, as though the hair follicles were individually objecting to existence. My right hand was in my pocket. The figurine. The thumb, circling.

The alley opened into a slightly wider street where a tea merchant's shopfront spilled onto the pavement—wooden crates stamped with characters I could read only in fragments, the word for "spring" and the word for "mountain" recurring. Beyond it, a provision shop. Beyond that, the entrance to a courtyard I knew as well as I knew the deck of any ship I'd served on.

"Ah. The commander's dog comes back to eat."

That was Lin Ah-Kee, who sat on a stool beside the courtyard gate as though the city had grown up around him and he had simply never moved. He was not old—forty, perhaps, perhaps less—but he had the economy of motion that belonged to old men or to those who had decided early that most human activity was not worth the expenditure. He brokered introductions, translated when it suited him, and operated as a kind of human valve between the foreign factories and the Chinese merchants who preferred not to deal with Europeans directly. His English was excellent and idiosyncratic, assembled from years of listening to sailors, traders, missionaries, and, I suspected, a stint in the employ of someone more educated than any of them.

"Good morning, Lin."

"Not morning. Sun is over the roof already. You are late today."

"I had duties."

"Duties." He smiled. It was the smile of a man watching a dog perform a trick that both of them knew was a trick. "You walk different today. Stiff. How long since?"

"I don't know what you mean."

"You don't know. Yes. Very mysterious. The foreign devil with the shaking hands comes here every day and doesn't know what I mean." He shifted on his stool and produced a fan from somewhere, opening it with a flick that was both practical and dismissive. The fan was painted with mountains and cranes. He used it as other men used punctuation. "Madame Xu asks after you."

"I'm not going to Madame Xu's."

"No? She says you owe for the broken screen."

"The screen was already broken."

"The screen was cracked. You made it broken. There is a difference which costs twelve cash."

"I paid her eight."

"Eight is an insult. Eight says: your screen was worthless and my debt to you is also worthless. She is still angry."

"Then I will avoid Madame Xu."

"You will avoid. Yes." Another flick of the fan. He regarded me over its edge. His eyes performed the same inventory they performed every time: the sweat, the grip of my jaw, the hand in my pocket, the particular quality of stillness that was not calm but its opposite—a stillness made of clenched things. "You look bad today, Merrick. Worse than last week."

"I'm aware."

"Awareness is not medicine."

The courtyard behind him was quiet. Through the gate I could see a woman sweeping with a bamboo broom, the bristles hissing on stone. A cat sat on a rain barrel and watched the broom with the ancient suspicion of its kind. The smells here were different: joss smoke from a household shrine somewhere, the green-water scent of the rain barrel, and beneath both the faint sweetness that I had learned to detect at a hundred yards—the sweetness that was not food; the sweetness that was the reason I was standing in this courtyard while the figures for Dowell and Gray sat on Hodges's desk and Endicott polished his contempt.

My left calf cramped. A single vicious contraction that locked the muscle into a knot and forced me to shift my weight, which Lin observed with the equanimity of a physician who has seen the disease before and no longer finds its symptoms interesting.

"You want to see Chen Bao-Yu," he said. It was not a question.

"Yes."

"Chen Bao-Yu is not generous today. His shipment from Patna was delayed and he is in a mood like a wet cat."

"I don't need his generosity. I have something to offer."

Lin's fan paused. "What do you have?"

"It doesn't concern you."

"Everything in this courtyard concerns me. I am the gate. Things pass through me or they do not pass." He folded the fan and pointed it at me. "Last time you offered Chen Bao-Yu the location of the excise patrol routes and he gave you four days' supply. The time before that, you drew him a map of the British anchorage positions. What do you have today that is worth his attention? Keep in mind his cat-mood."

The inventory was precise, and worse for being spoken aloud by a man who said it without judgment—the way a bookkeeper reads a ledger. These are the columns. This is the balance. The debits are as listed.

I said nothing. My thumb moved on the figurine. Lin watched my pocket.

"Go through," he said. "Second door on the left. His nephew will be there first. Be polite to the nephew—he is stupider than Chen Bao-Yu and therefore more dangerous."

"Thank you, Lin."

"Don't thank me. Thanking me suggests I have done you a favor, and I don't favor dogs. I open the gate. The dog walks through. What happens to the dog is the dog's business."

I walked through.

---

## III.

The second door on the left opened into a room that served, as far as I could determine, as a combination office, storeroom, and judgment hall for a man who operated across enough trades that no single word in English or Cantonese quite captured his profession. Chen Bao-Yu imported tea, exported silk, brokered arrangements between rural opium growers and the coastal distributors who moved their product to the dens of Canton and Macau, lent money at rates that would have made a London usurer wince, and maintained a network of informants that I suspected was more comprehensive than the East India Company's. He was fifty-three years old. He had a scar on his left hand where two fingers had been and a way of sitting that made the wooden chair behind his desk look like a magisterial bench.

He was not in the room. His nephew was.

The nephew's name was Chen Wei, and he was perhaps twenty-five, with the soft hands of someone who had never hauled rope or carried cargo and the hard eyes of someone who had learned, probably from his uncle, that softness of any kind was a liability to be concealed or shed. He sat at a smaller desk to the right of Chen Bao-Yu's empty chair, and he was eating dumplings from a bamboo steamer with the focused attention of a man for whom lunch was the most important event of the morning.

"Commander Merrick," he said, without looking up. His English was cleaner than Lin's—missionary-school English, pressed and starched, though he deployed it with an irony that suggested he knew exactly whose gift it was and what he thought of the givers. "Sit."

There was one other chair. I sat. The room smelled of the dumplings, of sandalwood from an incense coil burning on a high shelf, and the drier, archived smell of paper—stacked ledgers and rolled documents occupied most of the wall behind Chen Bao-Yu's desk. The light came from a single window whose oilpaper pane turned the sun into a pale square on the floor, and in that square a gecko held perfectly still, its throat pulsing.

"I need to see your uncle."

"My uncle is occupied."

"I can wait."

"Yes, you can." He selected another dumpling, bit it precisely in half, and chewed with his molars. "Waiting is something you are good at, Commander. You wait in our courtyard. You wait in our hallway. Very patient. Like a heron standing in the shallows." He swallowed. "The heron also waits because it is hungry."

"I have a proposition for your uncle."

"You always have a proposition. Last month's proposition cost my uncle a conversation with the Hoppo's deputy that was—how is the English—*delicate*. Your patrol routes were inaccurate. The excise men appeared where you said they would not. Two boats were seized."

"The routes changed after I provided the information. I gave what I had at the time."

"What you had at the time." He set down his chopsticks and looked at me for the first time. The appraisal was thorough—the cotton jacket that fooled no one, the sweat marks at my collar, the way I sat with my hands pressed flat on my thighs to keep them from performing their autobiography. "What you had at the time was worth four days' supply. What you have now—what is it?"

"I'd prefer to discuss it with Chen Bao-Yu."

"Discuss it with me. I am his ears today."

The cramp in my calf had migrated upward into the thigh, and a new sensation had joined the catalogue: a cold liquidity in my bowels, as though my intestines were filling with seawater. I had perhaps two hours before the full cascade—the sweating that soaked through any fabric, the uncontrollable yawning that looked like boredom but was the body screaming for a chemical it had been taught to require, the pain in the bones themselves, which was the cruelest symptom because it should not have been possible for bones to hurt and yet they did, they ached as though they had been replaced overnight with something poorly made.
