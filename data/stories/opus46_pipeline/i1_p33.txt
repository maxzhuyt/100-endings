# The Last Outpost

The trick to a good pour is knowing when to stop.

Most folks think it's about the wrist. It's not. It's about watching the surface tension right at the lip of the glass, that little meniscus where the liquid decides whether it's staying put or making a mess of your evening. You stop just before that moment. You develop a feel for it. Three years behind this bar and mine's pretty good.

I tip the Palladian ale to its proper line and set it on the rail. The foam settles in a pale green crown. Perfect.

"Nets, he's doing it again."

I look up. Corrigan's jerking her thumb toward the far booth where Dace has, for the third night running, deployed a portable holodeck and is projecting what appears to be a scale model of the Kepler-16 binary system directly into Maro's soup bowl. The twin stars orbit lazily through the minestrone, casting tiny amber shadows across the bread basket.

"Dace," I call out, not lifting my eyes from the ale. "Maro's soup."

"It's *science*, Nets."

"It's minestrone, Dace. Move your binary."

He grumbles but collapses the projection with a pinch of his fingers. The twin suns wink out. Maro rescues a bread roll from where Kepler-16b had been orbiting, inspects it for digital residue that doesn't exist, and gives me a grateful nod. I slide the ale down the bar to Corrigan, who catches it without looking, with the ease of someone who's been parking her elbows on my bartop for two standard years.

"You're a tyrant," Dace calls out.

"I'm a bartender. Same skill set, better tips."

This is the rhythm. This is what I built, or what built itself around me while I was busy pouring drinks and pretending I wasn't building anything at all.

The Last Outpost sits on a rock called Fenwick-7, which sits in an asteroid belt that sits at the outer spiral of Andromeda, which sits in the part of the universe most navigational charts label with the equivalent of *here be dust*. We get miners coming off shift with grit under their nails and a thirst that borders on the theological. We get merchants running cargo between the belt and the processing stations on Vega and Meridian, always in a hurry, always with one more story about a customs agent they outsmarted. We get the occasional lost tourist who booked a resort package on Vega Prime and ended up here instead, blinking at the low ceilings and the recycled air and the fact that our fanciest cocktail comes with a paper umbrella I fold myself from supply manifests.

I came here to disappear. That part's not a secret—half the people on the rim came here for the same reason. What you disappear from, though, that's yours to keep. The unspoken compact. Nobody asks. In return, you show up. You do your work. You become part of the organism.

Turns out disappearing is easier when you make yourself essential.

"Corrigan, you owe me for last Tuesday," I say, planting both hands on the bar.

"I owe you an *apology* for last Tuesday."

"You owe me an apology *and* fourteen credits for the chair."

"The chair was already broken."

"The chair was *vintage*."

"The chair was ugly, Nets. I did your décor a favor."

She tips her glass at me like that settles it. It doesn't settle it, but I let the beat pass because Corrigan runs ore shipments out of Fenwick-7's northern shaft and she's good for it, and because the chair really was ugly. I'd pulled it from a decommissioned freighter and told myself the scorch marks gave it character. Corrigan had put her boots up on it, leaned back, and the whole thing had surrendered to entropy with a spectacular crack that stopped every conversation in the bar for a full three seconds.

Dace has rebooted his holodeck at a lower intensity—I can see the faint ghosting of orbital lines hovering above his table, but they're staying clear of Maro's airspace, so I let it go. Maro's telling him something about crystalline formations in the northern tunnels, her hands making shapes in the air. At the corner table, two merchant pilots whose names I haven't learned yet are playing a card game I don't recognize with a deck that occasionally flickers, suggesting the cards are running some kind of augmentation layer I'm choosing not to notice. Old Fen—no relation to the rock, just an unfortunate coincidence he's tired of explaining—is asleep in his usual spot by the heat vent, his drink untouched, his tab running. He comes in for the warmth. I charge him for one beer and let him stay till closing.

This is the ecosystem. It breathes. I'm part of its respiratory system.

Behind me, there's a familiar sharp pop—a glass slipping off the drying rack, followed by the bright, brief violence of it shattering on the floor. I don't turn around. Kel, my barback, mutters a curse in Orioni—something about the mothers of inanimate objects—and I hear the broom come out.

"Rack?" I ask over my shoulder.

"Rack," Kel confirms.

It happens twice a week, sometimes three times. The rack's been slightly warped since the pressure fluctuation we had two months ago, the one that also killed the heating in the storeroom and made all the glasses on the north shelf sing a perfect C-sharp in unison. I'd gotten the heating fixed. I'd marveled at the glasses. And I'd kept meaning to recalibrate the rack and kept not doing it, because there was always a more immediate thing—a shipment to receive, a dispute to mediate, a paper umbrella to fold.

I hear Kel sweeping the shards into a dustpan. I hear the pieces clink together like small, defeated bells. I make a mental note about the rack that I know will dissolve by morning.

Add it to the list.

The door hisses open.

I notice her because she doesn't scan the room.

Everyone scans the room. It's instinct when you walk into a bar on the rim, especially one you've never been to before. You check the exits—we've got two, the front door and the service corridor behind the stockroom, though the second one sticks in cold weather and Fenwick-7 is nothing if not cold. You clock the faces, sorting them into categories: threat, neutral, potential ally, too drunk to matter. You calculate whether this is the kind of establishment where your drink gets doctored or your tab gets honored. It takes about four seconds. Everybody does it.

She doesn't do any of that.

She walks straight to the bar and sits down on the third stool from the left—my best stool, the one with the padding that hasn't completely compressed, though she couldn't know that—and she sits like she's been falling for a long time and this is where she finally hit the ground. Not a collapse. More like the moment a dropped object reaches terminal velocity and just... accepts the physics of its situation.

I take her in without staring. It's a skill. Three years of practice.

Tentacled dreads, dark and thick, hanging askew as if she's been sleeping on them at bad angles or, more likely, not sleeping at all. The dreads are a deep indigo at the roots, fading to something paler at the tips, and they move faintly on their own—a species trait, maybe, or biomod, not my business. Her jacket is the kind of hardwearing synth-canvas that field researchers wear, the kind with too many pockets, and every pocket looks used. Hands flat on the bartop. Fingernails bitten short. Fingertips slightly trembling, which she is clearly aware of and clearly unable to stop.

There's a particular expression I've learned to recognize. It's not despair—despair is theatrical, it wants an audience. This is something quieter. It's the face of someone who has been carrying a specific weight for a specific amount of time and has arrived at the precise point where the weight and the time have intersected to produce a kind of exhaustion that goes past the physical and into the structural. Bone-tired, soul-tired, math-tired.

She's got it.

"Vega whisky," she says. Her voice is steady, controlled. Her eyes aren't. "Neat. Better make that a double."

I pull the bottle from the second shelf—not the top shelf, where I keep the stuff that's more sentiment than liquor, and not the bottom shelf, where I keep the stuff that's more solvent than sentiment. Second shelf. The honest shelf. I pour. Two fingers, then a breath more. I know when to stop.

I set it in front of her.

She picks it up and drinks half of it in one smooth motion that tells me she knows the whisky and respects it but is not, at this moment, interested in savoring anything. Then she holds the glass in both hands, staring into the remaining amber like she's waiting for it to tell her something.

I give her space. This is the part of the job that isn't about pouring—it's about choreography. I pick up my rag, move down the bar, wipe a section that doesn't need wiping. Close enough to signal availability. Far enough to signal patience. The bartender's geometry. Most people come around within a minute or two. The ones who don't, you leave alone. Simple.

She takes forty-five seconds.

"How long has this outpost been here?" she asks.

Odd question. Not *what's good on the menu* or *where can I find a room for the night*. How long has this been here. Like she's calculating something.

"Fenwick station's been operational about forty years," I say. "The bar itself, twelve or so—previous owner built it out of a decommissioned cargo hold. I took it over three years ago."

"And the community? The people?"

"What about them?"

"Is it stable?"

I stop wiping. Not abruptly, not dramatically—I just let the rag come to rest and meet her eyes. They're amber, nearly the same shade as the whisky, and up close the exhaustion in them is layered. There's something recent on top—the travel, the not-sleeping—but underneath that there's something older and more structural.

"Stable enough," I say. "People come and go. Miners rotate in and out on contracts. Merchants pass through. But the core—maybe thirty, forty people who live here full-time—yeah. We hold together." I pause. "Why are you asking?"

She turns the glass in her fingers. One rotation. Two.

"My name's Sable," she says. "I'm a cartographer. Independent, contracted to the Meridian Consortium for the last three years. I've been running surveys on belt drift patterns in this quadrant—gravitational mapping, trajectory modeling, long-term orbital analysis."

"Okay." I lean against the back counter, arms folded. Listening posture. Open.

"The belt is moving."

"Belts drift," I say. "That's why they pay people like you to keep the charts current—"

"No." The word comes out harder than she intended. I can tell because she catches herself, recalibrates, drops her voice. "I don't mean drift. Drift is incremental. Drift is background noise. I mean the entire belt—this section, the Fenwick corridor—is undergoing a macro-orbital shift. The gravitational dynamics have changed. Something in the inner systems, a mass redistribution, maybe—I haven't been able to isolate the cause because no one will fund the follow-up survey—but the effect is clear." She pauses. Drinks. Sets the glass down. "Fenwick-7, this rock, your bar, all of it—in roughly eighteen months, the orbital trajectory puts it directly in the wash path of Andromeda's fourth spiral arm."

I hear the words. I process the words. Behind me, a glass slips in Kel's hands and doesn't fall—I hear the catch, the small exhale of relief. At the far booth, Dace laughs at something Maro said. Corrigan signals for another ale with a raised finger and doesn't notice I don't move to pour it.

"The wash path," I repeat, because the term is not unfamiliar, because anyone who lives on the rim has a passing relationship with astrophysics the way anyone who lives on a coast has a passing relationship with tide charts.

"The gravitational shear alone would destabilize the asteroid's rotation," Sable says. "Tidal forces, tectonic stress on a body this small—the station's structural integrity wouldn't survive it. That's not speculation: I've modeled it. I've modeled it three times."

"And you've reported this."

Now something crosses her face that isn't exhaustion. It's harder than that. "I filed my full analysis with Meridian six weeks ago. Detailed report, complete dataset, methodology documented to consortium standards. I followed every protocol."

"And?"

"They buried it." She says it without inflection. Like she's moved past the anger into the flat terrain on the other side of it. "I got a form acknowledgment. No follow-up. I pinged my project lead and got a message saying the report had been '*escalated for internal review*,' which, in Meridian's language, means it's been filed in the place where inconvenient data goes to decompose."

"So you went over their heads."

"I went to the Belt Authority. Filed an independent hazard report. Provided the same data. They told me my methodology was '*under review*' and that they'd be in touch." She almost smiles. "That was four weeks ago. They have not been in touch."

I pick up the rag again. Not to wipe. Just to have something in my hands. "And since then?"

"Since then, I've been to four stations in three weeks. Caris-12. Pemberton. The relay post on Duvall. And now here. At every stop, I've tried to find someone—an administrator, a local authority, anyone with pull or resources—who will listen to what I'm telling them."

"And?"

"And the people who understand the data don't have the authority to act on it. The people who have the authority don't understand the data. And the people who have both—" She stops. Wraps her hand tighter around the glass. "The people who have both don't live out here. The people who have both are on Meridian, in offices with windows that face *inward*, toward the nice systems, and they have never once looked out toward the rim and seen anything worth the budget it would take to save."

The bar hums around us. The soft clink of Corrigan's fingers tapping the counter. The ambient music—I keep it low, an algorithm that plays old Terran jazz and occasionally throws in something Orioni that Kel likes and I've grown to tolerate. Dace's holodeck whirring faintly. The environmental systems cycling. All the small sounds of a place that is functioning, that is alive, that has no idea it's been given an expiration date.

"So here you are," I say.

"Here I am." She leans back slightly. "Sitting in a bar on the rock I'm trying to save, drinking whisky I probably can't afford, talking to a bartender because—" She almost laughs. "Because bartenders listen. That's the thing, isn't it? Everyone else in the chain has a reason not to hear you
