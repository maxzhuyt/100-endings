# Offworlder

## I.

The corridor into Lithane Colony had been cut from the planet's own basalt, and the stone remembered every hand that shaped it. The envoy, Dael Omena, walked with the welcoming delegation through passages where glyphs lined the walls in continuous friezes—Prometheus split open, fire pouring not from his hands but from his ribs. Babel's tower rendered not as ruin but as root system, plunging downward. Demeter, faceless, holding grain that looked like it might be bone.

The reception hall was vast, vaulted, cold. Bioluminescent panels cast everything in blue-amber. The delegates of Lithane stood in formal rows, and Dael saw immediately what the transit briefings had described in careful, clinical language: the divergence was not subtle. Two centuries of isolated adaptation under different gravity, different light spectra, different atmospheric composition had done visible work. The Lithane-born were tall—many well over two meters—narrow through the ribcage, with skin that carried a faint mineral undertone, as though the basalt had made some quiet claim. Their breathing was slow and audible. Dael's own lungs, trained for standard pressure, worked harder here and made a thinner sound.

A woman in a formal wrap spoke words of welcome. Dael answered in protocol language, feeling the shapes of it go wrong in this air, this echo. On the far wall behind the speaker, another glyph: a human figure splitting into two figures, each walking away from the other into featureless dark. No myth Dael recognized. Something local. Something the colony had made for itself.

---

## II.

The reception lasted two more hours. Dael smiled, accepted a drink that tasted of copper and anise, and noted how the other offworld delegates stayed in a loose cluster near the entrance, a reflex formation none of them would have admitted to.

At the second shift of the formal rotations, when the delegation was meant to move as a body to the evening address, Dael turned left instead of right and went through a service door that had been propped open by a child's shoe wedged against the frame.

The corridor beyond was unlit except by the faint phosphorescence of lichen cultivated along the ceiling joints. It led to a stairwell. The stairwell led down.

Outside, the colony opened.

Lithane was not a sealed habitat; it was a canyon settlement, roofed in places by translucent polymer, open to the sky in others. The sky was a dim violet. Two moons sat close to the horizon like a pair of watching eyes. The air hit Dael's face—colder than the reception hall, tasting of mineral dust and something vegetal and sharp, like crushed stems.

The market district ran along the canyon floor. Stalls had been carved directly into the rock face or built from polymer scaffolding draped with woven cloth. Dael walked slowly, hands visible, trying to look like someone with a reason to be here rather than someone studying the place.

The briefings had described Lithane's three recognized morphic groups as functionally separated—the tall, narrow-boned Crest phenotype in governance and science; the denser, broader-set Basin phenotype in extraction and fabrication; the intermediate Terrace phenotype in commerce and services. Clean categories. Administrative convenience.

What Dael saw was different.

At a food stall selling skewered fungi and pressed grain cakes, a Basin woman with forearms like bridge cables was arguing prices with a Crest man whose elongated fingers moved in what Dael slowly realized was a gestural language—not the colony's official trade-sign but something older, faster, more intimate. They were laughing. The woman's daughter, perhaps ten, had the Crest height but her mother's heavy wrists, and she was sketching with a stylus on a polymer slate while eating a grain cake one-handed.

Dael bought a grain cake. It was dense, faintly sweet, and tasted of the copper-anise that seemed to be in everything here.

Further down the market, the mixing became less visible. A fabrication arcade staffed entirely by Basin workers had a Crest overseer standing on a raised platform, watching production through a heads-up display. The workers did not look up. The overseer did not look down at any individual. It was a geometry of authority that needed no signs or barriers—everyone knew where to stand.

An old man sitting against a carved wall noticed Dael and said something in the local dialect. Dael caught only the last word, which was either "tourist" or "ghost"—the phonemes were close. Dael nodded politely. The old man's eyes tracked not Dael's face but Dael's chest, watching the breathing rhythm, counting the frequency. Reading the foreignness in the body's most basic labor.

A child ran past, chasing another child. Both Terrace phenotype, both barefoot on the cold stone. One of them stumbled near Dael and looked up. The child's eyes were wide-set, heavily pigmented, adapted to Lithane's dim light. In those eyes Dael was a pale, strange, small-lunged thing from somewhere incomprehensibly far away. The child scrambled up and ran on without a word.

Near the end of the market row, Dael stopped. A glyph had been painted—not carved, painted, recently—on the wall of a water-reclamation station. It depicted Prometheus again, but this version was different from the reception hall's. Here the figure was not split open. It was being stitched shut by many hands—Basin hands, Crest hands, Terrace hands, and other hands Dael could not identify, hands that might have been meant to represent offworlders or might have been purely imaginary. Beneath it, in the colony's script, a line of text Dael could partially read: *What was taken lives in the taking.*

Dael stood with the grain cake going cold and memorized the image.

---

## III.

The communication was waiting when Dael returned to quarters. Text only, no voice. Command's style.

*Protocol deviation logged. Unescorted movement in a restricted-assessment zone is a Category 2 breach. Note: your observer status does not extend to independent reconnaissance. Reassigning: you will present credentials to Minister Yeven Salke, Sciences Directorate, tomorrow 0900 local. The Minister is aware of your arrival. Background: Salke holds Sciences as a political appointment; her research credentials are administrative, not disciplinary. Approach accordingly. Acknowledge.*

Dael acknowledged. The quarters hummed with recirculated air that was slightly too warm, slightly too humid—calibrated for offworlder lungs, not comfort. *Approach accordingly.* Which meant: be useful. Don't be curious. The briefing on Salke was a single paragraph, mostly titles. As much a gap as a portrait.

---

## IV.

The Sciences Directorate occupied the upper terrace of the eastern canyon wall, where the polymer roofing gave way to raw stone and the corridors narrowed to single-file. The glyphs here were older than those in the reception hall—deeper-cut, less decorative, their edges softened by centuries of hands trailing along the walls. But someone had installed polymer panels over several of them, backlit in official amber, turning the old carvings into display pieces. Museum logic. The myths pinned behind glass.

In the antechamber, Dael waited on a bench carved from a single basalt slab and studied the room. The staff moving through were uniformly Crest phenotype—tall, quiet, their slow respirations synchronized in a way that might have been unconscious or might have been cultivated. No Basin workers. No Terrace intermediaries. On the wall opposite Dael, a glyph of Babel had been partially covered by a directorate seal—the tower's roots obscured, only its upper reaches visible, reframed from descent into ascent. An institutional edit. The story made to point the right direction.

---

## V.

Minister Yeven Salke was shorter than Dael expected—Terrace phenotype, which meant she governed a directorate staffed by people who looked nothing like her. She wore her formal wrap loosely, which Dael had learned from the reception was either a sign of high confidence or deliberate informality. Her office was stone, unadorned except for a single shelf of physical books—an extravagance on a colony where mass was allocated by committee.

"Envoy Omena." She gestured to a chair. "You've had an eventful first day. Sit, please. The chairs were designed for Crest proportions, but we do have cushions somewhere."

"I'm comfortable," Dael said, though the chair was too tall and too deep, and both of them knew it.

Salke sat across a desk made of polymer over stone. Her eyes were unhurried, cataloguing. "Your delegation's mandate is cultural exchange and divergence assessment. Standard language. In your experience, Envoy, does the standard language ever describe the actual work?"

"Rarely. But it describes the boundaries of what I'm allowed to report."

Salke smiled. "Honest. Or performing honesty, which is nearly as useful. Tell me—what is your assessment so far? Of Lithane?"

"I've been here eighteen hours. Twelve of them asleep."

"And six awake, one of which was spent in our market district without escort." Salke's tone did not change. "You must form impressions quickly."

Dael let the silence sit for a moment. Then: "Your reception hall has a glyph of Prometheus with fire coming from his ribs. The market district has a version where he's being stitched up. By many hands."

Salke's expression shifted—not dramatically, but enough. A recalibration behind the eyes. "You read colony script?"

"Poorly. But I read images."

"That particular image," Salke said carefully, "is not an official installation."

"No. It was painted on a water-reclamation station. Recently."

"And what did you understand by it?"

Dael chose the next words with the full knowledge that the wrong ones would close every door in the colony. "That someone here believes what was divided can be repaired. And that the repair requires hands your directorate doesn't currently employ."

The silence this time was longer. Salke's slow Lithane breathing filled it—three of her breaths to every four of Dael's.

"You are young for this posting," Salke said finally.

"Yes."

"Command sent you because you are expendable, or because you are adaptable. Possibly both. They would not have told you which." She opened a drawer in her desk, removed a small polymer case, and set it between them. "Inside is a cultural introduction package. Official materials. Approved readings on Lithane's morphic history, social architecture, scientific programs. Everything in it is true. Do you understand?"

"Everything in it is true," Dael repeated. "And the things not in it are also true."

Salke regarded Dael for a long moment. Whatever she found seemed to neither satisfy nor disappoint her. "Read the materials, Envoy. Attend your scheduled functions. When you have questions—and you will—you may request a meeting through my office. Through official channels." She paused. "The market district is not a safe place for someone who breathes as loudly as you do."

"Is that a warning or a threat, Minister?"

"On Lithane," Salke said, "those are the same word." She stood, signaling the meeting's end. "We have sixty-three morphic terms that don't translate into your language. Threat and warning share a root with *care*. I'd suggest you learn that one first."

Dael took the polymer case. At the door, Salke spoke once more. "The glyph you saw. The one with the hands."

Dael turned.

"The text beneath it. You said you read images, not script. But you paraphrased it accurately." Her expression was closed, evaluating. "Either you are a better linguist than you admit, or someone in the market translated it for you. Both possibilities interest me, Envoy. For different reasons."

Dael said nothing. Salke nodded, as though the silence were answer enough, and looked down at her desk.

---

## VI.

The corridor back was long and dim. Dael walked it slowly, the polymer case heavy under one arm.

Near the stairwell, the old glyphs emerged again from behind their institutional panels—Demeter, faceless, holding her ambiguous grain. In the low light, Dael stopped and looked. The stone was warm to the touch. Two hundred years of hands. Two hundred years of breath slower than Dael's, lungs wider, bodies reshaped by a world that didn't care what name you gave it.

Somewhere below, the market hummed. Dael breathed in, tasting copper, and descended.
