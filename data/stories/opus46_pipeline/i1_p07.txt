# Lost & Found in Osaka

Den Den Town smelled like solder and warm plastic. Maki moved through it the way she always did — headphones on, volume up, eyes tracking the glass cases without stopping. The Korg Minilogue sat in its usual spot at Joshin, third shelf, same price tag she'd already memorized down to the tax. She ran one finger across the display knob. The shopkeeper behind the counter looked up.

"Just looking," she said, already turning.

Outside, a pachinko parlor threw noise like a wall. Neon stuttered against wet pavement — pink, then green, then a white so sharp it flattened the shadows between buildings. A man with a megaphone shouted about figurine discounts. Two tourists blocked the sidewalk taking a photo of a vending machine. Maki stepped around them without adjusting her stride. Her Audio-Technica ATH-M50x cans — matte black, replacement ear pads she'd installed herself, left cup slightly more worn than the right — pressed the world into a manageable frequency. In them, Boards of Canada played at a volume that made Osaka sound like it was happening to someone else.

She turned south toward Namba.

---

The guitar came in on the left side, under the kick drum, where there shouldn't have been a guitar at all.

She knew the melody before she could name it. Four bars of something shaped like the *Cowboy Bebop* opening — "Tank!" — but dragged through a fuzz pedal and pulled apart, the horns translated into bends that had no business working and then snapping, hard, into the main riff, unmistakable now. She stopped walking. Her left hand came up and pulled the headphone cup away from her ear.

The sound was coming from somewhere past the arcade entrance on the corner, raw and loud and alive in a way her headphones never were.

She followed it.

---

The busker played with her eyes closed. That was the first thing. She stood with her feet planted wide, a battered Fender Jaguar slung low across her hips, a small Roland Micro Cube amp buzzing at her feet. Her guitar case lay open on the concrete — a few coins, a couple hundred-yen bills. She wore a denim jacket with the sleeves cut off and her hair was short and bleached at the tips and she moved like the song was happening inside her body and the guitar was just how it got out.

A half-circle of people had gathered. Some watched. Most were passing through, caught for a few seconds before the current of the street pulled them on.

Maki stood at the edge and did not move.

The song ended in a feedback bloom that the busker choked off with her palm against the strings. She opened her eyes — dark, bright, scanning the crowd like she was counting — and they landed on Maki. On Maki, who was standing perfectly still with one headphone pressed to her ear and the other pulled away, mouth slightly open, a plastic bag from Joshin clutched against her chest.

The busker grinned.

"You were singing along," she said.

---

"I wasn't."

"You were. You were doing the horn part with your lips. The little—" The busker mimed a trumpet with one hand, fingers fluttering. "Bah-bah-bah-bah-bah. Nailed it, by the way."

Maki's face went hot. "I didn't — I wasn't trying to—"

"Hey, that's a compliment. Most people just stand there looking like I personally insulted their taste in music." The busker tilted her head, eyes dropping to Maki's headphones. "M50x?"

Maki blinked.

"The cans." The busker pointed with her pick — a thick, triangular Dunlop, bright orange. "Audio-Technica M50x. You swap the pads yourself?"

"The stock ones… they crack after a while. If you sweat. The pleather ones are—"

"Pleather's garbage. You go Brainwavz?"

"Velour."

"Oh, *nice*." The busker nodded slowly, the way someone nods when they've just revised an opinion upward. "Lost some low end, though. Right? Velour breathes, but the bass leaks."

"You get used to — I mean, the soundstage opens up. It's more—" Maki's hand moved involuntarily, drawing a shape in the air. "Everything separates. You can hear the layers."

"Layers," the busker repeated. Something shifted in her expression. She leaned the Jaguar against the amp and stepped closer — not too close, but into the range where conversation stopped being performative and started being actual. "Okay. So. That riff I was playing. You clocked it pretty fast."

"It's 'Tank!,'" Maki said, flat, as if being asked whether water was wet.

"Everyone gets that. What was the thing I did in the bridge?"

Maki hesitated. The headphones slipped during a half-nod and she caught them with her shoulder, pressing them awkwardly against her neck. "You — um. You quoted 'Rush' in the middle. The second Yoko Kanno arrangement, not the first. The one from the movie, not the show."

The busker stared at her.

"The intervals are different," Maki said, quieter now, as if she'd overstepped. "In the show version it resolves up. In the movie it—"

"Resolves down a half-step. Yeah." The busker was smiling now, but differently than before — not performing, just surprised. "Nobody catches that. I've been playing that arrangement for two months and *nobody* catches that."

"It's a good arrangement," Maki said. Then, feeling the sentence was too short to carry what she actually meant: "The fuzz tone — it's not just gain, right? You're rolling the tone knob back on the guitar before it hits the pedal. So it's — it's warm? It sounds like a saxophone pretending to be a guitar pretending to be a saxophone."

The busker laughed, sudden and sharp. "That's exactly what it is. That's literally — okay, who *are* you?"

The headphones slipped again. This time the busker caught the cord before Maki could fumble for it, guiding it back gently like it was a thing that happened between people, and Maki snorted — a small, involuntary sound she immediately tried to cover with her hand.

"Sorry," Maki said. "They don't — they're not good for, like. Moving."

"They're for sitting still and hearing everything."

"Yeah."

"Okay, but you can't just stand there hearing everything and not tell me what else you know." The busker was rocking on her heels now, vibrating with the energy of someone who has just found a frequency she'd been scanning for. "What do you play?"

"I don't — I make stuff. On a laptop. It's not—"

"What software?"

"Ableton, mostly, but—"

"What genre?"

"I don't really — it doesn't have a—"

"Good answer." The headphones had slipped again, resting now in the crook of Maki's neck like a collar, and this time neither of them reached for them. The busker didn't seem to notice. Maki noticed, and chose not to fix it.

---

"You hungry?"

The question came mid-sentence — Maki had been saying something about sidechain compression that, based on the busker's expression, was landing with more impact than she'd expected — and then the busker was already crouching, unlatching her guitar case, fitting the Jaguar inside with practiced speed, scooping the coins and bills into her jacket pocket without counting them.

"There's a kaitenzushi place around the corner," the busker said, snapping the case shut. "Hundred-yen plates. It's good — well, it's fine. The tamago is good."

Maki looked at the small crowd still dispersing. "You were — you still had people."

"They'll live." The busker stood, guitar case in one hand, amp cord already wound and tucked. "I'm Yui, by the way. I feel like I should've said that before I asked about your DAW."

"Maki."

"Maki. Cool." Yui shouldered the case. "So. Sushi?"

Maki looked at her headphones — around her neck now, Boards of Canada paused mid-track, the sounds of Den Den Town filling the space where music usually went. A truck beeped. Someone laughed in the pachinko parlor. Osaka pressed in, unfiltered, louder than she usually let it get.

"Okay," she said. "Yeah."

---

They walked south along the narrow street. Yui's guitar case bumped against her leg with every step, setting a rhythm. Maki matched it without deciding to.

"So you've got a whole Kanno theory," Yui was saying. "Like, *a whole theory*."

"It's not a theory. It's just — she uses different harmonic logic for film than for television. The movie scores assume you're only listening once. So she hides things deeper."

"Deeper," Yui repeated, grinning sideways.

The smell of takoyaki hit them at the corner — batter and dashi and the almost-burnt sweetness of the sauce. A shop sign above them buzzed and flickered, replacing a kanji with a pink absence of light and then bringing it back. Yui turned left. Maki followed.

The restaurant was small and bright and already half-full. They went in.

---

The plates moved past on the belt — yellowish, blue-rimmed, green-tagged — each one a small decision arriving and departing at the same steady speed. Yui grabbed an ika plate without looking, then a salmon, then something Maki didn't recognize. Maki watched. A tamago plate came around the bend and she reached for it with the timing of someone who'd been waiting.

"Okay, but back up," Yui said through a mouthful of rice. "You said you *make stuff*. What does that mean? Are you putting things out? SoundCloud? Bandcamp?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"It's not — finished."

"It's never finished. Music's not finished, it's abandoned." Yui pointed at her with chopsticks. "Kanno would agree with me."

"Kanno wouldn't put out a half-mixed track."

"Kanno has a whole studio. You have a laptop. Different rules."

Maki turned the tamago plate between her hands. "I just want it to sound like what I hear. When I'm—" She paused. A plate of chutoro passed between them, slow and inevitable. "When I'm listening, I hear how everything's supposed to fit. All the layers. And then when I try to make it, the layers won't—"

"They won't sit right."

Maki looked up. "Yeah."

"That's every musician who's ever lived. You just described every musician who's ever lived."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not supposed to be comforting, it's supposed to make you send me a track." Yui pulled her phone from her jacket like the gesture was already decided, already happening, inevitability on a conveyor belt. "What, you think I played a Kanno arrangement in the middle of Den Den Town so I could eat sushi alone?"

Maki laughed. It came out louder than she'd intended — a real laugh, ungoverned, surprising in her own mouth — and Yui's face did something Maki had never seen directed at her before, a kind of delighted recognition, the look of someone who'd pressed a key and heard a note she didn't expect but immediately wanted to hear again.

"You have a good laugh," Yui said. "You should do it more."

"You don't know how much I laugh."

"I've got a theory."

A plate of unagi came around the curve. Yui reached for it. "Okay. Here's what's going to happen. You're going to send me the most unfinished thing on your laptop. The one that makes you cringe. And I'm going to—"

"Why would I—"

"Because I need something to arrange for guitar, and you need someone to tell you your layers are better than you think. Deal?"

The belt hummed. Maki's headphones sat around her neck, quiet, the ear cups resting against her collarbones. Osaka moved past the restaurant window — neon and noise and all the people she'd been walking through and around and away from for as long as she could remember.

"The mixing is really bad," she said.

"I don't care."

"And one of the tracks has this hum I can't—"

"Maki."

"Okay," she said, and reached for a plate.
