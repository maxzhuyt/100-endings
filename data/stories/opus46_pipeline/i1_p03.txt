# Love in the Limelight

The bell above the door hadn't rung like that in years — not with that kind of violence, the clapper striking both sides in quick succession so the sound came out less like a chime and more like a small, brass scream. Elin looked up from the invoice she'd been pretending to read for the better part of twenty minutes and saw a man fall into her shop.

Not walk. Fall. He caught himself on the rotating postcard stand — the one with the watercolour puffins that hadn't rotated properly since 2019 — and it went over with him, scattering views of Tenby harbour across the floor. He was wearing sunglasses indoors, a baseball cap pulled low, and a jacket that cost more than her quarterly rent. Rain had flattened the cap against his forehead and darkened the shoulders of the jacket to the colour of wet slate.

He pushed himself upright, breathing hard, and looked at her.

She looked at the postcards on the floor.

*Oh, I know exactly who you are*, she thought.

"We close at five," she said.

"Right. Yes. Brilliant." He pulled the sunglasses off and immediately seemed to regret it, glancing back through the glass door toward the high street. His eyes were the precise grey-green that had stared out from the poster her university flatmate had Blu-Tacked above the kitchen radiator eight years ago — the one for the submarine film, the one where he'd played the doomed sonar operator. He was taller in person. They always were, or they never were. He was one of the two. "I'm — could I just — is it all right if I just—"

"Browse?"

"Hide."

"From?"

He gestured vaguely toward the door. Through the rain-streaked glass, Elin could make out two figures across the street, one of them adjusting something long-lensed beneath his coat. They had the restless, aimless pacing of people who were very much not from Carmarthen and very much looking for someone.

"You've knocked over the postcards," she said.

"I have." He looked down at them. He looked back at her. Then he dropped into a crouch and started gathering them up, which surprised her — not the act itself but the speed of it, the way he immediately committed to the task as if it were the most important thing he'd do that day. He stacked them neatly, tapped their edges against the floor to align them. "Tenby," he said, reading one. "I shot a film near Tenby. Well. Near Swansea. They said it was near Tenby. Everything in Wales is near everything else, I was told."

"You were told wrong."

"I'm beginning to suspect that." He stood up and set the postcards on her counter with a care that bordered on ceremonial. Then he smiled — the full one, the one from the chat shows, the one that started slow at the left corner and arrived at the right as if it had walked there on purpose. "I'm Callum, by the way."

"I know who you are."

The smile held, but something behind it recalculated. She watched it happen: a tiny flicker, a gear engaging. Most people wouldn't have noticed. Elin noticed. She noticed most things. It was the main occupational hazard of spending nine hours a day in a shop that averaged six customers.

"And you're thoroughly unimpressed," he said. He said it like a joke, but the pitch was a question.

"I'm thoroughly in the middle of an invoice. There's a Welsh history section against the back wall. True crime is by the window, but I wouldn't stand too near the glass if I were you."

He laughed. It was a good laugh — round, warm, slightly louder than the room wanted. "You're right. True crime by the window. Dead giveaway. So to speak." He waited for her to smile. She let him wait. "This is a wonderful shop, honestly. The smell alone — I'd kill for a candle that smelled like this. Old paper and — what is that? Cedar?"

"Damp."

"Damp. Even better. Very atmospheric. Very authentic." He was doing a thing with his hands now, a kind of open-palmed gesture that communicated casual ease so precisely it could only have been practised. He leaned one elbow on the counter, then seemed to sense that was too familiar for a man who'd been in the building for ninety seconds, and straightened again. "I don't suppose you get a lot of internationally recognised fugitives in here."

"We had a man from Llandeilo last week who hadn't returned a library book since 1987. So there's precedent."

"A hardened criminal."

"We don't judge."

He smiled again, but this one was different — smaller, startled. As if she'd landed a jab he hadn't seen coming and he was still feeling out the bruise. She could feel something warm climbing behind her sternum and she ignored it the way she'd ignore a car alarm: acknowledgement without engagement. He was handsome. Fine. Rain was wet. The sky was up. She pulled the invoice closer and underlined a number she'd already underlined.

"You don't get flustered, do you?" he said.

"I'm Welsh. We have centuries of practice in being unmoved by the English arriving uninvited."

"I'm Scottish."

"Then you should know better."

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again. "I genuinely don't know what to do when the charm isn't working."

"Buy a book."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he turned on his heel with a kind of theatrical obedience and walked toward the nearest shelf, which happened to be the poetry section — three tall cases along the east wall where the spines caught the grey afternoon light from the window. She watched him from behind the invoice. He moved through a room the way actors moved through rooms, with a faint awareness of being watched, adjusting his shoulders, finding his light. But the shop was dim and cramped and the shelves were too close together for his frame, and he kept having to turn sideways, which stripped the grace from it. Good.

She went back to her invoice. She'd read the same line four times.

"You have a lot of R.S. Thomas," he called from the stacks.

"We're a bookshop in Wales. It's compulsory."

"I played a poet once. Short film. Went to Sundance. Absolute disaster. I memorised fourteen poems and they cut them all in editing." He was pulling books at random, opening them to their middles the way people did when they weren't really reading, when they were using a bookshelf as a prop for conversation. "The director said my face was doing too much. When I recited. He said the poems didn't need my face."

"Maybe they didn't."

"Oh, they absolutely didn't. Poems don't need anyone's face. That's rather the point of them, isn't it? They're complete on the page. You don't need—" He held up a hand, gestured at himself, at the totality of whatever he was. "All this."

He said *all this* with a kind of wry, performative disgust that was clearly intended to be self-deprecating, but Elin heard the seam in it, the place where the joke was stitched over something that wasn't a joke.

She said nothing. She let the silence hold. The rain ticked against the window.

He pulled out a volume of Dylan Thomas — *Collected Poems*, the Everyman edition with the cracked blue spine — and flipped to a page. "'Do not go gentle,'" he read aloud, and his voice changed. It was involuntary, she thought. An actor's reflex. The words came out rounder, more resonant, and she could hear the training in them, the breath support, the precise placement — and also, beneath that, something that sounded like genuine feeling trying to claw its way through a decade of technique. He read four lines and stopped.

"My father loved that poem," he said. Then, quickly: "Everyone's father loves that poem. That's the thing about it. It's universal. Which is a polite way of saying it's a cliché, which is an impolite way of saying it's so true it embarrasses people."

He closed the book. She watched his thumb press against the spine.

"He died two years ago," he said. "My father. And I didn't go home for it. I was shooting the — the thing. The franchise thing. They built a whole set around my schedule and there were four hundred people waiting and the producer said they could give me three days, and the funeral was in Aberdeen, and the flights were — it doesn't matter, actually. The logistics. The point is I had a choice and I chose the wrong thing and I've been very professionally charming about it ever since."

He looked up from the book and his face was not doing too much. His face, for the first time since he'd fallen through her door, was doing almost nothing at all.

Elin set the invoice down.

Her shop was very quiet. It was always quiet — it was the first thing people commented on when they came in and the last thing she thought about when she locked up at night, the particular quality of the silence in a room full of books, a silence that felt *populated* — but right now the quiet seemed to have pulled itself tight around the two of them, holding everything still.

"I'll put the kettle on," she said.

It came out softer than she meant it to.

She turned toward the back room before he could read anything in her face, busying herself with the electric kettle and two mugs — the blue one with the chipped handle for herself and the one with the faded Ordnance Survey map of the Brecon Beacons, because it was the only one without a stain. She stood in the small back corridor with her hand on the kettle's base and felt the warmth of it seep into her palm and thought: *That was real. That was a real thing he said to a stranger in a bookshop and I don't know what to do with it.*

She brought the tea out without asking how he took it. She'd made it with milk, because he looked like a man who took milk, and if she was wrong he could say so.

He was sitting on the floor.

He'd folded himself down between Poetry and Welsh History, his long legs bent at the knee, the Dylan Thomas still in his hands. He looked up when she came around the shelf and his expression rearranged itself rapidly — he almost got the charm back in place, almost rebuilt the scaffolding, but not quite, not entirely, and the incomplete reconstruction was more disarming than either the mask or the absence of it.

"I've just told a stranger about the worst thing I've ever done," he said. "In her own bookshop. Which I broke into."

"You didn't break in. The door was open."

"I broke the postcard stand."

"It was already broken." She held out the Brecon Beacons mug. "Milk. If that's wrong, it's what you're getting."

He took it. His fingers overlapped hers for a half-second on the ceramic and she stepped back, not quickly but deliberately, and sat against the opposite shelf. The aisle between them was perhaps three feet wide. She could smell the rain on his jacket. Close up, she could see that his jaw had a small scar along the left side — the kind of scar makeup artists cover for cameras and no one covers for real life.

"You shouldn't feel bad telling a stranger," she said. "I'm very easy to tell things to. People come in here and confide in me about their marriages, their finances, their terrible reading habits. I had a woman last month tell me she'd never finished a book in her life. She was buying one for her sister. She cried."

"Why did she cry?"

"She wanted to like reading. She thought something was wrong with her because she didn't."

"Is something wrong with her?"

"Nothing's wrong with anyone who's honest about what they can't do."

He looked at her over the rim of the mug. The look lasted a beat longer than it should have, or exactly as long as it should have. She couldn't tell.

"What's your name?" he asked.

"Elin."

"Elin." He said it carefully, giving both syllables their weight, not anglicising it. "Am I saying that right?"

"Close enough."

"Elin who runs a bookshop in Carmarthen and is unmoved by celebrities and makes very good tea for terrible people."

"You're not a terrible person."

"I might be. You've known me eight minutes."

"Twelve. And you picked up the postcards. Terrible people don't pick up postcards."

He laughed — not the big one from before, not the chat-show laugh. This one was quiet and had a crack in it, like a clay pot that would still hold water. He set the mug down on the floor beside him and turned the Dylan Thomas over in his hands.

"I should probably buy this," he said.

"You should. It's a good edition. And you clearly need it more than my shelf does."

"Because of the father thing?"

"Because you read four lines aloud to a stranger and didn't perform them. You just said them. Which means somewhere underneath all — " She gestured at him, borrowing his own wave from earlier, the sweep of the hand that meant *all this*. "There's someone who actually reads."

He caught the echo. She saw it land — the way his eyes narrowed slightly, the way his mouth opened a fraction as if he'd been about to speak and the words had backed up behind his teeth. He looked at her the way people look at a line in a book they want to underline: with a focused, almost startled attention.

"You're quite devastating," he said. "You realise that."

"I'm quite sitting in my own shop drinking my own tea."

"That's what I mean."

The rain had softened. She could hear it tapering against the window, the rhythm loosening, the sky deciding to relent. Through the shelves, through the gap between Welsh History and Mythology, she could see the high street brightening by degrees, the wet pavement starting to catch light.

His phone buzzed in his jacket pocket. He pulled it out, glanced at it, and his face did the thing again — the mask reassembling, the public self clicking into place like a visor. It pained her to watch. Not because the mask was false, exactly, but because the transition was so practised it must have been performed ten thousand times, and each time it must have cost something too small to name and too large to ignore.

"My handler," he said. "Manager. Whatever. They've got a car at the end of the street."

"Then you should go."

"Yes." He didn't move. He turned the Dylan Thomas over in his hands one more time, then held it out to her. "How much?"

"Eight fifty."

He reached for his wallet, then stopped. "I don't have cash. I don't think I've had cash since 2020. Nobody in London — do you take—"

"Card machine's broken."

"Of course it is."

"Has been since Thursday."

"Naturally."

They looked at each other across three feet of bookshop aisle, and the silence between them was not the silence of strangers. It was the newer, stranger silence of two people who had said too much and not nearly enough and both knew it.

"I could hold it for you," Elin said. "Behind the counter. If you wanted to come back for it."

"Hold it."

"It's what we do. Customer puts a deposit of good faith. We hold the item. They come back."

"A deposit of good faith."

"It's a system."

"It's a very convenient system."

"It's a very old shop."

He stood up slowly, unfolding himself from the floor, and she stood too, and for a moment they were both standing in the narrow aisle with the shelves pressing in on either side and there was nowhere to look but at each other. She could see the exact moment he thought about saying something large — something that would match the scale of what he'd told her about his father, something that would acknowledge the strange, compressed intimacy of the last quarter hour — and she could see the moment he decided not to. Which was the right decision. Anything too large would have broken the container.

"I'll come back," he said.

"We close at five."

"Tomorrow?"

"We're open at nine. Except Sundays. And bank holidays. And sometimes Tuesdays if the mood takes me."

"A rigorous schedule."

"It's a system."

He smiled. It was the third kind of smile he'd shown her — not the public one, not the startled one, but something unfinished. A sketch rather than a painting. He passed her in the aisle and their shoulders didn't touch but the space between them grew warm and then he was at the door, pulling his cap down, putting the absurd sunglasses back on. The disguise that disguised nothing.

He opened the door. The bell rang, gentler this time. The rain had nearly stopped. The high street smelled of wet stone and the river.
