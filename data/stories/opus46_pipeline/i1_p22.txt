# Prison Strings

---

The light above her was dying in stutters.

Not the clean flicker of a bulb about to go—nothing in this place was clean. It pulsed at intervals she couldn't predict, a sick arrhythmic thing, and each time it guttered the corridor stretched longer in the dark before snapping back to its concrete dimensions. Jolyne Cujoh walked with her hands loose at her sides and her shoulders drawn in just enough to not brush the walls. The walls were too close. The ceiling was too low. The whole facility had been built by someone who understood that architecture could be a throat, and this hallway was the swallow.

She didn't know what the place was called. The transfer paperwork had listed a string of numbers and a jurisdiction that didn't correspond to any state she'd heard of. The guards wore no insignia. The cells she'd passed coming in held things she'd trained herself not to look at directly—shapes that pressed against observation windows with too many limbs, sounds that came from mouths she couldn't locate, one chamber that was simply cold, so profoundly cold that frost had crept across the corridor floor in a perfect circle around its sealed door, and the guard escorting her had stepped over the frost line without comment, without breaking stride, the way you'd step over a crack in a sidewalk.

Not Green Dolphin Street. She'd have taken Green Dolphin Street with gratitude. She'd have taken the swamp and the gators and the Stand users trying to kill her every third Tuesday. At least there she'd understood the grammar of the danger.

The light stuttered again. In the half-dark she almost walked past the cell.

It was the stillness that caught her. In a corridor of restless, writhing, howling containment, this cell held a woman sitting on the edge of a cot with the composure of someone waiting for a train she knew would come on time. Auburn hair, clean and soft-looking, falling straight past her shoulders—no prison had hair like that, no one in a place like this should look like they'd just left a salon. Her hands were folded in her lap. Her posture was perfect, the kind of perfection that wasn't disciplined but innate, as though her spine had never considered slouching as an available option.

Jolyne's jaw tightened. She felt it before she thought it—the mandibular clench, the back teeth pressing, the way her body had learned in Green Dolphin to register wrongness faster than her brain could name it. At her right wrist, beneath the cuff of her sleeve, a single thread of Stone Free twitched. Not unraveled. Not deployed. Just—alert.

The woman turned her head.

Yellow eyes. Not hazel, not amber—yellow, the color of something that hunted in low light, ringed with concentric circles that radiated out from the pupils like the growth rings of a tree or the ripples from a stone dropped into still water. The eyes caught the dying fluorescent light and held it longer than they should have.

She smiled.

It was a good smile. Warm, even. The kind of smile that said *I've been hoping someone interesting would walk by.* The kind of smile that had nothing to do with the eyes above it.

The cell door was open.

Jolyne saw it without stopping, the way you see a wasp on a doorframe—peripherally, completely, the information entering the body as voltage rather than thought. The lock mechanism hung loose. The door sat two inches ajar. The woman hadn't left.

She kept walking. The light stuttered behind her, and for a moment her shadow stretched impossibly long down the corridor ahead, thin and trembling, before the bulb caught again and pinned it back to her feet.

---

She heard the voice before she reached the meal line the next morning.

"Jolyne Cujoh."

It wasn't a question. It came from behind her and slightly to the left, spoken at the exact volume that would reach her ears and no further, as though the woman had calibrated the sound against the acoustics of the concrete cafeteria. Jolyne turned with her tray—gray plastic, gray food, a meal designed by someone who understood that even sustenance could be made joyless—and found Makima already seated at the table nearest the wall, her own tray untouched, her hands folded just as they had been in the cell. The same posture. The same clean hair. The same smile.

"How the hell do you know my name?"

"It's not a large facility," Makima said. "And you arrived with some noise."

"I arrived in a blacked-out van with a sack on my head."

"Yes," Makima said. "But the van had engine trouble on the approach road. Misfiring cylinder. I could hear it from my cell." She tilted her head slightly. "You have a lovely tattoo. The butterfly, on your arm—is it a monarch or a painted lady? I've always found it difficult to tell without seeing the underwing."

Jolyne didn't sit down. "Who are you?"

"Makima. Just Makima. Sit, please—the seats fill quickly, and they don't give us many options."

The cafeteria was almost empty. There were maybe eight occupied tables, and more than thirty open ones. Jolyne looked at the empty chairs, looked at Makima, and felt the specific brand of irritation she reserved for people who lied about things that were too small to lie about.

She sat. Not across from Makima—at the adjacent seat, ninety degrees offset, so she could keep the room in her peripheral vision and Makima in direct line of sight. Prison rules. Never give your back, never face only one direction, and never let someone choose your sightline for you.

"It's a stone-free butterfly," Jolyne said flatly. "Not that it's your business."

"Stone free. What a beautiful phrase. Freedom as a material, not a condition. Is that what it means to you?"

"It means mind your own goddamn business, is what it means."

Makima laughed. It was a real laugh, or the most perfect simulation of one Jolyne had ever heard—light, brief, the kind of laugh that made you want to say something funny again just to hear it. Jolyne's fingers tightened on her fork.

"You're very direct," Makima said. "I like that. Most people in facilities like this learn to speak in circles. They develop a whole vocabulary of indirection—requests that sound like observations, threats that sound like questions. You just say the thing."

"And you do the other thing."

Makima's smile didn't waver. "What other thing?"

"The thing where you talk like you're complimenting me but you're actually running inventory. Checking what I react to. Seeing which buttons light up." Jolyne bit into something that might have been bread. "I did three years in Green Dolphin Street with women who'd been conning people since they could talk. You want to profile me, lady, at least don't make it feel like a first date."

Something moved in Makima's expression—not a change, exactly, but a deepening, the way a lake looks the same on the surface when a current shifts beneath. Her gaze dropped, just for a moment, to Jolyne's right wrist. To the place where, beneath the sleeve, a thread of Stone Free had extended half an inch without Jolyne's instruction—reaching, hovering, tasting the air between them.

Jolyne hadn't noticed it. Makima had.

"You're right," Makima said. "I apologize. That was rude of me." She picked up her fork and ate a single bite, chewing with a delicacy that seemed anatomically impossible given what was on the tray. "I've been here a long time, Jolyne. I forget how to talk to people who haven't learned to perform. It's refreshing, truly. I'll try to be more honest."

"Great. Start by telling me why your cell's unlocked."

Makima set down her fork. She looked at Jolyne with those ringed yellow eyes, and for a moment the smile was gone—not replaced by hostility, not replaced by anything, just absent, the face beneath the face, neutral and vast as a field seen from high altitude.

"Because there's nowhere for me to go," she said.

The fluorescent light above the cafeteria table hummed. The thread at Jolyne's wrist retracted, slowly, on its own, and she felt it go—felt the whisper of it sliding back beneath her skin—and realized only then that it had been out at all.

---

Three days. Three encounters. Each one inevitable.

The first: the recreation corridor, a stretch of concrete with barred windows that admitted a light too gray to cast shadows. Jolyne was doing pull-ups on a pipe that ran along the ceiling—the facility didn't have a gym, but it had exposed plumbing, and she'd always been resourceful. Makima was there already, seated on the floor against the far wall, reading a book whose cover Jolyne couldn't make out. She didn't look up when Jolyne entered. She didn't need to.

"Your father," Makima said, turning a page. "Jotaro Kujo. He visited you once at Green Dolphin Street, at the very beginning. Then never again."

Jolyne's grip slipped. She caught herself, hung, arms burning.

"How do you—"

"He left something for you, didn't he? A gift. An ability." Page turn. "And then he left."

The second: the showers. A tiled room with drains that gurgled like throats. Jolyne had checked every stall, found them empty, turned on the water—and heard Makima's voice from the other side of the tiled partition, already mid-sentence, as though they'd been in conversation for hours.

"—loyalty is such a strange instinct. The people who hurt us most are the ones we'd die for. Don't you find that fascinating? Your mother, for instance. She tried so hard. But you blamed her anyway, didn't you? For being the one who stayed."

Jolyne punched the tile. Her knuckles split. Stone Free's threads laced automatically over the wound, stitching skin, and she watched them work and said nothing.

"I'm not judging," Makima continued. "It's natural. We don't resent the people who abandon us—we resent the ones who remain, because they're the proof that staying was possible. That the one who left *chose* to leave."

The third: back in the cafeteria, at the same table, as if the seating had been ordained. Makima slid a photograph across the surface—old, creased, a polaroid. Jotaro Kujo, fifteen years younger, standing on a beach Jolyne didn't recognize. He was almost smiling.

"Where did you get this?"

"I know a great many things about a great many people, Jolyne. It's the nature of what I am."

"And what are you?"

Makima retrieved the photograph with two fingers, slipping it into a pocket that shouldn't have existed in the facility-issue uniform.

"What would you sacrifice," she asked, "to have him look at you the way he's looking at that horizon?"

And Jolyne said nothing, because the answer was everything, and they both knew it, and the knowing was a sound like a lock engaging.

*She's circling me.* The thought arrived not as discovery but as confirmation—something her body had known since the first flicker of that corridor light, something her mind was only now authorizing itself to admit. *She's not having conversations. She's drawing a perimeter. And each time around, the circle gets smaller.*

---

She woke in the dark and felt the strings.

Not at her wrist—*everywhere.* Stone Free had partially unraveled in her sleep, threads extending from her forearms, her shoulders, the place just below her collarbone where her heartbeat ran closest to the surface. They trailed across the cot, over the edge, across the cell floor, and pointed—all of them, every silken filament, aligned like compass needles—toward the door. Toward the corridor. Toward Makima's open cell, three doors down on the opposite side.

Jolyne sat up and the movement pulled the threads taut and she felt it in her chest—a tension that wasn't pain, wasn't desire, was something older and worse than both. A leaning. A gravity. The feeling of standing at the edge of a high place and understanding, with sickening clarity, that part of you wanted to step forward.

She looked at her hands. The strings were gossamer-thin, almost invisible in the dark, but she could feel each one the way you feel a loose tooth, the way you feel a word you've almost remembered—a presence defined by the ache of its incompleteness.

*What is this.*

She knew what it was.

She'd fought Stand users who attacked the body. She'd fought Stand users who attacked the mind. But Makima wasn't doing either. Makima was—what? Sitting in a cell with an open door, speaking softly, noticing things, asking questions that landed like fishhooks in the part of Jolyne that still flinched when she heard the word *father*, the part that had saved every one of her mother's letters and read them in order and then reversed order and then random order looking for the sentence that would explain why she felt, always, like something left behind on a platform while the train pulled away.

The strings weren't reaching for Makima as a person. They were reaching for what Makima offered: the intoxicating, narcotic promise of being *known.* Of sitting across from someone who saw every broken piece and said *I understand* and meant it, or seemed to mean it, and what was the difference when you were this tired?

Jolyne ground her teeth until her jaw ached. She thought of her father's back, always his back, always walking away, coat trailing behind him like the memory of something that had never quite arrived. She thought of the way Makima tilted her head when she listened—a forty-five-degree incline, warm and patient, the exact angle that said *you are the most important thing in this room.* She thought of how long it had been since anyone had looked at her like that.

*She doesn't even need chains. She just needs you to be lonely enough.*

The thought was so clear and so cruel that Jolyne made a sound—not a word, not a scream, something between a laugh and a gag, the noise the body makes when it recognizes its own trap from the inside. She grabbed the strings. Physically, with her left hand, she gathered the threads that were trailing toward the door and she pulled. They resisted. Not with force—with reluctance, the way a child resists being led away from a window display, not fighting but *dragging,* making the effort of departure heavier than it needed to be.

She pulled harder. The threads came back. She watched them retract into her forearm, felt them rejoin the weave of Stone Free beneath her skin, each one a small bright sting like a suture taken in reverse. She wound them tight. She wound them until her arm ached, until it felt like she was wearing her own Stand as a tourniquet, until every string was accounted for and the only thing extending from her body was her body itself—skin, bone, the ordinary impermeable envelope of a human being.

Her arm throbbed. The phantom pull remained, fainter, a tidal suggestion.

She did not sleep again.

---

In the morning she went to the cell.

Not after breakfast, not en route to somewhere else—she went directly from her own cell to Makima's, walking the corridor under the dying light with a stride that was too fast for casual and too slow for attack, the stride of someone who has made a decision they haven't finished making. The concrete was cold under her bare feet. She'd forgotten shoes, or hadn't cared, and now the cold was grounding, a small blunt fact that kept her in her body and out of the drift.

The door was open. Two inches. Same as the first night.

She pushed it wide and stepped inside.

The cell was identical to hers in dimensions—eight by ten, concrete floor, concrete walls, a cot bolted to the floor, a toilet and sink unit in stainless steel—but it felt different. Smaller. The air was heavy, still, like the air in a room where a window hasn't been opened in years, where breath and exhalation have accumulated into a private atmosphere. It smelled faintly of—Jolyne searched for it—paper. Old paper, the sweet vanilla rot of books decomposing. There were no books visible.

Makima sat on the cot. Legs crossed at the ankle, hands folded, posture perfect. She was braiding something—a small, complex knot of what looked like dark thread, working it between her fingers with a dexterity that was almost meditative.
