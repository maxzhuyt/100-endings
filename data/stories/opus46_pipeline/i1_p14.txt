# Breaking Formation

## I.

The clock on the west wall read 14:07. Fifty-three minutes left.

I was standing wrong. I knew it the way you know a word is misspelled before you can name the rule—some misalignment between what I felt and what the mirror confirmed, that wall of glass running the full north side of Studio B, twenty-two feet of reflection that turned one room into two and made every error bilateral. In the mirror, twenty-three bodies stood in a six-by-four grid with a gap. I was the gap's neighbor: third row, second from the left, slotted between Minjun and a girl whose name I kept losing to the hangul on the attendance sheets. Minjun breathed in counts of eight even at rest. The girl had her chin raised at exactly zero degrees from horizontal, as though she'd been leveled with an instrument.

My chin was tilted three degrees left and maybe one degree up. I'd been told about it twice during the first week and once on video review. It read as defiance. It was just the angle from which I'd learned to watch—ciphers are circles, not mirrors, and in a circle you watch from the corner of your eye, chin turned toward the next dancer's chest so you can catch the moment their weight commits.

Here there was nothing to catch. The choreography had been distributed seventy-two hours ago via a shared drive: a three-minute, forty-two-second piece set to a track I'd been told not to name outside the building, pre-release, property of the label. The formation chart showed my position as a small circle with the number 19 inside it. All the circles were the same size.

Park Sunwoo stood at the front of the room, centered between the first row's middle pair, spine like a surveyor's stake driven into the hardwood. She was forty-one. She had choreographed eleven title tracks that I knew of and probably others I didn't, and she stood with her feet in a narrow parallel that made her body a single vertical line from crown to heel. No weight favoring either leg. No settling into a hip. The posture said: *I am not resting. I am between movements.*

She touched the speaker remote on her lanyard. "From the top of B section. Count of four."

Around me the grid consolidated. Twenty-three sets of shoulders squared to the mirror. Twenty-three pelvises leveled, ribs drawn in, cores pulled tight along the vertical axis. I squared. I leveled. I drew in. My body assembled itself into the shape of readiness the way it had been shown: feet hip-width, weight centered over the balls, arms soft at the sides, fingers closed but unclenched.

But my weight kept sinking into my left hip. A millimeter. Maybe two. The habit of standing loose, of keeping a spring-loaded asymmetry so the body could fire in any direction. In a cipher that readiness was survival. Here it was drift. In the mirror, that two-millimeter settlement made me look like a picture hung crooked on a wall of straight ones.

Park Sunwoo counted: "Five, six, seven—"

---

## II.

The track hit at eight.

B section opened with a chest isolation on count one—a sharp, percussive contraction of the sternum forward while the rest of the body stayed locked, as though the ribcage had been struck from behind by something small and precise. On count two, the arms came up: right arm to ninety degrees from the torso, locked at the elbow, fingers aimed at the mirror; left arm pinned across the chest at a diagonal, forearm flat, wrist dead straight. Count three was the freeze—every joint held, no breath visible, the body a structure instead of an organism.

I hit one. My chest punched forward. But it didn't punch—it *rippled.* The contraction started in my sternum and traveled, a wave rolling upward through the clavicles, reaching the shoulders a sixteenth-beat late, carrying the ghost of a body roll that belonged to a different room, a different music, a different me who'd learned to move in a park in Inglewood where the concrete had a crack that served as the circle's center line. In that park, a chest hit that didn't travel was dead. A hit that didn't *breathe* was mechanical, and mechanical was the worst thing a body could be.

Here, mechanical was the point.

On count two my right arm swung to its position, but my wrist opened at the apex—a fractional flare, a finger bloom, the kind of punctuation that in waacking or even in freestyle popping gave movement its signature, its *voice*. The arm was supposed to arrive like a beam of wood set into place. Mine arrived like a hand reaching for something.

Count three. I froze. But I was already a half-count behind the grid, because the ripple and the flare had eaten time, and in the mirror I could see it—twenty-two bodies locked in identical architecture, and one body arriving at the shape like a late guest trying to slip into a photograph.

The music cut.

Park Sunwoo hadn't moved. She didn't need to. Her hand was on the lanyard remote, thumb resting on the pause, and she was looking at the mirror, which meant she was looking at all of us, which meant she was looking at me.

"Nineteen."

The number landed in the silence like a coin on tile.

"The chest hit is a chest hit. It is not a roll. It does not have a sequel. It occurs on one and it is finished on one. If your body cannot distinguish between an isolation and a wave, you are not performing this choreography—you are performing a memory of something else."

She said this in Korean. I understood it two seconds after she said it, the delay a subtitled lag that I'd been living inside for eleven weeks now, every sentence arriving first as sound and then as meaning, the gap between them large enough to be lonely in.

"Reset."

The grid reset. Twenty-two bodies dropped their arms, rolled their shoulders once—mechanically, precisely, a programmed release—and resumed ready position. I reset. My arms dropped. My shoulders rolled. The roll was too loose, carried too much swing, and I caught myself and killed the swing but not before it had already happened, not before the mirror had seen it.

Minjun, beside me, had already returned to perfect zero. His reset was indistinguishable from his ready position was indistinguishable from his rest position. He was a body that had been formatted.

Park Sunwoo counted again. "Five, six, seven—"

I bit the inside of my cheek and tried to think in straight lines.

---

## III.

Water break at 14:19. Eight minutes gone. Forty-one minutes left.

We were allowed ninety seconds. I walked to the east wall where my bag sat, a black duffel with a broken zipper that I kept closed with a binder clip. The other bags on the wall were uniform—the company-issued Samsonite trainee kits, charcoal gray, each one containing a labeled water bottle, a microfiber towel, a foam roller the exact diameter of a regulation softball. I hadn't been issued a kit. My scholarship letter, which I kept folded in the duffel's inner pocket and which I'd read so many times the creases had gone translucent, said that material provisions would be *allocated upon satisfactory completion of the probationary evaluation period.* The probationary evaluation period ended in forty-one minutes. Or whenever Park Sunwoo decided it ended. The letter was not specific.

I drank from a CamelBak I'd brought from home. Home. LA. Specific: the intersection of Eucalyptus and Crenshaw, the parking lot behind the church where Dion first showed me a glide, my feet on asphalt that was still warm at 10 PM in August. The memory wasn't sentimental—it was *kinetic.* I could feel it in my soles: the grip of rubber on rough ground, the way a back-glide required you to trust the friction, to push weight into the standing foot while the sliding foot stayed light enough to skim, a negotiation between gravity and surface that you learned with your skin, not your mind.

Here the floor was sprung hardwood, Harlequin performance grade, and my feet in their indoor trainers—Nikes, old, the left sole starting to separate at the toe, because the stipend covered housing and meals but not shoes—felt like they were floating over the surface without ever arriving at it. The floor was *too good.* It forgave everything. It didn't push back.

I watched the other trainees hydrate. There was a quality to the way they drank—upright, controlled, the water bottle raised and lowered with a kind of unconscious formality, as though even thirst was a movement to be executed cleanly. Their skin was uniform in the way that a skincare regime begun at thirteen could make it—poreless, taut over cheekbones that caught the studio's fluorescent light in planes. They wore matching training sets, black compression tops and joggers from a brand I couldn't afford, and their bodies at rest held the invisible scaffolding of years of posture work—ballet, jazz, rhythmic gymnastics. The training pipeline started in middle school, sometimes earlier. Their muscles had been built inside institutions. Every tendon knew what a mirror expected.

My muscles had been built in ciphers and basements and once in a Circle K parking lot at two in the morning in Hawthorne with a Bluetooth speaker propped on a shopping cart. I'd been good. I need to say that without false modesty because it's the only thing I have in this room that functions as credit: I was *good.* In 2019 I battled a guy from Seoul—Structure Crew, I think, or maybe Fusion MC—at a jam in Long Beach and the crowd called it for me, and a woman in the crowd turned out to be a recruiter who turned out to be the reason I was standing in this studio on a scholarship that was not a guarantee, in a building where my talent was acknowledged the way a doctor acknowledges a fever: *yes, it's real. Now let's treat it.*

I screwed the cap back on the CamelBak. In the mirror, I could see my own posture at rest: left hip dropped, right shoulder slightly forward, head tilted at that three-degree cant. My body at rest told the story of every circle I'd ever stood in, waiting for the beat to open a door. The other bodies at rest told the story of every studio they'd stood in, waiting for the count.

*These people were built for this,* I thought, and then the rest of the thought completed itself without my permission: *I was rebuilt from something else.* Rebuilt, and maybe rebuilt wrong, the way a house framed for one climate will warp when you ship it to another.

Park Sunwoo said, "Time."

Ninety seconds. I hadn't felt them leave.

---

## IV.

We ran the B section three more times. I suppressed the ripple twice and couldn't suppress it once. Each time, my count-three freeze arrived closer to on time but not on time, the way an asymptote approaches a line it will never touch.

At 14:31, Park Sunwoo left her position at the front of the room.

This was unusual enough that I felt the grid notice—a micro-tension, the collective awareness of twenty-three bodies registering that the reference point had moved. She walked through the formation the way a bishop moves through chess: diagonal, purposeful, the bodies around her staying fixed as she passed between them. She adjusted someone in the first row—pressed two fingers against a shoulder blade, said a word I didn't catch, moved on. She paused behind Minjun—said nothing, which I understood was approval.

Then she stopped beside me. Not behind me, not in front. Beside and slightly forward: close enough that if I extended my right arm I would touch her shoulder. Close enough that in the mirror we were in the same frame, and the difference between her stillness and my almost-stillness was legible in the way that two similar paint samples are only distinguishable when placed side by side.

"Your musicality is extraordinary," she said.

She said it at conversational volume. The room heard it.

"You hear subdivisions that some of these trainees will train for years and never feel. You hear the space between the snare and the kick and your body wants to live inside that space. That is a genuine ability and I am not dismissing it."

She turned her head and looked at me directly, not through the mirror. The shift was violent in its intimacy—like a painting breaking the fourth wall, suddenly dimensional.

"But what you call musicality, this industry calls indulgence. The body that wants to interpret will never be the body that sells eleven million units in a synchronized global campaign. You are not here to *hear* the music. You are here to *serve* it. There is a difference and you do not yet understand that the difference is not a reduction. It is a discipline."

She reached out and placed two fingers on my right shoulder—the forward one, the one that crept ahead of the left because my body had been trained to blade sideways, to present a smaller target to a battle opponent, to be ready to redirect.

She pressed the shoulder back. One inch. My frame squared. In the mirror, I became one degree closer to the grid.

"You have more raw facility than anyone in this room. And it is *useless* to me as it is. I need you to understand that I am not asking you to become less. I am asking you to become *this* first, so that later what you are can be used. But you cannot skip the erasure. No one can."

She held my shoulder for another half-second, then released it. The heat of her fingers stayed on the deltoid like a brand.

She walked back to the front of the room. My shoulder crept forward half an inch within four seconds. I caught it and pushed it back. It crept forward again. The habit was hydraulic—I could dam it, but the pressure just built behind the dam and found another route. My body didn't know how to stand square. My body knew how to stand *ready,* and ready was never square.

She'd been right about something, and I could feel the rightness like a stone in my shoe—uncomfortable and impossible to ignore. The subdivisions. The space between the snare and the kick. I *did* live there. That space was where the groove breathed, where a body in a cipher could hang for a sixteenth note and make the crowd lean forward. But that space was a gap in the choreography, an error in the grid, and living there made me a syncopation in a system that had been engineered to eliminate syncopation.

She had seen me. That was the worst part. Being invisible I could have survived. Being seen—specifically and accurately, my talent named and then *diagnosed*—left me nowhere to hide. She hadn't said I was bad. She'd said I was *wrong,* which was more precise and more lethal, because wrong could be corrected, and correction was the thing I was supposed to want.

## V.

14:38. Twenty-two minutes.

Park Sunwoo called the C section, the transition into the final chorus block. This was the sequence that had broken me in the practice room at 1 AM two nights ago, the one I'd drilled alone until the security guard knocked on the glass.

It ran sixteen counts. I'll describe it because the description is the point—because what happened to me inside these sixteen counts is the only thing I have to offer as evidence that a body can be a country forced to adopt a foreign constitution.

Count one: drop. Both knees bend simultaneously, center of gravity sinks six inches, arms pull in to the chest, fists closed. This is a compression—the body becomes a spring being loaded. Count two: explode upward, arms thrown to full extension overhead, fingers splayed, back arched exactly five degrees—not six, not four. The arch was precise because the camera angle for the eventual performance would be low, and five degrees of arch at this height read as power on a wide lens. I'd been told this. The choreography was designed for an audience I hadn't met yet, an audience that would experience it as a two-dimensional image, and the three-dimensional body had to be shaped in service of that flattening. Counts three and four: the arms came down in a controlled fall—right arm sweeping clockwise, left arm sweeping counter, both arriving at waist level simultaneously, palms down, a bilateral symmetry that if you watched from the front made the dancer look like a machine folding, a geometric collapse.

Count five was the kill shot. A full-body freeze in the low position, weight dropped into the heels, every joint locked, face neutral, eyes to the mirror. The freeze had to be *dead*—no residual oscillation, no micro-adjustments, no breath visible in the shoulders. They called this *jukda* in the studio, which I'd learned meant "to die." You died on count five so that count six could resurrect you.
