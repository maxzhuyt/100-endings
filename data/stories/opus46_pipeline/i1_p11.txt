# Overthinking It

The projector squeaks.

It's the old one, the one Mr. Kessler wheels out on the steel cart because the ceiling-mounted Epson died in October and the school district apparently operates on a procurement timeline designed by people who have never met a teenager, never stood in front of thirty-one of them and tried to project a legible spreadsheet onto a wall using a machine that sounds like a small animal being stepped on. The squeak comes from the left wheel. Or the right wheel. Or from inside the projector itself, some plastic gear worn to a nub, crying out at the indignity of continued service. It doesn't matter. What matters is that the squeak happens at the exact moment Mr. Kessler advances to the slide that reads **LAB PARTNERS — SEMESTER PROJECT** and my name is third on the list, right there in Calibri Bold, and next to it, after the hyphen, after the small typographical abyss of that hyphen, is his.

Ethan Russo.

Desks are already moving. The classroom rearranges itself with the chaotic efficiency of an organism that doesn't care about me specifically — chairs scraping, backpacks thudding, someone laughing about something unrelated, the whole room in motion like a shaken snow globe — and I am sitting here, still, experiencing what I can only describe as a full-system diagnostic failure. Every circuit lit up. Every alarm sounding at once, not because something has gone wrong, but because something might be about to, and my body cannot tell the difference between those two things and has never been able to, not once, not even one time in sixteen years of being this particular arrangement of nerves.

I don't move yet.

I am calculating.

---

Okay. I will need to walk to his table. His table is four rows to the left and two seats forward, which is approximately eleven feet of open classroom between here and there, which is approximately six seconds of walking if I walk normally, which I will not, because I have never in my life walked normally toward someone I—

No. Start over.

I will need to say something when I get there. A greeting. An opening. Something to bridge the gap between "I have arrived at your table" and "we are now two people who must speak to each other for forty-five minutes." This is a problem with a finite set of solutions. I am good at problems. I am good at analysis, at prediction, at modeling likely outcomes based on available data. I have a 4.1 GPA and I read Camus in ninth grade and I can tell you the problem with every argument in any essay you hand me. I can do this. I just need the right opening line.

Option one: "Hey."

"Hey" is fine. "Hey" is what normal people say. But "hey" from me, directed at him specifically, in the context of being assigned to work together, carries an inflection I cannot control. It will come out too bright, like I've been waiting for this, or too flat, like I'm performing indifference so hard it becomes its own confession. "Hey" is a word with no place to hide.

Eliminated.

Option two: "So, ecosystems."

This references the project topic. It is direct, functional, mildly amusing in its bluntness. A person with social ease could land this — could deliver it with a half-smile and a raised eyebrow and it would scan as charmingly dry. But I am not a person with social ease. I am a person who will say "so, ecosystems" and it will arrive at his ears stripped of all intended irony, just a weird girl making a weird declaration about ecosystems, and he will blink, and in the silence after the blink, I will die.

Eliminated.

Option three: A joke. Something self-aware about the randomness of the pairing, the absurdity of forced collaboration, a light meta-commentary on the social experiment that is high school lab assignments. I could say — I could say something like — "I hope you're ready to carry this project while I nod and look thoughtful." That's funny. That's self-deprecating in a way that signals confidence. That's the kind of thing a person in a movie says, a person played by someone who gets paid to seem effortlessly human.

But I will not deliver it like that person. I will deliver it like someone who rehearsed it, because I will have rehearsed it, and the rehearsal will show, the way rehearsal always shows, the seams visible, the laugh not quite earned, and he will do that thing people do when a joke doesn't land — that small merciful exhalation through the nose, that *heh*, that act of charity I cannot bear to receive.

Eliminated.

Option four: Silence. Just sit down. Say nothing. Let him initiate. This is the coward's option, which is also the strategist's option, which is also the option most likely to result in me sitting in a chair next to Ethan Russo in a silence so loud it develops its own weather system.

Eliminated.

Option five—

The projector squeaks again, and I realize that in the time it has taken me to construct and demolish four conversational gambits, most of the class has already regrouped. Two-thirds of the desks are rearranged. Mira Patel is laughing with her partner. Derek Huang is already opening his textbook. The room is settling into its new configuration and I am still sitting in the old one, stranded at my original desk like a piece of furniture no one remembered to move, and every second I stay here is another second that makes the eventual walk stranger, the eventual arrival more noticeable, the eventual greeting more freighted with the accumulated weirdness of delay.

I stand up.

---

Here is what I know about walking: it is a controlled forward fall. Each step is a tiny catastrophe of imbalance, corrected just in time by the next step. I learned this in AP Physics, junior year, and I have never been able to unlearn it, and I am thinking about it now, which means I am thinking about the mechanics of walking while walking, which means I am no longer walking naturally, which means I am performing the act of locomotion with the stilted self-consciousness of someone who has just been told to walk naturally for a camera and has forgotten how their arms work.

My arms. What are my arms doing? They are swinging. Are they swinging correctly? Is there a correct ratio of arm swing to stride length? There must be. There is biomechanical literature on this. I could find it. I could find it and study it and optimize my arm swing and it would help nothing, nothing at all, because the problem is not that I don't know how to move my body, the problem is that I know my body is being *observed*, that it exists in a social field, that it is generating data — visual, spatial, kinetic data — that other people are processing, and I cannot control what that data says about me, and this is the thing, this is the central unbearable thing: **I am a broadcast system I cannot shut off**.

I arrive at his table. I pull out the chair. The chair makes a sound against the floor — a scrape, a small violence — and I sit down.

I can feel the blush already. It starts at the base of my neck, a slow thermal event, a betrayal so predictable I should be used to it by now but am not, will never be, because the blush is not just a blush, it is a *disclosure*. It is my circulatory system telling Ethan Russo something I have not authorized it to share. I can feel it climbing — clavicle, throat, jawline — a tide of visible involuntary confession, and I am aware of it, and my awareness of it makes it worse, because awareness triggers the stress response that fuels the blush that I am now aware of being aware of, and this is the loop, this is the trap, this is the architecturally perfect prison of self-consciousness: the guard and the prisoner are the same person and neither can leave.

My hands. I put them on the table. No — that looks aggressive, or eager, or like I'm about to arm-wrestle him. I put them in my lap. That's what nervous people do. Am I nervous? I am nervous. But I don't want to look nervous, which is itself a nervous thought, which is—

I swallow. The swallow is audible. I hear it from inside my skull, grotesquely amplified by bone conduction, a thick wet click that surely — *surely* — he heard too, because we are eighteen inches apart and the room has settled into a working hum and that swallow was a gunshot, an announcement, a press release issued by my throat without my consent.

He does not react to the swallow.

This means either he didn't hear it, which seems impossible, or he heard it and has chosen not to acknowledge it, which is worse, because it means there is now a swallow in the room that we are both pretending didn't happen, and the pretending is a social collaboration I did not agree to, and—

Stop. *Stop*.

I know this is absurd. I know that a swallow is just a swallow. I know that no one in the history of human interaction has ever thought, "She swallowed audibly, I now think less of her as a person." I know this the way I know the earth orbits the sun — it is factually unassailable and emotionally irrelevant. The knowing doesn't help. It has never helped. My intelligence is very good at informing me that my anxiety is irrational, and my anxiety is very good at not caring, and the distance between those two — between understanding and feeling, between the mind that *sees* and the body that *panics* — is the distance I have been trying to cross my whole life, and I cannot cross it, I cannot cross it, and I am sitting next to Ethan Russo with a blush on my neck and my hands in my lap and my throat still echoing with that swallow and he is right there and I—

"Hey," he says.

---

"Hey," he says, like it's nothing. Like "hey" is a word you can just *say*, without vetting it through seventeen internal committees, without stress-testing it for ambiguity, without running a simulation of every possible interpretation and its downstream consequences. He just opens his mouth and the word comes out and it sounds — it sounds like a person talking. Easy. Unpremeditated. The word of someone who occupies his own body like a house he's lived in his whole life and has never once thought about the foundation.

I need to respond.

The response window is open. It's been open for approximately one second. Social science research — I read this, I have *read* this — suggests that a conversational pause becomes awkward at roughly four seconds. I have three seconds left. Two and a half.

"Hi," I say.

I say it. It comes out. It comes out at a pitch approximately fifteen percent higher than my normal speaking voice, which means it sounds like I'm audibly happy to see him, which — I *am*, but that's not the point, the point is that involuntary pitch elevation is a known marker of social submission and I have just, phonetically, submitted, my vocal cords betraying the same information my blush already disclosed, my entire body conspiring to make legible the one thing I most need to conceal.

But he doesn't flinch. He doesn't smirk. He just nods, like "hi" was a perfectly acceptable thing to say, and looks down at the assignment sheet Mr. Kessler handed out.

"So we're doing, what, a food web thing?" he says, scanning the paper.

Now: a fork in the road. I can say something about the assignment. I know about food webs. I know about trophic cascades and keystone species and the reintroduction of wolves to Yellowstone and the downstream effects on river morphology. I know *so much* about food webs that the challenge is not finding something to say but selecting from the overwhelming catalog of things I could say, and this is its own paralysis — the paralysis of excess, the buffering wheel of a mind with too many tabs open, and while I'm buffering, the silence is expanding, and I can hear it expanding, and I—

"Yeah," I say. "I think so."

Four words. Four words where I could have said something interesting, something that showed I am a person with a mind, that I have read about wolves and rivers and the beautiful terrifying interconnectedness of everything, but instead, I said *yeah I think so*, which communicates nothing except that I am present and minimally literate, and he nods again, and looks back at the paper, and the conversation — such as it was — is already over, and I am already replaying it.

*Yeah, I think so.* Why did I stress the "think"? Was there a stress on "think"? I'm not sure. I'm reconstructing the audio from memory and my memory is unreliable because it was recorded under duress. But I think — there, I'm using "think" again, even internally — I think I may have inflected it in a way that sounded uncertain, as if I hadn't read the assignment sheet, as if I weren't sure whether the project was about food webs, when in fact I knew it was about food webs the moment Mr. Kessler handed it out because I read the entire sheet in eleven seconds while everyone else was still writing their names at the top. I *knew* the answer and I delivered it like a question and now Ethan Russo has a data point that says: she's tentative. She's unsure. She might not be a good partner.

Unless he didn't notice the inflection. Unless I'm projecting meaning onto a phonemic variation so slight that no human ear would register it without spectrographic equipment. Unless this entire post-mortem is a waste of cognitive resources being conducted in real time while the actual conversation tries to continue around me, like a city carrying on daily commerce while its mayor sits locked in his office reviewing security footage of a car driving past the building at a speed that might, from a certain angle, if you squint, be considered suspicious.

"I was thinking we could start with the research part," he says, "and then split the write-up."

"Okay," I say.

I said okay. That's fine. Okay is fine. "Okay" is a perfectly functional word that conveys agreement and no further information and is therefore safe, inert, a conversational Switzerland, and I should feel relieved but instead I feel like I am disappearing, like each monosyllabic response is making me smaller, less visible, less *there*, like I am slowly editing myself out of the scene until I am nothing but a body in a chair, warm and mute and blushing.

He is looking at me now. Not in a pointed way, not with expectation or judgment — just looking, the way you look at someone you're working with, checking in, and I should say something, I should contribute, I should—

The projector squeaks.

---

He's left-handed.

I notice this because he's writing something in the margin of the assignment sheet — a note about primary producers, the word *autotrophs* in surprisingly neat handwriting — and his hand curls around the pen in that particular way left-handed people have, the wrist hooked slightly so the ink doesn't smear, and his sleeve rides up just enough to show the knob of his wrist bone, and there's a doodle on the inside of his forearm. He's drawn on himself. A small, absent drawing — it looks like a mountain range, or a sound wave, or the jagged line of a heart monitor — done in blue ballpoint, the kind of thing you draw during a boring class when your hand has more energy than your attention.

He drew that. At some point today, in some other class, he picked up his pen and drew a small mountain range on his own skin because — because he was bored, or because his hand wanted to move, or because some part of his mind that doesn't answer to logic or strategy looked down at the blank canvas of his forearm and thought: *I'll put something here*.

The thought arrives without commentary. Without analysis. Without a seventeen-point evaluation of what it means or what I should do with it. It's just — I see it. The blue ink against the skin of his inner arm. The tiny imprecise peaks. The way the line wobbles at the third peak, like his hand slipped, or like the mountain was supposed to be that shape all along.

I want to ask about it.

Not in the way I normally "want" things — not as a desire immediately seized upon and interrogated and found guilty of excessive vulnerability. I just want to know what he was thinking when he drew it. Whether the mountains are a place or an idea. Whether he always draws on himself or whether today was different. I want to know this in the uncomplicated way you want to know anything that catches your eye — a door left open, a sound from another room, a line of blue ink on a boy's arm in a biology class on a Tuesday.

For a moment — two, three seconds at most — my mind is quiet. Not silent. Quiet. The way a room is quiet when the television is turned off and you realize there was noise all along, a hum you'd stopped hearing until it stopped. The surveillance cameras are still there but they've panned outward, away from me, toward him, and what they're recording isn't evidence. It's just — attention. Simple attention. The kind that doesn't want anything from what it sees.
