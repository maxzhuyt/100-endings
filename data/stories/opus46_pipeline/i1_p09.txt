# Darkroom Revelations

The safelight turns everything the color of a held breath. I know that's not a color. I'm saying it anyway. Under this bulb the world is red-amber-rust, and Elliot's hands in the developer tray look like they belong to a painting nobody's finished yet—something old, something where the saints are always reaching for things they can't quite touch.

We've been in here forty minutes. Our prints for the semester portfolio are due in eleven days and we have, between us, exactly one usable negative and a disagreement about cropping that we've shelved three times. The chemicals smell like vinegar and something sharper underneath. The enlarger hums. Our shoulders are close enough that I can feel the heat off his arm without touching it, and the timer on the counter ticks with the persistence of a small and very committed insect.

He looks different in here. Everyone does—the red light flattens you, steals your depth, makes your eye sockets into dark pools and your skin into one unbroken tone—but Elliot looks *particularly* different. Not better, not worse. Other. Like a second version of himself laid over the first on a transparency, not quite aligned.

I notice this the way I notice everything: completely, and out loud.

---

"You're staring," he says, not looking up from the tray.

"I'm *observing*. I'm a visual artist. It's my whole thing." I say this with the delivery I've perfected—wry, unbothered, the cadence of a person who has never once been caught off guard. It is, if I'm being honest, a performance I've gotten so good at that most days I can't find the seam between the bit and the person doing it.

Here's what I know about myself: I go first. Into rooms, into conversations, into whatever's happening between two people when the air changes. I went first with Priya sophomore year, first with Marcus last summer, first when I told my mother I was bisexual over pasta carbonara and she cried into her wine and I held her hand and managed the whole thing like a shift supervisor of my own coming-out. I lead. That's the shape of me.

And here's what I know about Elliot: thrift-store silver rings on four fingers, black nail polish that he lets chip rather than repainting, a way of talking about photographs—about light and composition and negative space—that makes me feel like he's translating from a language more precise than English. I've been watching him all semester. I am watching him now. I want to say something about the way the red light catches in his collarbones, visible above his stretched-out crew neck, but what comes out instead is: "Hand me the tongs."

My hand hesitates over the tray. Just slightly. Just enough that I notice it, and file it away, and don't look at it.

---

We're waiting. The print is in the developer and there is nothing to do but stand here and watch chemistry become art, which is either a miracle or a process, depending on whether you're a poet or an engineer, and I have never been able to decide. The image surfaces slowly—our test shot, a hallway in the arts building, light through a window hitting the floor in a way that Elliot had noticed and I hadn't, which quietly infuriated me.

The shapes swim up gray, then darker. We watch.

"Can I tell you something," Elliot says.

It's not a question. His voice is lower than usual. Quieter. He's looking at the print and not at me, and his hands are at his sides, and I realize with a start that I have never seen Elliot without something in his hands.

"Yeah," I say.

"I've been—" He stops. Starts again. "I've been thinking about—this is going to sound—" Another stop. He breathes. The timer ticks. The print in the tray is almost fully developed now. His face in the red light is unreadable, or maybe I just don't have the fluency I thought I did.

"I've been questioning some things. About myself. About my gender. About whether the—whether the way people see me is—" He presses his lips together. "I don't have the right words yet. I'm not sure the right words exist yet. But I think I needed to say it out loud to someone and you're—you're who I wanted to tell."

The safelight hums. A bubble rises in the developer tray and pops quietly against the surface of our half-formed image.

I open my mouth to say something. I'm already assembling it—something warm, something that communicates *I see you, this is safe, I've got this*—and then I stop. Because I hear it. The managerial tone. The shift-supervisor voice. And I realize that what I want to say is for me. To prove that I'm the kind of person who handles this well.

So I don't say it.

We stand there. The print darkens by a quarter-tone. The timer ticks. I let the silence be silence.

---

What happens next is my fault, or possibly the fault of the counter, which is too narrow for two people's equipment, or possibly the fault of gravity, which has never been on my side. I step back—some instinct to give him space, to physically make room for what he's just said—and my elbow catches the edge of the black plastic sleeve where we've been storing our unexposed paper. Eight-by-ten silver gelatin, the good stuff. Thirty sheets at three dollars a pop, bought with the last of our pooled supply budget.

The sleeve tips. I grab for it. I grab wrong.

The paper fans out across the counter, across the wet surface near the trays, some of it sliding to the floor—unfurling from its protective packaging into the amber wash of the safelight, which is *safe* for a few seconds of incidental handling but is absolutely not safe for this, not for this duration, not played open like this, every sheet drinking in light it was never supposed to touch until we chose exactly what to print on it.

"No, no, no—" I'm already scrambling, scooping sheets, but even as I do I know. You can't undo exposure. The latent image is already forming—not an image of anything, just fog, just undifferentiated gray that will bloom across every sheet when developed. Useless. The paper still exists. You can hold it, feel its weight and curl. But it cannot become what we planned.

"Ninety dollars," I say. "That's ninety dollars of paper."

"Yeah," Elliot says. He's picked up a sheet. He's holding it up to the safelight, tilting it, and there's something in his face—not panic, not even disappointment—something closer to curiosity. "What if we develop them?"

"They're fogged. They'll just come out gray."

"Not uniformly. Some got more light than others. And some of them hit the developer tray—they'll have chemical marks, tide lines." He turns the sheet. "What if we expose them *more*? Deliberately. Lay objects on them, make photograms. Or fold them and expose them so the creases leave white lines."

He's looking at me now and there is something awake in his face that I haven't seen before, or haven't let myself see—a quality of reckless, gentle invention that makes my stomach flip in a way I am not prepared to taxonomize.

"We can't submit photograms," I say. "The brief says—"

"The brief says *original darkroom prints exploring process*."

He's right. I know he's right. And I know that my urge to argue is not about the brief.

---

We lay the fogged sheets on the counter and I stand there looking at them—these ruined things, full of unintended light—and I try to think about the project, but what I'm actually thinking about is the way I cataloged my attraction to Elliot like an inventory. His aesthetic. His hands. His quiet precision. I built a reading of him. I collected the details that fit a shape I already understood—a certain kind of beautiful boy, a type I could want fluently, a desire I could *name*—and I was so pleased with my own clarity.

And now the reading is shifting. Not dissolving. Shifting. Like a print that turns out to be a double exposure—the first image is still there, but something else is emerging through it, and the composite is more complex than either layer alone.

I am attracted to Elliot. That has not changed. But the *structure* of the attraction is changing, and I—Dani, who names things, who goes first, who manages the narrative—I don't have a name for this yet.

I catch myself reaching for one. Reaching for the label that will make this legible, that will let me file it in the bold and organized architecture of my identity. And for the first time—standing in this red-dark room with fogged paper and the smell of fixer and a person who just trusted me with something unfinished—I stop reaching.

I let my hand hang in the air, empty.

It's terrifying. It's a little bit like flying.

---

We pin a sheet of the fogged paper to the drying line. It's the one that slid closest to the developer tray—one corner is already blooming with a dark, wavering tide mark where the chemistry touched it, a ghostly topography of accident. Elliot wants to expose it further tomorrow, lay a glass negative over it and print through the fog, see what a planned image looks like when it's laid down on a surface already marked by unplanned light.

"It might not work," I say.

"I know."

"It might be ugly."

"I know that too."

We're standing close again, the way the room demands, and I can see the safelight reflected in his eyes—two small red points, steady and warm. He's holding the edge of the paper and I'm holding the other edge and our fingers are a half-inch apart, and I understand that this is not the moment for a kiss, or a declaration, or a name. This is the moment for standing in the red dark with a piece of ruined paper that might become something neither of us has seen before.

The timer ticks. The chemicals breathe their slow acid breath. Somewhere in the paper's emulsion, silver halide crystals are holding the ghost of an image that won't exist until tomorrow, until we come back to this room and find out what the light has done.

I don't know what this is. I don't know what he is to me, or what I am becoming, or whether the picture we're making will be beautiful or a disaster.

I stay in the room.
