# Gone South

The saxophone is the first thing. Before the smell, which is river rot and sugar and something fried at an hour when nobody should be frying anything, before the light, which is wrong—too gold, too horizontal, like Tennessee's sun got drunk and fell sideways into the Gulf—before any of that, there's this saxophone threading through the air on Decatur Street and I think: *okay. Okay. You're here now.*

I'm here now.

Sixteen years old, two states from home, phone at nine percent, and in possession of exactly thirty-seven dollars because the woman who drove me from Memphis to Hattiesburg didn't ask questions and the man who drove me from Hattiesburg to here asked too many and I gave him twelve dollars to stop. He had opinions about my hair. He had opinions about the way I was sitting. He said *you one of them boys or one of them girls* and I said *can you let me out at the next exit* and he said *I'm just asking* and I said *twelve dollars to not just ask* and he took it and we drove the last forty-seven minutes in a silence that tasted like his menthol cigarettes and my own held breath.

I'm fine. I want to be clear about that. I am standing on a sidewalk in New Orleans, Louisiana, at—I check—7:48 a.m. on a Thursday in July, and the heat is already the kind of heat that has opinions about your body, that presses its whole self against you like a drunk uncle at a cookout, and my black t-shirt is absorbing every degree of it, and I am fine. I have my backpack. I have my phone, dying. I have my shoes, which are Converse I stole from the lost and found at school three months ago because Mom stopped buying me shoes after the first conversation, the one I'm not thinking about right now because I am fine.

Mom's face does the thing. It just appears, summoned by the word *Mom* the way saying *Bloody Mary* in a mirror supposedly summons something you can't take back. Not the yelling face, which came second, which was almost a relief because at least yelling is a form of belief—you don't yell at a person who hasn't said something real. And not the crying face, which came third and was worse, so much worse, because crying meant she understood and was grieving, which meant she'd received the information and decided it was a death. The first face. The blankness. The total evacuation of the person I knew from the features I'd memorized since birth. Like I'd said something in a language she not only didn't speak but refused to believe existed. Like the words had come out of my mouth as a series of sounds that didn't correspond to anything in her reality.

*Taylor, what are you telling me.*

Not a question. She didn't inflect it as a question. A question opens a door. That sentence boarded one up.

I blink it away. Practice. I've gotten good at blinking it away. Forty-eight hours of practice, which in running-away time is a kind of PhD. The saxophone helps. It's coming from a guy set up near the café with the green-and-white-striped awning, case open on the sidewalk, not many coins yet because it's too early for tourists and the locals walk past musicians like they walk past fire hydrants—aware, but unstopped. He's playing something slow and bruised, something in a minor key that leans on its own notes like a person leaning on a wall after bad news, and I don't know the name of it but my chest knows it. My chest says: *that one. That's the one.*

I stand there too long. A woman pushing a stroller detours around me. A man in a white apron sweeps the stoop of the café, and for one full second he looks at me—not my face, but the backpack, the shoes, the way I'm standing, which is the way you stand when you don't have a destination behind the standing—and his expression does a complicated thing. Recognition, maybe. Or pre-recognition. He's seen my species before. He goes back to sweeping.

I start walking because walking is a thing a person with a plan does, and I need to be a person with a plan. The French Quarter unscrolls itself around me in blocks of pastel shotgun houses and wrought-iron balconies and courtyards you can see through gates into, green and dripping, like the city keeps its secrets in foliage. Everything is wet even though it hasn't rained. Everything smells like last night's party and this morning's coffee and underneath both of those, the river—wide, brown, ancient, carrying the whole middle of the country past on its back and not complaining about it.

I catalogue. This is what I do. Threat assessment dressed up as observation. The tarot reader on the corner of St. Ann, spreading her cards on a TV tray, who looks at me and then looks at me *again*, which I don't like. The man sleeping in the doorway of a closed souvenir shop, his mouth open, his shoes tied together by the laces and looped around his wrist—even in sleep, he's holding on. The group of college-aged kids stumbling out of a bar that is apparently open at eight in the morning, one of them carrying a bright green drink the size of a toddler, all of them loud in the way of people who have never had to be quiet to survive. I note: the bar has outlets visible through the open door. I note: the gas station on the corner sells those cellophane-wrapped sandwiches and I could eat one for two dollars and save the rest. I note: the saxophone is still audible from here, two blocks away now, threading between buildings, and I don't know why it's the thing I'm using as a compass but it is.

I need to charge my phone. I need to eat something that isn't the gas station peanuts I bought in Hattiesburg eleven hours ago. I need to figure out where a person sleeps when they're not a tourist and not a local and not, technically, anything yet—not a resident, not a runaway on paper because nobody's reported me, probably, because reporting me would require Mom to pick up the phone and describe me to a stranger, and describing me to a stranger would require using words she has decided don't apply.

What I need, if I'm being honest, which I'm trying not to be because honesty is what got me here, is to stop hearing the school counsellor's voice in the back of my skull, the one that said *concerning behavioral patterns* like it was a weather report. What I need is to stop seeing Marcy Brewer's face in second-period English when I walked in wearing the skirt, which was dark blue, knee-length, not remotely provocative, the most boring skirt in the history of skirts, but on my body it became breaking news. What I need is my phone to have enough battery to text Jamie in Knoxville and tell her I made it, so she can stop worrying, so at least one person in the state of Tennessee can stop worrying about me and start worrying about something productive, like climate change or the Titans' offensive line.

I need to stop standing in the middle of the sidewalk.

"You're standing in my bedroom."

I look down. A sleeping bag, dark green, rolled tight against the building's base, partially hidden by a blue recycling bin. The roll is deliberate and practiced—sleeping-bag origami. Next to it, a pair of flip-flops lined up neatly, which is the kind of detail that rearranges you if you let it. Someone lives here. Someone lives here neatly.

Then I look up.

She's my age. Maybe older by a year, maybe younger, it's hard to tell because her face has the kind of sharpness that could be bone structure or could be hunger and I'm not going to think too hard about which. Brown skin, deep brown, shaved head that shows the shape of her skull, which is a good shape, a deliberate shape—this was a choice, not a circumstance. She's wearing a white tank top and cargo shorts and boots that look too heavy for July, and she has a backpack that's the real tell: every zipper duct-taped shut. Not with neat strips either. Wound around, sealed, the way you'd tape a package you didn't trust the world not to open.

She's sitting on a milk crate I hadn't noticed, positioned against the wall at the exact angle that catches the building's narrow strip of morning shade. She's eating a banana. She is eating this banana the way I have never seen anyone eat a banana—slowly, with a focus that suggests she has done the math on this banana, knows precisely how many bites it contains, and is apportioning her enjoyment across them like a budget.

"Sorry," I say, and step sideways off the sleeping bag. "I didn't—"

"You just get in?" she says. Not unfriendly. Flat. A customs agent's energy.

"No," I say, too fast. "I've been—"

"You just got in." She bites the banana. Studies me with those eyes, which are the specific dark brown of coffee before you add anything to it, undiluted, and which are—I recognize this—reading me the same way I was reading the street. Threat assessment. Categorization. But also something else. A question she's asking without asking. "Bus or car?"

"Car. Two cars." I don't know why I'm being honest. I had a whole system. The system was: say nothing, trust no one, find an outlet, charge phone, text Jamie, figure out next. The system did not include truth-telling to strangers on milk crates. But something about her eyes—no. Something about the way she's not performing. She's not asking me to perform, either. She's not generating the specific frequency of pity or wariness that adults have been directing at me for forty-eight hours, the one that says *you are a problem I would like to either solve or avoid*. She's just looking. Like looking is enough.

"I'm Dee," she says.

"Taylor."

"Taylor." She says it like she's weighing it on a scale, checking it for hollowness. I wait for the follow-up—*Taylor what? Is that your real name? Is that a boy name or a girl name?*—but it doesn't come. She just nods, like the name passed inspection. "You eat yet, Taylor?"

*Yet.* That word does something. *Yet* implies a future in which eating happens. *Yet* implies she has an opinion about whether and when I eat. My throat does a thing I'm going to classify as allergies.

"Not since Mississippi," I say.

"Mississippi." She says the word like it's a diagnosis. She stands up from the milk crate in one motion, folds it flat—it's hinged, a milk crate that folds, which means she's been doing this long enough to acquire equipment—and tucks it behind the recycling bin with the sleeping bag. She shoulders her duct-taped backpack. "Come on," she says. "I know a place."

I follow her. I don't decide to. My feet decide.

---

The place she knows is on Chartres Street, which she pronounces "Charters" and corrects me on before I even attempt it, which means she heard me form the *ch* sound in my throat and preempted the humiliation, which is either kindness or control and I haven't decided which.

It's a café the size of a hallway, open to the street on one side, with a counter and a woman behind it who looks at Dee and says, "Morning, baby," and looks at me and says nothing, but not in a hostile way. In the way of someone who understands that Dee sometimes arrives with people and the people don't require commentary.

Dee orders two beignets and a coffee with chicory, which she pronounces "CHICK-ory" and which arrives in a white mug with a chip on the rim. She pays with coins she produces from seven different pockets—shorts, backpack, a tiny zippered pouch inside the cargo pocket, another one I swear she pulls from her boot. The coins are organized. Not by denomination. By something else. Maybe by which pocket she wants to reach into in which context. This is a person with systems.

The beignets come out on a paper plate, buried under powdered sugar, steaming. My body responds before my brain can get involved. My mouth fills with saliva so fast it's almost violent.

"Eat," Dee says, pushing the plate toward me. She picks hers up, takes a bite, chews with her eyes closed. A brief, total absence of guardedness, and then she's back, eyes open, scanning the street through the café's open side. We are sitting at a counter that faces the sidewalk. She chose this seat. She chose it so she could see who's coming.

The powdered sugar gets on everything. It gets on my black t-shirt and my fingers and somehow my left eyebrow. For a second I look like I've been caught in a gentle, localized snowstorm, and Dee looks at me and does something that isn't quite a smile—more like the place a smile would be if she had the bandwidth for one.

"How long have you been here?" I ask, and the question comes out more careful than I planned, because I'm already watching her, because I'm already tracking the ways her body knows this city.

"Couple days," she says. She sips the chicory coffee.

But her hands know the Quarter. She navigated us here without looking up once, without pausing at a single intersection, without the barely perceptible hesitation of someone reading their environment for the first time. She nodded at the tarot reader on the corner—not a *hello* nod but a *still there* nod, the nod of mutual surveillance. She stepped over the same cracked flagstone I tripped on, sidestepped it without looking, the way you sidestep the broken stair in your own house.

Couple of days is a lie.

I note this. I put it in the mental filing cabinet under *Things About Dee That Don't Add Up*, a file that currently also contains: *why duct tape all the zippers, why boots in July, why does the woman behind the counter know your name.* I don't say anything. I eat my beignet. The powdered sugar coats the inside of my mouth and for three full seconds there is nothing in the world except sweetness.

The saxophone has followed us somehow, or it's a different one—a second musician, or the sound bending through the Quarter's narrow streets the way sound bends through a hallway. You hear it around corners. Between buildings. The whole city seems to breathe through brass instruments, to speak in a register just below language, and it should be beautiful and it is beautiful but it's also doing something to my chest that I didn't authorize.

"You know about the shelters?" Dee says. She's not looking at me. She's looking at her beignet, pulling off a small piece, placing it in her mouth. Economy of motion. "There's four or five that aren't trash. Two or three that are actually okay. Some of them check ID. Some don't. Some—" She pauses. "Some of them separate you by what's on your birth certificate."

She says this like she's reading aloud from a brochure. Neutral. Informational. But she leaves space after it. A specific, measured space, the way you leave a space for someone to step into if they choose.

"That matter to you?" she asks. Not looking at me. Looking at her beignet.

My whole body goes tight. Not just tight—rearranged. Every muscle reclassifies itself as a defensive structure. My hands, which were reaching for the second half of my beignet, stop midair and then complete the motion too deliberately, too casually, the way you'd reach for something if you were acting in a play about reaching for something.

Because here's the thing.

I left Tennessee exactly because of what's on my birth certificate and what it doesn't say and what my mother's face did when she contemplated the distance between the two. I left because—
