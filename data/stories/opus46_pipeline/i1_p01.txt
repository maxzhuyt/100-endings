# What the God Heard

I woke to my shoulder first. It preceded me into every day—that seized knot below the left scapula where Macro's practice sword had found the joint wrong three months prior and the medicus had ground it back into place with his thumbs while I bit leather. Then the rest: the ridge of scar tissue across my fourth and fifth ribs, a Thracian's parting gift from the September games. The stone's cold through the straw mat. The smell of the lime they packed into the latrine channel, which did not defeat the smell beneath it. Urine, sweat worked into wool and never quite boiled out, and below that something older—decades of men's fear soaked into the walls and become the walls.

Through the partition I heard Danaos praying in a language I did not recognize, and from farther down the corridor, someone grinding his teeth with a sound like a boot turning on gravel. I reached under the straw and touched the figure I had carved from a rind of hard cheese and then hardened further over the lamp—my Hercules, small enough to close inside a fist. His club was only a nub. His face was nothing. I held him and said nothing because the god already knew.

---

They oiled us in the yard while the sky was still the color of ash. Sosias worked the oil into my legs and I watched his hands move with the impersonality of a man greasing a hinge. The medicus came behind him, took my left arm, rotated it, pressed the shoulder until I hissed, and said *adequate* to the doctore's assistant, who marked a tablet. I was adequate. Down the line: Danaos, adequate. The Gaul they called Cassock because he'd arrived in one—adequate. Macro, who had damaged me and whom I would likely face again in the Quinquatria games—preferred, which meant a different quality of oil, another egg in his porridge.

We ate in a line on the stone bench. Barley porridge with ash mixed in for the calcium—they had explained this to us as though the explanation were a kindness, as though knowing *why* our food tasted of hearth-scrapings made the eating easier. Beans. A broth with something in it. I ate next to a freeborn Roman named Auxentius who had sold himself to the ludus to clear a debt of fourteen thousand sesterces and who chewed with his eyes closed, and I understood this was because if he did not see the men around him eating the same food in the same way he could maintain some inner fiction that he had chosen this, specifically this, the way a free man chooses a restaurant.

We were, by certain measures, fed better than the poor who would watch us die.

---

At the palus I worked the standard combinations—advance, cut, cut, shield check, cut—and on the fourth repetition my arm remembered something else: a doorframe in Thessaly, my hand against the wood, pushing it open into a room where light fell across a floor and someone had left olives in a bowl on a table and beyond the table a window giving onto a field so green it seemed to vibrate, and in the field—

The doctore's switch caught my calf. *Again.*

I went again. The dust rose and settled on my oil and became a skin. Hercules had served Eurystheus for twelve labors. He had not chosen the labors, nor the order, nor the reason. But he had been a god the entire time and I was not a god, which was the difference, unless the difference was smaller than I thought—unless being owned did not reach as far down as they needed it to reach.

---

At midday we sat in what shade the colonnade offered and a litter passed beyond the gate, its curtains drawn but its bearers' calves visible—oiled, muscled, African—and behind it a man on foot who spoke to the lanista through the bars. I heard: *the aedile's funeral games, four pairs minimum, one to the death confirmed, the rest crowd-decision.* I heard a figure in sesterces. I heard my name, or thought I did—sound bends in heat, and a man hears his name in everything when his name is the only thing between himself and a scheduling tablet. Cassock spat and said nothing. That was the correct response. We were the calendar. We were how Rome marked its time.

---

In the baths, the water was almost warm and almost enough. I sat on the submerged bench and Danaos lowered himself in across from me, and for a moment neither of us performed anything. His body was a record I could read—the burns from the net-weights he trained with, the place below his knee where the flesh dipped inward like a thumbprint in clay. He looked at me and I looked at him and the water was between us and I understood that this was the closest I would come to being seen without being assessed.

He said: *Do you still talk to your cheese god?*

I said: *He listens.*

*Does he answer?*

I didn't know how to say that the answer was the listening—that the god's silence was a room I could enter, a room with a door I had not been given but had carved, the way I had carved his body from a thing meant to be consumed. That the field in Thessaly might not be real. That the olives might be invented. But the god held all of it for me the way a hand holds a small thing in the dark, and did not ask whether I was adequate.

*No,* I said. *He never answers.*

Danaos nodded as if this were the only theology that made sense.

---

Evening. The cell. The lock turned or did not turn—I had stopped checking. The carts began their legal rumbling through the streets and somewhere a flute played something in a mode I almost recognized. I lay on the mat and the shoulder settled into its complaint and tomorrow I would be oiled and examined and found adequate or not found adequate, and the Quinquatria games were eleven days away or twelve, and Macro was somewhere in this building eating his second egg.

I held the god in my fist. His club fit exactly against my heart line.

Just in case.
