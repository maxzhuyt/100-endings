# The Rewind

The word that came out of Eliot's mouth was "orgasm."

The word Mr. Tierney had asked him to define, from the vocabulary sheet, third row down, was "organism." But Eliot's eyes had snagged on the wrong line, or his tongue had, or some dark saboteur nested in the meat of his brain had decided that today — a Tuesday, fourth period, in front of thirty-one seventh graders including Nia Whitfield — was the day to detonate his life.

The laughter didn't come all at once. It came in a wave, the front row first, a sharp bark from Devon Masters, then the awful ripple backward through the room, every face turning, mouths open, the particular savagery of twelve-year-olds who have just been handed a gift they did not earn and do not deserve. Nia covered her face with both hands but her shoulders were shaking. Mr. Tierney said something — "Okay, okay, settle" — but his voice was buried. Eliot stood there. His ears were not hot; they were molten. His hands were fists and one fist was gripping the opposite wrist, gripping the watch — the new watch, the birthday gift from his grandmother, the one that didn't look like any smartwatch he'd seen before, its face slightly concave, the screen not sitting *on* the surface but somehow recessed beneath it, like a puddle of dark water pooled in a dish, always a little too warm against his skin — and he was squeezing and twisting because people in pain do that, they grip what's nearest, and beneath his thumb the watch's crown turned with a click that he felt in his teeth.

The laughter stopped.

Not faded. *Stopped*. Like a recording dragged backward. Eliot felt his stomach lurch the way it lurched on the downswing of a roller coaster, a full-body wrongness, and then he was standing at his desk — not in front of the class — standing at his desk, looking down at the vocabulary sheet, and Mr. Tierney was saying, "Eliot, number fourteen, please."

Third row down. *Organism.*

"A living thing," Eliot said. His voice was steady. "An individual form of life. An organism."

Mr. Tierney nodded and moved on to Rachel Park.

Eliot sat down. His heart was hammering so hard he could feel it in the watch, in the warmth of it, the pulse bouncing back at him from that dark little screen as if something in there had a heartbeat of its own. He didn't look at it. He pressed his hand flat against his thigh and stared at the vocabulary sheet until the letters blurred and the bell rang and he was free.

---

Outside, the air was sharp with October. Eliot walked fast past the bike rack, his backpack slung over one shoulder, the triumph of it swelling in his chest — not just the save but the *secret*, the private architecture of a disaster that had happened and unhappened and now existed only inside him, a scar the world couldn't see.

By the dumpsters near the east lot, a cat sat on the pavement.

It was small and wrong-looking, the way strays get: an orange tabby paled to something closer to apricot, one ear torn so the tip folded like a dog-eared page, fur matted along the spine in hard ridges. It wasn't eating or hunting. It was sitting upright, paws together, facing him.

Eliot slowed.

The cat didn't flinch. Didn't flatten its ears or tense to bolt. Its eyes — copper, unblinking — tracked him with a quality he couldn't name, something that wasn't startled and wasn't predatory but was more like the look a neighbor gives you when you come home at the wrong hour. *Oh. You again.*

He walked past. When he looked back from the sidewalk, the cat was still facing the spot where he'd been, as if he were still standing there. As if some version of him was.

---

He rewound the pop quiz in science. Heard the first question — *What is the powerhouse of the cell?* — let the answers propagate through Mrs. Huang's review, twisted the crown, and aced it cold.

He rewound the conversation with Nia at the water fountain, killed the stammer, found the joke, landed it, watched her laugh in a way that was *with* and not *at*.

Rewound the dodgeball hit that would've caught him in the neck. Ducked.

Rewound the hallway collision with an eighth grader's open Gatorade. Sidestepped.

Rewound the awkward wave at Devon that Devon didn't return. Didn't wave.

Just didn't wave.

A day that felt like a week. A week in an afternoon. Everything optimized, every sharp edge sanded, and Eliot moved through the halls like water, like someone who had never once in his life said the wrong word in front of the wrong room, and it was — God, it was *good*, the way being good at a video game was good, the way finding the perfect run was good, every input precise, every outcome clean, and faster now, faster, the crown so easy to turn, his thumb finding it without looking, *click*, and the world obeyed.

---

The mirror in the upstairs bathroom was unforgiving in its specificity. Eliot knew this mirror. He had studied his own face in it the way cartographers study coastlines — every contour mapped, every blemish catalogued, the mole below his left ear, the way his eyebrows grew slightly thicker on the right. He knew this face.

The face in the mirror was not quite it.

The jawline had sharpened. Not dramatically, not movie-star chiseled, but *harder*, the soft pad of childhood receding to show the geometry beneath. He turned his head. The skin along his cheeks, which yesterday had been smooth and faintly oily in the way of thirteen-year-old skin, was textured now — rougher, with the faint suggestion of pores doing something they hadn't done before. He leaned closer. Along his upper lip, a shadow. Not a mustache. The preliminary sketch of one.

He pulled back.

His shirt was tight across the shoulders. He'd put it on that morning and it had fit the way it always fit, slightly loose, the way he liked it, but now the seams sat differently, pulled taut across a torso that seemed longer, the sleeves ending higher on forearms threaded with veins he didn't recognize. He turned to the doorframe where his mother had penciled his height every September since first grade. The most recent mark, labeled *Sept — 7th grade!!!*, was level with his chin. It should have been level with the top of his head.

He looked at his hands. They were his hands, mostly. But the knuckles were more pronounced, the fingers longer, and on the right hand — he brought it close to his face, not breathing — a single hair grew dark and coarse on the first knuckle of his middle finger.

In the kitchen, his mother glanced up from her laptop. "Dinner's at six, hon." Then she squinted. A micro-expression, there and gone — the flicker of someone looking at a word they've misspelled, knowing it's wrong but not yet finding the error. "You okay? You look tired."

"I'm fine."

At school the next morning, he reached for the seat next to Nia in homeroom and she shifted — barely, an inch at most, her body angling the slightest degree away — then caught herself and smiled, but the smile was the kind you give to someone older, a friend's brother, a young teacher, polite and sealed. She'd never smiled at him like that before. She'd smiled at him yesterday like he was *funny*. Today she smiled at him like he was *sir*.

In the bathroom between periods, he found the gray hair. Just one, bright as wire, sprouting from his left temple. He pulled it. The root came out thick and pale and he stood there holding it between his fingers like a fuse.

---

He couldn't keep going. He understood that in his body before he understood it in his mind — a cellular alarm, every nerve ending screaming that something was being *spent*, some finite reserve draining with each click of the crown. But the gray hair. But Nia's sealed smile. But the jeans that had fit last week and now ended above his ankles like a cartoon of a boy who'd outgrown his life.

He had to go back. Not thirty seconds. Not a minute. Back to *before* — before the quiz, before the dodgeball, before the hallway, before any of it. Back to the morning of the vocabulary test when the watch was just a watch and he was just a kid who hadn't broken anything yet.

He gripped the crown and twisted hard, past the usual click, past the resistance, forcing it the way you force a jar lid when your grip is desperate and slippery and you don't care about the mechanism, only the opening, and the crown *gave* — not a click this time but a grinding, a deep mechanical shudder that traveled up his wrist and into his teeth and behind his eyes, and the world lurched not like a roller coaster this time but like a house shifting on its foundation, a structural failure, walls holding but the angles wrong —

He gasped. Hallway. School. The fluorescents buzzed. Lockers on both sides, same olive green, same combination locks, but the banner above the main office read SPIRIT WEEK — MARCH 14–18 in puffy painted letters and that was wrong, that was *deeply* wrong, because it was October and Spirit Week was in October and March 14 was nothing, was months away or months behind, and the date on the banner's corner said a year he didn't recognize as the current one.

He walked to homeroom. His homeroom. Room 114. Ms. Kazemi was at the whiteboard, and the seating chart taped to the door had every name he knew except the one that sat next to him. Where Nia's desk should be — where Nia's desk *was* — sat a boy named Theo Grimes. Eliot didn't know Theo Grimes. Had never heard that name in his life.

"Can I help you?" Ms. Kazemi said from the doorway, and her tone was the tone of a teacher addressing a student from another class, another grade, a stranger in the wrong hallway.

"I'm — I'm in this homeroom. Eliot. Eliot Voss."

She checked her roster. An eyebrow went up. A small frown. "I don't have an Eliot Voss. Are you sure you're—"

"I sit right there," he said, pointing, and his voice cracked — not upward like a boy's voice cracks, but downward, a full octave drop that felt like falling, and Ms. Kazemi took a half step back, the same recalibration Nia had done, the social distance widening because his voice and his height and the new architecture of his face all said *you do not belong here*, and he turned before she could say anything else, turned and walked and then ran.

Outside. East lot. Dumpsters.

The cat was on the wall.

It was closer than before. Not by the pavement near the bike rack, but on the low brick wall that bordered the walkway, three feet from where he stood, at eye level. Same torn ear. Same matted ridges. Same copper eyes. But its expression — and he knew it was insane to read expression in a cat's face, he *knew* that — its expression was different. The last time, it had looked at him with the flat recognition of someone noting a familiar stranger. Now it looked at him the way his mother's face had looked in the kitchen. The way you look at something you cannot fix. The way you look at someone who has done a thing that cannot be undone and does not yet understand the full shape of what they've done.

Pity. The cat looked at him with pity.

Or warning. He couldn't tell which, and it occurred to him — standing in a schoolyard where his name had been erased, in a body growing past him like a vine past a fence — that pity and warning might be the same thing when it's already too late for one of them.

---

He stood at the edge of the east lot, the wall behind him, the school in front of him, a building full of rooms where he used to exist. The watch was warm. It was always warm, but now it was feverish, the concave screen pulsing faintly with a light that seemed to come from beneath the glass, from deep inside its architecture, the way light comes from the bottom of a well.

He could feel the crown under his thumb. Smooth. Ridged at the edge. Ready.

One more rewind. That was the logic. One more to reset the reset, to undo the undoing, to reach past this broken timeline and find the original, the one where he was thirteen and short and stupid and said *orgasm* in front of Nia Whitfield and survived it the way everyone survives it, by being embarrassed and then being less embarrassed and then, eventually, being someone with a story about the dumbest thing they ever said in seventh grade.

But one more turn and his body would — what? He looked at his hands. The knuckles stood out like knots in young wood. A dark hair curled on each finger now. His wrists were thick. His wrists were a stranger's. One more turn and maybe the gray hair becomes gray *hairs*, becomes a hairline that recedes, becomes a face his mother wouldn't squint at but would *not recognize at all*, becomes a man standing in a middle school parking lot with no name on any roster and no bed in any room and no life that fits because he'd spent it — all of it — thirty seconds at a time, click by click, trying to be perfect in a world that was never going to let him be.

The cat sat on the wall. Still. Calm. Its torn ear canted slightly forward, listening, as if it could hear the tiny mechanical teeth inside the crown waiting to engage.

It had been here before him such a long time. He understood that suddenly, in the way you understand things in nightmares — not learned but *known*, like a fact that arrives fully formed in the chest. The cat had watched others. Sat on other walls outside other schools. Tracked other desperate hands reaching for other dials. It was not a symbol. It was a witness. The last thing that sees you before you become someone else.

Eliot pressed his thumb to the crown.

He didn't turn it.

Not yet.

His heart banged in a chest that was broader than it had been yesterday, in a body that was betraying him in the oldest, slowest way — the way all bodies betray their owners, by *growing* — and the question wasn't whether to turn the dial, not really, because he was going to, they both knew that, the cat and the boy, the watcher and the watched. The question was what would be left of him on the other side. What age. What face. What world.

The crown was warm under his thumb, alive with the promise of thirty stolen seconds, and Eliot stood there — not boy, not man, not anything with a name that fit — suspended in the space between one moment and the one that comes before it, hand on the dial, heart in his throat, the stray cat's copper eyes holding him gently, gently, the way you hold something that is about to break.
