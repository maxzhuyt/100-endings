# Gunpowder Rendezvous

---

The jukebox has been playing the same song for forty minutes.

Spike knows this because he's been counting, the way a man counts when he has nothing else to spend. The track is something with teeth—an electric guitar riff that claws its way up a minor scale, collapses, and starts again. The needle catches at the bridge every third rotation, a small hitch in the loop, a stutter the machine can't heal. He's memorized it. He could hum it. He won't.

The bourbon in front of him is the color of iodine and probably not much better for his stomach. He drinks it anyway, slowly, because it is his last seven dollars rendered liquid and there is an argument for making seven dollars last. The bar is a concrete-block building off a highway that doesn't appear on the maps he can afford, in a city whose name he didn't catch when the Swordfish touched down. The stools are cracked vinyl. The bartender has the face of a man who stopped being curious about his clientele around the same decade the jukebox was new. There are four other drinkers, all of them performing the same studied act of disinterest that means at least two are armed.

Spike is waiting for a callback on a skip trace. The target is a money launderer named Dominic Thorell—mid-level, paranoid, worth enough to keep the Bebop fueled for another month if Spike can find him before the lead goes cold. Jet's information placed Thorell somewhere in this district, this grid of walk-up motels and cinder-block bars, but *somewhere* is a word that costs nothing and earns the same. So Spike waits. The bourbon thins. The guitar riff climbs.

The door opens and the humidity shifts. He doesn't look up immediately—that's amateur mechanics, turning toward every hinge-creak like a dog hearing its name. He looks at the bartender's eyes instead, because other people's reactions are cheaper than his own attention, and the bartender's eyes do something they haven't done all night. They move.

Then Spike looks.

She comes in the way weather comes in. There is no preamble. The door is open and she is through it and the door is closed again and the room has rearranged itself around the fact of her, the way a room rearranges around a new center of gravity. She's wearing cutoff denim and a black tank top and boots that have been resoled at least twice, and none of this is what Spike sees first.

What Spike sees first is the Berettas.

Twin shoulder holsters, worn soft, the kind of leather that only gets that way through years of daily use. The pistols sit modified and well-oiled, Inox frames with aftermarket grips, adjusted for a hand slightly smaller than the standard the manufacturer assumed. The holsters are positioned for a simultaneous cross-draw. He notes the angle. He notes that the retention straps are unsnapped. He notes all of this in the time it takes the jukebox needle to skip past the bridge, and then—only then—he lets himself see the rest.

She's compact and coiled, East Asian, with dark red-brown hair that falls past her shoulders without any apparent effort toward aesthetics. Tanned skin. A quality of stillness in the limbs that he recognizes because he has it too: the particular economy of motion that belongs to people who understand that wasted movement gets you killed. Her eyes are already scanning—exits, sight lines, the four drinkers, the bartender, and him. He watches her gaze complete its circuit and arrive at him, and he watches it stop.

She's seen his eyes on the Berettas. She knows what that kind of looking means—not that he's afraid of them, but that he read them, the way a person reads a sentence, subject and verb and object, the whole grammar of what she is compressed into two steel-and-polymer clauses.

Her expression doesn't change except at the mouth. The corner tightens by a millimeter. It's not quite a smile. It's the look a predator gives when it spots another predator at the same watering hole—not threat assessment, not yet, but the cooler thing underneath it: *recognition*.

Spike lifts his bourbon and drinks, holding her gaze over the rim, and the jukebox loops back to the riff's opening snarl.

---

She takes the stool three seats down from his. Not adjacent, not opposite. Three seats is a specific distance: close enough for conversation, far enough to draw.

"Bacardi. Neat. The 151 if you have it, not that flavored shit."

Her voice is lower than he expected and rougher than it needs to be, as if she's spent years using it as a weapon and it's calloused now, the way a boxer's knuckles are. The bartender pours. She doesn't thank him. She drinks the way Spike drinks—not for pleasure, but to fill a space that would otherwise require thinking.

The jukebox catches at the bridge. Resets.

"You keep looking at my guns," she says, without turning her head, "and I'll give you a closer look than you want."

Spike exhales through his nose. It might be a laugh. "Sword Cutlass Berettas," he says. "Custom recoil springs. You replaced the barrels—factory length was bothering you." He pauses. He swirls the last half-inch of bourbon. "Trigger pull's been adjusted too, I'd guess, but I'd have to fire one to tell you how far."

Now she does turn. She turns the way a gun turret turns, the whole mechanism at once, and her eyes are dark and flat and not even slightly friendly and absolutely, unequivocally interested.

"The fuck are you?"

"Broke," Spike says. "Mostly."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the most honest answer I've got."

She stares at him for a three-count—he can feel her measuring him, disassembling him the way he disassembled her Berettas at a glance, reading his shoulder holster and the Jericho 941 that lives inside it, reading his frame with its odd combination of ranginess and density, reading the way he sits with his weight shifted forward onto his left foot, which is the foot you balance on if you've been trained to kick with your right. He watches her read all of it and he does not perform. He just sits there, being what he is, and lets her finish.

"Bounty hunter," she says. It's not a question.

"Sometimes."

"Dominic Thorell."

The bourbon stops moving. His hand is steady—it is always steady; it is steady, he suspects, in a way that will survive him—but the liquid in the glass goes still, and that's enough. She sees it. Her mouth does the not-smile again.

"Small goddamn world," Spike says.

"It's not small. You're just in my way." She flags the bartender. "Another. And whatever he's drinking." She glances at his glass with visible contempt. "Which clearly isn't much."

The bartender pours. Spike doesn't protest. Pride is a commodity he learned to trade at a discount. The fresh bourbon is better than what he was nursing—she's paying, so the bartender reached for a higher shelf—and the Bacardi 151 she's drinking is functionally jet fuel, and she handles it the way some people handle water.

"Dutch and the others are already moving on the location," she says. "I've got a lead on where he sleeps. This is my job."

"I've got a lead on where he eats," Spike says. "People who eat also sleep. Eventually. This is my job."

"I was here first."

"You walked in after me."

"I was on this bounty first, dipshit. Three days. Rock pulled the intel, Benny confirmed it, and I'm here to collect."

"And I've been in this bar for two hours waiting on a callback that's going to tell me which room number. So it seems like we're both here."

She leans forward. The Berettas shift in their holsters, and the motion is a vocabulary she's fluent in, a sentence she speaks with her shoulders and her collarbones and the specific angle of menace she can produce just by letting the weapons remind a room that they exist.

"I don't split bounties," she says.

"Neither do I."

"Then we've got a problem."

"We've got the same problem," Spike says. "Which is different."

She looks at him. He looks at her. The jukebox needle skips the bridge, catches, finds itself, starts over.

"You always talk like a fortune cookie?" she says.

"You always drink like you've got something to prove?"

"I drink like I'm thirsty. You talk like a man who's been alone too long and forgot how people work."

"Maybe." He tips the fresh glass toward her in a gesture that could be a toast. "But I know how Berettas work, so that's something."

She doesn't clink. She doesn't refuse. She just drinks, watching him over the rim the way he watched her, and for a moment the bar is quiet except for the guitar riff's endless ascent, and the air between the three empty stools holds a charge that the bartender can feel, that the four other drinkers can feel, that Spike can feel in his sternum like the pre-vibration before a string sounds.

The drinking continues. She matches him and exceeds him. He is aware he is outgunned in this particular caliber and adjusts his pace, sipping where she swallows, spacing his intake against the jukebox resets, every third cycle, turning the loop into a clock. She notices this, and what she notices is not the restraint but the rhythm—that he's listening to the jukebox closely enough to use it, that his attention, even when it appears lazy, is a fine and calibrated instrument.

"Spike," he says at some point. He doesn't extend a hand. His hands stay where she can see them, which is the only politeness that counts.

"Revy."

"Just Revy?"

"Just Revy." She finishes her third glass. "You got a last name, Spike?"

"Spiegel."

"German?"

"Martian."

She almost laughs. It comes out as a sharp exhale, an aborted thing, as if laughter is a reflex she trained out of herself and it still sometimes breaches the perimeter.

"So, Spike Spiegel from Mars," she says. "How do you want to do this? You want to draw on me? Because I'll win. You want to race me to his door? I'll win that too. Or you want to sit here drinking my bourbon until one of us passes out, in which case—" She leans back, crosses her arms beneath the Berettas. "—also me."

Spike tilts his head to one side, and the overhead light catches his left eye differently from his right—one brown, one paler, the prosthetic never quite matching the original—and he says, with the absolute disinterest of a man who means it: "I was thinking we could flip a coin."

"I'll flip you the fuck off. How about that."

The jukebox resets. The riff climbs. He finishes his drink. She finishes hers. The bartender watches them with the expression of a man who has seen enough preludes to identify the overture.

"Look," Spike says. "You've got the sleep location. I've got the room number coming. Neither piece is worth much without the other. You know this. I know you know this because you're still sitting here instead of—" He gestures loosely toward the door, toward the night, toward everything neither of them trusts.

"Instead of what?"

"Instead of being the kind of person who walks away from a useful stranger."

"Every stranger's useful until they shoot you."

"Sure," Spike says. "But you've been sitting three stools away for twenty minutes and you haven't shot me yet, so either you've gotten slow or you've already decided this is interesting."

The word *interesting* lands between them like a coin spinning on its edge. She stares at him. Her jaw works. He can see the musculature of her decision-making, the physical process by which Revy allocates trust—not from the head, not from any rational calculus, but from somewhere lower and older, the body's own intelligence, the animal assessment that operates beneath language.

"Sixty-forty," she says.

"Fifty-fifty."

"You haven't done shit yet. Sixty-forty."

"I identified your barrel replacements from eight feet away. That's worth at least ten percent."

"That's worth exactly nothing to my bank account."

"Fifty-fifty," he says again, "or I take my room number and go find his sleep location myself."

"You won't find it."

"Try me."

The jukebox loops. The song is so deep in his skull now it's become architecture, a room he's living in that won't stop rebuilding itself around him.

"Fifty-fifty," she says. "And I lead."

"Fine."

"And if you slow me down, I'm taking your cut and your cigarettes."

"I only have three left."

"Then you better keep up."

She signals for another round. He lets her. The negotiation has concluded and what replaces it is something neither of them would name, a current that runs beneath the transactional language and hums at a frequency they both pretend not to hear. The drinking isn't celebration. It's something between a ceasefire and a dare—each glass a test of the other's composure, each swallow a small act of faith that the other won't use the impairment as an opening. Spike understands this. More to the point, his body understands it. He has been in exactly this kind of conversation before, in exactly this kind of bar, with exactly this kind of person—someone whose entire being is a clenched fist around the same bruise he carries—and he knows that the drinking is not about the alcohol. It is about proximity. It is about the slow, dangerous process of two armed people choosing not to be alone for one more hour.

The jukebox needle catches. Skips. Catches. Begins.

---

The back door comes off its hinges at 11:47 p.m.

There is no prologue to the violence. This is something Spike has always understood and most people don't: the movies are wrong. There is no slow-motion entry, no lock of eyes across the room, no moment where you understand what is about to happen and prepare yourself. There is the moment before and then there is the shooting, and the difference between people who survive and people who don't is the width of the gap between those two states.

Revy's gap is zero.

The door splinters inward and two men come through it—heavy, armed, wearing the particular expression of professional enforcers who have been told a location and a description and not much else—and Revy is off her stool with both Berettas drawn before the door hits the floor. She doesn't aim. That's the wrong word. Aiming implies a process, a sequence of decisions—target, sight picture, trigger. What Revy does is closer to speech: the guns are extensions of her syntax, and she simply *says what she means*.

Two shots, then a third. The first man's weapon leaves his hand in a spray of disassembled metal and finger bones. The second catches a round through the meat of his shoulder and spins, and as he spins a third figure is already through the doorway behind him—faster, leaner, a woman with a submachine gun who reads the room correctly and does the only smart thing, which is to target whoever just fired.

She targets Revy, and that is a mistake, because by then Spike is already moving.

He doesn't think about it. This is the truth of violence, the one thing Spike has never been able to explain to Jet or Faye or anyone who asks: it's not that he's brave, it's that the calculations happen somewhere his conscious mind doesn't live. His body knows trajectories. His body has always known trajectories—the arc of a foot, the geometry of a close-quarters intercept, the particular distance you need to close before a gun becomes a liability for the person holding it. He covers it in two strides and a pivot, the Jeet Kune Do flowing like water through the shape of the room, and his right foot connects with the submachine gun at the precise point where the woman's grip is weakest.

The gun skids. His elbow follows, a short sharp movement that ends the engagement. She drops.

Silence, sudden and total, except for the song.

The jukebox is still playing. Of course it is. The guitar riff climbs its minor scale. The needle catches at the bridge. The song knows nothing of what just happened and will go on knowing nothing, and this will be true of the song long after Spike and Revy and everyone in this bar have stopped being anything at all.
