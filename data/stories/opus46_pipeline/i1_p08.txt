# Stolen Moments in Roma

The motorcycle was not a Vespa. Ben would learn this later, in the way you learn things from people who correct you with their entire body—a stiffening, a look, a recalibration of respect. It was a Piaggio Liberty 125, and it had a scratch down the left fairing the color of exposed bone beneath turquoise paint, a long ugly groove that suggested a story she had no intention of telling, and it stopped close enough to his left knee that he felt the heat of the engine through his chinos.

He had been standing in the middle of a lane off Via delle Muratte with a Lonely Planet open to page 94, which contained a sidebar about the Trevi Fountain's nightly illumination schedule, when the world changed pitch. The engine note. Then the brake squeal. Then a silence that was not silence at all but the pressurized instant before someone starts yelling.

"*Ma che cazzo fai?*" The rider pulled off a helmet that turned out to contain a dark-haired woman in her late twenties or early thirties—he could never reliably guess ages, especially when the person was vibrating with fury. "*Stai in mezzo alla strada come un—*"

"I'm sorry," he said. "I'm so sorry. *Mi scusi. Mi—*" He reached for the phrasebook tucked inside the guidebook and the guidebook fell. It landed open-faced in a puddle, the kind of shallow, malignant puddle that had no meteorological explanation and existed solely to receive dropped things. The pages began drinking. He watched them drink.

"*Dio santo.*" She killed the engine. She swung one leg over the seat in a motion that looked practiced, or athletic, or both—the economy of someone who had been getting off this particular machine in this particular way for years. Black boots. Courier bag slung cross-body, its flap tattooed with stickers: a defunct Roman bike messenger collective, a café that had closed in 2019, a band whose name was half-peeled into abstraction. She picked up the guidebook, held it at arm's length, and let it drip.

"English?" she said.

"American, actually."

"Same thing."

"It is definitively not the same—"

"You were reading about the fountain." She gestured with the waterlogged book toward the Trevi, visible in slices between buildings: a shoulder of marble, a jet of engineered water, the cruise-ship murmur of three hundred tourists with selfie sticks. "You know they vacuum the coins every Monday night? The money goes to Caritas. The Catholic supermarket charity."

"Yes." He straightened. He was taller than her but somehow took up less space. "I know that."

"Then why—" She held the guidebook aloft like evidence in a trial. Water fell from page 94 in a continuous thread.

"I like to be thorough."

She stared at him. He was wearing a linen shirt buttoned one off, so that the left placket hung an inch lower than the right, and he'd clearly ironed it—the creases were deliberate, which made the misbuttoning worse, a betrayal of his own system. Khaki chinos with a crease. Leather shoes that had been appropriate for an office in—she guessed—somewhere with seasons. His hair was the brownish non-color of a man who'd never made a single decision about his hair.

"Thorough," she repeated.

"I'm a researcher. Was. I'm—between things." He didn't know why he'd said that. He wiped his palms on his chinos.

She looked at his face. She looked at the guidebook. She looked at the puddle, which had accepted his phrasebook too now, a smaller drowning alongside the first. Something crossed her expression—amusement, or recognition, or the particular cruelty of someone who spots a soft thing and has to decide whether to protect it or poke it.

She laughed. It came out like a bark, unexpected, almost unwilling, the laugh of a person who hadn't planned on finding anything funny that afternoon. He felt something give way behind his ribs—not break, exactly. Shift. Like a door that had been painted shut, cracking along its seam.

"You are going to die in this city," she said. "You understand this? Standing in the road with your little book. You will be killed."

"Statistically—"

"Get on the bike."

He looked at the bike. He looked at her. The scratch on the fairing winked turquoise and white in the sun.

"I'm Ben," he said.

"I didn't ask. Get on."

---

Her name was Alessandra, and she had a delivery to make in Trastevere—a portfolio of architectural drawings for a firm that employed her at freelance rates she described as "criminal, but in the wrong direction"—and she did not make it.

He sat behind her the way a man sits behind a woman on a motorcycle when the man has never been on a motorcycle: rigidly, his hands uncertain of where they were permitted, his center of gravity a full emergency somewhere above his actual body. She said, "*Hold my waist or die, these are the options,*" and he held her waist. Through the leather jacket he could feel the mechanics of her breathing, the slight tension before a turn, and Rome opened around them like an argument against straight lines.

The streets were not streets. They were propositions. They curved and narrowed and poured into piazzas that opened like palms, and then they closed again, intimate and sudden, and the buildings on either side were the color of everything he'd never had a name for: not orange, not gold, not pink, but the specific hue of plaster that had been absorbing centuries of sun and exhaust and rain and washing hung from iron balconies and the jasmine that climbed the walls with a kind of botanical aggression, sending its scent across the diesel and the hot stone and the bread smells that would not leave him alone.

She drove the way weather happens: without consultation.

An umbrella pine shattered the sky into green and blue. A man on a corner sold coconut from a cart and shouted something that made Alessandra shout something back without slowing down. A church became a tabaccheria became a courtyard became a fountain that wasn't the Trevi but was, Ben thought, better than the Trevi, smaller and quieter and populated by a single pigeon drinking from its lip with the self-possession of a Roman senator.

They stopped first at a courtyard behind Via dei Giubbonari. She parked the Piaggio with its front tire against a chain that bore a sign he couldn't read, and she stepped over the chain the way you step over a crack in a sidewalk—automatically, without looking down.

The courtyard held ruins. Not the kind roped off behind Plexiglas with explanatory plaques and suggested donation boxes, but the kind that had been abandoned to time and cats. A column base. A fragment of what might have been a wall or might have been decorative—the distinction, after two thousand years, had ceased to matter. Cats draped themselves across the ancient stone with the entitlement of creatures who knew they'd been worshipped here once and hadn't adjusted their expectations. A tortoiseshell with a torn ear regarded Ben from atop a Corinthian capital and found him precisely as inadequate as he felt.

"This was part of the Theater of Pompey," Alessandra said. She sat on the column base. The cats ignored her, which seemed like high praise. "Where Caesar was killed. Maybe. Historians argue."

"I know. The Curia of Pompey was—"

"Please don't." She held up a hand. "Don't know things right now. Just look at it."

He looked at it. He looked at cats sleeping on the stones where the Roman Republic had ended. He looked at weeds growing through mosaic fragments that nobody had bothered to excavate, and Alessandra sitting among all of it as if she'd been placed there by the same hand that had placed the ruins—carelessly, beautifully, without a plan.

"You always follow rules?" she said. She'd watched him step over the chain with the exaggerated care of a man disarming a bomb.

"I find them reassuring."

"*Gesù.*"

---

The coffee place had no sign. It had a window and a woman behind the window and nothing else: no menu, no posted hours, no chalkboard listing oat-milk alternatives. The woman was perhaps sixty, with arms that suggested a past in either baking or boxing, and she looked at Alessandra the way a mother looks at a daughter who has shown up unannounced after too long, which is to say with a compound emotion—pleasure alloyed to grievance—that produced the word "*disgraziata*" spoken with unmistakable love.

"*Due caffè, Maria. E questo è Ben. È americano. Sii gentile.*"

Maria looked at Ben. Maria was not gentle.

The espresso arrived in cups the size of thimbles and hit his palate like an argument. It was not coffee in any sense he understood. It was a substance that had been coffee once and had since become something theological—dense, bitter, with a crema the color of a hazelnut and a finish that tasted, improbably, of dark chocolate and smoke and his own startled gratitude. He winced. He drank it. He drank all of it. Alessandra watched him drink it with the close attention of a scientist observing a controlled reaction.

"Good?" she said.

"Violent," he said. "But good."

She grinned. Maria brought something without being asked—a small pastry, warm, filled with ricotta and flecked with orange peel—and Alessandra ate half and gave him half and licked her fingers with the unselfconsciousness of a child, and when they left he noticed, in the peripheral way you notice a bird landing or a cloud moving, that she was holding a ceramic spoon. Small, painted with a blue rooster, the kind of thing that sat in a jar by a register. It was in her hand and then it was in her jacket pocket and then it was nowhere, and he wasn't certain he'd seen it at all.

He said nothing. The saying-nothing felt deliberate. He catalogued it beside the espresso and the pastry and the way Maria had said *disgraziata* and filed the whole encounter under a heading he didn't yet have a name for.

---

The church was near the Pantheon but was not the Pantheon, which Alessandra held in a contempt she declined to justify. "*Troppi turisti. It has become a handbag.*" The church had no tourists. It had, in the dim interior that smelled of beeswax and cold stone and the particular mustiness of faith continued past the point of logical support, almost no one at all. An elderly woman in black knelt in a pew near the back. A man in paint-stained clothes sat in the confessional reading a newspaper, either waiting for the priest or having given up on him.

"What church is this?" Ben asked.

"If I tell you the name, you'll look it up later. You'll read a Wikipedia article and it will replace what you're seeing now with what somebody else saw and wrote down. Don't do that."

"You're asking me to experience something without researching it first."

"I'm asking you to be a human being and not a search engine."

She walked past the altar ropes. Not over them—she simply went around the end, where the rope met the wall, as if the barrier were a suggestion she'd considered and declined. She moved through the chancel with the ease of someone who'd done this before, in this church or churches like it, a woman who understood that the distance between sacred and profane was largely administrative.

She took a long match from a box near the votive candles, lit it from a candle already burning, and used the borrowed flame to light a fresh one. The new candle guttered, caught, steadied. She stood in its glow.

Ben, who had followed her past the ropes in a state of moral vertigo that felt pleasantly criminal, stood beside her. Her face in candlelight. He thought of Caravaggio—not the paintings specifically, but the principle: the way light could carve a face out of darkness and make it the only thing in the room.

"Make a wish," she said.

"I think that's fountains."

"Make one anyway."

He closed his eyes. He wished for something that, had he examined it directly, would have embarrassed him to his foundations. He let it exist in the dark behind his eyelids and did not give it words.

When they left—when he turned to follow her down the nave, past the kneeling woman, past the man with his newspaper confessional, into the assault of daylight outside—he saw the chain. A glint at her collar, slipping beneath her jacket. Silver. A small medallion, round, catching a sliver of light from the open door before it disappeared. He knew what a saint's medal looked like. He knew it hadn't been around her neck before.

He said nothing. But this time the nothing was different. This time it was a decision made with the full weight of his attention, and the silence that followed had edges.

---

She escalated.

He didn't recognize it as escalation at first. It felt like continuation—the same momentum that had carried them from the courtyard to the coffee window to the church simply carrying them further, faster, toward wherever Alessandra was always going, this woman who seemed constitutionally incapable of stillness.

But it was escalation.

She took him through a neighborhood she called "the intestines of Trastevere" on the Piaggio at a speed that made the buildings into a single smeared surface, terracotta and ivy and open windows through which he glimpsed half-second lives: a woman ironing, a man shouting at a television, a child standing on a balcony holding a cat like a prize. He gripped her waist and his knuckles went white and the air was hot and tasted of exhaust and something sweet—fig, he thought, or jasmine, or the city itself metabolizing the afternoon.

She stopped at a gate set into a wall topped with broken glass. The gate was padlocked. She looked at it the way a person looks at a problem they've solved before.

"Boost me," she said.

"What?"

"Put your hands like this"—she clasped her own hands together to demonstrate—"and boost me over."

"That is trespassing."

"That is a rooftop garden with the best view in Rome, and the woman who owns it is in Sardinia until September."

"How do you know that?"

"I delivered her mail for two years. *Dai, andiamo.*"

He boosted her. She went over the wall with the fluency of a cat over a fence, and then the gate opened from inside—she'd found the mechanism, or broken it—and he stepped through into a staircase that smelled of cat urine and time, a century's worth of footsteps worn into marble treads, and they climbed.

The rooftop was not a garden so much as an argument that a garden could exist anywhere. Terracotta pots held tomatoes, basil, something thorny and purple he couldn't identify. A lemon tree grew from a wooden half-barrel, its fruit hard and green and determined. And beyond the pots, beyond the half-wall with its crumbling balustrade, beyond the potted herbs and the single rusted chair and the ashtray full of cigarette butts that testified to someone else's secret visits—Rome.

Rome from above was a different proposition than Rome from within. From within, the city pressed against you, hot and loud and smelling of bread and gasoline. From above, it became arrangement. He could see the geometry now—the domes, dozens of them, scattered across the skyline like the city had been designed by someone who loved circles, and the antennae, and the satellite dishes, and the laundry lines strung between buildings like the strings of instruments nobody was playing, and above it all the sky, which was doing something so absurd with the light that if he'd seen it in a painting he would have called it sentimental.

"Sit," Alessandra said.
