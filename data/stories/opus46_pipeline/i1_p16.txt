# Hell is Other Demons

---

The throne listed three degrees to the left on good days.

Today was not a good day.

Lucifer Morningstar, Prince of Darkness, Lord of the Pit, the Adversary, sat with his weight shifted carefully to the right hip and pretended the grinding sound beneath him was not structural. Beneath the throne—iron, ancient, once quite imposing before entropy and dentition had conspired against it—something chewed. Not gnawed. *Chewed.* With the slow, luxuriant dedication of a creature that had found its vocation.

"Stop that," Lucifer said, without looking down. He said it the way one says *bless you* after someone sneezes: a reflex emptied of all original meaning.

The chewing paused. Considered. Resumed at marginally increased tempo.

He turned back to the reports.

Soul intake was running fourteen percent above projections for the quarter, which should have been gratifying but mostly meant a backlog at Processing. Three hundred thousand new arrivals in the past week alone and only two functioning intake portals because *someone*—he would find out who, and they would come to understand the true meaning of their job title—had used Portal C's runic array as a surface for heating leftover brimstone casserole. The stain, apparently, had become sentient and was now filing for residency rights.

He initialed the form. Denied.

Next: a maintenance request from Furnace Level Nine. The language was bureaucratic, the subtext was panic. *Thermal output inconsistency* meant the flames had gone paisley again, which happened every few centuries when the underlying geology shifted and was, in the grand scheme, more aesthetic than dangerous. But the foreman of Level Nine was new—relatively speaking, appointed only six centuries ago—and had written the request in a font Lucifer could only describe as *pleading.*

He scrawled *cosmetic, deprioritize* in the margin and let the parchment curl shut.

Beneath it: a labor grievance. He felt the specific heaviness behind his eyes that in a human would presage a migraine but in an archangel merely presaged annoyance that would persist for geological epochs.

The Torment Division, Third Circle, Subsection Avarice, was lodging a formal complaint against the Torment Division, Third Circle, Subsection Gluttony, over jurisdictional overlap in the case of one (1) soul, a fourteenth-century Venetian merchant who had been both spectacularly greedy and grotesquely fat, and whose punishment had been the subject of inter-departmental argument since roughly the fall of Constantinople. Avarice wanted him on the gold-melting detail. Gluttony wanted him for the eternal feast of ash. Both divisions had cc'd Legal. Legal had cc'd Lucifer with a note that read, simply, *Please advise at your earliest eternal convenience,* which was either a joke or a cry for help or both, and either way Lucifer was going to ignore it until it composted into precedent.

He set the grievance aside on the pile he privately called *Let It Rot.*

The throne groaned. The chewing had migrated to the front-left leg—the load-bearing one—and he could feel, through the iron, the vibration of each dedicated bite translated upward through millennia of claimed authority directly into his sacrum.

"I will unmake you," he said to the space beneath the throne. "I will return you to the void from which I sculpted you. I will unthread the very concept of your existence from the weave of—"

A tongue, enormously hot and indiscriminate, lapped across his ankle.

Lucifer closed his eyes.

The hellhound had been a miscalculation. Aeons ago—he could not recall exactly when, which itself was unusual for an entity with perfect memory, suggesting he had chosen not to remember—he had needed a guardian for the Lower Gates. Something fearsome. Something that would give pause to the ambitious and the foolish. What he had gotten was a creature roughly the size and disposition of a Labrador retriever if the Labrador were made of obsidian and thermal vents and had three heads, two of which were usually asleep while the third carried out its campaign of structural sabotage with the others' tacit approval.

All three heads answered to "Dog." He had never named it anything else. Naming things was a dangerous business. He'd learned that from the Other Place.

He turned to the next report—a statistical analysis of despair metrics across the Upper Circles, which someone had optimistically formatted with color-coded graphs as though data visualization might please him—and the throne shifted another degree.

---

It was the coffee that alerted him.

Lucifer did not need coffee. He was a being of celestial origin, older than caffeine, older than the plants that produced it, older than the soil those plants grew in. He had watched the first stars ignite, had sung harmonies that predated the concept of sound, had fallen from a height that made the word *height* a parochial joke.

He drank coffee because it was bitter and warm and because the mug gave his hands something to do during the parts of eternity that were merely boring rather than actively terrible.

The coffee, this morning, was perfect.

This was wrong.

Hell's coffee was produced by a machine of uncertain provenance located in what passed for a breakroom on the administrative level. The machine bore no brand name, responded to no input, and produced, at intervals it alone determined, a substance that was to coffee what Hell was to a vacation: technically meeting the minimum definitional requirements while missing the point so aggressively it constituted a philosophical assault. Some days it dispensed lukewarm tar. Other days, a clear liquid that tasted of static. Once, memorably, wasps.

Today it had produced a perfect dark roast with notes of chocolate and a whisper of cardamom, served at precisely the temperature that meant *someone cared.*

The machine did not care. The machine was barely sentient and what sentience it had was *hostile.*

Lucifer set the mug down and examined his office with the specific attention of a detective arriving at a scene.

The bookshelf: one volume returned. *Erta's Compendium of Forgotten Geometries,* third edition, which had been missing for—he calculated—eleven months. It was placed correctly, alphabetically, between Dorsmouth and Faal. But it was turned spine-inward, which was either carelessness or a message, and with her it was never carelessness.

The desk: undisturbed, excepting a single pen rotated forty-five degrees from where he'd left it. Pointing at his chair. Or pointing at nothing. Or pointing at his chair *because* it pointed at nothing, which was the kind of semantic recursion she favored and he pretended to find exhausting.

He opened the top drawer. His lieutenant's head was not in it. This was notable because the last time, it had been. Forneus had gotten it reattached eventually—demon physiology was forgiving about that sort of thing—but had developed a stammer and a tendency to flinch near filing cabinets that persisted to this day.

He closed the drawer and thought.

The pattern—if it was a pattern, if *pattern* was a word that could be applied to a woman who had refused to be patterned since before patterns existed—had begun approximately two years ago. Hell-time, which moved at its own sullen pace, like a bureaucracy unto itself. She had started coming by.

Not *visiting.* Visiting implied invitation, and neither of them would ever extend one. She simply... manifested, in the margins. Evidence accumulated:

The book, returned.

Forneus, disassembled.

A fissure in the western wall of his study, patched. The mortar bore the faint scent of amber.

A minor demon from Correspondence, set aflame. (This one, admittedly, may have been unrelated. Correspondence was widely loathed.)

The thermostat in his personal quarters, adjusted two degrees warmer.

A report on his desk defaced with a single word in the margin—*tedious*—in her handwriting, which had not changed in millennia: all angles, no curves, as though the letters themselves refused to soften.

And now the coffee.

He stared at the mug. The steam curled. He tried to arrange the visits into a syntax that made sense—violence, domesticity, violence, domesticity, repair, destruction, warmth, criticism—but the grammar wouldn't parse. The conjugation didn't track. She wasn't saying *I love you* or *I hate you* or even *I remember you.* She was saying something for which there was no language, or for which language had been specifically invented and then burned, which amounted to the same thing.

He drank the coffee. It was, infuriatingly, still perfect.

Dog nosed out from under the throne, all three heads alert now, sniffing the air with six nostrils flared. The middle head whined—a sound like a pipe organ with feelings—and turned toward the door.

"Yes," Lucifer said. "I know."

He did not say what he knew. Naming things was dangerous.

---

The door opened without ceremony—it would not dare ask for a knock—and Glasya-Labolas entered in a posture that was attempting dignity but achieving something closer to a controlled stumble. He was a mid-tier demon of the operational caste, responsible for Infrastructure and Zoning, and he had the demeanor of an actuary who had seen things that made actuarial tables weep.

"My Lord."

"Glasya."

"There is—" He paused, recalibrated. "We have a situation."

"We always have a situation. Hell is definitionally situational."

"Yes, my Lord. But this situation has a form." He held up a document. It was thick. It had tabs.

Lucifer looked at it with the weariness of the immortal confronted with pagination. "Summarize."

"The Phlegethon is in violation."

"The Phlegethon is a river of fire. It exists to violate."

"Of *zoning,* my Lord." Glasya-Labolas swallowed, an unnecessary biological affectation he had picked up from too many centuries of observing human middle managers. "The river has expanded its banks by fourteen cubits in the northeast tributary. This puts it in direct encroachment upon the Allocated Pastoral Zone."

"We have a pastoral zone?"

"Mandated under the Seventh Revision to the Infernal Charter. Subsection 9, paragraph—"

"Why do we have a pastoral zone?"

Glasya-Labolas blinked with three of his five eyes. "Morale, my Lord. It was part of the compromise following the Imp Uprising of—"

"I remember the Imp Uprising." He remembered it the way one remembers a toothache: an experience whose unpleasantness was exceeded only by its pettiness. "How bad is the encroachment?"

"The sheep are on fire."

"We have sheep?"

"We *had* sheep, my Lord."

Lucifer pinched the bridge of his nose—a gesture he had learned from humans and adopted because it was, he had to admit, expressively efficient in a way that celestial body language lacked. Angels communicated displeasure through subtle modulations of divine radiance. Nose-pinching was faster and didn't set things alight.

"Redirect the northeastern tributary seven cubits south. Reinforce the bank with cooled basalt—pull it from the reserves under Level Twelve, they've been hoarding and this gives us a pretext. File a geological impact report, backdate it to last century so it looks planned. And—" He paused. "—restock the sheep."

"The—the sheep, my Lord?"

"Fireproof ones this time. There's a requisition process. Use it."

"My Lord, I don't believe fireproof sheep exist in any—"

"Then make them exist. You have a budget. You have a department. You have, presumably, some residual ambition that hasn't curdled into despair. Use these things." He let his voice drop half a register—just enough that the temperature in the room adjusted itself in sympathy, just enough that Glasya-Labolas remembered, between one heartbeat and the next, that he was receiving instructions from the being who had once held the geometry of the cosmos in his voice and had broken it out of *preference.* "Or shall I solve it myself?"

"No, my Lord." The words arrived faster than the thought that preceded them. "The sheep. Fireproof. Immediately, my Lord."

"Good."

Glasya-Labolas turned to leave,  then hesitated. His gaze drifted, involuntarily, to the base of the throne, where the front-left leg now boasted a constriction of chew marks so severe it resembled an hourglass. The iron was warped. The structural integrity was, by any reasonable engineering standard, fictional. The throne was being held upright by habit and possibly spite.

Dog yawned. The left head. An elongated, humid yawn that displayed rows of obsidian teeth and smelled of sulfur and iron filings.

Glasya-Labolas looked at the leg. Looked at Dog. Looked at Lucifer, who was already reading the next report and did not look up.

He said nothing.

He left.

The throne settled another degree. Somewhere in its iron bones, something that might have been a rivet and might have been a prayer made a sound like surrender.

---

She came through the wall.

Not the door—the door was for subordinates and solicitors and other things that operated within the permissions of architecture. Lilith came through the south wall of his office as though the wall had merely been a suggestion she had considered and declined, the stone rearranging itself around her passage with the quiet deference of matter that knew better than to argue.

She looked the same. She always looked the same, which shouldn't have been remarkable for an immortal but was, because *the same* with Lilith meant a kind of composed fury so habitual it had become elegance. Dark hair, sharp face, eyes that were older than his office and less patient than the rock she'd just walked through. She wore something that was not quite armor and not quite a dress and had the general aura of a woman who had been walking for a deliberate amount of time and had arrived at exactly the moment she intended and not one second before.

She did not greet him.

"You moved my boundary ward," she said.

"I adjusted a zoning marker."

"My *ward.* The one anchored to the southern escarpment. The one I spent three centuries calibrating—"

"The one that was encroaching on administrative territory by six hundred yards and interfering with our spectral communications. Yes. I moved it."

"You had no right."

"I had *jurisdiction.* Which is a different thing, but reliably more useful."

Her eyes narrowed. The room's ambient temperature, already warm by the standards of most realms, developed an opinion. "I want it back."

"File a petition."

"I don't *file.*"

"I know. That's why I moved it." He turned a page in the report he was no longer reading. "Filing implies operating within a system, and operating within a system implies accepting that someone else built the system, and you would rather eat your own teeth. It's one of your more predictable qualities."

"Whereas you," she said, "built a system so you'd have something to hide behind. Filing cabinets. Intake forms. The entire apparatus of this—" she gestured at the office, the walls, the parchment-strewn desk, the general *Hellness* of it all—"this *performance.*"

"Administration is not a performance."

"Everything you do is a performance. You fell from Heaven performing. You built this place performing. You sit on that ridiculous—"

She stopped.

She stared at the throne.

"—what happened to the leg?"

"Ongoing situation."

As though summoned—and perhaps it was; the creature had a sense of narrative timing that Lucifer found suspicious—Dog emerged from beneath the throne. All three heads operated simultaneously for the first time that day: the left yawned, the right shook off a fragment of iron, and the middle head locked onto Lilith with an expression that could only be described as *delight.*

Unconditional, stupid, full-bodied delight.

Dog crossed the room in two bounds. Its tail—singular, massive, made of something between smoke and obsidian—swept a stack of reports off Lucifer's desk. All three heads competed for position. Six eyes, each the deep orange of cooling magma, gazed up at Lilith with the kind of guileless adoration that had not existed in Hell since before Hell existed.

She looked down at the creature.

"Ridiculous animal," she said, and put her hand on the middle head.

She scratched behind its ears.
