# Sisyphus Gives Advice

The boulder was about a third of the way up when Crow landed on it.

"You're late," Sisyphus said, not looking up. His shoulders were deep in the work, feet braced against a rut that had been worn into the hill over—well. A long time. A very long time.

"You're slow," said Crow. She had a leather satchel strapped across her body that was almost larger than she was, and she was already pulling envelopes out with her beak, sorting them into the crevices of the boulder's surface as though it were a desk. Which, in fairness, it was. "Six today. One's perfumed."

"Save the perfumed one for third."

"I'll save it for whenever I feel like saving it." Crow pinned a letter under one talon and tore it open with a practiced jerk of her head. She unfolded the page, smoothed it against the granite, and cleared her throat with a sound like a small engine failing to start.

Sisyphus pushed. The boulder moved. Crow rode it without comment, the way someone rides an escalator.

"Go ahead," he said.

---

*Dear Sisyphus,*

*My husband and I have been married for eleven years. He is a good man. I want to say that first because I think it matters, and I don't want you to think I'm writing to complain about him. But recently I realized something that frightened me: I can't remember the last time he asked me a question. Not a real one. Not "how was your day" but actually wanting the answer. He tells me things. He sits with me. He is present in every technical sense. But I have become — I don't know how to say this — a wall he talks at. A pleasant wall. A wall he loves, probably. But I don't want to be a wall. How do I say that to him without breaking something?*

*— Plastered Over in Thessaly*

Crow looked up. "Plastered Over. That's a bit on the nose."

"It's honest," Sisyphus said. He was quiet for several paces. The boulder crunched over a patch of loose scree and he adjusted his grip, fingers finding the familiar grooves, the places where the stone had been worn almost smooth by the oils of his skin. Centuries of grip. "All right. Ready?"

Crow produced a quill from somewhere in the satchel—he'd never asked where—and flattened a blank sheet against the rock.

"Dear Plastered Over." He exhaled through the next step, a hard one, the grade ticking upward. "You are not frightened because something is wrong. You are frightened because you noticed. Those are different things, and the difference matters, so I want you to sit with that for a moment before we go any further."

Crow scratched. Her handwriting was terrible, but legible in the way that a doctor's prescription is legible—you could decode it if your life depended on it, and sometimes it did.

"Your husband has gotten comfortable. I don't mean that as an insult. Comfort is what we build love *for*—it means the architecture held. But comfort has a shadow, which is that it can make us stop reaching for the other person. Not out of cruelty. Out of trust. He trusts that you are there, that you are fine, that the wall is load-bearing and does not need to be asked how it feels about the roof."

"That's a bit much," Crow said. "The wall metaphor. She already started it. You're leaning on it. No pun intended."

"Keep it."

Crow made a sound that, in a human, would have been an eye-roll.

"Here is what I'd suggest. Don't have a conversation about the *problem*. Not yet. Instead, tonight or tomorrow, ask *him* a question. A real one. Not about logistics—not about the bills, not about who's picking up what from where. Ask him something you genuinely don't know the answer to. What he's been thinking about lately. What he misses. What he noticed today that surprised him. You might find he startles. Good. That's the wall opening a door. People remember how to walk through doors. They just need to see one."

He paused. Shifted his weight. "And if he doesn't walk through it the first time—leave it open. Ask again tomorrow. Curiosity is patient, and so are you. You wrote to a stranger for help and you opened with kindness about the man who is hurting you. That's patience. That's the beginning of every repair I've ever seen work."

"Sign-off?"

"Yours, in the hope that he hears you. Sisyphus."

Crow finished writing, blew on the ink with a short, sharp breath, folded the letter into thirds, and slid it into an envelope. She licked the seal.

"You know," she said, "you could have just told her to talk to him."

"I did."

"In four hundred words."

"She needed to hear her own courage described back to her before she could use it. People usually do."

Crow said nothing to that, which was her version of agreement.

---

The summit was not dramatic. It wasn't a peak so much as a flattening—a place where the uphill simply gave up and became a brief, defeated plateau before the other side gave way to a slope that was, depending on your perspective, either a cruel joke or a structural inevitability.

The boulder teetered. Sisyphus stepped back. The boulder tipped, caught, held for one tendon-taut moment—and then rolled. It was loud on the way down. It was always loud on the way down.

He watched it go the way a man watches the bus he's just missed: mild displeasure, total unsurprise.

Crow was already airborne, gliding down in lazy circles. She landed on a rock near the bottom and began reordering the remaining letters by some system that seemed to involve sniffing them, holding them up to the light, and occasionally stabbing one with her beak.

Sisyphus walked down. His knees knew the descent better than his mind did. He cracked his neck, rolled his shoulders, and arrived at the boulder the way an employee arrives at a time clock.

He set his hands against it. He pushed.

"Next," he said.

---

*Dear Sisyphus,*

*I work in a bureaucratic office that processes permits for land use in the greater Peloponnese region. I have worked there for nine years. My supervisor is a man named Demetrios who has never, in my presence, completed a thought. He begins sentences and then wanders off—literally, sometimes, mid-word—to start new projects, new complaints, new sentences. He is not unkind, merely impossible. Everything I submit is lost. Every process takes twice as long because he must touch it, add nothing, and pass it on. I have tried speaking to his superior, who is also him (he promoted himself during a restructuring). I cannot leave this job because the pension is extraordinarily good and my mother depends on it. How do I survive this without becoming a person I don't recognize?*

*— Buried in Permits*

"Oh, this one's good," Crow said, with the enthusiasm she usually reserved for finding something dead.

Sisyphus was in the early part of the hill, the gentle grade, where the push was almost meditative—a warm-up for the body, a lull for the mind. "Read it again."

Crow read it again.

"All right." He settled into his pace. "Dear Buried in Permits. First: I admire you. Nine years in a broken system, for the sake of someone you love, without losing your clarity about what the system is doing to you—that is not small. Don't let anyone tell you endurance is the same as passivity. You are making a choice every day, and the fact that it doesn't feel like one is precisely what makes it hard."

Crow wrote. The quill moved in her talon with a fluency that was almost unsettling, like watching a cat operate a typewriter and realizing it's been doing it for years.

"Now. Demetrios. You cannot fix Demetrios. I want to be clear about that, because I think part of your exhaustion is the energy you're spending trying to make him function as he should. He won't. Some systems don't resolve. The task in front of you is not to repair the mechanism but to find a way to move through it without letting it grind away the parts of you that matter."

He paused. Breathed. The grade was steeper now, and his words came with more space between them.

"Here is something practical. Keep a record—for yourself, not for anyone else—of what you accomplish each week. Not what Demetrios loses. Not what the office fails to do. What *you* did. What you completed. What you pushed through despite the resistance. You will find it is more than you think, and on the days when the work feels like nothing, you will have evidence—"

He stopped.

Crow stopped.

There was a silence.

"—evidence that it is not," he finished.

Crow lifted her quill and looked at him with one round, black, absolutely merciless eye. "You will have evidence that it is not."

"Yes."

"That the work—the work you push through despite the resistance—is not nothing."

"That is what I said."

"Mm-hm."

"It's general advice. It applies broadly."

"To pushing things."

"To *persevering*."

"Through resistance."

"Crow."

"Uphill, one might say."

"It is a common metaphor available to anyone and I will not be prosecuted for using the common language of—"

"Should I add a footnote? 'The author wishes to clarify that this metaphor is entirely coincidental and any resemblance to his own situation, living or mythological, is purely—'"

"Next letter."

"We haven't finished this one."

"Sign it. 'With respect for your endurance, Sisyphus.' Next letter."

Crow made a sound that was unmistakably a laugh—a horrible, croaking, deeply satisfied sound—and signed the letter with a flourish that was, Sisyphus suspected, deliberately illegible.

---

The boulder reached the top. The boulder went back down. Somewhere in the distance, Tartarus made its ambient noise—the deep, thermal groaning of a place that was never quite comfortable with its own existence. It sounded, today, like plumbing.

Sisyphus walked down the hill. His calves ached in a way that had long since become indistinguishable from not-aching. Crow had the next letter already open.

"The perfumed one?" he asked.

"The perfumed one."

---

*Dear Sisyphus,*

*I'll keep this brief because I suspect you'll appreciate brevity more than most. I'm sleeping with two people. They are both aware of each other. No one is being deceived. The arrangement works beautifully in practice but is devastating me in theory. I feel marvelous every evening and guilty every morning. My sister says I'm greedy. My priest says I'm lost. My body says otherwise. I don't want to be told what I'm doing is fine—I know it's fine. I want to understand why I can't stop punishing myself for something that no one involved is asking me to stop.*

*— Greedy in Corinth*

Crow blinked to the end of the letter and then blinked again. She ruffled her feathers in a way that Sisyphus had come to understand meant she was rearranging her opinions.

"Well," she said.

"Well," he said.

"This is—"

"Honest."

"I was going to say 'detailed.' There's a second page. It gets... specific."

"Read the second page."

Crow read the second page. Halfway through, she paused, tilted her head, and said, "Huh." Then she finished.

Sisyphus was in the middle of the hill now. The part that required commitment. His forearms were taut and his breath was the steady, rationed breath of a man who has learned exactly how much air each section of the slope costs.

"Dear Greedy in Corinth," he said. "You are not greedy. Greed takes. You, by your own account, are giving—to two people, fully, with their knowledge and consent. That is not greed. That is generosity complicated by the fact that no one gave you a word for it that isn't borrowed from someone else's disapproval."

He pushed. The boulder moved.

"The guilt you feel in the morning is not yours. It was installed. Your sister gave you some of it. Your priest gave you more. And before them, a long chain of people who were made uncomfortable by joy that didn't fit their architecture. You are living in a house you built, and every morning, someone else's voice tells you the house is wrong. The house is not wrong. The voice is old."

Crow was writing very fast. She had not made a single editorial remark. Sisyphus noted this and chose not to comment on it.

"You asked me not to tell you that what you're doing is fine, so I won't. Instead I'll ask you this: the evenings, when you feel marvelous—what exactly does that feel like? Sit with it. Describe it to yourself, in your own words, not your sister's, not your priest's. If the word that comes is 'whole,' or 'seen,' or 'free,' then you have your answer, and it was always yours. Guilt that isn't rooted in harm done to another person is just an old song stuck in your head. You don't have to sing along."

He paused.

"Sign it—'Without judgment and with more admiration than is probably professional, Sisyphus.'"

Crow finished writing. She folded the letter. She sealed it. Then she said, almost carefully: "That was decent."

"Thank you."

"The 'old song' bit. That worked."

"I know."

"You're insufferable when you know."

"Also known."

---

The summit. The tipping. The sound of the boulder gathering itself into its long, inevitable return, stones scattering, the whole hill briefly vibrating with the physics of failure. The walk back down.

Sisyphus sat, for a moment, at the bottom. Not resting—he didn't rest, exactly—but existing in the pause between efforts the way a breath exists between sentences. Crow landed beside him with the remaining letters fanned out under one wing.

"Two left," she said.

"Save one for tomorrow."

"You always say that, and I always ignore you."

"One day it'll work."

"Today is not that day." She opened the next letter. Her beak moved more slowly over this one. She read it once silently, which she never did.

"Well?" Sisyphus said.

Crow read.

---

*Dear Sisyphus,*

*I don't really have a question. I'm sorry. I know that's not how this works. I just don't know who else to write to.*

*I'm 34. I work. I come home. I work again tomorrow. I have friends but I can't explain to them what's wrong because nothing is wrong. Everything functions. My life looks, from the outside, like a life. But I am so tired of it. Not in a way that frightens me—I'm not in danger, please don't worry—but in a way that confuses me. I always thought that by now I'd have found the thing. The purpose, the thread, the reason you get up and it feels like getting up and not just like obeying gravity.*

*I guess my question is: does it come? The meaning. Or is this it. And if this is it, how do I stop minding?*

*— Just Tired in Athens*

Crow finished reading and did not look up. She studied the letter with the focus she usually reserved for shiny things at a great distance.

Sisyphus said nothing for a while. He set his hands on the boulder. He did not push yet.

"Dear Just Tired," he said. His voice was quiet. Not solemn—he didn't do solemn—but quiet in the way of a room where someone has finally said the thing that has been sitting in the center of every conversation for months.

"Thank you for writing to me. You don't need a question. You never needed a question. Sometimes a letter is just a hand extended into a dark room to see if anyone is there. I am here. Someone is here."

Crow's quill moved without sound.

"You said nothing is wrong, and I believe you. But I want to say something about that nothing, because I think it's heavier than you're giving it credit for. The presence of something wrong is loud—it announces itself, it gathers sympathy, it gives you a story to tell. The absence of something right is silent. It doesn't bruise. It doesn't show.
