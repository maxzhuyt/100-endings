# Lost & Found in Osaka

The logic of Den Den Town is this: everything has a designation. Every component sits in its labeled bin—capacitors sorted by microfarad, resistors by ohm, the whole taxonomy of electronics arranged so that if you know what you're looking for, you will find it. Noa had been coming here since she was twelve, first with her father and then alone, and she had never once felt the particular friction of not knowing what to say or how much space she was allowed to take up. The bins didn't care. The voltage specs didn't care. She could stand in front of a wall of oscillators for twenty minutes reading the back panel of a single unit and the wall would simply wait for her, patient and indifferent in the way that she found, privately, to be the most comfortable kind of attention.

She was seventeen now and she had a list. Patch cables, three meters, mono. A replacement LFO module for the build she'd been working on since February. Possibly—if they had it, and they sometimes did—a second-hand spring reverb unit, the kind that sounded broken in exactly the right way. She had the list in her phone and also, because phones failed, committed to memory. She had a budget. She had a route through the district that minimized backtracking.

She had all of this, and then she heard the guitar.

She didn't decide to follow the sound. She was standing in front of the oscillators and then, some moments later, she was moving toward the arcade entrance, her internal list still running on some back channel she was no longer attending to. The music moved through the district's ambient hum—through the ventilation fans and the recorded announcements and the tinny speaker bleed from a dozen storefronts—and it had a quality she couldn't immediately name. Not loudness. It wasn't loud. It was more that it occupied the air differently than the other sounds did, the way a particular frequency could cut through a mix not by volume but by timbre, by the specific shape of its attack.

She found the source at the arcade entrance on the east side: a girl, maybe nineteen or twenty, sitting on a folding stool with an acoustic guitar across her lap and a small crowd arranged in a loose semicircle around her. The guitar was steel-string and slightly battered. The girl playing it was not. She sat straight-backed with her feet planted and she played the way some people moved through crowds—with the assumption that the space would accommodate her. Brown jacket, dark hair pulled back, one ring on her right hand catching the afternoon light when her fingers shifted position.

She was playing *Yoru ni Kakeru*.

Noa knew the piece. She knew its structure, knew the key changes, knew the specific intervallic skip in the chorus that gave it its feeling of suspension and fall. She had heard it arranged for piano and for orchestra and processed through enough synthesis patches that she could identify them by their envelope settings. She had never heard it played like this—like something private being said out loud, like something that had been held in the chest for a long time and was now being let go with tremendous, unhurried care. The crowd was quiet in the attentive way of people who hadn't expected to feel something and now found themselves feeling it anyway.

Noa stood at the edge. The crowd watched the busker. Nobody watched Noa.

---

The song ended. The crowd gathered itself back into ordinary time—applause, a few coins into the case, people drifting away into the business of Saturday afternoon. The busker stretched her right hand open, checked her tuning on a chord, looked up.

Noa was still there.

The busker looked at her with the direct, unhurried quality of someone who was accustomed to being looked at and was now, with interest, reversing the arrangement.

"You stayed," she said.

"The voicing," Noa said. And stopped. Because that was not—that was not the beginning of the sentence she had meant to say, or possibly it was the middle, or possibly she had not had a sentence at all and that was simply what came out when she opened her mouth. "In the bridge. The chord voicing. Most arrangements go to the four-chord but you—" She stopped again. "It was good. It was—I liked it."

The busker's expression did something that wasn't quite a smile. Her attention had a quality like a lens adjusting. "The voicing," she said.

"The sus4 resolution. Going to the sus4 before the—I'm sorry. Most people would have just said that was good."

"Most people did." She leaned forward with her elbows on the guitar body. "What do you play?"

"I don't. I mean—I build. I build synthesizers. I don't—" Noa's hand described something in the air that was not a word. "Not in the way you do. There's no performing. I just—build them and then use them, and nobody sees that part."

"What does 'use them' mean?"

"It means—" She looked somewhere past the busker's shoulder for a moment. "It means I make sounds."

The busker looked at her for another moment. "Okay," she said, and it didn't sound dismissive. It sounded like the beginning of something.

---

Her name was Sae. She was twenty, a music student, and she busked on weekends because the practice was different when there were people—you couldn't stop, couldn't edit, couldn't go back. Noa learned this as they stood at the arcade entrance after the crowd had fully dispersed, having arrived at the conversation sideways, through Noa's attempt to explain what she was doing in Den Den Town on a Saturday, which had required explaining the LFO module, which had required explaining what an LFO was, which had somehow led here.

"I'm in Den Den Town," Noa said, "because there's a part I need. For a build I've been working on. It's a modular—a synthesizer, but modular means you patch the components together with cables, so you design the signal flow every time. You're not fixed to one instrument. It's more like—" She paused, aware suddenly that she was talking with the velocity of someone who had been asked a question they had been waiting a long time to be asked. "Sorry. This isn't—"

"What sound are you chasing?"

Noa looked at her. "What?"

"You said you've been working on it since February. You must be chasing a specific sound."

The specific sound was this: she had heard, on a record she couldn't now identify, a synthesizer texture that sat underneath everything else in the mix like something geological—not a melody, not an effect, just a presence, like the sound of a room that had held something important a long time ago. She had been trying to reconstruct it for eight months. She had built most of the signal chain from scratch. The LFO was the last piece.

She explained this. She explained the filter resonance she was working toward, and the way certain waveforms behaved at low frequencies in ways that were mathematically predictable and yet, in practice, continued to surprise her—the satisfaction of that, the way a thing could be precisely understood and still have room in it to be strange. She explained the specific pleasure of a parameter that did exactly what it was supposed to do. She explained, somewhere in the middle of all of this, that she found it easier to work with sounds she had built herself because then she understood everything about them, their entire history, nothing hidden.

At some point she became aware that Sae had not said anything for a while and that this was not because she was waiting for Noa to stop, but because she was listening. Not the polite listening that people performed when they needed the sound to end before they could respond. The other kind.

"The appeal," Sae said, when Noa finally paused, "is that you know where it came from."

"Yes." 

"You made it, so it can't surprise you in a bad way."

Noa opened her mouth and then closed it again, because she had not thought about it in those terms and now that she had she felt something in the vicinity of being understood, which was a feeling she did not have a robust framework for.

"I think," she said slowly, "that's probably true."

---

The fireworks were not supposed to happen at four-thirty in the afternoon.

Later, someone would mention a grand opening—a new pachinko parlor two blocks over, or possibly a real estate office, or a chain restaurant with theatrical ambitions—but at the moment of the first crack and bloom overhead, there was no context, only the sudden white fracture in the sky and then the sound catching up to it, not a single explosion but a sequence, rolling through the district's canyon of storefronts in a way that the bones registered before the ears did.

The crowd that had mostly dispersed re-materialized from nowhere, compressing at the arcade entrance to look up, and Noa was abruptly in the middle of it, pressed on three sides, and then there was a hand around her wrist—not grabbing, just finding, the way you'd reach for something in the dark—and she looked down and then up and Sae was next to her, guitar case over one shoulder, both of them looking at the sky as another sequence detonated, gold this time, spreading and fading.

"I wasn't expecting that," Noa said. The words felt inadequate and also exactly right.

Sae laughed—a real laugh, not performed, startled out of her by the absurdity of it, and Noa found that she was laughing too, which was also not a thing she had decided to do. The fireworks kept going. Someone to their left cheered. The smoke drifted south in the light. Sae's hand was still loosely at her wrist and neither of them had acknowledged this and Noa was aware of it with the total, helpless awareness of someone who has decided not to move.

The last firework went up and spread and faded into the haze. The crowd exhaled. The normal noise of the district reasserted itself—the fans, the speakers, the recorded voices—and it sounded different now, or perhaps she was hearing it differently, from a slightly different place than the one she'd been standing in before.

Sae dropped her hand. "Are you hungry?"

---

Noa did the calculus. It was not a simple or a fast calculation and she was aware of Sae watching her do it with what appeared to be patient and genuine curiosity rather than the particular discomfort that her hesitations sometimes produced in people.

The variables were: she did not know this person. She had not finished her list. She had not eaten since the rice ball at nine this morning. She was aware, in a way that she didn't normally have occasion to be, of her own reflection in the darkened storefront glass beside them—her oversized linen shirt, her specific and unstrategic hair, the general impression she gave of someone who had dressed for a task rather than for being looked at.

The variables were also: she had stopped thinking about the LFO module somewhere in the middle of explaining it.

"Kaitenzushi?" Sae said. "There's one on the next block. We can be done before your Den Den Town place closes."

"I know the one you mean," Noa said. "I've eaten there alone before." She heard herself say this and understood immediately that it was one of those disclosures she made without meaning to—the kind that placed more of herself in the open than she'd intended. "I like the salmon roe."

"Me too." Sae picked up her guitar case. "Shall we?"

Noa said yes. It came out clearly and without revision, which surprised her.

They walked through Den Den Town together, east toward the restaurant. The afternoon light had shifted while they were standing at the arcade—it was lower now, coming at a different angle through the gaps between buildings—and the district looked slightly changed, or possibly the same and she was seeing it from a different calibration. She was aware of the space between them, the practiced ease with which Sae moved through a crowd, the way Noa's own walking felt newly visible to her, self-conscious in a way that walking through Den Den Town alone never was.

She was also aware that she had stopped thinking about how she walked somewhere around the second storefront.

"Can I ask you something," Sae said.

"Yes."

"The sound you're building—the one from the record. When you find it, what do you do with it?"

Noa thought about this. "I don't know," she said. "I've never finished before."

Sae nodded, like this was a complete answer. Then: "Maybe this time."

Noa did not respond to this. But she didn't look away from it either. She carried it alongside the other things she was carrying—the uncompleted list, the afternoon's surprise of itself—as they turned the corner and the restaurant's sign came into view, and Sae held the door, and the warm smell of rice and dashi reached them, and they went in.

---

The conveyor belt moved at its fixed, unhurried pace. Small plates went past them in a procession—salmon, mackerel, egg, the bright-orange ikura that Noa had mentioned, a pale slice of squid she couldn't identify, a matcha pudding in a ceramic cup.

Sae pointed at something small and pale going by. "What is that?"

Noa looked at the plate. "Tai, I think. Sea bream. The white fish—it says *tai* on the—"

"I always say it wrong. I say *tie* and they look at me."

"*Tah-ee*," Noa said. "Two syllables."

"*Tah-ee*." Sae tried it. Made a sound like she wasn't convinced her mouth had done it correctly.

"That was right."

"Was it?"

"Yes." Noa reached past her and collected the pale plate as it came around again. Set it between them. "Try it and tell me if I'm correct about the identification."

Sae tried it. Considered. "You're correct," she said. A beat. "I'm glad you stayed."

The belt moved. A red plate went past, and a white one, and a green one with something folded neatly on it. Noa reached for the ikura and felt the small familiar pleasure of the plate in her hand—the ceramic cool and then warming, the weight of it, the ordinary specificity of a thing that was exactly what it appeared to be.

"So am I," she said. 

The belt kept moving. Outside, somewhere, the last of the festival smoke was drifting over Den Den Town in the early evening light, and neither of them was thinking about leaving yet.
