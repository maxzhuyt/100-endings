# Sisyphus Gives Advice

The boulder weighed approximately the same as it always had. This was one of the few constants in the underworld—that, and the filing.

Crow had the filing.

He sat on a ledge of black basalt at roughly shoulder height to a standing man, which put him at convenient speaking distance to Sisyphus during the uphill portions of the route. He was a large crow, professionally glossy, with the expression of someone who has been repeatedly promised a promotion and has begun to regard the promise itself as an insult. Before him, wedged into a crack in the rock with the patience of long practice, was a clipboard. Attached to the clipboard was a sheaf of correspondence. In his right talon he held a quill. In his left he held a small pot of ink with a fitted lid, because the route was uneven and he had learned.

"Next," said Sisyphus.

Sisyphus was a broad man going gray at the temples, dressed in the practical manner of someone who had long since made his peace with not impressing anyone. His hands were calloused in specific places. He pushed the boulder with his shoulder on the steeper sections and with both palms on the moderate grade, and he had opinions about the grade—not complaints, opinions, the way a carpenter has opinions about a particular wood's tendency to split.

Crow turned a page.

"Thessaly," he said. "Unsigned, which means she signed it 'Worried in Thessaly,' which she seems to think is different. She writes—" He cleared his throat in the manner of someone who does not strictly need to clear their throat but finds it useful for establishing register. "She writes that her partner of eleven years does not listen to her. She says she will be speaking and can see from his eyes that he has stopped receiving the information approximately three words in. She has tried: speaking more slowly, speaking more briefly, writing things down, and what she describes as 'meaningful pauses,' although she acknowledges she may not be deploying them correctly. She asks what she is doing wrong."

Sisyphus was quiet for a moment. The boulder made a sound against the slope that was less like grinding and more like settling, a minor seismic adjustment. He shifted his shoulder an inch.

"She's asking," he said, "if it's something she's doing."

"That's what she's asking, yes."

"Tell her it isn't. Tell her she's spending her energy finding a better way to be heard by someone who has made a private decision not to hear her. Tell her the decision isn't about her. It predates her, probably. Some people build a very small room inside themselves very early and they never let anyone actually in, they just show people around the outside and hope that's enough." He paused. "Tell her to notice whether he listens to anyone. His friends, a boss, anyone. If he does, she has a different problem than she thinks. If he doesn't, she has the correct problem."

Crow wrote. His penmanship was excellent and he knew it.

"And the meaningful pauses?" he said.

"Tell her the pauses are fine. Tell her to stop worrying about the pauses."

Crow made a note. The boulder reached a particular outcropping it always reached, and Sisyphus adjusted his grip without looking, both thumbs finding the familiar groove.

"Next," he said.

---

Crow turned two pages at once, examined the second, turned it back.

"This one is longer," he said.

"How long."

"He's written on both sides, which I've noted for the records as a discourtesy."

"Read it anyway."

Crow read. The writer—a man, from the handwriting and the specific quality of his obliviousness—worked in what he described as a demanding field, though he did not specify the field, which Crow considered additionally inconsiderate. He worked, he said, from before the sun came up until after it went down. He had not eaten a meal at a table in four months. He could not identify the last time he had done something that was not work or the logistical maintenance of himself as a work-capable unit. He was aware, he wrote, that this was not healthy. He was aware. He was asking how to stop.

"He's asking how to stop," Crow said, in summary.

"He knows how to stop," Sisyphus said. "He picks a time. He stops at that time. When the work comes back to him—and it will, it'll come back—he notices it came back, and he puts it down again."

"He'll say that's not realistic."

"Tell him his current arrangement is also not realistic. It's just a different kind of not realistic that he's had longer to get used to." He was breathing slightly harder now, near the top of the current uphill section. "Tell him—" He exhaled. "Tell him that the work will always be there when he comes back to it, and that this is not a reason to never leave it. It's just a fact. The work doesn't need him to feel guilty. The work doesn't notice."

Crow wrote this down. Then he said, without particular emphasis: "You've split an infinitive."

"I've split—"

"'To never leave it.' Textbook."

Sisyphus turned his head approximately fifteen degrees, which was as far as was practical. "Crow."

"I'm noting it for the records."

"Don't note it for the records."

"It's already noted." Crow re-inked his quill. "Shall I fix it in the final copy?"

"Do whatever you think is right."

"I always do," said Crow, which was true and both of them knew it.

The boulder crested the section and the grade shifted. Sisyphus's breathing settled. Crow made one small additional note involving the filing category—*Occupational, Boundaries, Self-Inflicted*—and turned to the next page.

"After this one," he said, "there's a deity."

"What kind."

"The prefectural kind. He's filed a complaint."

"In triplicate?"

"Is there another way?"

Sisyphus made a sound that was not quite a sigh and not quite a laugh. "Read the next letter," he said.

---

The letter was from a woman who was having an entirely reasonable amount of difficulty with her sleep schedule, her appetite, and her ability to complete sentences without losing the end of them somewhere in the middle. She was thirty-four. She had recently been passed over for a promotion she had earned, in favor of someone who had not, and could not, she believed she was, that is to say—

"That's the whole sentence," Crow said. "She leaves it there."

"I know what she means."

"So do I. I'm recording it as written."

Sisyphus pushed. "Tell her she isn't losing her mind. Tell her losing a thing you earned to someone who didn't earn it creates a very specific kind of wound that looks like confusion from the inside and looks like depression from the outside and is actually—" He thought for a moment, and the boulder went slightly sideways, and he corrected it without looking. "It's actually a crisis of evidence. She's spent a long time believing that effort produces outcomes, and now she has evidence that it sometimes simply doesn't, and her whole system for making sense of the world needs recalibrating, and that hurts, and the hurt is correct and proportionate."

Crow's quill paused.

"And then what?" he said.

"Tell her the recalibration is worth doing. Effort still matters. The connection is just—" He considered. "Noisier than advertised. Tell her to rest if she can. Tell her the sentences will come back." He shifted his weight. "Some days you push and the ground changes under you partway up and you spend the whole climb just keeping the thing from rolling sideways into nothing, and at the end of it you're at the top anyway. That still counts."

Crow finished writing.

He did not say anything about this. He turned the page.

---

The deity arrived from the direction of the administrative quarter, which was downhill and smelled of sulfur and vellum. He was not large enough to be important, which had given him, over the centuries, the tight, effortful bearing of someone who compensates. His robes were the color of official displeasure. He was carrying three copies of a document, and even from a distance it was possible to sense that he had been looking forward to carrying them.

"I'll handle the summary," Crow said, under his breath.

The deity drew up to them with the bearing of someone who has rehearsed.

"Sisyphus," he began.

"Hello," said Sisyphus, pleasantly. He did not stop pushing.

The deity produced the documents. He spoke at some length about: the Bureau of Eternal Penalties and its mandate, the deterrent function of exemplary punishment, the concept of dignity as it pertained to eternal sentences, the problematic optics of condemned souls appearing to find their situation *sustainable*, and, finally, a specific clause in what he referred to as the Foundational Torment Statutes, which Crow could have recited from memory having heard it referenced on eleven previous visits across three administrations.

"Mm," said Sisyphus, when the deity appeared to have reached a natural pause.

"The column," the deity said, with emphasis, "is not sanctioned."

"I know," said Sisyphus.

"It reflects poorly on—"

"Yes."

The deity stopped. He had prepared for argument. He had, possibly, prepared for contrition. He had not, it seemed, prepared for *yes*, delivered in the tone of someone who understood the deity's point and found it genuinely uninteresting.

Sisyphus said: "The people write. I write back. If you'd like to file a formal injunction I won't stop you." He adjusted his grip. "You know where the Bureau is."

The deity stood for a moment. He was experiencing, visibly, the particular frustration of a man who has come to a fight and found no one willing to have it.

"This isn't over," he said.

"No," agreed Sisyphus, in a tone suggesting that very few things were.

The deity left the way he came. Crow watched him go with the expression of a professional who has clocked the interaction and found it within expected parameters.

"Eleven," he said.

"Is that the count?"

"By administration. Forty-three overall." He made a note. "Next letter."

---

The letter was from someone who was not sure how to begin.

They said this—*I'm not sure how to begin*—and then they began anyway, which Crow considered the correct approach even if the writer did not know they had made it.

The letter was about a mother. The mother had views. The views were about: how a house should be kept, what constituted a real profession, the specific failure represented by the writer's marriage, the failure represented by the writer's neighborhood, the failure represented by the writer's hair, and, perhaps most pressingly, the failure represented by the writer's apparent contentment with all of the above failures—which was, the mother had made clear, the most troubling failure of all, because it suggested a fundamental misunderstanding of what contentment was *for*.

"She says," Crow read, more slowly than usual, "that she loves her mother. She says this very clearly, then says it again."

Sisyphus nodded.

"She loves her mother," Crow continued, "and finds her mother genuinely incomprehensible, and spends every visit trying to translate between the language she speaks and the language her mother speaks, and after each visit feels that she has failed at the translation again, not because her mother is unkind, necessarily, but because her mother looks at her and seems to see someone entirely different from the person she knows herself to be, and she can't decide if she should keep trying or if the trying is itself the problem."

The quill rested on the page.

Sisyphus was quiet long enough that the slope made itself heard—the friction of the boulder, the specific silence of the underworld in between.

"Tell her," he said, and stopped.

He started again. "Tell her that loving someone and being unable to reach them are not contradictions. Tell her some distances are real. Not metaphors—actual distances, in the same way that some mountains are steeper than others, not because of anything you did. She hasn't failed at a translation. There are languages that don't share roots. You can learn everything there is to learn about both languages and some things still won't cross." He paused. "Tell her the trying isn't the problem. Tell her to try without expecting to arrive. It's different. Harder, at first, and then—" His voice did something minor. "Then it's just what you do with someone you can't reach but won't leave."

Crow wrote.

He adjusted his grip on the quill and wrote the rest more slowly, not for any reason he would have volunteered.

"Next," said Sisyphus, after a while.

---

The final letter Crow read in the tone he reserved for things he considered professionally unremarkable regardless of their content.

The letter was from a man who wished to know what to do about the fact that he was in love with his neighbor's wife, who was, he had come to believe, also in love with him—or possibly in love with the version of herself she was when she was around him, which he acknowledged was connected but might not be the same thing—and who was, further, unhappily married to a man she respected but did not, as she had told him on one occasion, feel particularly *seen* by, and who had recently taken the step of leaving a small potted herb on his windowsill with no explanation, which he was—the letter writer—interpreting as significant, and he wanted to know: was it significant? And: what should he do?

Crow set the page down. He looked at it. He looked at Sisyphus.

"He's asking about a basil plant," Crow said.

"He's asking about a great many things," said Sisyphus. "Some of them have to do with the plant." He thought for a moment. "Tell him that leaving a plant on a windowsill is an invitation to a conversation, not a conclusion to one. Tell him to go talk to her—outside, somewhere with other people around—and to ask her, directly, what she wants, and to listen to all of it, including the parts that aren't what he's hoping for." He shifted the boulder fractionally. "Tell him that being seen is not the same as being loved, and being loved is not the same as being chosen, and she has choices in front of her that are more complicated than he's letting himself think about. Tell him to let her make them."

He considered.

"Tell him to water the basil in the meantime. It'll die otherwise."

Crow wrote this down. He sealed the letter without editorial comment, added it to the outgoing stack, and made a notation in the filing column: *Interpersonal, Desire, Botanical Dimension*.

The slope ran upward. The boulder moved.

Crow capped the ink.

"That's the afternoon post," he said.

Sisyphus said nothing. He was thinking about the angle, the particular resistance of the ground here, whether the left side wanted more push than the right. He made a small adjustment and the boulder tracked straight. The sky of the underworld maintained its usual position on the subject of hope, which was to say it had no position, being simply the dark above a place where things continued.

The boulder moved and he moved with it, shoulder to stone, ascending.

Crow turned to the evening mail.
