# Therapeutic Boundaries

The room smells like lavender.

That's the first wrong thing. I catalogued it the moment I pushed open the door—a diffuser on the windowsill, ceramic, pale blue, quietly releasing something floral into air that should by rights smell like rubber mats and antiseptic. It's such a deliberate softness, such a careful intervention against this room's essential purpose, that I find it almost offensive. Like putting a throw pillow in a boxing ring.

I know the room's essential purpose. I've spent enough time in places like this over the last eight months that I've developed opinions about their design philosophy. The ratio of window to wall. Whether there's a clock visible from the table—there is, here, which is either considerate or cruel. The treatment table's paper cover, whether it's the smooth kind or the textured kind. This one is smooth. I note that.

I sit on the edge of it and wait. The paper crinkles under my weight and then goes quiet. Above me, a laminated poster of the human musculoskeletal system stares back at me with the flayed authority of a thing that has no secrets left. I recognize parts of myself in it. The gracilis. The iliotibial band. The posterior labrum, illustrated here in its ideal state, holding the femoral head exactly as it was designed to hold it. Mine no longer holds that way. Mine has been surgically repaired and is learning, slowly, to hold in a different way that will have to be good enough.

There's a second poster beside it: the rotator cuff, in cross-section. On the counter, a model spine, three anatomy textbooks, a jar of pens. The room is arranged for learning. I catalog every element and file it under threat assessment, which tells me something about myself, and I let the information land without doing anything with it.

The door opens.

I know it's him before I look up, which is irrational. I haven't seen Marcus Adeyemi in four years. But there's something in the way his footsteps distribute his weight—heel-light, the particular stride of someone who spent years learning not to telegraph movement, not to give the race away before it began—that my body identifies before my brain knows what it's identifying. I have spent my entire adult life reading bodies in motion. My body apparently still does this work when I've given it no instruction to.

"Kayla."

He sets a folder on the counter. His voice is the same as I remembered and also different—lower, or I held it wrong in memory. Four years is time enough to distort a voice. "It's been a while."

"It has."

He looks well. This is not a neutral observation, and I don't let myself make it like one. He looks well in the specific way of someone who has completed a transition I have not—not just survived the end of a competitive career but actually arrived somewhere on the other side of it, settled into a new competence and wearing it without self-consciousness. The way a scar looks when it's finished becoming scar tissue. His scrubs are navy. He has more gray at his temples than he did at the last camp we shared, which was Daegu, four years ago, before either of us knew Daegu would be the last time.

His wrists, I notice in passing and against my better judgment, he no longer tapes.

"Former training partners isn't a situation we usually encounter," he says. He's professional in his phrasing. He gives me the exit. "We can request someone else if that feels like it would—"

"I've had six physiotherapists in eight months." I meet his eyes for the first time and hold them. "You came recommended."

A pause that contains something neither of us names. He opens the folder.

---

The intake assessment is language I know. He asks about pain on a scale of one to ten and I have learned to use decimals, because a five and a five-point-five are not the same thing and the difference matters. He asks what movements provoke, and I list them: stairs on descent, sustained external rotation, the specific loading angle of a push-off I cannot currently execute. He asks about sleep, about swelling, about whether I've been performing the home exercise program the clinic in Calgary prescribed.

I have. I list the exercises and the sets and the compliance rate as though reading from a chart. He writes. His pen moves in quick, economical marks, the same economy I remember in everything he did on the track—nothing wasted, nothing performed, each action in service of the next. I watch him write and then look at the ceiling.

"Stand for me."

I slide off the table. He watches me walk to the far wall and back. His gaze is technical, moving across me the way a surveyor reads terrain—he's reading for the compensation patterns, the hitched hip, the forward lean I've developed to protect the joint on loading. He sees everything I've learned to hide and he sees it immediately because hiding things from this kind of assessment requires knowing which things to hide, and a physiotherapist who ran the relay circuit for a decade knows where to look.

"Remove your shoes."

I do. He comes forward and crouches in front of me without ceremony—this transition from standing to crouching, direct, unannounced—and places both hands around my right ankle. His thumbs find the anterior talofibular ligament with a precision that communicates knowledge of exactly what happened to me and what he expects to find, and the touch is completely professional, and my nervous system does not receive it that way but that is my nervous system's problem.

"Tell me when you feel resistance."

He rotates my foot into dorsiflexion, slowly, watching my face. I look at the anatomy poster on the wall.

"There." The word comes out half a tone tighter than I want it to. He makes a note, and we continue.

---

The table is angled. He positions my right leg with my heel on his shoulder, and I know this technique—standard hamstring mobilization, first-year curriculum, nothing anomalous—and knowing its name and its rationale does not change the geometry of it, which requires that his face be level with my knee and my back be flat and my hands be folded on my sternum with nowhere else to be. I look at the ceiling. He looks at the angle of my hip.

"Breathe out as I move into it."

He presses forward, knee extended, and I feel the pull begin along the posterior chain. Not pain—not yet, not quite—but the deep, structural resistance of months of protective tightening being asked to account for itself. The hamstring has learned to guard the joint. The hamstring is not wrong to guard the joint. This is the argument we are having.

"Good. Stay there."

His right hand is flat against the back of my knee. His left guides my heel. I can feel the steadiness of both points of contact, the distinct temperature of each palm, and I focus on this the way I once focused on the sound of starter pistols—with the total, enforced attention of someone who cannot afford to miss the signal.

"What number?"

"Five." I consider. "Five and a half."

"That's appropriate." He holds the position. I count the seconds because it gives my mind a task. Thirty seconds is a long time when you are counting it. He says, "There's significant restriction in the proximal attachment. We'll work into it gradually."

"How gradually?"

"As gradually as it needs." He meets my eyes briefly over my knee. "Not a race."

He lowers my leg with deliberate care and says nothing else about it. We repeat on the left.

---

We talk while he works. He moves through the hip internal rotation sequence and asks about my training history—four years, national team, two relay gold, the ACL, the complications, the surgery that corrected the surgical complications, and then the long strange country of the eight months after. He knows most of this from my referral file. He asks anyway, which means he's asking me to choose how to tell it.

"The return-to-training timeline they gave me after the first surgery was sixteen weeks," I say to the ceiling. His palm is on the lateral aspect of my thigh, guiding the rotation, while his other hand stabilizes my pelvis just above the iliac crest. "Then the complication added another twelve. Then—"

"I know the timeline," he says, gently. "How are you feeling about the recovery?"

The question sounds clinical. It almost is.

"I'm doing it," I say. "I'm here, I'm doing all the exercises, I'm showing up."

"That's not what I asked."

I look at him. He doesn't look up from my hip.

"No," I agree. "It's not."

He advances into the rotation—a degree, two—and something releases. Not the joint: the iliotibial band, finally giving an inch it's been hoarding since September, and the release travels up through the hip and into the lower back in a warm, reluctant wave, and I make a sound. Not pain. Adjacent to pain. On the border of pain where the territory doesn't have a good name.

He goes still.

The room is quiet. The diffuser emits. The second hand of the clock that I can see from the table moves through one full rotation and I am aware of every degree of it.

He sets my leg back onto the table in the same way he has set it down twenty times in this hour—with both hands, with graduated care—and he picks up his pen and makes a mark in the folder and says: "That's the range we're working toward. You'll feel that in the morning."

"I know," I say.

My voice is professional. His voice is professional. We are both professional.

He moves to the counter and writes his clinical notes. He schedules the next appointment. Thursday, eleven o'clock. He reads the appointment out to me and I repeat it back, and this small exchange—the information, the confirmation—structures the next forty-eight hours into something with coordinates. I find I am grateful for this in a way I am not going to examine.

I put on my shoes. I collect my bag. At the door I stop—a half-second, no more, which is not long enough to constitute a pause, not long enough to mean anything—and then I step into the hallway.

The hallway smells like nothing at all. Linoleum and recycled air and the faint antiseptic I expected from the treatment room, which kept it for itself. The ceiling is white. Around me, two nurses pass with purpose; someone in a waiting room turns a page; the ordinary architecture of a building with no diffuser in it resumes.

I walk to the elevator. My left hip is warm and my right one aches in the specific purposeful way he said it would, the way that means the tissue is being asked to change, and change is not comfortable, and the discomfort is not a problem.

Thursday. I'll be better prepared.

I turn that over on the elevator down.

I will probably not be better prepared. I will be exactly as prepared as I am now, which is to say: professionally, fully, and in no other way whatsoever. This is the thing about training partners—you cannot un-know each other's tells. He read my hip the way he always read a relay exchange, with the easy precision of someone who has simply looked long enough to stop trying.

The elevator deposits me into the lobby. Outside, the afternoon is ordinary—cold and overcast, the sky doing nothing dramatic, the parking lot full of sensible cars. I find mine. I sit in it without starting it for a moment.

The lavender is probably still diffusing.

That changes nothing. It was never meant to.

I start the car.
