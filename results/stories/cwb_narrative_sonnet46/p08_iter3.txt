# Stolen Moments in Roma

The motorcycle came out of nowhere, which was not strictly true—Alessandra had been coming out of nowhere at approximately sixty kilometers per hour for the better part of a decade—but Ben Hartley, who had been deeply absorbed in the Trevi Fountain chapter of his 2019 Rough Guide to Italy, experienced it as an act of God.

She braked. He lurched backward. The guidebook hit the cobblestones with a sound like a small tragedy. A cascade of Italian followed, musical and absolutely furious, and Ben's response—"Oh, terribly sorry, I do beg your pardon, I genuinely thought I was on the pavement"—landed on her like three wet fish dropped from a considerable height.

She pulled off her helmet. Dark hair, dark eyes, the expression of someone who has been grievously wronged by the universe and has decided to take it personally.

"You thought you were on the *pavement*." Her English was excellent and wielded with surgical contempt. "This is not pavement. This is *Roma*. The pavement is"—she gestured comprehensively at the surrounding city—"somewhere that is not here."

"Yes," Ben said. "I see that now."

Behind him the fountain roared, twenty-six metric tons of Baroque indifference. Around them, the crowd had redistributed to accommodate the spectacle and was now watching with the particular Roman attention that stops short of actual intervention.

"You could have been killed," she said. "Or I could have been killed."

"I'm most sincerely—"

"Or *both* of us could have been killed, which would have been very bad for the fountain."

"I genuinely thought—"

"You were not thinking. You were reading." She said *reading* the way other people said *jaywalking* or *arson*. "In the middle of the road. In front of the most photographed fountain in Europe. With a book that is five years out of date."

Ben looked at the guidebook on the cobblestones. He had bought it secondhand. He considered explaining this and decided against it.

Something in his silence—its complete absence of defensiveness, its puppyish, load-bearing remorse—made her stop. She looked at him properly for the first time. He looked back. He was tall and slightly professorial, wearing a linen jacket that belonged on a different continent in a different decade, and he was looking at her with an expression of such genuine, dignified contrition that she felt the anger go sideways in her chest, which annoyed her.

She laughed. It was brief and involuntary and it clearly annoyed her further.

"Where are you trying to go?" she asked, her voice stripped now of its formal fury.

"I was attempting," Ben said, with some care, "to look at the fountain."

"You are one meter from the fountain."

"I was navigating emotionally."

She studied him. He retrieved his guidebook from the ground and held it in both hands like a man presenting evidence at his own trial. The crowd, sensing the drama had peaked, dispersed. She leaned on her motorcycle and crossed her arms and thought about her afternoon, which had been a series of deliveries across a city that had lately felt like a maze she'd memorized so thoroughly it had stopped meaning anything.

"Give me that," she said.

"I beg your—"

She took the guidebook, examined its cover with an expression of genuine pity, and slid it into the side compartment of her courier bag. "You don't need this. It will make you stupid." She snapped the compartment shut. "I am Alessandra. I will show you Rome."

Ben opened his mouth. He closed it. He thought about his afternoon, which had consisted of a solo lunch at a restaurant explicitly recommended to tourists, forty minutes of note-taking on the symbolic vocabulary of Baroque fountains, and a gelato he'd eaten standing at a bin because all the nearby benches had been occupied by people who appeared to be living their actual lives.

"All right," he said.

---

She took him first to the Campo de' Fiori, cutting through traffic on the motorcycle with the total serenity of someone for whom traffic laws have always been a theoretical framework rather than a binding agreement. Ben sat on the pillion with both hands gripping the seat and his feet searching for purchase and a very specific expression on his face—the expression of British stoicism under duress, which is to say: serene on the surface, catastrophizing at depth, determined to give nothing away.

Alessandra drove with her body, leaning into turns, nudging pedestrians aside with the horn, slipping through gaps in traffic that shouldn't have existed and ceased to exist the moment she passed through them. She rode the way she apparently did everything—assuming the world would make room.

The market smell hit him before they arrived: cut flowers, warm vegetable, coffee, fish. Stalls packed so close they overlapped. She moved through it as through water, fluent, unhurried, stopping to handle a blood orange and put it back, to sample an olive and hand him one without asking whether he wanted it, to argue briefly about price on a bunch of herbs she apparently had no intention of buying.

At a stall near the far end of the campo, she stopped at a display of handmade ceramics—small tiles, painted dishes, beaded things, a rooster with an improbable amount of dignity painted in cobalt blue on a white square. She picked the tile up. Held it to the light. Turned it over and asked the vendor its price with an intake of breath that conveyed, at once, deep admiration for the craftsmanship and deep offense at the extravagance.

Ben had been watching a woman at the next stall lose an extended argument with a pigeon over a corner of focaccia and was not, therefore, watching Alessandra's left hand, which moved from the tile to her jacket pocket in the same unhurried gesture, continuous as water over stone.

They were walking before he'd turned back.

"The rooster was nice," Ben said.

"Too expensive," Alessandra said.

---

She took him for coffee at a bar near the Pantheon—dark wood, amber light, the snap of espresso cups on saucers, football on a muted television above the bar. She ordered for both of them without consultation, which Ben found he did not mind. He was learning, with some speed, that being with Alessandra required the suspension of several habits, including the habit of agency.

The man at the bar was heavyset and cheerful in the manner of someone perpetually entertained by the gap between people's intentions and their outcomes. He saw Alessandra from across the room and his face did the particular thing that faces do when they see someone they have known long enough to have opinions about—warmth and wariness arriving in the same expression.

"*Ale*." He came around the bar and embraced her with the careful warmth of old familiarity. Then he assessed Ben with visible interest. "*Inglese*," he said.

"Guilty," Ben said.

"Marco," the man said, and shook his hand with a firmness that was thorough without being aggressive. "You are seeing the city?"

"I'm being shown it," Ben said. "There's a distinction."

Marco appeared to find this funnier than it warranted. "*Esatto*. Yes. This is correct." He looked at Alessandra with a fondness that contained a specific kind of knowledge. "She is a good guide. The best." He paused. "Also the worst."

Alessandra said something in Italian that made Marco laugh and wave it off. Then the barista called something from down the bar and Alessandra turned, already beginning to argue, already moving.

Marco leaned slightly across the bar.

"She's my old friend," he said, quietly, with the care of someone choosing words that will be heard accurately. "I love her. But she's—" he thought "—*furba*. Wily. You understand the word? She watches people. She wants to see what they do." He smiled, not unkindly. "She puts people in situations and she watches."

"What kind of situations?"

"Various." He tapped the bar twice, a kind of gentle punctuation. "Just keep your eyes open, my friend. That's all I'm saying." He straightened as Alessandra returned, his expression converting seamlessly back to warmth. "She drove you here on that motorcycle, yes? You're very brave."

"Or very trusting," Ben said.

Marco looked at him. "These are not the same thing," he said.

Alessandra said something sharp in Italian. Marco responded with something that made her narrow her eyes and smile at the same time. They argued for another two minutes with the fluency of people who have been arguing for years and find it, on balance, enjoyable. Then she was pulling Ben by the sleeve back into the afternoon.

He kept Marco's words in his jacket pocket alongside a question he hadn't asked and wasn't sure how to form.

---

The rooftop was reached through a building he wasn't sure they were authorized to enter, via a stairwell that smelled of old stone and somebody's dinner, through a door at the top that was secured by a lock Alessandra opened with a key she produced from somewhere inside her jacket without any particular explanation.

Ben followed her out and stopped.

Rome opened below them in the late afternoon light—terracotta and ochre and the green of umbrella pines and somewhere the copper dome of something ancient, everything going amber in the sun that had dropped to a long, generous angle. The sounds of the city arrived softly: a scooter, a bell, someone calling from a window below.

"This is not somewhere we're supposed to be," Ben said.

"No."

"That key—"

"I know someone," she said, which closed the subject.

She moved to the railing and leaned on it, looking out. She had shed something up here—the forward momentum, the performing of certainty. She looked at the city the way you look at something that has cost you something.

"You live here," Ben said. It came out as something between a fact and an accusation of unfair advantage.

"All my life." She was quiet a moment. "Sometimes I want to throw the whole thing into the sea." She said it without heat, almost conversationally. "The tourists and the politicians and the history everywhere, always—this weight of it, always."

"But you don't mean it."

She was silent long enough that he thought she might not answer. "No," she said finally. "I would miss it more than anything in the world." The words were simple, unguarded, delivered to the horizon rather than to him. "There is nowhere else. There is nowhere I want to be instead. This is the problem."

Ben looked at her. She was still looking out at the city, and there was something in the line of her profile—not vulnerability, exactly, but the shadow of it, the outline.

He opened his mouth.

"You're going to fall off if you stand like that," she said, nodding toward his feet, which were too close to the edge.

He adjusted. She pushed off the railing and turned back toward the door, and by the time she was moving again she had put herself back together entirely, and the moment had closed over like water over a stone that has been dropped.

---

She took him to Trastevere when the light began to change—the early evening softness that Rome does better than any city has a right to—and then across a bridge she refused to name on the grounds that if he wanted to know which bridge it was, he should have kept his guidebook. They walked without a fixed direction, which turned out to be, Ben was realizing, the only direction she ever moved.

She showed him a doorway carved with a face whose mouth was a hollow through which, centuries ago, anonymous accusations had been slipped to the city's authorities. She showed him a mark on a wall where a flood had reached in 1870, halfway up a building, an old disaster made permanent. She showed him a tiny bar with three tables in a space the size of a generous wardrobe, where a man who appeared to be approximately one hundred years old poured them wine from an unlabeled bottle and asked no questions.

"How do you know all of this?" Ben asked.

"I've been here all my life," she said. "And I pay attention."

"To what?"

She turned her glass in her hand. "To what's underneath the version that people agree to see." She said it lightly, as if it were obvious, as if everyone did this. "Every city has the version for the outside and the version for the people inside it. Rome has ten versions. Maybe a hundred." She glanced up. "Yours is the version with the fountain and the guidebook."

"And yours?"

She smiled. It was not evasive, exactly. It was the smile of someone who has been asked a question they have no particular intention of answering. "Mine is this," she said, and gestured at the unlabeled wine and the ancient barman and the narrow room. "Sometimes."

He let it go. He was learning when to let things go, which was not a skill he had arrived in Rome with.

---

She returned him to the Trevi Fountain at dusk because, she said, it was structurally necessary.

"Structurally," he said.

"You began here. You should end here. Otherwise the day has no shape."

The evening crowd was different from the afternoon crowd—looser, warmer, the particular ease of people who have eaten and drunk and released the obligation to see things. The fountain was lit now, the water white against the stone, and it was, Ben thought, objectively absurd—overscaled, operatic, completely earnest about its own grandeur—and also, in the falling dark, genuinely beautiful.

They stood beside it for a moment without speaking.

"You have my guidebook," Ben said.

"Yes."

"Are you planning to return it?"

She appeared to consider this with genuine seriousness. "No," she said. "I think it is better for everyone."

He stood looking at the fountain. Then he reached into his jacket pocket and produced the small ceramic tile—the rooster, cobalt blue, absurdly dignified. He held it out flat on his palm, not offering it so much as presenting it. Giving it back to the air between them.

Her face changed. Not the sharp blankness he might have expected, and not guilt—something more private than either of those things, something that was almost but not quite surprise. She looked at the tile. She looked at him.

"Marco told you," she said.

"Marco told me nothing specific."

"Then how—"

"I saw you," he said. "Not then. Later. I reconstructed it." He kept his hand steady. "At the market."

She was quiet. She took the tile from his palm and turned it over, examining it the way she had at the stall, though differently now. She was not performing admiration; she was looking at it the way you look at something that has become more complicated than it was.

"Why did you take it?" he asked.

She ran her thumb along the edge of the tile. "I don't know," she said, which he was almost certain was true and also almost certainly not the whole truth.

"The acquaintance—" He reconsidered. "Marco said you watch people. That you put them in situations."

"Marco is dramatic."

"He's not wrong, though."

She looked up at him. The fountain made its noise. Around them the crowd moved and the lights held steady against the darkening sky, and she was watching him the way, he understood, she had been watching him all day—with the particular attention of someone taking inventory.

Then she laughed. Not the involuntary laugh of the afternoon, the one that had annoyed her—this one was deliberate and warm and arrived in her eyes before it reached her mouth.

"Same time tomorrow?" she said.

He looked at the fountain, its tiers and gods and rushing water, its total indifference to the two of them standing at its edge.

"Yes," he said. "All right."

She pocketed the rooster tile, and pulled on her helmet, and was gone before he'd fully decided what any of it meant—her presence, when she was gone, as easy to reconstruct as a sentence heard only once, replayed until something in it made sense. He stood for a while in the noise and the light. Then he turned in what he was fairly confident was the wrong direction and began to walk.
