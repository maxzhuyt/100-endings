# Offworlder

The envoy left the compound at first light, without escort.

The corridor to the outer door was lit by panels that did not flicker, did not warm, did not vary across the sixteen hours of the Kaleth day. She had been on-station eleven days and had not once seen a shadow form naturally inside. She noted this, as she noted most things, without deciding what to do with it. The door opened on a pressure differential; recycled air pushed past her into the street, and was gone.

---

The market occupied three blocks of the Sethanel Quarter, which the briefings called a mixed-use commercial district and which was, in fact, a place where people sold things to other people in the morning.

She walked into it slowly.

The first stall was draped in fabric—bolts of it stacked horizontally, some folded over a wooden arm at eye level so that the weave caught what light there was. The colors she recognized: a deep red that was not quite ochre, a near-white with blue undertones. But at the back of the stall, wound loosely around a clay post, was a length of fabric she could not resolve. She looked at it directly and the color refused. Not iridescent—she knew iridescence, knew how structural color shifted with angle—this was fixed, consistent, and somehow perpendicular to the part of her visual field she used for naming. She thought: *like green, but gone further than green goes*. She thought: *blue, if blue turned a corner*. She stopped thinking about it and looked away, and found the color still present in her peripheral attention, quiet and patient.

She moved on.

The second stall sold tools whose function she could not immediately parse—handled instruments of varying length, the working ends suggesting neither blade nor grip but something in between. A child of perhaps six was examining one with the professional seriousness of children who have been brought to market all their lives. The stall-keeper, who belonged to what the briefings catalogued as the Meridian subspecies—broader in the shoulder, the facial structure lower-set and more pronounced in the brow—watched the child without impatience.

The child returned the tool. The stall-keeper said something, brief. The child moved away, and she registered that the child's build was entirely different from the stall-keeper's, the proportions suggesting what the briefings called the Littoral subspecies: long-limbed, the neck column unusually extended. The briefings had described the relationship between Meridian and Littoral populations as "historically adversarial, currently managed." She had read that phrase several times and had understood it as meaning: *there is tension here; be aware of it.*

There was a woman at the third stall who wore a coat the color that had no name, and who was buying fish.

The envoy watched the transaction. Money changed hands, or its local equivalent—a small disc, passed, accepted, pocketed. The woman was Littoral. The fish-seller was Meridian. They spoke to each other without particular warmth and without friction, the way two people speak who have no reason to be either friendly or hostile at six in the morning when one of them wants fish.

She did not conclude anything. She watched, and moved on.

---

She had known, in the abstract, that eleven days in the compound had not prepared her for proximity. The briefing imagery was accurate in its components—pigmentation, proportion, the particularities of adapted bone structure after four generations of higher gravity and a stellar spectrum slightly red-shifted from Sol—but accurate components are not the same as a face. She understood this clearly when a man walking toward her on the street declined to move and she stepped sideways to let him pass and they were briefly very close. She saw the architecture of his jaw and the particular depth of his eye-set and the way his collarbone described a different geometry than hers did, and for a half-second she saw herself from the outside: pink-beige and improbably narrow, in clothes that fit wrong for this gravity.

He did not look at her. He had places to be.

A child, later, did look—a child of the Meridian build, moving with a small group that appeared to be family, who stopped walking entirely and stared. The envoy looked back. After a moment the child's mouth changed; they were laughing, the way children laugh at something that is funny, which is different from the way they laugh at something that frightens them. She could tell the difference.

She thought about that.

An elder sitting outside a doorway did not look up as she passed. The elder was peeling something—a root, or a fruit—with a small blade, and the motion was practiced and complete, and the envoy did not exist.

Around the corner, alone for a moment in a side street, she stopped and stood. The gravity was point-one-two over standard, a constant low pressure on her joints, a reminder that this was not a place that had been made for her body. She breathed the outside air, which was cold and carried something organic and not unpleasant. She thought about the fabric in the first stall, the color she could not name.

Then she went back.

---

The reprimand was delivered by Director Aune via screen, her face in the upper third of the frame with the Council seal visible behind her, which meant the conversation was on record.

"The solo excursion has been logged," Director Aune said. "As a breach of the Offworld Personnel Security Protocols, subsection four. You are aware of subsection four."

She was.

"It has been determined," the Director said, "that your current assignment interface will be redirected. Rather than the market-access cultural program, you are to proceed to a meeting with Minister Casaverde of the Colonial Science Authority. Scheduling will be provided within the hour."

"I understand."

"The meeting is an opportunity," the Director said. "It should be treated as one."

"Yes."

"Further solo excursions are not permitted. Subsection four is not a formality." The Director paused. "You're young, Envoy. That's worth remembering."

The screen returned to blue.

She stood in front of it for a moment, in the compound's air, which had no temperature and no smell. She did not say anything. There was no one to say it to.

---

Her quarters were a nine-by-twelve rectangle with a desk, a bed, a window that looked at the side wall of an adjacent structure. She had been given briefing materials on Minister Pilar Casaverde of the Colonial Science Authority, and she read them now for the third time, more slowly.

The Ministry of Science had, under Casaverde, received funding increases in four of the last six budget cycles. The Minister had held office through two changes of government. The Minister had degrees in political science and administrative law; the briefings noted this in a neutral tone that was not, she thought, actually neutral.

She read the words *consolidation*, *leverage*, *longevity*. She understood, reading them this time, that she was not being sent to accomplish anything. She was being sent because she had gone where she wasn't supposed to go, and now she would be placed across a table from someone who wanted to find out what she was made of, and then a report would be filed.

She thought about what she was made of.

She looked at the window and found herself thinking, without intending to, about the cloth on the clay post—the color that had moved away from her each time she approached it linguistically. *The green that green doesn't reach.* She had been trained in four languages and could function in two more, and none of them had a word for it.

She slept, briefly, and in the morning put on her formal clothes.

---

The Minister's office was not what she had expected an office to be. There were low chairs in a warm amber material, and a table of dark wood—real wood, which was not grown everywhere—and a window that faced east, toward the mountains, which were visible in morning light as a long dark specific shape. Tea had been set out. The room was at a temperature that was comfortable, which she suspected was not the Minister's comfortable temperature but was calculated to be hers.

Minister Casaverde rose when she entered, which was a choice—protocol did not require it—and extended a hand. Her grip was brief and certain.

"Envoy Morran." The Minister was sixty or thereabouts, gray-haired, with the set jaw and deep eye-socket architecture of the Littoral build prominent in this region, and the particular quality of stillness that belonged to people who had been in rooms like this one for a long time. "Sit, please. The tea is from the Harrow Valley, if you take it."

She took it.

"I understand your work here is progressing," the Minister said.

"It's early yet," she said.

"Of course." The Minister lifted her own cup. "I heard you walked in the Sethanel Quarter yesterday morning."

She had been preparing for this. She had not been prepared for it to come before pleasantries had fully agreed to conclude.

"I did," she said.

"The market."

"Yes."

The Minister set down her cup and looked out the window for a moment. Against the mountains, this was a very composed gesture. "What did you find there?"

"Fabric," she said. "Tools. Fish."

Something in the Minister's expression changed in a way she could not immediately categorize. It was not quite amusement. "Yes. Those tend to be in markets." A pause. "What else?"

She made a decision. It was the only decision available, given what she actually knew, which was the market and not the briefings. "I found a color I couldn't name. In a bolt of fabric. I don't have a word for it in any language I know."

The Minister looked at her.

"I also found," she said, "that whatever the conflict between Meridian and Littoral populations is described as, it looked different at six in the morning in the Sethanel Quarter than it looks on paper."

"Did it."

"A Littoral woman bought fish from a Meridian seller."

"People tend to buy fish from whoever has fish."

"Yes," she said. "That's my point."

Silence. The Minister lifted her cup again, and drank, and set it down. The mountains were still there, in the window. "You were sent to assess the feasibility of a collaborative research arrangement, Envoy. That is your stated mission."

"It is."

"What we are actually being asked," the Minister said, precisely, "is whether we are stable enough to be trusted with Earth's continued interest. Whether the, as your briefings no doubt call it, managed tension between communities constitutes a risk to partnership." She let that sit for a moment. "What we are actually being asked, that is, is whether we are civilized."

She did not answer immediately. She thought about the child who had laughed, and the elder who had continued peeling a root or a fruit, uninterested. She thought about the inadequacy of the word *civilized*, which she did not say.

"I think," she said carefully, "that I was sent to answer a question that was already answered before I arrived."

"And what question is that?"

"Whether you'll accept the terms."

The Minister was very still. "And what do you think?"

"I think you've been accepting and declining Earth's terms for forty years. I think you know which ones matter and which ones are face-saving, and I think the difference between those two categories isn't something that shows up in a research arrangement."

"No," the Minister said. "It doesn't."

"I also think," she said, and was surprised to find that she meant it, "that I'm not the right person to be doing this assessment. I spent eleven days in a corridor with fluorescent lighting and one morning in a market, and I know more from the morning than from the eleven days, which suggests the methodology is wrong."

"The methodology," the Minister said, with what was possibly the first unguarded expression she had produced in this conversation, "has been wrong for forty years." She looked out the window again. "The color in the fabric. In Kala—the substrate language of this region—there is a word. *Sethien.* It has no Earth cognate because the wavelength sits outside what Earth's standard illuminants emphasize. Your eye can see it. Your vocabulary was not built for it."

She said nothing.

"You can learn the word," the Minister said. "That is less difficult than learning what it means to a Littoral weaver in the Harrow Valley at four in the morning, deciding how tightly to twist the warp." She stood. The meeting was over. "Thank you for your time, Envoy."

"Thank you for the tea."

The Minister's expression did something brief and private. "Come back," she said, "when you've been here longer."

---

She stood at the window in her quarters, looking at the wall of the adjacent structure, which was limestone or something that behaved like limestone, with a long irregular crack that ran from upper left to lower right and had been there long enough for something small and green to be growing in it.

The city beyond the compound was audible, slightly, if she stood close to the glass. Low voices, a cart, a sound she had not yet identified that happened at irregular intervals and might be machinery or might be something living.

*Sethien.*

She said the word quietly, to herself, in the empty room. It had two syllables and sat in the mouth somewhere between a question and a statement, neither arriving nor departing. She thought: the fabric on the clay post. She thought: the woman in the coat, buying fish. She thought: *your vocabulary was not built for it*, which was true, and was also not only about blue.

The political situation was unchanged. Her standing was uncertain. Somewhere in the compound's system, a report was being generated that described her as a breach of protocol, a redirected asset, an envoy of uncertain judgment and unverified utility.

She looked at the limestone wall with its crack and its small green inhabitant, and thought that she would like to know the name for the color of it in the light that was not quite Earth's light, in the language she did not yet know, in the place she was only beginning, at significant cost, to see.
