# A Gladiator's Day

---

The smell arrives before waking—before the eyes, before the mind knows where it is. Straw and unwashed men and beneath that the iron-and-salt smell of blood that has gone into the floor so many seasons it has become the floor, as permanent as limestone. The lamp in the corridor has burned low on its tallow and sends through the door's gap a thread of smoke that is almost sweet, almost like rendered fat, like something that was once an animal and is now just fire and diminishment. The light through the window slot is the particular grey of Roman dawn, not gentle, not anything so considered as gentle—simply the first light, which is the color of nothing yet decided.

He lies still for a moment. This is permitted. Not by the lanista, who permits nothing that does not serve the school's investment, but by the body itself, which has learned to take what it can in the interval between sleep and the bell. His left knee aches. It always aches in the cold months, which is most months now—the autumn has come without ceremony, the way most things come to a man who lives inside walls. He maps himself in the dark: knee, the old tear in his right shoulder that pulls when he reaches overhead, the new bruise along his ribs from Decimus's elbow three days ago, deliberate or accidental he still cannot say. His body is a ledger he reads each morning before anything else. Credit and debt. What remains functional. What must be compensated for.

From beyond the locked door he can hear the barley porridge. Someone has begun cooking it over the brazier in the main corridor, and the smell of it arrives like a slow tide—grain and hot water and something faintly scorched at the bottom of the pot. He knows this smell as well as he knows any smell in the world now. Better than his mother's cooking, which grows less specific each year, and more like the idea of a mother cooking, blurred and generalized into sentiment. The barley he knows exactly. It is always slightly scorched. The slave who tends it—a boy with a scarred lip whose name no one uses—always lets it sit too long on the heat. This is the texture of his mornings.

He rises.

---

The trident stands in the corner where he put it last night, its tines wrapped in cloth against the wall. He crosses to it before he has fully decided to. His feet know the way in the dark, seven steps on the cold stone, and his hands find the haft with the ease of long practice.

He unwraps the cloth slowly.

His father had a fishing boat. Not a large one—a coastal skiff that worked the shallower waters near their village, the kind of craft a man could put in and take out alone if his sons were busy or useless. The nets they used were drop nets, weighted at the circumference, thrown with a particular rotation of the shoulder that his father tried to teach him perhaps forty times and which he never mastered to his father's satisfaction. *You throw like you're afraid of the water*, his father told him. This was accurate. He was afraid of the water. He is not afraid of water anymore. There is not enough water inside the ludus to be afraid of.

The trident is not a fishing tool. He knows this. Any man who has actually fished knows this—a trident is for shallow water and slow fish, for the theatre of fishing rather than fishing itself. When the lanista first showed him the weapons of the retiarius and said *this is what you will be*, he had laughed before he could stop himself. Not from pleasure. From the particular absurdity of it—that they had looked at him and seen water, had decided that because he smelled of the coast he should carry the sea with him into the arena like a joke at his own expense.

He did not refuse. Refusal is not a tool available to him.

He runs his thumb along the central tine. There is a nick near the base from a bout eighteen months ago, a Thraex who had been better than expected and had caught the trident on a downswing with his curved blade. The Thraex had not survived the afternoon. The nick had. He thinks of this sometimes—that the mark of that fight lives in the metal when the man who made it does not live anywhere. He oils the haft now with a strip of cloth and the small vessel of olive oil he keeps beneath his sleeping mat. This takes as long as it takes. He does not rush it. The bell has not rung.

This is the one thing they cannot take without making him useless: his knowledge of this particular weapon, its weight in his particular hands, the slight drift to the left when thrown that he compensates for without thinking. They have taken his name—he is Corvus now, which the crowd enjoys shouting, which the lanista wrote into the contract as though it had always been true—but they have not taken the adjustment in his wrist, the callus on his right palm that corresponds exactly to the place where the haft binds against the grip. He is still the sum of all the ways his body has learned this instrument.

He wraps the cloth back around the tines. Sets the trident in its corner. The bell rings.

---

The yard is sandy dirt gone hard with use, ringed by the low arcaded gallery where the lanista sometimes watches from shadow, eating fruit and making notations. The morning light has come fully over the wall now and it is flat and white and indifferent.

Mago goes through his footwork drills near the east wall, as he always does, alone, because Mago does not speak to men he does not consider worth speaking to, and his list is very short. He is the school's star—has been for three years, since his fourth arena victory, which is not many but was achieved against a man who should have killed him and which the crowd witnessed and memorialized into legend. The lanista feeds Mago's legend the way one feeds a profitable animal: carefully, and with an eye always on what it produces. Mago knows this and is not bothered by it. He has the practical intelligence of a man who has decided exactly how much of himself to spend and has budgeted accordingly.

He watches Mago's footwork without expression. Technically it is excellent. The economy of it is excellent. What it contains is not his business.

Near the center of the yard, two new men practice with wooden swords under the eyes of one of the doctores—a free man named Calvus who teaches with his fists when words fail, which is often, because the new men understand perhaps three words of Latin between them. They are from somewhere eastern, he thinks—Cappadocian or perhaps further, their faces carrying the particular exhausted flatness of men who have only recently stopped trying to understand how they arrived here. He was that flat once. He remembers the flatness without nostalgia. One of them, the taller one, holds his practice sword with both hands on the hilt, which is wrong, and Calvus demonstrates this by hitting him smartly on the knuckles. The man does not flinch. He simply adjusts his grip. This minor piece of discipline is already in the man somewhere—the knowledge that the correction matters more than the pain of receiving it.

He will survive, probably. The other one—smaller, with the look of a man arguing silently with himself—probably will not. This is not cruelty, assessing this. It is arithmetic.

In the corner by the water trough leans the one they call Bulla—no arena name, just the amulet he wears, the old protective charm that ought to have been given up with childhood but which he has managed to keep through intake and inspection and two years at the school by means that remain his own secret. Bulla is forty-something, perhaps, or older, the kind of age that becomes unreadable when a body has been through enough. He was a good fighter once. He is not a good fighter now. His left arm has been reset twice, and does not entirely close at the elbow, and the lanista retains him on the roster out of some calculation of value that he cannot fully read—sentiment seems unlikely but the lanista is not entirely without it, which is one of the few surprising things about the man. Bulla does not drill anymore. He watches the new men with the expression of someone watching a fire they know will not warm them but which they watch anyway because it is something to watch.

He does not feel contempt for Bulla. He does not feel what he would call compassion. He feels a clean recognition, which is different—the kind of recognition that passes between two people who have looked at the same thing from the same angle long enough to stop needing to discuss it.

He turns to his own drilling. His net work needs attention. It always needs attention.

---

The lanista comes through the main gate at midmorning with a man he has not seen before—civilian dress, the good kind, and the particular way the civilian moves through the yard without quite looking at what he's looking at, which is the way of men who have come to purchase something that embarrasses them slightly or which they wish to appear not to need as badly as they need it. An agent, perhaps, or a client. Someone with games to plan.

The word moves through the yard before the lanista has said anything—it moves the way words always move in the ludus, in the compressed efficient language of men who have few legitimate channels of communication and have therefore developed excellent illegitimate ones. A senator's funeral games. Three weeks. Full program, morning, afternoon, the works. Names are already being speculated.

He drills. He watches the commotion of speculation without participating in it. The new eastern men do not understand what is happening but can read the change in the air—they look around with the alertness of men who have learned that when the room changes quality, one pays attention. This too is something that will keep them alive, maybe.

A senator's funeral games. Three weeks. This is not nothing. This is money, which the lanista takes the largest portion of, which the next portion goes into the school's accounts, which the remainder—the remainder is not nothing. More bouts also means more chances of dying, which is the mathematics that everyone in the yard is currently performing and no one is discussing.

He has done this calculation before. He will do it again. The results are what the results are.

He has survived seven bouts in the arena. This number is high enough to have meaning and not high enough to have legend. He is a working fighter—competent, not celebrated. He knows his value to the school is that he is reliable: he performs within the expected range, doesn't die unexpectedly or disappoint the crowd. There is a dignity in this that is not visible from the outside. He has decided it is there anyway.

The civilian in the good clothes glances at him as he passes, the assessing glance, and he meets it flatly and the man looks away first.

This too is something. Not much. But something.

---

The shade comes off the east wall at midday and makes a strip wide enough for a few men. He sits in it with his water cup and his portion of the bread—dense, good bread, they feed you well at the school, the lanista understands that the product requires maintenance—and eats without tasting it, which is not depression but simply the absence, temporarily, of the effort of being present.

The yard is quiet in the midday heat. Most men are inside.

He thinks of his brother's wedding, which happened three or four years before he was taken—he cannot fix the number precisely—which is strange because all the other years of his life arrange themselves in order, before and after the ludus, but that particular year has become elastic. His brother's wedding was on the beach. Their village does weddings on the beach when the weather permits because there is simply more space there, and his brother had wanted space—his brother has always wanted more room than available circumstances provided, which is either a character flaw or a quality depending on the day. There was a fire when the sun went down. There was wine that was slightly too sweet. He danced badly, which everyone knew and no one was cruel about because it was his brother's wedding and cruelty would have been misplaced.

He is sitting in the sand in the shade of the east wall. He is sitting on the beach in the light of the fire. Both of these things are true for a moment and then only one of them is true.

The smell of the barley at the bottom of the pot still hangs in the air from the morning.

He finishes the bread.

He does not imagine returning home. This is a discipline he has enforced on himself for long enough that it has become simply the shape of his thinking—the way a river bends around an obstacle long enough that the bending becomes the river. He does not imagine returning home because that imagining costs more than he can afford and produces nothing he can use. He imagines, instead, specific and manageable things: the weight of the trident in tomorrow's drill, the footwork sequence he has been working, the way his shoulder will need warming slowly in the cold morning.

These are the thoughts that spend within his means.

He drinks the water. It tastes of the clay vessel and nothing else.

---

The physician makes his rounds in the late afternoon—a Greek freedman named Sosias who smells of garlic and wound-vinegar and who treats the gladiators with a brisk efficiency that is professionally indistinguishable from indifference but which, if you have watched him long enough, is something else. He has opinions about what constitutes unnecessary suffering and acts on them with the limited means available to him. This is more than most.

He stops before him and gestures at the shoulder. He has not mentioned the shoulder.

"I saw you favoring it in the afternoon drill," Sosias says. He says this in Greek, then in Latin, in case. They communicate mostly in Latin, which is neither of their first languages, which gives their conversations an accidental neutrality, all the roughness of each man's thinking smoothed into a shared imprecision.

"It pulls overhead. Nothing new."

Sosias sets down his kit and takes the arm without asking, which he accepted a long time ago. He manipulates the joint in a slow circle—not gently but carefully, which is better than gently—and the shoulder makes the sound it always makes.

"Before the games," Sosias says. "Come to me before the games. There's something I can pack around it."

"Does it work?"

Sosias's expression changes in a way that is too small to have a simple name. "It helps," he says. "Which is the most I can promise anything."

He nods. Sosias picks up his kit. He is already looking at Bulla across the yard, doing the calculation of Bulla's arm, which everyone does eventually.

"Sosias."

The physician turns.

He does not know what he intended to say. He had not intended to say anything—the name came out of him without direction, which is unusual. Sosias waits with the patience of a man who is accustomed to waiting for things that are difficult to say.

"The new men," he says finally. "The tall one. He holds his hilt wrong."

Sosias looks across the yard at the tall eastern man, who is sitting in the shadow of the arcade eating his bread. There is nothing Sosias can do about a grip. They both know this.

"I'll mention it to Calvus," Sosias says, which is not really a lie.

He nods again. Sosias goes. The afternoon light has begun to angle and turn gold in the way it does before losing interest entirely, and the yard holds that last warmth the way the stone does—briefly, and then not.

---

The lamp in the corridor has been replenished. Its light comes under the door now yellow and steady, the faint smell of new tallow replacing the old scorched smell, and this small replacement of one known smell with another known smell is the signal, without anyone announcing it, that the day has completed.

He lies on his mat. The knee aches. He inventories: the shoulder will need packing before the bouts. The ribs are resolving. The knee is the knee—he has stopped hoping the knee will become something different than what it is.

Outside someone coughs, long and serious. Not Bulla, he thinks—someone new. The eastern men have been put in the adjacent cell. He listens to the cough without concern. Either it will resolve or it won't. Either is outside his management.

He looks at the ceiling, which he can barely see. He has looked at this ceiling for long enough that he knows every crack in it, which is not knowledge he asked for and which has no use. This is what the ludus does—fills you with the specific and intimate knowledge of your enclosure. He knows the ceiling and the walls and the distance from his mat to every other surface. He knows which stone in the corridor floor rings different when you step on it. He knows the timing of the lamp-filling and the smell of the changed tallow and the sound of every cough in the adjacent cell, eventually.

He knows the trident in his hands. This is the part that matters.

He closes his eyes. Through the window slot the night air comes in—cooler now, carrying nothing particular, just the dark and the smell of a city turning over in its sleep, enormous and entirely unconcerned with the small machinery of men it operates for its own pleasure.
