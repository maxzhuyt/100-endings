# The Watch

The lunch tray had exactly three things Mara actually wanted on it: the chocolate milk, the roll, and the corner brownie she'd grabbed before Declan Price could reach past her and take it. Everything else — the gray green beans, the mashed potatoes that looked like they'd been applied with a spackling knife — she'd eat anyway, because her mom had said *you eat what they give you* in that tone that meant the conversation was already over before it started. Mara carried the tray to the table where Priya was saving her a seat, and she sat down, and she said nothing about the fact that Priya had let Declan and his entire circle fill in the rest of the bench, which now meant Mara was eating with her shoulder blades pressed together and her elbows hovering uselessly over her own food. She ate. She was fine. She was invisible in the way that was different from lonely but lived in the same building.

The watch had appeared on her wrist four days ago.

Her dad had given it to her for her birthday, which was in September, and which they had celebrated late because of a work thing he couldn't get out of. It came in a white box with a magnetic lid, nested in foam the color of a new eraser. *Lumis Pro*, it said on the box, in silver letters over a gradient that went from midnight blue to a kind of expensive gray. The face was round, with a glass surface that caught light and held it. When she put it on in front of him it lit up with a soft pulse — not a harsh flash, a pulse, like something just starting to breathe — and displayed the time in a font that looked like it belonged on a spaceship's control panel. It had a heart rate monitor. It tracked her steps. It had an interface she hadn't fully figured out, menus within menus, tiny icons she was still decoding.

Wearing it made her feel, not older exactly, but *more accounted for*. Like she had appointments. Like her wrist was, finally, worth looking at.

She was looking at it now, pressing the crown gently to wake the face, when Declan Pryce reached across her for the salt and his elbow connected with her chocolate milk and the chocolate milk went directly, completely, horizontally across her tray, her roll, her brownie, and the front of her white shirt.

The table went quiet in that specific way where you could feel everyone noticing.

Declan said, "Oh, whoa —"

Mara said nothing. Her face went the temperature of a radiator. The milk was still moving, finding the edge of the tray, beginning to drip. The brownie was destroyed. Priya had half-stood with napkins but the napkins were going to do approximately nothing.

Mara's thumb pressed the watch's crown.

She didn't mean to. She'd been holding her wrist — a reflex, protecting it from the spill — and the pressure of her own grip hit the crown sideways and then she must have dragged across something on the face because the interface opened, and a little animation she'd never seen before played: a small arc of time, like a progress bar going backward, with a number in the center. 00:08. And then, before she fully understood what she was looking at, she tapped the face.

The world stuttered.

It was like a record scratch but inside her eyes. Like static, but spatial. The cafeteria lurched, not violently, just one frame of wrong, like a film splice — and then everything was exactly as it had been nine seconds ago. The tray in front of her was dry. The chocolate milk stood upright. The brownie was intact. Her shirt was white.

Declan reached for the salt.

Mara picked up her chocolate milk and moved it six inches to the left.

His elbow swept through the space where it had been. The salt was retrieved. He said nothing. He had never noticed her.

Mara sat very still and looked at the watch.

*It worked*, she thought. A word that did not feel large enough. *It actually worked.*

She pressed her tongue to the back of her teeth. There was a pressure there, a very slight tightness in her jaw, like she'd been clenching it in her sleep. She rolled her jaw once, side to side. The feeling faded. The brownie tasted fine.

---

She rewound the free throw on Thursday.

The gym class arc was a stupid ritual — half the class waiting while they each attempted a free throw, Coach Billings with his clipboard, the other kids watching with the particular attention kids give to situations where someone might embarrass themselves. Mara's shot left her hands in the wrong direction, grazed the backboard sideways, and fell not even close. She heard someone laugh, low and not quite hidden.

She was already walking back to the line. The crown was already under her thumb.

*00:04.*

The ball was back in her hands. She took a breath. She set her feet. She let it go and it rattled around the rim and dropped through and Coach Billings made a checkmark and moved on.

"Decent," said the girl behind her, and Mara felt something warm move through her chest.

She did it twice more that day. Once for a text she sent to Priya — *I think Declan is actually kind of cute* — that auto-populated into a group message instead. She felt the mistake happen, the small electric horror of watching the send button confirm, and she had rewound before anyone could have read it. Once for something that hardly counted: she had said *you too* when the librarian told her to have a good afternoon, and then caught herself and thought *no one heard that* and rewound it anyway, because she could, because it took four seconds and four seconds was nothing.

She noticed her voice sat a little different in her throat. She was getting a cold, probably. She'd been sleeping badly, which could do a lot of things. She looked at her hands once, both palms up on her desk during history, and thought they looked unfamiliar — the proportions of things, the length from knuckle to knuckle — and then Mrs. Abernathy called on someone else and Mara closed her hands and forgot about it.

In the bathroom before sixth period she passed the mirror without really looking and then did look, quickly, and saw her own face look back but with something off about the angle or the light. The fluorescents in the east-wing bathroom had always been bad. She looked tired. Growing pains, she thought, which was a phrase she knew but had never really interrogated.

She kept the watch face-up on her desk that night and fell asleep looking at it.

---

Priya said, on Friday: "You seem different lately."

They were at Priya's locker, Mara leaning against the adjacent one. It was said with genuine uncertainty, not the performed uncertainty of someone who already knew what they meant. Priya's head tilted. "Like older. Is that a weird thing to say?"

"I got a haircut," Mara said. She hadn't.

"Maybe," Priya said. "Are you feeling okay?"

"I'm fine. I'm literally fine. I got more sleep this week."

"Ms. DiMartino asked me if you were in seventh or eighth grade."

"That's because I'm tall."

"You're five-two, Mara."

"Can we drop it?"

They dropped it. But in third period, Marcus Hale — a sixth grader who had never spoken to Mara in his life — held a door open for her with such deference that she actually looked behind herself to see if there was an adult he was addressing. There was no adult. She walked through the door and said thank you and Marcus Hale said "of course" with the inflection of someone addressing a distant authority figure.

She told herself this didn't mean anything. Sixth graders were just strange. Middle school was just this — strange, disorienting, a period of physical change during which your sense of proportion was reliable approximately never. She knew this. Her mom had explained this to her. The awkwardness of it, the way you outgrew yourself in visible lurches. It was all normal. She had done absolutely nothing wrong.

The watch pulsed softly blue against the inside of her wrist, and she felt, very faintly, like she was wearing something with a live circuit buried under the glass.

---

The thing with Priya happened on Monday.

It was not one thing. It was the accumulation of small things — the group message (which Mara had already erased, already rewound, which Priya did not know about and yet somehow sensed at the molecular level the way Priya sensed all things Mara would prefer she didn't know), and a comment Mara had made last week about Priya's presentation, and the fact that Mara had been distracted and flat and not quite present for two weeks now, watching her wrist instead of listening. It came out at the lockers again, after school, in the thin-voiced way Priya expressed hurt, which was to say very precisely and without raising her pitch at all.

"I feel like you're not here when I'm talking to you."

"I am here."

"You're looking at that watch."

"My dad gave it to me."

"I know he gave it to you, Mara, I was there when you told me. I'm saying you look at it more than you look at me, and I feel like ever since you got it you've been different, and I've told you that, and you told me it was a haircut."

"Priya —"

"Forget it. Never mind. I'm just going to go —"

"Priya, I'm sorry —"

But Priya was already walking, the way Priya walked when she was done with a conversation: not fast, just final. And Mara stood at the lockers alone, and felt the specific weight of having been seen and found lacking, and looked down at her wrist, and pressed the crown.

The interface opened.

She scrolled back further than she ever had. Not seconds. A full four minutes — she needed to be back before the conversation had started, before *I feel like you're not here* had been said, before the whole wreckage of the afternoon had accrued. She found the slider and dragged it back. The number read 04:12. The arc bent.

She confirmed.

The world did not stutter this time.

It came apart.

It was like the stutter but stretched, and terrible — like a frame rate so low she could see the machinery inside each moment, the gears and pulleys of cause-and-effect, and then everything collapsed inward and there was a sound she could not describe except that it was the inverse of sound, it was anti-sound, it was silence that pressed against the eardrum from inside — and nausea bloomed through her so completely that she grabbed the locker for balance and missed and had to catch herself on the wall.

When the world settled, Priya was gone.

Not walking away. Gone. The hallway was empty in both directions. Mara looked at the clock on the wall and it said 2:51 and school got out at 2:50 and the hallway was empty and Priya, who should have been mid-sentence, who should have been right here, was not here. Had not been here. The lockers around Mara's own bore no fingerprints, no smudges. No one had been standing here. There was nothing to correct. The argument had been rewound to a point before Priya had arrived to have it, and so Priya was gone, and Mara was alone in a hallway that felt like a hallway in a school that had been closed for years.

She pressed the crown. The interface was different.

The arc animation played, but slower. The glow pulsed orange instead of white. The confirmation screen that usually displayed *REWIND CONFIRMED* displayed instead a sentence Mara didn't understand and stood there reading with the particular horror of not-understanding, reading it again and again: *TEMPORAL DISPLACEMENT CONFIRMED. ANCHOR VARIANCE: 4 MIN 12 SEC. SUBJECT INTEGRITY: 73%.*

Her hands were shaking.

She looked at them.

They were wrong. The thought arrived so cleanly it didn't even frighten her yet: *those are not my hands*. The fingers were right — the same slightly crooked left pinkie she'd had since hitting it with a softball at ten — but the length, the tendons visible under the back of the hand, the way the skin sat over the knuckles. She pressed her right thumb into her left palm and felt the pressure with total fidelity and her hands still looked like someone else's. Someone older. Someone whose hands had carried things for longer.

She walked to the east-wing bathroom.

The fluorescents were still bad.

She stood in front of the mirror.

Her face looked back at her.

And then it didn't.

For one moment — one moment, not long enough to count in any official register of moments — the face in the mirror was hers the way a word you've heard a hundred times suddenly sounds like a foreign language: recognizably, technically correct, and also completely alien. The angles had sharpened. The eyes — still brown, still her specific shade of brown that her dad called *good coffee* — sat differently in their sockets. The jaw. God, the jaw. Lines she hadn't put there, lines that required time to put there, time accumulating in a body while the mind of that body was busy erasing things.

The face was hers at twenty-two. Twenty-five. She didn't know. She was thirteen and she did not know what twenty-five looked like from the inside but she knew what it looked like in the mirror and it was there and then it was gone, her own face back, flushed and thirteen and undeniably, tremblingly, still a kid.

She raised her hands to touch the glass.

She looked at them.

She counted backward.

*How many times.* The free throw. The text. The *you too* at the library. The lunch tray — the first one, the accident, eight seconds. The subsequent ones she'd stopped cataloguing because they'd stopped feeling like events and started feeling like using an eraser. *Each second taken back. Forward somewhere. Compounding.* She tried to do the arithmetic and her mind slipped off the numbers the way a foot slips off a wet step, and the nausea came back, and she gripped the edge of the sink with those wrong hands.

She had wanted small things.

She had wanted to not have chocolate on her shirt. She had wanted to make the free throw. She had wanted to not have said *you too* to the librarian like an idiot.

She had done this by being reasonable, by being measured, by rewinding only what was necessary, only what was proportional, only what any reasonable person would agree was worth it for the relief it bought.

She had been the only one signing the contract.

---

Priya texted at 4:17: *are you okay? i waited outside but you weren't there*

The argument had not happened. Priya did not remember it. Whatever Mara had returned to, it was a version of the afternoon where they were still fine, still normal, still Priya saving her a spot at lunch and Mara looking at her watch.

Mara sat on her bed. She looked at the watch.

There was still the matter of Priya being somewhere wrong, or having been somewhere wrong, or: she did not fully understand the mechanics of what she had displaced or where it had gone. She knew only that she had done something structural and large and that it had been different from the others the way a tipped-over glass of water is different from a burst pipe.

To fix it, she would have to go back further.

She looked at the watch. The face glowed.

She looked at her hands in the lit screen's reflection. She looked and then she looked away and picked up the watch and turned it over and there was the crown and there was the glass and there was the interface waiting, that little backward arc, that progress bar that always moved in the wrong direction.

The question sat in the room with her like a third person.

She thought of Priya's voice: *I feel like you're not here when I'm talking to you.*

She thought of Marcus Hale holding the door with his eyes slightly too wide, deferring to something in her he couldn't explain.

She thought of Ms. DiMartino. *Seventh or eighth grade?*

She thought: *subject integrity: 73%.*

She thought: *what does it say at sixty? at forty? at what number does it stop displaying a number?*

She set her thumb against the crown.

The interface opened.

The arc appeared.

In the center of the arc, the watch asked her a question in the same clean spaceship font that had appeared on the morning of her birthday, when her dad had set it in the foam box and said *happy birthday, kiddo* and looked proud of himself for once getting something right.

The screen glowed.

*CONFIRM REWIND?*
