# THE AZRA GAMBIT
## *The Polite Abduction*

---

The transfer vessel's air filtration system hums with a persistent mechanical drone, and Arthur Neegan decides, somewhere over the rust-coloured nothing between his claim and wherever they're taking him, that the sound is the most honest thing aboard.

Everything else is performance. The four men stationed at intervals along the corridor — upright, measured, their sidearms holstered in that particular way that means *we'd like you to notice these* — are performance. The iced water someone set at his elbow without being asked is performance. Even the viewport, through which Mars recedes at a rate his engineer's mind converts automatically into velocity-and-time, feels staged. The recession is too smooth, the angle flattering. Someone chose this trajectory for what it would look like.

Only the filtration system just *is*.

He'd been given forty minutes to pack a bag. He'd spent the first ten standing in his kitchen doorway watching the retinue fill the space between his equipment locker and his front step, doing nothing in particular with the efficiency of men accustomed to being watched doing nothing. He'd spent the next thirty reminding himself that men who intend to make you disappear don't usually ask about your luggage preferences. It was thin comfort. He'd brought a change of clothes, his field notebook, and his slide rule out of habit — the slide rule because it had no antenna, no transponder, no way to be remotely queried, and right now Arthur finds that property appealing in an object.

He returns his attention to the emblem on the nearest man's chest. He's been returning to it at intervals, the way a tongue finds a loose tooth.

*Antares Diplomatic Corps*: a stylized ellipse — a planet's rings or an orbit, depending on your optimism — bracketing a single star. Arthur has seen the emblem twice before in his life. Once on a newsreel covering the Callisto Accords, behind the shoulders of seven men signing something in a room that had been selected for the gravitas of its furniture. Once on the tunic of the man who had overseen the Deimos land expropriation, where forty-three settler families had been removed with extraordinary courtesy over eleven days and never compensated for the structures they left behind.

*Diplomatic*, in the outer colonies, meant that what was being done to you had been agreed upon in writing first, by people who would not be in the room when it happened.

He catalogues what he knows. He catalogues what he doesn't. The gap between the two columns is the thing that would concern him if he let it, so he doesn't let it. He takes a sip of iced water instead and watches Mars shrink to a dot and reminds himself: luggage. They asked about luggage.

---

The station they docked at wore no identifying marks on its exterior.

This was itself identifying. Registry transponders are legally required for all fixed installations throughout the inner colonial belt — the regulation is one of the few jurisdictional points on which the Colonial Authority and the inner-system governments had found genuine consensus, largely because both wanted to know where the other's assets were. The absence of a transponder signal announced money, jurisdiction, and a specific kind of confidence that only comes from knowing your legal exposure and having made peace with it.

Arthur noted this as they approached through the viewport and said nothing, because there was no one to say it to and because he was still thinking it through.

Inside, the corridors were the blue-white of expensive lighting, the kind designed to replicate a full solar spectrum without replicating the sun. The floors were panelled — *actually panelled*, wood-grain composite laid in a chevron pattern that someone had specified in consultation with someone whose job title involved the word *ambience*, and someone else had purchased and shipped out here at a cost per linear metre that Arthur's mind calculated involuntarily, the way it always did. He had four years of supply manifests in his head. He knew what things cost to get off a gravity well.

Someone had wanted him to make that calculation. He made it, and let the number settle.

He was shown through two security checkpoints — polite, efficient, the sort that apologize while they scan — and then along a final corridor to a room with a table, two chairs, a carafe of coffee, and a viewport overlooking nothing in particular. He sat. The men who had escorted him did not enter. The door closed.

He waited. He poured coffee and found it was, absurdly, excellent — not processed, not the chicory-protein blend that passed for coffee on the Martian surface, but something dark and actual that had been grown somewhere and roasted by someone who cared about the result. He drank it slowly and thought about what it meant to offer a man excellent coffee before you offered him anything else. You were telling him something about the scale of what was coming.

Twelve minutes passed. He counted them.

---

The man who entered was perhaps sixty, compact, with the careful posture of someone who had spent years on stations with variable gravity and learned to hold themselves ready for the floor to change its relationship to their feet. His suit was grey, well-cut, the kind of corporate that precedes governments and survives them — not the suit of a man who reports to an organisation, but of a man who has learned to wear organisations the way other men wear suits.

"Mr. Neegan." He sat without offering a hand, which Arthur read not as rudeness but as calibration — the deliberate removal of a social ritual that would have been false. "My name is Farren Cault. I represent certain parties with a substantial interest in your recent survey results."

"Which parties."

"Consolidated ones." A slight smile — the smile of a man who has said this before and found it did what he needed. "You'll understand I'm not being evasive for the pleasure of it. The interests I represent are, let's say, architecturally complex. What matters at this stage is what I'm authorized to offer you, not who authorised it."

"You could have sent a letter."

"We could have," Cault agreed equably, pouring his own coffee, "and you'd have spent a week wondering who else had received one. This way, you know you were the first conversation." He set down the carafe with the small precision of a man who was precise about small things. "That's worth knowing."

"Is it."

"It will be." He met Arthur's eyes. "The vein."

"We're talking about the vein."

"Seventeen kilometres of primary Azra deposit, if your survey is accurate — and our own preliminary work, conducted discreetly, suggests it is. Accessible. Minimally faulted. Favourably situated relative to your existing transit channels and, more relevantly, relative to the three nearest colonial processing hubs." Cault folded his hands on the table in a gesture that managed to be both relaxed and deliberate. "Mr. Neegan, you are sitting on top of enough Azra to fuel a mid-range naval engagement or supply drive cores for approximately four hundred interstellar vessels. Those figures are not mutually exclusive."

Arthur looked at him, because looking at him was what he was supposed to do, and because sometimes the thing a man's face does while he delivers a number is more informative than the number. Cault's face did nothing he hadn't chosen for it.

The filtration system in this room was quieter than the one on the transport, or perhaps a higher pitch. Arthur found himself listening for it.

"The current lease structure," Cault continued, "would in principle permit a corporate acquisition offer, which you could accept or decline. The rates would be standard. Regulated. Fair, by most governing definitions." Another pause, and the pause was architecture — a room he was being invited to stand in. "We are not here to make that offer."

"What offer are you here to make."

"We want you to keep the claim." Cault's voice carried no persuasive inflection. He spoke the way an engineer describes load-bearing specifications — not to convince, but to convey. "We want you to continue operating it. Under a licensing arrangement through which we provide equipment, processing infrastructure, security apparatus, and — in return — a participation rate of eleven percent of gross yield."

Arthur did the arithmetic.

He had a knack for large numbers, which is to say that he had a knack for refusing to be frightened by them and instead converting them into their components. Eleven percent of gross yield on a seventeen-kilometre Azra primary. He went through the calculation in the familiar way: local extraction rate, refinery conversion, current market grade, probable fluctuation. He arrived at a figure. He recalculated it because the first result had seemed unreasonable. He arrived at the same figure.

He had spent four years on Mars in a pressurized module with a cracked secondary seal he'd repaired with cured polymer and a kind of stubbornness he'd mistaken, at the time, for optimism. He said: "That's a generous number."

"It is."

"Which tells me the other eighty-nine percent is a more generous number still."

"Also correct."

"And the security." Arthur turned the word as he would turn something he'd found on the ground — examining it for what it was before deciding whether to pick it up. "That covers a great deal of ground."

"It does." Cault nodded slowly. "There are three other parties currently aware of the scope of what you've found. The first is the Martian Colonial Authority, whose interest is primarily bureaucratic in nature and — I say this as an assessment, not a reassurance — predictably manageable within standard procedural timelines. The second is a consortium I'm not in a position to name today, whose interest is neither bureaucratic nor manageable and whose methods I'd characterise as direct." He held Arthur's gaze throughout this with the equable steadiness of a man delivering a weather report. "The third is below us at this moment. Docked on the port-side ring. They were not invited to this conversation, and their being here tells you something about how quickly the situation is developing."

A silence occupied the room.

Arthur counted three seconds of it. "If they're docked here, you know who they are."

"I know who sent them."

"And you're not going to tell me."

"Not today." Cault rose, smoothing his jacket in one practised motion. "You have until the end of this week. The formal terms will be transmitted to your claim terminal — encrypted, single-read, which you'll appreciate isn't standard practice and reflects the sensitivity of the arrangement." He moved toward the door with the unhurried economy of a man who has managed exits the same way he managed entrances. "Read it carefully. The annexure on jurisdictional provisions in particular. Section four is the part most people skim and shouldn't."

"Cault."

He paused. Turned. The door was in reach and his hand was not raised toward it, which gave Arthur the sense that he had, briefly, something.

"The licensing arrangement. Who signs the counter-party?"

The slight smile, again. Calibrated, again. "You've read about the Callisto Accords?"

"I have."

"Read about what they replaced." Cault opened the door. "I'll be in touch, Mr. Neegan."

He left.

The door closed behind him with the soft, pressurized click of expensive seals, and Arthur sat for a moment in the excellent coffee smell and the blue-white light and thought about a room that had held two intelligent men having a conversation in which approximately forty percent of the relevant information had been transmitted and the remainder had been transmitted by its absence.

---

Mars fills the viewport.

The transfer vessel drops back along its return trajectory and the planet resolves by degrees — craters becoming geography, the long brown plains revealing geometry, the glint of dome-glass, the ruled lines of transit channels. Down there, four years of his life sit inside a pressurized module with a repaired secondary seal on top of seventeen kilometres of a substance that can drive warships between stars.

He thinks about Section Four.

He thinks about the ship docked uninvited on the port-side ring, and whether the person sitting inside it watched him leave Cault's station, and whether they noted the time, and what they concluded from it.

He thinks about *what the Callisto Accords replaced*, which is not a thing he knows precisely enough and which Cault knew he didn't know before he said it. That was the move — the thing said too casually to be casual, the reference offered with the ease of a man dropping a key in front of a locked door and walking away. Arthur is supposed to go find the door.

He thinks: *three parties.* He thinks: *the third was not invited.* He thinks about the architecture of a meeting in which you reveal the existence of an uninvited guest and decline to name them. Cault had given him that information the way you give a man running a maze a glimpse of the exit — not to help him, but to make sure he keeps running in the right direction.

The slide rule is in his jacket pocket. He turns it over in his fingers without taking it out, working the familiar ridged edge against his thumb.

The filtration system drones on. He closes his eyes and does what engineers do when a problem exceeds the scope of immediate calculation: he breaks it into components, identifies the first-order unknowns, assigns provisional values to what can be estimated, and sets down what cannot yet be approached. Then he finds that the first-order components are each themselves problems that exceed the scope of immediate calculation. He builds a second tier of smaller boxes. He opens his eyes.

Mars is very close now.

The one thing he does not doubt — the one entry in the assets column that he trusts — is this: he has been the first conversation.

Whether that is fortune or the specific and deliberate shape of a trap, he has not yet determined. He suspects, in the way that he suspects a thing when his better judgment is busy with other concerns, that it is both, and that this may be precisely the point.

He watches the domes resolve below him, and the transit channels, and the particular gleam of a pressurized module on a stretch of survey-marked ground.

He thinks: *Section Four.*

He thinks: *what they replaced.*

The vessel begins its descent.
