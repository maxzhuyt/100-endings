# Breaking Formation

The floor is wrong first. Too smooth—the kind of smooth that reads as a warning, that tells the foot there is nothing to grip, no texture to negotiate. Then the mirrors, wall to wall on the east side, too many surfaces sending back too many angles of the same body, and the silence before the music is the kind of silence that has been practiced, cultivated, maintained by the six trainees already in position on the taped grid, spines pulled to a geometry the room seems to require of them, hands at angles that belong to a diagram rather than a body.

I find my mark. Third row, second from the left. Someone has reinforced it with blue tape over the original yellow tape, the corner edges curling up where shoes have caught them all week or all month, I don't know, I have not been here a month yet. I plant my feet on either side of the cross and I already want to shift weight. Not to the right exactly—the weight wants to go somewhere that isn't a direction, somewhere lateral to the room's axes, somewhere the grid has not allotted.

Forty-seven minutes on the clock.

The other trainees do not look at me. They are looking at Park or at their own reflections, at some point of focus that the discipline requires, and their looking has a quality of practiced vacancy I don't know how to manufacture. Park stands at the front, slight, with the kind of posture that is not held but inhabited, that was decided about years ago and then simply became the body's preferred state. I look at her. She does not look at me yet.

My hands want to do something. They settle.

---

Park says: and five, six, seven, eight.

She doesn't count the preparation counts out loud. She gives them in stillness, her own body receiving the counts as visible event, shoulders settling on five, sternum lifting on seven, and the six trainees receive the same counts in their bodies simultaneously, so that by eight the room has aligned into a single held breath, arms fixed, weight centered, spines sustaining themselves, and the silence before the music is now a different silence—taut, directional, aimed.

My weight centers. My arms fix. My fingers do something without consulting me: they release by a degree, the fifth finger of my right hand drifting a centimeter from where it was, and there is a small tilt through my wrists like a door opening a quarter inch in a wind, and then something through my torso—not a sway, smaller than a sway, the thought of a sway, the grammar of a sway without any of its vocabulary—and Park, at the front of the room, looking somewhere past the top of my head in the mirror, does not say anything.

Forty-five minutes.

The word she has used twice now is *lock*. *Lock the hips on the downbeat. Lock the arms on the isolation.* The word sits in my body somewhere close to the sternum and my body does not know what to do with it, because to lock requires a single answer to what the music asks, and the music—not yet playing, but already implied by the room, by the formation, by the eight counts of held stillness—the music has always asked me something I answer with a sentence, not a word.

*Lock*, my body rehearses.

My fifth finger drifts back. I bring it home.

---

The music starts.

The first eight-count sequence: chest pop on one, arm isolation through two and three, hip lock on the downbeat of four. The chest pop I know. My sternum moves and it moves correctly, I can feel that, and for one moment the sequence and my body are running in the same direction—

The isolation: the choreography demands a snap, the arm arriving at its position like punctuation, like a period the music places and the body lands on, and my arm *rolls* through it instead, the movement not traveling from shoulder to elbow to wrist in sequence but moving the whole limb as a conversation, as an answer that takes longer than a single beat because the question was more interesting than a single beat warranted, and my arm is in the right place at, I think, just slightly past the right time—

The downbeat: my hip doesn't lock. My hip is already there, already on the beat, ahead of the beat, a sixteenth note early with a weight shift that begins at the hip but becomes the whole left side, knee bending, heel releasing, a little traveling through my left shoulder, and it is a correct answer to something the music said a half-count before it said it, which means it is not an answer the choreography recognizes.

I can feel it. The translation happens somewhere I cannot intervene. The choreography arrives in my body as a second language and my body gives it back in the first one.

The other trainees: *chest*, one—*snap*, two—*lock*, four. Mine: *chest*, one—*conversation*, two-and—*already*, four.

The sequence looks like a different dance because it *is* a different dance. The music asked a question and I answered from inside the groove's pocket, from that place where the beat breathes before it lands, where you can feel the rubber-band pull of the rhythm and lean into the snap before it arrives—and the choreography wants zero counts, wants formation lock, wants the uniform line, wants me to wait for what the music says and *then* say yes.

Park stops the music.

---

She crosses the studio floor. Six steps from the front to the third row—I count them without deciding to count them—and she stands behind me, and I see her in the mirror, and the way she stands behind me is careful, like she is choosing not to occupy my space, like the geometry of her position has been chosen to be instruction and not intrusion.

She does not touch me.

She says: your right arm. She demonstrates with her own arm, moving it through the isolation without music, and in her arm the isolation is a statement, closed, certain, a shape that does not suggest anything beyond itself. She says: you roll through it. You're asking a question when the choreography is giving an answer. She says: your hip. She says: you live in the anticipation. She says: you have the most interesting raw instinct in this room.

She pauses.

She says: and it is destroying you.

She says: watch the clock.

In the mirror I see the clock above the door. Forty minutes. I hadn't looked at it before. Now I see it and I understand from the way she says *watch the clock* that I will not be able to stop seeing it.

Forty minutes, she says, and either you own the sequence or you don't come back tomorrow.

She crosses back to the front in six steps. The other trainees have held their positions. Their stillness has the quality of not having witnessed anything, which requires more discipline than anything else they've done.

The clock. Thirty-nine minutes, fifty.

---

I watch myself in the mirror.

The other trainees watch themselves in the mirror and they watch Park in the mirror and they watch the grid through the mirror, and their watching seems to produce a feedback loop that calibrates them, that lets them read their own bodies from the outside and correct. I have not used the mirror this way before. The mirror where I trained before was a single panel in a room that had other things in it—a water-stained ceiling, a window, a stereo on a folding table—and the mirror was one kind of information among several kinds of information, not the primary instrument.

Here the mirror is everything. The mirror is the room's true wall. Everything else—the clock, the door, the instructor—exists inside the mirror, behind me, available only as reflection.

I break the sequence into components. I say the counts under my breath, one and two and three and four, I say *lock* on the downbeat the way Park says it, and I watch the person in the mirror run the sequence. The arm isolation: I tell my arm to stop. It stops. It snaps. The person in the mirror lands on two counts with a snap. My arm says: all right. Thirty-seven minutes.

Hip on four: I wait. The count comes in my head and I wait one more fraction and I land on it. The person in the mirror lands on four. Park, at the front, does not move for a moment. Then she nods once. Once. A fractional movement of her own head that means something technically not nothing.

The thing is: stopping the arm means stopping where the arm had been going, and where the arm had been going was into the body of the phrase, into the music's interior, which is a place where time moves differently and the count is a suggestion and the groove is a conversation, and stopping there means losing the address. Following the count precisely, landing on the count, locking there: the person in the mirror does it correctly and I watch them and I know they are looking back at me and I am not sure we know each other.

The person in the mirror: chest pop. Snap. Lock.

I run it again. Thirty-five minutes. Chest pop. Snap. Lock. Park nods again. The architecture of the movement is right. I am inside the architecture and the architecture holds me upright and produces the correct shapes at the correct times and I look, I think, like the others—like someone who has agreed to the choreography's terms—and it feels like nothing. Not neutral nothing. Nothing like the removal of something that had been there, which leaves a shape behind, a hole in the arrangement of what the body says.

Thirty-three minutes.

I try not to feel it. I run the sequence. I watch the person in the mirror executing what is asked. I try to believe they are me. The attempt is grammatically complicated, so I try to stay inside the counts instead: one and two and three and four. One and two and three and four. The counting fills the space where the other thing had been. The syntax of it holds me upright the way the choreography holds the person in the mirror upright, by giving the body something to be obedient to, and obedience is a structure, and the structure does not ask whether you are inside it willingly.

Thirty minutes.

---

Park restarts the music for the full run. Twelve minutes on the clock.

First eight-count: I execute. The arm snaps. The hip holds until four. Park is watching and the other trainees are watching themselves and the room is running at the correct speed, everything happening in time, and I am in my correct position on my tape mark with my correct arms and my correct hip making its correct declaration on the correct count. Second eight-count: I execute. I say the count under my breath, one and two and, I watch the mirror, I land on four with the weight centered, the weight stays centered, there is a place down through my left side that wants to go somewhere but I hold it, I say *lock* to it and it stays.

Third eight-count.

I count to three and something happens that I did not decide.

The arm goes through the isolation—not rolling, not the rolling arm, not the full conversation—but there is something inside the snap that shouldn't be there, a breath of the roll caught at the beginning of the snap before the snap closes, and you would not see it if you were not watching closely, and then the hip: I land on four, I land on four, but I land from just barely early, from a place that is technically zero counts before four but experientially a fraction before, controlled—controlled, not sloppy, not traveling—a specific kind of early that acknowledges both the pocket and the count, the pocket and the count simultaneously, a sixteenth note of syncopation compressed inside a landing that the clock would call correct.

It's new. It has happened once. It doesn't have a name in the K-pop vocabulary and it doesn't have a name in the other vocabulary either, and if I had to name it I would say something like *the answer that knows it's late but arrives on time*, except that's wrong too, it's more like *the count receiving the groove without losing either*, but even that is wrong and I drop the attempt.

Park stops the music.

She points at me in the mirror. She says: *that*.

She says: *that is it*.

The room is very quiet. I don't know what she saw. I don't know if she saw the thing I felt or a different thing, if *that* is the moment the body finally agreed to be correct or if *that* is the moment the correct body found room for the other body inside it, if she is pointing at my compliance or at the thing I preserved inside my compliance, if she is saying *you've finally understood what I'm asking* or *you've finally made my choreography into yours*, and the gap between those two versions of *that* is either a gap I have just crossed or standing water, a gap so thin it might not exist or wide enough to drown in.

I look at the clock. It reads zero.

---

The trainees file out. They go in an orderly line through the door to the left and their departure is organized, sequential, without acknowledgment of me or the mirror or the moment. Park collects her things from the table by the front wall. She does not say anything else to me. She goes through the same door, and then the door is closed, and the studio is empty except for my body on the tape mark.

The clock is at zero. The clock is irrelevant. The clock at zero means it has no instructions for me.

I run the sequence without music.

One and two and three—the arm snaps. The hip lands on four with the weight centered. Technical. Contained. It looks, in the mirror, the way it looked when Park nodded and the nodding meant something. I run it again. One and two and three, the arm snaps. Four. The weight holds. Clean. Correct. I watch myself do it correctly six times and the mirror sends this back without comment.

I try to find it.

The third eight-count, the thing from before, the breath of the roll and the hip landing from the pocket: I aim for it. I count to three and I try to give the arm the beginning of the roll before I close it, I try to arrive on four from just before four, from that sixteenth-note earlier place that was also on time, that was both things—

The arm snaps. The hip lands. It's correct. It's only correct.

I've run it eleven times now. The room is the same dimensions as when I entered. The mirror wall, east side, the clock above the door at zero, the instructor's position at the front where no one stands, the grid of marks on the floor, mine third row second from left with the blue tape over the yellow tape. All of it here, containing me, reflecting me.

I go back to the beginning.
