# Blood Moon

The cart wheel has been broken a long time. Long enough that the wood has swollen with rain, long enough that the iron rim has bitten rust into the road. Yennefer studies it for a moment before stepping over it, because a wheel abandoned at the village gate tells her everything she needs to know about how quickly people left.

No bodies. She notes this carefully. No bodies usually means the thing that emptied the village is still inside it.

Lübeck, or what was Lübeck. The houses stand intact, which is worse than rubble, somehow. Rubble implies a clean violence. These shuttered windows and unlatched doors imply something that had time to be selective.

She moves through the main road with her hands ungloved, ready. The blood moon hangs overhead like an open wound.

She smells him before she sees him.

Not lilac and gooseberries — this is the inverse. Something ancient, like earth that hasn't seen light in centuries, like iron left inside a body. She stops walking. The smell has a *location*, which means he's already placed himself deliberately.

The man in the red coat is leaning against the well at the village center as though he's been waiting long enough to get comfortable about it. The wide-brimmed hat obscures his face until it doesn't. Orange glass catches the moonlight. He's smiling.

"Sorceress," he says. Not a greeting. An identification.

"Vampire," she replies, giving him the same courtesy.

Neither of them moves.

"You tracked whatever this is here too," she says. Statement, not question.

"I was curious." He tilts his head at an angle human necks don't accommodate easily. "I'm less curious now. The thing that cleared this village is dead in the church. Took four minutes." A pause that enjoys itself. "You've been tracking it for three days, I assume."

She has. She doesn't confirm this.

"The girl was already gone," he continues. "If that concerns you."

"She was already dead when I crossed into Pomerania. I wanted the thing that killed her." Yennefer moves closer to the well, not toward him, but not away. "Congratulations on your four minutes. Were you after anything in particular, or do you collect villages?"

He laughs. It's a genuine laugh, which is more disturbing than a performed one. "I'm after something difficult." He looks at her the way she's been looked at by men who thought they were looking at a woman, except this is nothing like that. This is assessment without appetite. "I thought you might be it."

"I'm not interested in being something you're after."

"You haven't heard what I'm after."

"I don't need to." She meets the orange-glass gaze without softening her expression. "Whatever you want from me, the answer is structured around the word no."

They stay like that for a moment, and then the east wall of the church explodes outward.

Not the thing from before — something else entirely, something that wore the original predator's death as a door to walk through. It is immense and it is wrong and it has approximately thirty seconds before Yennefer incinerates it, which it uses to throw Alucard through the church's remaining wall.

He hits stone. The sound it makes is the sound of something that should not survive impact.

She doesn't look. She's busy with the fire.

The incantation she uses is one she hasn't unfolded in forty years, because the last thing that required it is buried under Nilfgaard. The fire is violet-edged and structured, building itself between one breath and the next into something with intention. The creature doesn't have forty seconds after all.

The ash settles.

Behind her, the sounds of reconstitution. Bones finding each other. Wet things becoming dry things. She turns only when she's certain her expression is correct.

He's standing. He shouldn't be standing. The red coat hangs in pieces, and beneath it, she watches the last of his ribs slide back into position with the unhurried confidence of something that has done this before.

"Useful," he says, nodding at the ash.

"You're welcome," she says, as though she did it for him.

He looks at her for a long moment, and she sees something move behind the orange glass that isn't amusement. It's there and then it isn't, folded away somewhere she's not invited.

She knows that particular motion. She's made it herself, for centuries, every time something got close enough to find the seam.

She doesn't say anything about it. Neither does he.

"You're leaving," he says.

"I'm finished here." She's already pulling the portal open. The edges of it glow against the blood moon. "Don't follow me."

"I wasn't considering it." He's already looking away from her, back toward the ruined church, and she understands that this is the version of him that is true: not the performance at the well, but this — genuinely uninterested in what doesn't interest him, genuinely transfixed by what does.

She steps through.

The portal closes behind her like an eye.

At the village gate, the broken wheel sits in the road where someone left it, iron gone to rust, wood warped beyond repair. Somewhere inside what remains of Lübeck, a man in a destroyed red coat stands at a well and does not watch her go.

She doesn't look back to confirm this.

She doesn't need to.
