# Turn Back

The tray went first.

Maya grabbed for it—too late—and watched in the kind of slow-motion that only ever happens to you, never to anyone else, as the corner caught the cafeteria bench and the whole thing tipped. Chocolate milk detonated against the linoleum. A bowl of tomato soup spread like a crime scene. The tray itself clattered and spun with the specific acoustics of a school cafeteria, which is to say it was the loudest sound that had ever been produced in the entire history of the universe.

Every face between her and the far wall turned.

For a moment the whole cafeteria held its breath—that collective intake, that pre-laugh pause—and Maya stood in the center of it with soup on her left shoe and her face going the color of the soup and nowhere, absolutely nowhere, to go.

Then the laugh broke.

She picked up the tray. She stood up. She kept her chin level and walked—not ran, she would not run—to the napkin dispenser and cleaned her shoe with the paper shredding apart in wet strips in her hands, and the cafeteria moved on because cafeterias always moved on, but Jordan Marsh's table was still murmuring and she heard *Maya* and she heard *every time* and she did not look.

She hadn't eaten yet. She wasn't hungry anymore.

She sat down at the end of a half-empty table, alone with a replacement apple she didn't want, and that was when she noticed the watch.

She'd found it that morning—or it had found her, which wasn't quite the same thing but felt like it. Third period, she'd opened her gym locker and there it was, sitting in the back corner on top of her folded jacket as if it had always been there. No note. No box. Just the watch: matte black band, round face, a small brass crown on the right side. She'd looked around the locker room. Nobody.

She'd almost turned it in to the front office. Almost.

But it fit perfectly, and it looked—despite everything—genuinely cool, and the face had this interesting backwards design where the numbers ran counterclockwise, twelve at the top and then going eleven, ten, nine the wrong way around. Vintage, maybe. Or a quirk. She'd kept it on through the rest of the morning telling herself she'd deal with it later.

Now, sitting at the end of the lunch table with soup on her shoe, she turned it over in her fingers. The brass crown moved under her thumb like it was meant to be touched, like it was inviting the gesture, and she turned it the smallest amount without thinking—

The sound was like a gasp played backward.

The tray lifted from the floor.

The milk unspilled itself. The soup vacuumed back into its bowl. Every face between her and the far wall unturned. Maya's hand shot out and caught the corner of the tray and she held it, trembling, absolutely still, while the cafeteria continued its lunch business around her as if none of it had happened.

Because none of it had happened.

She stood there for five full seconds before she sat down, very carefully, with the tray balanced perfectly in both hands.

Then she started shaking.

---

She used it twice more that day.

Once in the gym, when she air-balled a free throw in front of Coach Deluca and the whole second period class, and the laughter started up, and she found the crown with her thumb without even deciding to. The ball reversed through the air and slapped back into her palms and she set her feet and shot again and it went through clean and she heard the sound of the net and felt stupid for how good that felt.

Once that evening at home, when she texted *I honestly think Riley is being so dramatic about the whole thing* and then realized she had sent it to Riley and not to her friend Priya. She had one heart-stopping second of staring at the message delivered notification before her thumb found the crown.

That one left her sitting on her bed hyperventilating quietly into her pillow.

She didn't know what the watch was. That was the part she kept circling and couldn't land on. She turned it over on her wrist in the lamplight. The face still ran backward. There was no brand name anywhere, no serial number she could find, nothing on the band. It fit like it had been made for her.

She should turn it in. She would turn it in.

But not yet. Maybe not yet.

She went to brush her teeth and caught herself in the bathroom mirror and leaned forward a little, frowning. There was a red cluster of acne on her left jaw that she was pretty sure hadn't been there this morning. She pressed her chin down to look closer. Her pants had felt odd all day—a little high at the ankle. She'd figured she was retaining water. She'd figured she'd grown a little. She was thirteen; that still happened.

*Stress,* she thought. She had a lot of stress.

She went to bed.

---

The next morning Priya said, *"Did you get taller?"*

Maya said, "I don't think so."

Priya shrugged. "You look taller."

"I'm probably just wearing these shoes funny."

She was wearing the same sneakers she wore every day.

She told herself that.

---

It happened on Wednesday.

She was cutting through the back parking lot near the gym—the shortcut she wasn't supposed to use—when she heard the tire screech. She turned and saw the whole thing in a single instant: Mr. Herne's old Honda fishtailing on the wet asphalt, the bike rack, Danny Obasi on his bicycle not yet looking up from his phone, and the distance between them closing with a speed that seemed both very fast and horribly, deliberately slow.

Maya screamed. Danny looked up.

The car hit the bike rack instead—Danny had jerked the handlebars at the last second—but the rack went over and Danny went with it and hit the asphalt hard, and the crack his head made against the pavement was a sound she felt in her back teeth.

She ran to him. He was conscious. He was trying to get up and couldn't. His arm was at an angle it shouldn't have been and there was blood coming from somewhere near his hairline and she could hear Mr. Herne out of his car and already on his phone and two other kids had come out of the gym doors and were running over and everything was—

Danny looked up at her. His eyes were wrong. Too dark, too confused.

"Hey," he said, like he was greeting someone at the end of a long hallway.

"Don't move," she said. "You shouldn't move."

"My arm hurts," he said.

"I know."

She looked at the watch.

She knew, distantly, that this was different. She knew that this was more than a lunch tray, more than a free throw, more than a text she regretted. She knew this with the part of her brain that was clear and calm and small and not in charge.

The other part—the part that was in charge—looked at Danny's too-dark eyes and his arm at its wrong angle, and her fingers found the crown.

She turned it.

Not a small turn. Not a thirty-second turn.

She turned it hard.

The sound was enormous—louder than she ever could have imagined, a shredding rewind-whine that came from everywhere and nowhere, from inside her skull and inside her chest, and the world smeared—color bleeding sideways, faces blurring into streaks—and she felt the ground tilt and pressed her palms hard against it only there was no ground—

She hit the pavement on her hands and knees and threw up.

She stayed there for a moment, gasping. Rain-wet asphalt. Her palms stinging. The smell of exhaust and lunch garbage. She made herself breathe through her nose, slow and even, until the dizziness passed enough to let her raise her head.

The parking lot was empty.

No Mr. Herne. No bike rack on its side. No other kids. The door to the gym was closed. She looked at the place where Danny had fallen and the asphalt was dry and unbloodied and looked the way asphalt looks when nothing has happened on it.

She stood up. Her legs felt like the legs of a chair that someone had cut a centimeter off each.

She walked back through the gym doors and into the hall.

Priya was standing at her locker.

"Hey," Maya said.

Priya looked up. "Oh, hey. Have you seen Danny? He was supposed to meet us out front."

"He—" Maya stopped. "He's probably still in class."

"He didn't have seventh period today. He texted me like twenty minutes ago that he was coming."

"He'll show up."

Priya looked at her a little longer than usual. "Are you okay?"

"I'm fine," Maya said. "Totally fine."

She walked to the water fountain and bent over it and drank for a long time. Then she straightened up, and the lights in the hallway felt too bright, and a teacher she didn't recognize at all walked past and nodded at her pleasantly, and she had the sudden certain feeling that the nodding teacher had not been here yesterday—that something was different in a way she could not diagram but could feel the same way you feel a room that someone has been rearranging while you slept.

She walked to the bathroom.

She looked in the mirror for a long time.

---

The shop on the corner of Fifth had a big plate-glass window, and she looked at herself in it because the bathroom mirror had been small and the light had been bad and she needed to be sure.

She stood on the sidewalk and looked at herself in the window, and after a moment the woman folding clothes inside glanced up at the strange kid standing frozen on the opposite side of the glass, and Maya barely noticed.

Her jaw was squarer. Not enormously—not freakishly—but measurably, definitely. The line of her cheekbones had sharpened a little. She was—she knew this suddenly and completely—taller. Not half an inch. Not a growth spurt, not retaining water, not wearing her shoes funny.

Taller.

She lifted her hand and looked at it. The same hand she'd always had. But she spread the fingers and studied the knuckles and looked for something she couldn't name, some proof that she was imagining it. She counted her fingers. Five. She counted the wrinkles of each knuckle.

She was thirteen years old and her hands looked older.

A kid from the grade below hers—she thought his name was Marcus, she'd seen him at the bus stop before—came around the corner with his backpack hanging off one shoulder and looked at her.

"Sorry," he said. "Are you like—are you visiting someone? At the middle school?"

"What?"

"I just thought maybe you were a high schooler or something. Because you—" He stopped. "Sorry. Never mind." He moved past her politely.

She stood on the sidewalk with the cold wind pushing at her hair and listened to the sound of the watch, which wasn't ticking—the watch didn't tick—but which she imagined she could feel, a slow and steady pulse against the inside of her wrist.

She reached for it.

And stopped.

She stood there with her fingers near the crown and her eyes on her own reflection in the plate glass, and she did not turn it.

Not yet.

She walked home.

---

The cat was on the front step.

She'd seen it before, she realized—a stray, she'd assumed, gray-striped with yellow-green eyes that were unusual enough that she would have remembered it. She'd seen it outside school on Monday. She'd noticed it on the wall Tuesday afternoon. And now it was here, on her front step, sitting with its tail wrapped around its feet in that precise and self-contained way cats had, looking at her.

She stopped on the walk.

The cat looked at her.

The yellow-green eyes were very still. The cat was very still. The whole street felt, for a moment, very still.

"You've seen this before, haven't you," Maya said.

It wasn't quite a question by the time it came out.

The cat blinked once—a slow, deliberate motion, like a door swinging shut and opening again.

Then it sat down, settling its weight, and waited.

Maya sat down on the step beside it. Not touching it. Just close.

She thought about Danny on the pavement. The angle of his arm and his too-dark eyes. She thought about the parking lot afterward, clean and dry, and Danny safe at school looking for Priya. She thought about the teacher she hadn't recognized, and the timeline that felt like a map where someone had moved a road.

She thought about her face in the shop window.

*Each time,* she thought. *Each time I go back, I go forward.*

She looked at the watch. The black face with its counterclockwise numbers. The brass crown.

She wasn't sure exactly how much she had aged. Two years, maybe. Maybe less. Enough that Marcus had asked if she was in high school. Enough that her own hands looked wrong. The magic—and she was deciding to call it that, because she needed a word and she didn't have a better one—the magic was eating her from the other direction, taking the time she borrowed and paying it back from her own body.

And the timeline was still wrong. The teacher she hadn't recognized. The small off-note in Priya's voice. She had gone back far enough to fix Danny, but she had gone back so far that something else had shifted, and she wasn't sure what, but she could feel it the way you feel a stone in your shoe that you can't quite reach.

She could fix that too. One more rewind. Not so far—just far enough.

But how much would it cost?

She didn't know.

She looked at the cat.

"If I use it again," she said, "I lose more. Right?"

The cat looked at her. The yellow-green eyes moved, just slightly, to the watch. Then back to her face.

"I know," she said. "I know."

She sat there in the cooling afternoon and thought about being fourteen, fifteen, sixteen. Thought about walking into school tomorrow with a face that had outpaced her life by years she hadn't lived. Thought about her mom seeing her. Thought about what that conversation would look like.

She thought about the teacher who hadn't been there yesterday. The small wrongness under the surface of the fixed day, like a bone that had healed crooked.

She thought about not being the kind of person who left things crooked if she could straighten them.

She thought about Danny's eyes.

She thought: *I'm the only one who knows what it cost to fix it. Everyone else got to keep not knowing.* 

She thought: *That's how it should be.*

She found the crown with her thumb.

---

This time it hurt.

Not the way pain usually worked—not from outside in. It came from the center of her, from somewhere behind her sternum, a pulling sensation like she was being turned inside out and then turned back, and the sound was enormous, was filling, was the sound of every cassette tape and every clock and every second she had ever lived running backward all at once—

She hit the pavement. She had stopped falling to her knees. She just hit the pavement sitting down and sat there with both palms flat on the cold concrete and her eyes closed and breathed.

One breath.

Two.

She opened her eyes.

The school steps. She was on the school steps, at the far end, near the bike rack, and the bike rack was standing upright and empty and there was no Mr. Herne, no blood, no scuff marks on the parking lot. A group of seventh graders she recognized spilled out of the side doors and walked right past her without looking twice.

The watch on her wrist was dark.

She turned her arm over and looked at it. The face was black—not displaying anything, not running, not counterclockwise or clockwise or any direction at all. She pressed her thumb to the crown and it didn't move. The brass had gone dull and fixed, fused somehow, a part of the watch rather than a mechanism. Like it had never been designed to do anything at all.

She looked up, and the school was there, and the afternoon was there, and Danny came through the front doors with his backpack and his bike helmet hanging off his wrist and no damage, no blood, no wrong-angle arm.

"Hey," she called.

He looked over. "Hey, Maya." Normal. Just normal.

She exhaled.

She looked down at her hands. She spread the fingers and studied the knuckles in the afternoon light. They were still older than they should have been. Not as old as she'd feared. But older. The kind of older that was going to require a mirror and several minutes of not looking away.

She was still taller.

She could feel it—the different angle of the world from up here, the slight foreignness of her own height. Somewhere between thirteen and sixteen, and she would have to be that now, and that was what it was.
