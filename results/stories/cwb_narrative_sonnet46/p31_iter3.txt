# Gone South
## Chapter One: The First Day

The man who drove me here—Gary, or maybe Jerry, I didn't really catch it over the radio—said *good luck, sweetheart* when he dropped me on Canal Street, and I stood on the sidewalk and watched his truck merge back into traffic and thought: sweetheart. Two days ago I told my parents who I actually am and my dad looked at me like I was something that had crawled out of a drain, and now a stranger with a confederate flag air freshener is calling me sweetheart. Tennessee is a hell of a place.

That was twenty minutes ago. Now I'm standing in front of a CVS with a dying phone and seventy-three dollars and a backpack that will not—*will not*—stay on my right shoulder. I hitch it up for the fifth time. Packed the thing in thirty minutes at midnight, grabbed what felt important, and apparently what felt important was: three t-shirts, my sketchbook, a charger for a phone I have no way to plug in, and the green hoodie with the thumb holes that I have worn approximately six hundred times because it makes me feel like a person. I forgot socks. I have the socks I'm wearing and no others which is, objectively, a catastrophic oversight.

The city smells like something fried and something rotten and underneath both of those things, the river. It's so *heavy* here. The air has texture. I keep feeling like I need to swim through it, but the weird thing—the thing I don't know what to do with—is that I've been walking for twenty minutes and nobody has looked at me. Back home everybody looks. Everybody in our whole stupid town has a file on everybody else, a permanent record, and I have been in that file since fifth grade and it says: *weird. Difficult. Concerning behavioural patterns.* I know exactly how Mrs. Delacroix said that on the phone to my mom because my mom does the face—the tight, careful face—whenever she's about to recite something back to me that a professional said, like the professionalism is what makes it true.

Two days ago I told them I'm trans.

I'm not going to think about that right now.

The backpack slips. I grab it.

Okay. I need: food, water, a place to sit that isn't a sidewalk, a plan. I have seventy-three dollars and the charger is useless without power and my phone is at eleven percent which means I can either use it or save it, and either way it's the same outcome eventually, so.

"You look like you're doing math in your head," someone says. "Bad math."

She's sitting on a planter outside the CVS—maybe seventeen, maybe older, wearing a denim jacket with something embroidered on the chest that I can't read from this angle. Hair in two buns, one of which is somewhat defeating the other. She's eating chips from a bag she's holding like it's a chalice.

"I don't have enough variables yet," I say. "To do the math."

She looks at me for a second. She has the particular look of someone who is deciding something quickly, running the numbers I don't have access to. "Where'd you come from?"

"Tennessee."

"When?"

"Last night."

She nods like this confirms something. "You eaten?"

"I'm fine."

"That's not what I asked."

I notice she's shifted her bag of chips slightly toward me, a small sideways gesture. I notice I'm going to cry if someone is actually nice to me, so I look at the street instead. A woman in an enormous feathered hat walks past with a small dog in a backpack and the dog is wearing a matching hat and somehow this doesn't seem strange. Nothing seems strange compared to the last forty-eight hours. My threshold for strange has been structurally altered.

"I'm Taylor," I say.

"Simone." She holds the chips out. I take some, because I'm human and I haven't eaten since a gas station hot dog at two a.m. that I'm actively trying not to think about.

We eat for a minute. The salt is incredible.

"So what's your deal," she says. It's not really a question.

"I came out." I say it before I plan to. "To my family. Two days ago. Trans." The words feel weird outside of my head, in the actual air, in a city that doesn't know me. "It went badly."

"Yeah," she says. Not *I'm sorry*, not *that's awful*, just *yeah*, like she's noting weather. Like it's a data point in a model she's already built. "You got people here?"

"No."

"Money?"

"Some."

"Plan?"

"Working on it."

She tips the chip bag toward herself, shakes the crumbs into her palm. She's quiet for long enough that I think the conversation is over, and I'm already doing the thing where I'm mad at myself for trusting a stranger, already constructing the internal monologue where this is one more data point in the case against me—*concerning behavioural patterns*—

"There's a place," she says. "Not amazing. But it's not outside."

I look at her. "Why would you tell me that?"

She gives me the look that means: *you really have to ask?* She opens her mouth, closes it, chooses something different than what she was going to say. "I know what you are," she says finally, "because I was it."

Was. Past tense. I don't ask what she is now.

"Okay," I say. "Okay, yeah."

She stands up, adjusts her own backpack with the practiced efficiency of someone who has been carrying it longer than I have. She starts walking and I follow her and the French Quarter opens up around us—loud and strange and queerer than anything I have ever physically stood inside—and on a balcony above a bar there are two men in sequins having what looks like a cheerful argument, and somewhere a trumpet is playing something slow and mournful and honestly exactly right, and I hitch my backpack up again.

I don't know if Simone is safe. I don't know what the place is or what it costs or what it costs. I know I have seventy-three dollars and no socks and the hoodie with the thumb holes, and that for the first time in sixteen years, nobody in this entire city knows my name.

That's something. I think that might be something.

The strap slips.

I grab it, and I keep walking.
