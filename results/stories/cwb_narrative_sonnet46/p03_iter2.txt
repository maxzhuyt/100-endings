# Love in the Limelight

The door opened the way doors open when someone needs them to close again immediately, and the man who came through it had the specific, practised quality of a person folding himself small—collar to his jaw, dark glasses that belonged to no particular Cardiff afternoon, breathing controlled but only just. He pulled the door shut behind him and stood with his back to it, and she looked up from the invoice she'd been losing an argument with for twenty minutes.

She looked back down.

The invoice had a discrepancy of four pounds eighty. She was fairly sure it was Geraint's fault. She picked up her pen.

"Hi," the man said.

"We're open," she said, which was true and sufficient.

She could hear him not moving. It was a particular quality of stillness, the kind that expected to be noticed. The shop was narrow—two rooms, low ceiling, the particular smell of old paper that she'd stopped perceiving years ago and which visitors always mentioned first, before anything else, as though they'd discovered it. Welsh-language titles along the left-hand wall, poetry facing them across the aisle, and then the longer room at the back where the secondhand stock accumulated in a way that was almost but not quite organised. She had been meaning to sort the secondhand stock since 2019. She was still meaning to.

"Right," the man said. "Good. Great."

He wasn't moving toward the books. She put down the pen.

He had taken the glasses off, which was when she understood. Not because she watched the programmes—she did not especially watch the programmes—but because his face was the kind of face that had been used as a reference point in conversations she hadn't started and couldn't stop for the better part of two years. Her sister. Her sister's friends. The internet, in its democratic and inescapable way. He was taller than she would have expected. This was also something her sister had mentioned.

He was looking out through the window in the door with an expression of focused neutrality that she recognised as performed calm, and she could hear, faintly, the particular audio signature of a cluster of people doing something purposeful with their phones.

"Paparazzi," she said.

The word seemed to reach him slowly. He turned. "Sorry?"

"Outside. I assume."

"Yes." A pause. "I lost my—there was a crowd at the Senedd. I just needed a—" He stopped. Started again with more control. "Could I possibly—"

"How long?"

He blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"How long do you need?"

He looked back at the door, calculating something. "Ten minutes? They'll move on. They usually move on." Something crossed his face that might have been the word *usually* catching on the way out. "Maybe fifteen."

She looked at him for a moment. He had the glasses in his hand and was doing something with them that wasn't quite fidgeting. She looked back at the invoice.

"Don't touch the display by the door," she said. "I've just done it."

---

He didn't touch the display by the door. He moved into the shop with the careful step of someone uncertain of the rules, which was, she thought, probably not his natural register. She kept working. The discrepancy was four pounds eighty and it was definitely Geraint's fault because Geraint had done the last order and Geraint believed in rounding, which was not the same as accounting.

"These are beautiful," the man said. He was standing in front of the Welsh shelf.

"Yes."

"I can't read them."

"No."

A short pause. "That came out wrong. I meant—I can't read Welsh. I don't speak it."

"I know what you meant."

She heard him exhale. It was the exhale of a man revising his approach. She had a brief, clear image of a toolbox being sorted through.

"Do you have any—" He moved along the shelf, tilting his head to read spines. "Do you stock Chatwin? *On the Black Hill?*"

She looked up at that. "We do, actually."

"My mother gave it to me. Years ago. I've never—" He shook his head. "I keep meaning to."

"It's in the back," she said. "Second shelf from the left, bottom half. It'll be an old Picador if it's the copy I'm thinking of."

He went. She listened to his footsteps on the floorboards and the particular small sounds of someone looking at books properly—the draw of a spine, a pause, the return. He came back holding the Picador.

"Orange cover," he said.

"Yes."

"It's yours, isn't it."

She looked up again.

He was holding it with the particular care of someone who had noticed it was a personal copy. Which it was. She'd put it out last year in a clear-eyed moment that she'd been regretting intermittently ever since, not because she'd stopped loving it but because she had loved it quite intensely at seventeen and some feelings were easier to keep at arm's length on a shelf than in a drawer.

"Mark it five pounds," she said. "There's a sticker sheet by the till."

He looked at the cover, then at her. Something in his expression was trying to work out whether this was a test.

"Are you sure?"

"I'm sure I'd like four pounds eighty," she said, "to balance an invoice."

She heard him laugh—a real one, not the performed kind, shorter and slightly surprised—and he went to the till and found the sticker sheet with only minor difficulty, which meant he had been paying attention to the layout of the shop since he came in. She wasn't sure why she noticed that.

---

"What's your name?" he said.

He was leaning against the shelving unit opposite the till now, the book held loosely in one hand. He'd settled into the shop the way a person settles when they've decided to stay somewhere they weren't sure about. She'd watched it happen without appearing to watch it happen, which had required a low-grade effort she was finding mildly annoying.

"Sioned," she said.

He said it back, correctly, on the first try. She raised an eyebrow.

"I've been filming in Cardiff for four months," he said. "You pick things up."

"Some people don't."

"Some people don't try." He said it without self-congratulation, which surprised her more than an attempt at charm would have. He turned the book over and read the back. "Did you grow up here?"

"Rhondda. Came here for university. Stayed."

"What made you—" He gestured at the shop.

"I worked here as a student. The owner retired." She looked at the invoice. "It made more sense than it should have."

"It doesn't look like a mistake."

"No," she said. "It's not."

She was aware that this was more than she usually offered. She was also aware that it was in response to a real question rather than a performing-interest question, and she had decided, without deciding, to answer accordingly. She kept her eyes on the page.

"I like it here," he said. Not performing it. Just saying it.

She said nothing. Four pounds eighty. It was definitely Geraint.

"I mean the city," he said. "Not just—" He stopped. "Though I do mean the shop, as well." He looked up at the low ceiling, at the water stain in the corner that she'd been documenting with photographs since 2021 in case it was ever relevant, at the string of postcards tacked along the beam that had accumulated, one by one, from no particular intention. "It's a real place," he said. "Some places you go to for work and they're just a backdrop. This isn't a backdrop."

"No," she said. "It isn't."

The rain—which had been implying itself for an hour—began. It came against the window with the lateral commitment of Cardiff rain, which was not dramatic but was thorough, the kind of rain that didn't announce itself as an event but simply became the condition of things.

She heard him breathe in.

"My agent thinks I should move to LA," he said. "Permanently."

She hadn't asked. She looked up.

"She's probably right," he said. "Pragmatically." He was looking at the rain. "I keep not doing it."

"Why?"

He turned his head. He seemed faintly surprised to be asked, which was its own kind of information.

"I don't know," he said, which seemed true. "I think I'm afraid it would—" He stopped. He looked at the book in his hand, and then back out at the rain, and he said carefully: "I think I'm afraid I'd stop being able to tell the difference between what I actually think and what would be useful to think."

The shop was quite quiet. The rain did what it was doing. Somewhere in the back, a draft moved through the secondhand room.

She could feel the sentence she might say, and she didn't say it, and he didn't seem to need her to.

---

Her phone rang.

It was Geraint. She knew it was Geraint before she looked because Geraint was the only person who rang rather than texting, a practice she had complained about for three years without effect. She looked at the screen. Geraint.

"I need to take this," she said.

"Of course." He straightened from the shelf. "Of course, I should—" He looked at the door. The rain had not let up. She watched him make a decision about the rain, and the decision was that the rain was fine. "Thank you," he said. "For the—" He held up the Chatwin.

She nodded.

He was already doing the collar and the glasses, reassembling himself, and she watched it happen in the way you watch a landscape change from inside a moving vehicle—nothing you can arrest, not quite your business. The performances going back on like a coat. He had a good coat, she noticed. She had the brief, clear thought that she was going to tell her sister absolutely nothing.

---

He paused at the door.

"Is there—" He stopped. He seemed to be weighing whether to ask something, and the weighing looked real. "If I came back," he said. "To buy something. Would that be—"

"We're open Monday through Saturday," she said. "Ten till six."

It was not a yes. It was also not a no. He seemed to understand the distinction. Something moved through his expression that she couldn't quite name and then he smiled, briefly, privately, not the smile from the things she'd seen him in but a smaller one, and he went out through the door.

The bell sounded.

She stood at the till. The rain was still coming down the window in its patient lateral way. Geraint had rung off, leaving a voicemail she would listen to when she was ready. The invoice sat on the counter with its discrepancy of four eighty.

She thought about the sentence she hadn't said. *I think I know what you mean.* She'd held it back with some effort, and the effort had been worth it, and she was not going to think about whether that was quite true.

She went to the back room.

The secondhand stock needed sorting. There were water stain photographs to log. There were a number of practical and pressing things that required her to be exactly here, in this particular city, in this particular room that smelled of paper and belonged to her, and she was going to do them, and she was going to be fine, and in a while she was going to find the window in the organising and she was going to stand in it for just a moment, unhurried, looking at nothing.

She picked up a box.

The rain came on.
