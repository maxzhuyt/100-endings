# Love in the Limelight

The bell above the door released a sound like a small animal meeting its end, and Efa Rowlands looked up from her invoice spreadsheet to find a man pulling the door shut behind him with the specific, contained urgency of someone who had made a calculation and acted on it before anyone could stop him.

He stood with his back to her. His shoulders were moving.

He was catching his breath.

"We're open," she said. "In case that's useful information."

He turned around. The rain had done something unflattering to his hair — one side pressed flat, the other deciding independently to go in a different direction — and his face was flushed in the particular way of someone who had recently been running and didn't much care for it. Even so, he was striking in the way that certain people were striking, as though bone structure were a form of diction.

Efa returned her eyes to the spreadsheet.

"I'm so sorry," he said. His voice landed in the room the way expensive furniture did: with a certainty about its own quality. "I only need—" He turned briefly to indicate the window, the high street beyond it, the small migrating herd of photographers she could see tracking slowly northeast with their long lenses tilted toward the sky like damaged antennae. "A few minutes. Genuinely."

"It's a bookshop. Minutes are complimentary."

He smiled then. She recognised it the way one recognised a landmark from a postcard — the thing itself, present and undeniable, bearing little relationship to the experience of actually being there. It was an extraordinary smile in the technical sense, and she could see exactly how it had been refined over time, which made it difficult to feel the full intended force of it.

"Thank you," he said. He let the smile do the work it was built for. "Honestly. You're really—"

"Travel section is to your left if you need cover. The Pembrokeshire Ordnance Survey from 1987 is large enough to get your face behind if it comes to that."

He blinked. The smile adjusted slightly — recalibrating. "Right."

He moved toward the desk with the easy flow of someone accustomed to crossing unfamiliar rooms as though they'd been expecting him. He put out his hand. "Callum."

"I know." She took it briefly. "Efa."

"Is that—"

"Welsh, yes. We're in Wales."

"I knew that."

"Did you."

"I did," he said, with tolerant amusement at himself, looking around the shop now — the low ceilings pulling the space in close, the woodburner putting out a steady red glow in the corner, the handwritten section cards in her own sloped writing, the shelves neither antiquarian nor newly curated but simply *full,* in the way of a room inhabited rather than arranged. "It's lovely in here."

"You say that with some surprise."

"I'm not surprised, I only—" He stopped. He looked at her with frank attention, as though she were a particular kind of problem he hadn't encountered before. "Are you like this with everyone, or have I done something?"

"Like what?"

"Efficient," he said.

"I'm like this with everyone who isn't browsing." She nodded at the shelves. "If you're going to wait for the cameras to move on, you could at least be useful about it. Pick a section."

He drifted, with the slight sheepishness of a man following an instruction he hadn't expected to obey, toward the nearest row of shelves. She watched him read the section heading. She watched him stop.

He stood there a moment before he reached up and touched the spine of the book with one finger, as though confirming it was real.

"You've got the *Handbook of the Birds of the Western Palearctic.*" His voice was different — quieter, stripped of the baritone projection. "The condensed version, but still."

"The full nine volumes are in the back."

He looked over his shoulder. "They're for sale?"

"No."

"Even—"

"They're mine."

He turned back to the shelf. He drew the condensed volume out slowly and opened it at the centre, and the care he took was not performed. She noticed the ease of it — the habitual way his hand smoothed the page, the way his eyes moved and stopped and moved again. He had spent time inside this book in another form, somewhere else.

"I go to Cors Caron," she said, and wasn't sure why she said it. "In October, mostly. The red kite roost. Forty birds on an ordinary evening, fifty if conditions are right."

He looked up. The professional brightness had gone off him like a switch tripped, and what was beneath it was simply a face at rest, more interesting than the other thing by some margin. "I did the Dyfi roost a few years back. January. Counted sixty before the light went."

"That's a reasonable count for January."

"I nearly drove into a ditch on the way back. I'd told my publicist I was having a—" He made a gesture that seemed to encompass the whole infrastructure of what it meant to be him. "Wellness afternoon. Some nonsense. I couldn't exactly—" He stopped.

"Explain what you were actually doing."

"Why a man goes and stands in a field in the dark on his own," he said. "In January."

She looked at the woodburner for a moment. The wood shifted and re-settled and the light changed briefly. "Because it doesn't know who you are," she said. "The kite. It has no idea. You could be anyone standing in that field."

He didn't say anything immediately. She'd expected him to pick it up and turn it into something charming — the flash of self-deprecating wit, the pivot that made vulnerability attractive and therefore safe. It didn't come. He just stood there with the book in his hands and didn't say anything, and when he did speak the sentence had no architecture to it at all.

"That's exactly right," he said. "That's precisely — yes."

It was so plainly meant that she had no prepared response, which was unusual enough that she was glad of the excuse to look back down at her desk and straighten papers that didn't need straightening. Outside, the photographers had migrated further up the street. Neither of them noted this.

"There's a wader question," he said, after a moment, in a tone that suggested he was choosing a less exposed route back into conversation. He was still holding the book. "Temminck's stint. Every time I think I've got one definitively, someone reliable tells me I've been looking at a little stint and I should revise my expectations."

"Leg colour," she said.

He waited.

"Temminck's has yellow-green legs. Little stint runs darker. Next time you think you're looking at one, start with the legs." She found a pencil behind her ear, found the back of an old receipt on the desk, and wrote: *legs: yellowish-green* in her sloped handwriting. She pushed it across the desk without looking up.

He took it. He read it. She didn't watch his face because she had made a decision not to.

"You could have just let me wait out the five minutes," he said.

"You hadn't settled the bill."

"I haven't bought anything."

"You've had a conversation." She pulled the spreadsheet back toward her. "I'm counting it."

She heard him move toward the door. The woodburner ticked. She did not look up.

The bell shrieked. He paused — she could feel it, the pause, the hand on the frame.

"Efa."

"Mind the kerb when you go," she said. "It's uneven. The council have been notified."

She heard him laugh — short, unpolished, the other version of the sound he made — and then the door swung to, and she listened to his footsteps go, and she waited until she was certain he was beyond the glass before she looked up.

The gap on the shelf was small. Just the one condensed volume, and the nine in the back were untouched, and it was a minor enough theft to overlook.

She looked at the receipt still on the desk — she'd given him the one she'd written on, but the next one down had the impression of the pencil through it, ghost letters: *legs: yellowish-green.*

She folded it along that impression and put it in the pencil drawer.

Then she returned to the invoices, with the fire going in the corner, and the rain spotting the glass, and the kite roost still three weeks off, out on the bog in the dark, not knowing the name of a single living soul.
