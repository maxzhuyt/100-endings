# The Morska Conspiracy
## The Underhold

The door opened onto silence, which was worse than anything else.

Aya had prepared herself for guards. For the mechanical click of crossbow triggers, the low animal grunt of men paid to be obstacles. She had not prepared for *this*—the underhold spread before them like a held breath, lit by the blue-green glow of whale-oil lanterns that turned every shadow aquatic, submarine, wrong. The artifact cases lined the walls in their ironbound rows. And at the center of the room, seated at the guild master's table as though she had been there for hours—

She had.

Maren Voss. Decidedly, insultingly alive.

Aya's hand found the door frame. Behind her, she heard Simeon's pocket watch tick once, twice, three times in the cavern of his waistcoat, each second dropping like a stone into still water.

"Close the door, children." Voss did not look up from the papers before her. "There's a draft."

She was older than her reputation suggested—older than the broadsheets, older than the whisper-portraits traded in back rooms. A woman assembled from severity: grey-streaked hair pinned to military precision, a coat that had absorbed a decade of pipe smoke, hands that rested flat on the table with the patient authority of someone who had already won. Two bodyguards materialized from the shadows at the room's far corners. A third stepped in behind the trio. The door swung shut.

*Count the exits*, Aya told herself. *Count exits and breathe and think—*

"The raid," Katra said flatly, beside her. "It was—"

"A performance," Voss agreed. "The watch plays at theatre when I require it. Sit down."

Nobody sat.

Simeon's watch ticked. Aya felt it in her back teeth.

Voss looked up then, and her gaze moved across the three of them with the dispassion of a magistrate reviewing ledgers. She paused on Aya the longest. Her expression did not change.

"Remove the mask," she said.

"I'll do no such—"

The bodyguard behind her was faster than she was. A hand at her collar, a jerk of motion, and the painted leather mask hit the floor and skated two feet across the stone. Aya stood exposed in the blue-green light—high cheekbones, dark eyes, the particular quality of face that money manufactured over generations and that could not be disguised by guild-work and bravado.

The silence that followed was different from the first one.

"*Ah,*" said Maren Voss.

Aya watched the calculation happen behind the woman's eyes. She had seen that look before—on her father's solicitors, on the men across the table at the merchant tribunals her aunt made her attend, on everyone who had ever assessed her net worth before her personhood. She hated it with a ferocity that was nearly marital. But she filed the hatred away. She would need both hands free.

"Aya Renault," Voss said, tasting the syllables. "Renault's daughter. The *elder* Renault's granddaughter." A pause. "Your mother was the witch."

The word landed where it was aimed.

"My mother," Aya said carefully, "was hanged for a crime the crown has never adequately defined."

"They defined it well enough with the rope." Voss folded her hands. "I thought the family had gone quite far underground after that business."

"Far enough." Aya held the woman's gaze with a steadiness she did not feel anywhere below her throat. Simeon's watch. Tick. Tick. Tick. *Think.* "Far enough that my father would pay considerably to ensure the nature of my recent evenings remained a private matter."

"Would he."

"He is a man of position. I am the last of the name. Whatever his feelings toward me—" and they were complex, and cold, and she would not examine them here, not in this room, not with these eyes on her— "he cannot afford the alternative."

Katra, to her left, was very still in the particular way of someone who has been in worse rooms and survived them by becoming furniture. Simeon had not moved either, though she could hear him breathing. The watch ticked on.

Voss leaned back. "You came for the Scythan piece."

"We did," Aya said. Denial was its own kind of insult.

"And what," Voss said pleasantly, "were you proposing to do with it?"

"Leverage." She had rehearsed this answer for a different conversation, but the shape of it still held. "The artifact establishes claim. Claim establishes authority. The guild requires leadership." She paused. "It still does. Whatever tonight was meant to demonstrate."

Something moved through Voss's expression—not quite amusement, not quite respect. The bodyguards had not lowered their weapons.

"You have," Voss said slowly, "your mother's nerve."

"I have her other gifts as well." Aya let that sit in the air between them, let Voss's imagination do the cartography. In this world, men trusted brass and oil and steel. Magicka was the shape of their nightmares, the thing that happened to other people's daughters, the charge read aloud before the gallows drop. She watched the flicker of it move through the bodyguards—a shoulder shifting, a jaw tightening—and she had not even done anything. Only named the possibility. Only let them hear the echo of the word *witch* in a closed stone room three floors underground.

"Hurting us is wasteful," she continued, each word placed with the deliberate care of a surgeon. "I am worth more intact and cooperative. The guild is worth more with a resolution than a bloodletting." She met Voss's eyes. "And I suspect, given that you staged your own arrest rather than vacated the city, that you are not yet finished here. You need something. I would like to discuss what that is."

The watch ticked.

Voss studied her for a long moment—the cold, appraising look of a woman weighing one kind of risk against another.

"Sit down," she said again.

This time, Aya sat.
