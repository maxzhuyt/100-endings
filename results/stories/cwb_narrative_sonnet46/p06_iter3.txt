# Carnival of Dangerous Intentions

She did not go to the shooting gallery.

She had told herself, on the subway uptown, that she would not go at all — that she would step off at Stillwell and walk the avenue once, take the measure of the evening, and if nothing happened, nothing would happen and she could call it a decision. At Coney Island in August the avenue was its own reassurance: ordinary people eating ordinary things, families dragging small children toward the water, vendors calling into the general noise with the faith of men who expected nothing and therefore risked nothing. She had walked it once. She had not turned back.

Now she stood twenty feet from the shooting gallery and watched a boy miss a tin duck three times while the smoke from the rifle drew a thin question mark into the air above him.

The lights had come on around her without her noticing when they came on, the way it's always happened — one moment dusk, the next this amber and red announcement, the bulbs humming at a frequency she felt in the back of her teeth. They had been strung in haste, probably, along wire already strung by someone else, and they would outlast the carnival and the summer and perhaps several other things Eleanor preferred not to enumerate.

She was here because he had suggested it and she had said yes before she'd finished hearing the sentence. That was the whole of the explanation and she knew it and did not intend to pursue it further.

---

The cotton candy stand was two stalls to her left.

She hadn't moved toward it. The smell arrived anyway — or she arrived at the smell, or the crowd shifted and the warm air moved and suddenly it was simply there: a sweetness that had detached from its source and was traveling on its own, looking for wherever it belonged. It was not quite the smell of cotton candy. It was the idea of that smell, distilled, the way certain songs played in the wrong key become briefly more themselves. It reached some interior she hadn't left a door open for, and a Saturday arrived in it — sun-yellow, specific in texture if not in location, a fairground she could place in neither city nor year. There had been a hand on her shoulder. Her father's, she thought, but the hand would not resolve into a person. It had steered her toward something bright and she had gone without being asked and it had been correct: she had wanted the bright thing, she had simply not said so. She remembered the feeling of that — of wanting and not saying, of being understood preemptively — with a clarity she could not attach to any actual face.

She bought a cone she had no intention of eating and stood in the crowd holding it.

---

*His locks.*

It arrived the way it had been arriving all week, uninvited, slightly mortifying, wearing the diction of her grandmother's novels like a costume that didn't quite fit the occasion. Her grandmother had read in secret, which Eleanor had understood as a child to mean privately and now understood to mean something else. *His locks:* obsidian curls, she had written in no letter to no one, in the interior place where she stored things she would not account for in daylight. She was not a woman who used words like *locks.* She was a woman who typed correspondence for an importer of dry goods on West Forty-Second Street and ate lunch at her desk and had once, at a party in someone's apartment in the West Seventies, stood at a window for twenty minutes because the lights on the river demanded it, and said nothing to anyone, and gone home.

She pressed the word down. It surfaced. She pressed it down again and moved on, which was not, she was aware, the same as winning.

---

"You came."

He had appeared beside her in the way that crowds allow — without transit, without approach, simply present where a moment before there had been only strangers. The curls were exactly as she'd described them to herself. The eyes were darker than she'd remembered and possessed of a quality she would require several more minutes to name: they were attentive and remote simultaneously, focused on something that she was perhaps obscuring rather than constituting.

"I was in the neighborhood," she said.

It was a lie both of them were too intelligent to pretend was anything else, which meant it was functioning not as deception but as a kind of courtesy — an acknowledgment that they had agreed, without discussing it, to begin at a slight angle to the truth and see how far that carried them.

"You were in the neighborhood." He repeated her words without mockery, rolling them for hidden weight. "How is the neighborhood treating you?"

"Loud." She turned toward the shooting gallery, where the boy had finally struck something and was being handed a stuffed bear with the expression of a man confronting unexpected consequence. "He didn't deserve that."

"No one deserves what they win at carnivals." Daniel studied the bear with the brief, cataloguing attention he gave to most things — a glance that implied an entire assessment. "That's the structure of it. You pay for the attempt, not the result."

She looked at him because the sentence wanted looking at. Up close he was less beautiful than she had assembled him in memory and more precise — the face becoming specific in the way any face does upon second meeting, trading the general impression for something harder and more accountable. There was a scar at the outer corner of his left eye, faint as silverpoint, a notation in a margin she couldn't read.

"I don't know your name," she said.

"You've known you didn't know it all week." He smiled. The smile had a quality of accuracy rather than warmth — it arrived where it intended to. "You simply haven't asked."

"I'm asking."

"Daniel." A pause that she recognized as deliberate and allowed anyway. "And you have been Eleanor from the beginning."

She had not told him that. She turned the week over in rapid review — the party, the window, the conversation that had been somehow both brief and total — and she was nearly certain she had not told him that.

*Nearly.*

She let it pass. This was its own kind of information, which she received and did not open.

---

A child ran between them and was recovered by a woman who scooped him up with the automatic grace of long practice and carried him sideways into the crowd. The moment lasted two seconds and occupied the space where a response might have gone.

"Walk with me," Daniel said.

It was not a question, precisely. It was shaped like a question and inhabited the grammatical space a question would use, but it did not carry the structural uncertainty of one — the slight upward modulation, the implied willingness to hear no. She noticed this. She filed it in the same place as his knowing her name, the place she was assembling without intending to, the folder labeled *things that would be simple to look at directly.*

"All right," she said.

---

They moved along the boardwalk together, which meant they moved inward, which meant with each step the ocean became less visible behind them and the crowd thickened and the light changed quality in the way that light changes when it has fewer surfaces to escape from. Eleanor noticed this in the abstract, dispassionate register she used for things she preferred to observe rather than interpret, the register that left her hands free.

The cotton candy was still in her hand. She hadn't tasted it. The pink of it was already beginning to collapse in the humid air, drawing inward on itself, becoming something smaller and denser than it had been. She carried it anyway.

"What is it you do?" she asked. She had been raised to ask questions. She had been raised to keep the conversation moving as a matter of social decency, as a way of honoring the other person's presence, and she had done this so long that she could no longer always be certain whether she wanted the answers or was simply upholding a practice.

"Currently?" He paused before a ring-toss booth, examined it without any apparent interest in playing, moved on. "I wait."

"That's not a real answer."

"It's the accurate one." He glanced at her sideways — not fully, not enough to constitute a full look, just the edge of his attention angled her way. "When most people ask what someone does, they mean: help me decide who you are. Give me the category. I'm sparing you the construction."

She should have bristled at this. She found it, instead, interesting — the way she found certain problems interesting, not because they were pleasant but because they had a hidden structure she wanted to locate. That she found it interesting rather than irritating told her something, and she placed the something in the folder without examining it and walked on.

---

They stopped at a small stage at the interior of the lane where a magician in evening wear was checking his equipment with the air of a man reviewing the same documents he reviewed every evening at the same hour. His assistant — a woman in blue sequins whose boredom had been crystallized by repetition into something that resembled dignity — was arranging herself to be sawed in half. She climbed into the box with her limbs composed into the necessary shape, cooperating with a procedure she hadn't designed and couldn't see the whole of from the inside. The crowd pressed together. People who had no particular investment in the outcome pressed closer anyway, because that is what proximity to the ambiguous instructs you to do.

The lid of the box came down.

Daniel said nothing beside her. Eleanor watched the magician place his hands on the saw.

There was a child in the crowd to her left who was eating caramel corn and watching through the gaps between adult bodies with an expression of complete and unguarded attention, the face of someone who had not yet learned to hold the self in reserve while observing. She had been that, once. Before she had learned — and she had learned, she had learned it young and learned it thoroughly — that watching openly is a form of exposure, that wanting plainly is a kind of vulnerability from which careful people generally protect themselves.

The saw began to move.

Daniel made a small gesture — not toward her, not quite, a movement of the hand that implied the crowd, the stage, the evening generally. "Another hour?" he said.

Eleanor looked at the cotton candy. The pink had collapsed almost entirely now, dissolving in the heat and the salt air, becoming a residue of itself. The Saturday floated past again — that hand on her shoulder, that steering toward the bright thing she had wanted without being asked, the particular feeling of being known before she'd spoken. She had been grateful for it, she remembered now. She had understood it as kindness of a high order: to be seen accurately by someone, and moved accordingly, without having to articulate your own wanting.

She dropped the cone in the bin without having eaten any of it.

"One hour," she said.

The magician raised the saw above the divided box. The crowd contracted another inch. The lights held everything in amber, that particular amber that flattens time and makes the present tense feel like the only one, and somewhere behind them — behind the noise, behind the lanes filling with evening and strangers and the accumulated human project of having a good time in the face of everything — the ocean continued its indifferent business, the water arriving and withdrawing and arriving without memory of what it had already done.

They moved together, further into the crowd, further from the railing where the dark water was still just visible if you turned around, which Eleanor did not do. Daniel walked beside her with the ease of a man to whom the outcome of an evening has already presented itself, who is now simply accompanying events toward their natural completion, who offered a door and watched it open and is not surprised by what follows — only, perhaps, mildly satisfied, in the offhand way of someone who has done this before and will likely do it again and has learned not to invest too heavily in any particular evening's details.

The lights buzzed on around them. The cotton candy smell moved through the warm air between the stalls.

The magician's assistant lay in her divided box and looked at the sky above the stage with an expression that gave nothing away, that had learned — through practice, through repetition, through the discipline of someone who has climbed into the same box enough times to stop being afraid of it and has not yet decided what to feel instead — to simply wait for the halves to be rejoined, for the lid to come up, for the crowd to exhale and applaud the restoration of a wholeness that had never actually been interrupted.

Eleanor moved with the crowd. The hour had begun.
