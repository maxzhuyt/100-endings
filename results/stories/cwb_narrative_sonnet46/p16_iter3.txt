# Hell is Other Demons

The quarterly suffering reports were late again.

Lucifer initialed the third page of the Cocytus maintenance request without reading it, which was how he initialed most things, and reached for the stack of backlogged torment appeals that had been threatening to slide off the left side of his desk since February. February in Hell meant little except that February in Hell was the same as every other month in Hell, which was the point of Hell, which he had explained to the appeals committee no fewer than four hundred times and would explain again.

The office was quiet in the way that only very large organizations could be quiet—not absence of sound but the accumulated drone of ten thousand small processes running simultaneously at a distance. Somewhere below, the grinding continued. It always continued. He'd stopped hearing it sometime around the invention of the printing press and now it registered as a kind of inside silence, which was the worst kind.

He moved the Cocytus request to the signed pile.

He moved it back to the unsigned pile.

He signed it.

It was a Tuesday. It was, in the bureaucratically meaningful sense, always a Tuesday.

---

Grix, his third assistant of the century, appeared in the doorway with the expression of someone who has encountered a smell they don't have adequate vocabulary for.

"There's—" Grix said.

"I know," said Lucifer.

He hadn't, until that moment. But the sulfur had changed quality, the way a room's pressure changes before weather, and he'd been in enough rooms before enough weather to know.

She was already past Grix before Grix finished not finishing the announcement, which was, he reflected, very much like her. Her coat was the color of something that had been red once. Her hair was the same as it had always been, which was to say it was different every time in the same fundamental way, the way the ocean is the same as it has always been while never being still.

The purse was over her left shoulder. Scorched leather, brass clasp, the size of something that shouldn't hold as much as it held.

"You look terrible," she said, by way of greeting.

"You haven't seen me in forty years."

"And you look terrible."

She crossed to his desk, picked up the Cocytus maintenance request, read the first line with the air of a woman awaiting a verdict she'd already rendered, and set it down on the wrong pile.

He moved it back.

"I was passing through," she said.

"You were *passing through.*"

"Don't do that thing with your voice."

"I'm not doing anything with my voice."

"You're doing the thing."

He was doing the thing. He stopped doing it. "What brings you to this particular neighborhood, then, given that you were passing through it."

"I had business across the Phlegethon."

"You don't have business across the Phlegethon."

"I had something *like* business." She had moved to the window—there was no window, strictly speaking, but what served the function of one, an aperture of smoked amber through which the lower tiers were occasionally visible, churning as churning does—and was looking out at whatever was visible, which was the churning. "I wanted to see how you were managing."

"You could have sent Grix a message."

"And miss your face doing what your face is currently doing? No."

He became aware of his face and adjusted it. "I'm managing."

"Yes," she said, still looking out. "I can see that."

---

There was a silence, which he occupied by reviewing the first page of the torment appeals and she occupied by unclasping the purse.

He watched without watching, in the way that long practice produces, as she moved through its contents with the focused inefficiency of someone who knows exactly where everything is and chooses not to go directly to it. There was the glossy edge of something laminated. A length of what appeared to be string. A small cylinder of something he couldn't immediately categorize. She produced a tube of lip balm, applied it with the meditative self-sufficiency of someone who had stopped caring whether she was watched sometime before recorded history, and returned it to the purse.

Then she kept looking.

He caught a glimpse of something pale and rectangular—the corner of paper, soft, frayed at the edges. A ticket stub, maybe, or a photograph, no, the weight was wrong for a photograph, more like—

Like a drawing, he thought. Made in crayon. In orange and yellow, the colors a child reaches for when drawing fire.

She found what she was looking for, which appeared to be a small metal lighter that she had no use for in Hell, and put it on his desk with a deliberate absence of explanation.

"So you're managing," she said.

"We've established that."

"The levels?"

"Running."

"The—"

"All running," he said. "Everything is running. It is Hell. It has yet to stop."

"I heard about the Lethe overflow."

"That was handled."

"Four square miles of induced amnesia—"

"That was *handled,*" he said, with precision, "and the quarterly report will reflect that."

She sat, finally, in the chair across from his desk that he had not offered and that she would not have waited to be offered, and looked at him in the way that she had always looked at him, which was directly and from a significant ontological elevation even when she was seated and he was standing.

"You've been reorganizing," she said.

"The lower tiers needed—"

"The furniture."

A pause. "It needed reorganizing."

"You always reorganize when you're—"

"The filing system was a catastrophe."

"—restless," she finished.

"The filing system," he said carefully, "was a catastrophe. That is a structural observation about the filing system. It is not a confession."

She did not smile. She did something near a smile's latitude without committing to it. He had, over the long centuries, developed a detailed internal taxonomy of Lilith's near-smiles, but he kept it to himself as a general policy.

"It looked fine from outside," she said.

"The filing system?"

"Hell."

"Hell always looks fine from outside," he said. "That's part of its appeal."

---

She crossed her legs, and adjusted the purse on her knee, and said: "I've been thinking about Eden."

And there it was.

"Of course you have," he said.

"Don't do that."

"I'm not doing anything."

"You're dismissing it before I've said anything."

"You've said 'I've been thinking about Eden,' which you've said approximately—" he considered "—between ten and twelve thousand times, at similar intervals, and it precedes the same conversation it has always preceded, so I am not so much dismissing as I am *previewing,*" he said, "which is more respectful of your time than the alternative."

"My time," she said, "is not a concern."

"No. Nor mine." He put down the appeals file. "Say it, then."

She uncrossed her legs. Crossed them the other way. "You could have stayed."

"I left."

"Before that," she said. "Before the argument, before everything, you could have—"

"We've had this one."

"—just *talked* to me."

"We talked constantly."

"You talked at me. You made pronouncements. There's a difference." She was looking at the desk now, not at him. "You never asked me what I wanted."

"And you left," he said, "before I could."

"Because you *didn't.*"

"Because you didn't give me the—"

"Because you *didn't ask,*" she said, and her voice had dropped out of its usual register into something older and less performative, and he recognized the terrain the way you recognize a road you've driven dark. "That was always the thing. You were so certain you knew."

"I *did* know."

"You knew what you'd decided. That's different." She looked up. "And then you made all of this." The gesture took in the office, the tiers below, the entire infernal apparatus. "And kept at it long past when it would have made you feel anything."

"It runs itself now, largely."

"That," she said, "is extremely not the reassurance you think it is."

He picked up the appeals file. Put it down. "You left first."

"You created the conditions."

"I gave you a *choice.*"

"You gave me a verdict with the grammar of a choice." She touched the lighter on his desk, turned it once with two fingers, and did not pick it up. "You were very good at that."

"And you were very good," he said, "at leaving."

"Yes," she said, without inflection. "I was."

---

The emergency arrived in the form of Grix at the door again, having achieved the full expression of someone for whom emergency is an ordinary condition.

"The Sixth Tier is—"

"Tell Beelzebub."

"Beelzebub is *part* of the—"

"Tell whoever Beelzebub's deputy is."

"Namriel is in the Phlegethon conference."

"Call Namriel out of the conference."

"The conference is the thing—"

"I," said Lucifer, with the full weight of office, "will sign off on whatever protocol Namriel recommends when Namriel has had time to recommend it. Do you understand? Grix. Do you understand."

Grix understood and disappeared.

In the resulting silence, which was the particular silence of a room from which a commotion has just departed and not yet been replaced, she said: "You used to handle those yourself."

"I used to do a great number of things myself."

"You liked it."

"I managed it adequately."

"You *liked* it," she said, gentler than he'd expected, which was a thing she occasionally deployed with precision. "You liked being in the middle of things. You liked having a reason to be in the room."

He did not answer, which was its own answer.

"There are people in my sector," she said, "who've been around as long as we have, and they've all—" she paused. "They've all found things. Small things. That they follow. It doesn't have to be—"

"Lilith."

"—it doesn't have to be—"

"Don't," he said, and it came out stripped of its usual delivery, the bureaucratic irony and the cosmic authority, just the word, just the register of someone saying a word they mean. "Please don't do the thing where you tell me what I should want in order to be less of whatever it is you've decided I am."

A pause.

"I wasn't going to say that," she said.

"You were moving toward it."

"I was moving toward—" She stopped. Looked at the lighter. "I was going to say that you're allowed to still be in orbit around something. It doesn't have to be—it doesn't have to have a *name.*"

The silence was the kind that either ends very quickly or does not end.

It ended.

"What's in the purse," he said, not because he expected an answer, but because the alternative was continuing in the direction they'd been going.

"Everything," she said.

"Lilith."

"Debts. Notes. Things I meant to return." She didn't open it. "You know how it accumulates."

"I don't, actually. I threw everything out."

"Yes," she said. "I know."

---

She left at the next natural pause, which she created by standing and locating her coat and producing from the purse what appeared to be a parking validation she didn't need, examining it, and putting it back in a way that suggested she knew it was meaningless and kept it anyway.

"Grix has my contact details," she said, "if the Sixth Tier situation escalates."

"I'm aware of your contact details."

"I wasn't sure whether you'd—" She stopped. "I wasn't sure."

"I have everything," he said. "I didn't throw *everything* out."

She absorbed this without reacting to it, in the way of someone who has trained herself not to react and occasionally succeeds.

"The filing system genuinely needed reorganizing," he said.

"I believe you," she said.

She moved toward the door. At the threshold, she turned.

"It does look like you," she said. "This place. It looks like what you'd make without meaning to, without noticing. That's not—" She adjusted the purse. "That's not an insult."

"It sounded like one."

"Most things do, with me," she said. "That's not the same thing."

The dimensional rift, which served the function of a door and operated with approximately the reliability of one, closed behind her in the ordinary way.

---

The torment appeals were still there. The Cocytus maintenance request was still in the wrong pile—she had moved it again before she left and he hadn't noticed until now.

He moved it back.

The lighter was still on his desk, small and silver and of the mortal world, the kind of object you found in a coat pocket and couldn't account for. He had never smoked. She had never smoked. It had presumably meant something to someone else first.

He initialed the fourth page of the Cocytus maintenance request.

He picked up the lighter.

He held it for a moment, not long, then set it at the edge of the desk, just inside rather than outside the field of his immediate vision.

The grinding continued.

He turned to page five.
