# Hell is Other Demons

The damnation queue had backed up again.

Lucifer reviewed the morning's intake forms with the particular expression of a man who has asked, repeatedly, for the filing system to be updated and has been told, repeatedly, that an update is coming. Three thousand souls condemned for pride, which was standard, manageable, unremarkable. Forty-seven for sloth, which created a processing problem because the sloths never filled out their own intake paperwork and someone had to do it for them. One soul condemned for the specific and novel sin of having invented a social media feature that LinkedIn would later adopt as its own, which was frankly excessive in Lucifer's opinion but the judgment system operated above his pay grade and he had learned, over the long millennia, to stay in his lane.

Outside his office window, the ash fell. It fell the way it always fell—steadily, without drama, the color of old bureaucratic paper. It did not accumulate. It simply fell, and then was falling, and then fell some more.

His assistant Malphas knocked twice before entering, which Lucifer had asked him to do and which Malphas had initially done ironically and now did out of habit, a reformation so small it had gone unremarked.

"The Ninth Circle managers want to schedule a comprehensive review of their overtime policies." Malphas consulted his clipboard, which was made of bone, which Malphas felt was dashing but which Lucifer privately found ostentatious. "Also, Beelzebub has filed another formal complaint about the breakroom situation."

"The breakroom situation has not changed."

"The breakroom situation has not changed."

"Then the complaint is also unchanged, and he should file it in the same place as the previous thirty-one complaints."

Malphas made a note. "And the three o'clock reservation at—"

"Cancel it."

"Yes, my lord."

"And Malphas."

"My lord."

"The form for Seventh Circle variance requests has the wrong signature block again. I need you to have it corrected, printed, and redistributed before the end of the week."

"It will be done."

"That's what you said about the filing system."

Malphas left before this observation required a response, which was, Lucifer thought, really coming along nicely in terms of professional development.

He turned back to the queue. Pride. Sloth. Innovation. The ash fell outside. He poured a coffee from the carafe on his desk—coffee being one of the things Hell got excellently right, perhaps because so much of it was consumed there—and opened the next folder.

On the shelf behind him, facing his desk so he could see them when he looked up, were two wine glasses. One of them had been there long enough that the dust on it had developed its own character, grown considered. The other was clean. He used it most evenings.

He did not think about this.

---

Lilith arrived at a quarter past two without an appointment, which was not remarkable because Lilith had never made an appointment in her life and was not about to pioneer the practice on his behalf.

"There's a matter," she said, settling into the chair across his desk with the familiar economy of motion that was either grace or a very sophisticated contempt for effort, "with the eastern territories."

Lucifer set down his pen. "Which matter."

"The Asmodeus restructuring. He's been encroaching on the third ring of the fifth circle, which falls under my jurisdiction by the treaty of—" she waved her hand, "—whichever one. The relevant one."

"I'm aware of which one."

"Then you're aware that this constitutes a violation."

"Asmodeus has been restructuring for eleven months. You've been aware of this for eleven months."

"I've been monitoring the situation."

"You've been in Mesopotamia."

"I have a presence in Mesopotamia." She paused. "It's a significant presence."

The ash fell outside. One of his assistants was crossing the courtyard below, head down, robes grey with it. Everyone was always grey with it. You stopped noticing.

"I'll have a letter drafted," Lucifer said. "Asmodeus will receive a formal notice of the jurisdictional matter and be asked to provide a response within—"

"I don't need a letter drafted."

"No?"

"I need the matter addressed."

"A letter," he said, "addresses the matter."

"A letter," she said, "is what you write when you want to feel like you've done something and then put it in a folder and tell your assistant the folder exists."

He looked at her. She looked at him. Then she looked out the window at the ash, and it occurred to him—not for the first time, but in the particular way that recurring thoughts arrive slightly differently each time, carrying new evidence—that she had not come here for Asmodeus.

Neither of them said this.

"It's a reasonable preliminary step," he said.

"It's a reasonable preliminary step," she agreed, which was not agreement.

She looked around the office then, with the slow attention of someone cataloguing changes since their last visit. He watched her notice the things she noticed. He did not watch her notice the wine glasses.

"You've changed the artwork," she said.

"I haven't changed the artwork."

"The Bosch used to be on the other wall."

"The Bosch has always been on that wall."

She looked at it. "It used to be higher."

It was, possibly, slightly higher.

---

"The Sixth Covenant," she said, twenty minutes later, without preamble, as though she were continuing a conversation rather than exhuming one. "You've always been very free with your interpretation of the Sixth Covenant."

"The Sixth Covenant," he said, "is a document we negotiated in bad faith six thousand years ago and have both been misquoting defensively ever since."

"I don't misquote. I recontextualize."

"You once recontextualized an entire ocean."

"The Caspian Sea situation was—"

"A unilateral recontextualization."

"—complicated." She sat forward slightly. "You knew it was complicated. You knew it was complicated and you let me take the position alone by not saying, publicly, that you also knew it was complicated."

"I thought you wanted independence of operation in the northern territories."

"I wanted backup, Lucifer."

The word landed and sat between them. It was a small word for the size of what it was carrying.

"You didn't ask," he said, finally.

"You knew."

"I thought I—yes." He picked up his pen and put it down. "Yes. I knew."

Outside, the ash. Below, dimly, the eternal machinery of consequence, processing and processing, endless and indifferent as weather.

"The Arrangement of 4000 BCE," she said, moving on with perfect efficiency. "Your interpretation of clause seven was creative."

"Clause seven was ambiguous."

"Clause seven was ambiguous and you wrote it, which I have always found suspicious."

"We both wrote it."

"You held the pen."

"You dictated the terms."

"I dictated the *spirit*. You captured the letter. The letter was suspiciously clause seven-shaped." She leaned back. "And then there was the matter of the solstice gathering, which—"

"That was a misunderstanding."

"—which you knew about and attended anyway, which I only know because Belphegor mentioned it six hundred years after the fact in a context that I suspect was intended to be destabilizing—"

"It was intended to be destabilizing."

"—and which I have never raised with you directly in six hundred years—"

"You're raising it now."

"—because," she continued, "I thought the time might come when it was relevant and it seems to be relevant." She pulled a piece of ash from her sleeve, regarded it, set it on the desk. "Also you didn't extinguish the eternal flame correctly when you left. I came home to an unextinguished eternal flame."

He stared at her.

"It's a fire hazard," she said.

"We live in Hell."

"That," she said, with great precision, "is not the point."

---

He almost said something then.

She was looking at him with an expression that was not quite the expression she usually wore, the one she used for diplomatic engagements and territorial disputes and long conversations with people she was outlasting. This was underneath that expression—something provisional, something that had been waiting with the particular patience of things that have a great deal of time and are not certain they will use it.

Her hand was on the desk.

He was aware of where his hand was.

The ash fell.

The door opened.

"My lord." Malphas, visible in the gap, clipboard raised, apologetic in the way that suggested he was not actually very apologetic. "I'm sorry to interrupt, but the Ninth Circle managers have declined to reschedule and insist they need a response to the overtime matter before—"

"I'll be with them in five minutes."

"They're suggesting the response needs to be in the form of a meeting."

"Tell them the meeting will be scheduled."

"They're suggesting the meeting needs to be today."

Lucifer looked at Malphas. Malphas looked at his clipboard. The clipboard, being made of bone, offered no counsel.

"Tell them," Lucifer said, "I'll be there in ten minutes."

"Yes, my lord." The door closed.

When he turned back, Lilith had arranged her expression carefully into something that was not what it had been. She had done this quickly and she had done it well, and if he had not known the face as well as he knew his own, he might not have caught the arrangement happening.

"I should let you get to that," she said.

"Lilith—"

"The Asmodeus matter." She gathered herself to stand with the kind of practiced naturalness that is only practiced under observation. "If you could have the letter drafted. Whatever process you think is appropriate."

"Yes. The letter. I'll have it drafted."

"Good." She was standing now, smoothing something that didn't need smoothing. "That's good."

He walked her to the door because they were civilized, or because they were something, or because it was a long walk and he was aware that it was a long walk and that it would end.

"The eastern territories," she said, at the door. "I'll be watching."

"Is that a threat, or—"

"It's information," she said. "What you do with it is your business."

She left the way she had arrived—without announcement, without lingering, without giving him anything that could be used as evidence of anything.

He stood in the doorway until she rounded the corner and was not visible.

Above, below, around: the machinery. The ash. The sound of Hell being Hell, which was not a dramatic sound, which was the sound of office ventilation and distant filing and something, somewhere, that was probably not crying.

---

The Ninth Circle overtime meeting lasted forty minutes and resolved nothing, which Lucifer had anticipated, and he returned to his desk at half past five and sat in the chair that had acquired his shape over the long tenure of his occupancy and looked at the damnation queue.

Pride. Sloth. Innovation.

He poured one glass of wine from the bottle in the cabinet, which was a very good bottle because he was the Devil and if there was one advantage to that it was wine. He did not pour a second glass. There was no particular thought attached to this, only motion, or the end of motion, or the habit of not reaching for something twice.

The Bosch looked down at him from the wall. The ash fell outside.

He reviewed the queue for another hour, or two hours, or some amount of time that was indistinguishable from the time before it, and then he set the papers down, and then he closed his eyes, and then he was asleep in the way that powerful things sleep—badly, lightly, in the chair rather than anywhere more deliberate.

The ash fell on the rooftops and the courtyards and the shoulders of the damned making their administrative way through Hell's various departments and on the window of the office where Lucifer slept, and it did not accumulate into anything, and it did not stop, and in the morning someone would make him a coffee and slide a revised schedule under the door and somewhere across the territories Lilith would be doing whatever she was doing, and at some point someone would ask about the reservation and he would say to cancel it and then, later, he would say something different, and the ash would be falling then too.

It was falling now.

It was very sad, if you looked at it. It was only the weather, if you didn't.
