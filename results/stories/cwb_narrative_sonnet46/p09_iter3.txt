# Darkroom Revelations

I have a reputation to maintain.

Ask anyone in the experimental photography program and they'll tell you: Dani Reyes is the one who makes things happen. She books the studio time, brings the coffee, introduces herself to the person she wants and tells them exactly why. My last two relationships started the same way—me, walking across a room with full awareness of what I was doing and doing it anyway. Both ended on my terms. My name stayed clean. I am, according to the general consensus I have done nothing to discourage, a person who acts.

I cannot explain, then, with any dignity at all, how I spent the better part of a semester watching Elliot Torres from across the studio without saying a single direct thing to him.

It started with his boots—I know how that sounds, but stay with me. Thick-soled, silver-buckled, paired with fishnet tights under a cardigan the color of old lace. Not a costume. Just Tuesday. He moved through critiques the way genuinely attentive people do when they're also constitutionally quiet: standing at the margins, eyes moving over the prints, offering nothing until he offered the one thing that rearranged everyone's thinking. He would turn nineteen in the spring. I knew this because someone mentioned it in passing. I remembered it the way you remember things you're not tracking.

The final project pairing was random. I have chosen to believe this.

---

The darkroom on the third floor smells like developer fluid and old rubber and something chemical-sweet underneath both of those—a smell I've learned to associate, over two years in this program, with the specific quality of patience that darkroom work requires. The red safelight does strange things to faces. It flattens them. Removes the readable layer, the social layer, the part of expression that communicates *I am presenting myself to you now*. Under red light, faces become harder to perform. Forty minutes in, with Elliot's prints cycling through the development trays and my contact sheets still in the fixer, neither of us had said much, and the silence had that particular darkroom texture—pressurized, but not unpleasantly. Outside, things happened in real time. In here, you waited. The image decided when it was ready.

Elliot was humming.

Soft, tuneless—or not quite tuneless; there was a shape to it he kept not quite finishing. He stood over the developer tray with his arms loose at his sides, watching the paper, and the sound seemed less like a choice than like a condition of his being there, the way a room hums when the radiator is on. He seemed entirely unaware of it. That was the part I kept returning to, over weeks of noticing it: it wasn't for anyone. There was no performance in it. He was alone in it even when he wasn't alone, and for reasons I hadn't had the patience to examine, I had started to anticipate the sound before I heard it, the way you anticipate the number of stairs in a staircase you've climbed before.

I became aware that I was standing in the dark listening to someone hum and experiencing this as the best part of my week.

"Your contact sheets are in the fixer," I said, because I needed to say something.

"I know." He didn't look up. "Thank you."

---

The print in the developer tray was beginning to announce itself—coming forward from the white paper the way all darkroom images do, like a figure walking toward you through fog. A silhouette, backlit, standing in what resolved gradually into shallow water. The reflection broken by the figure's legs. Elliot had spent three consecutive weekends on this series. I knew because we had been here for all of them. I had not mentioned this.

"The water one," I said.

"Yeah." He tilted the tray gently, rocking the chemicals across the surface of the paper. "I keep coming back to it." A pause. The hum started again, briefly, and then stopped. "I keep feeling like I don't know who I'm photographing."

I waited. This was new.

"I know it's me," he said. He was still watching the image. "The subject is me. But the me in the water doesn't look like—" He touched the edge of the tray without touching the print. "I've been thinking a lot about who looks back from photographs. Whether it's ever the same person who took them."

The red light had made his profile into something medieval—flattened and serious, a face on an old coin. I was very aware of my own hands, of where I had put them and what I was doing with them.

"What does the one in the water look like?" I asked.

He glanced at me. Quick, assessing. Then, apparently satisfied by something in my face, he looked back at the print and said: "Honestly? Like someone I recognize more than the way I usually look. Which is either meaningful or just—" he made a small gesture toward the tray, toward the water, toward the whole apparatus of the question, "—wet."

I laughed. He laughed too, low and a little surprised, and the sound of it changed the air in the room.

---

"I've been trying to figure out what to call it," he said. The image was almost fully present now—the backlit figure emerging from the paper with its ambiguity intact, its edges neither committed nor evasive. I had looked at this photograph in its contact sheet form and thought: *bold compositional choice.* I understood now that I had been reading a question as a statement. "I don't know if I'm working something out about gender, or working something out about how I've been seeing myself, or if those are even different things." He was quiet for a moment. "Sorry. That's a lot to say to someone you're basically doing a project with."

"Don't apologize." The words came out sharp, from somewhere more certain than I'd been.

He looked at me. This time he didn't look away.

I want to be precise about what I understood in that moment, and what I didn't. I understood that something was being disclosed—not announced, not declared, but offered carefully, the way you lower something fragile. I understood that I was being trusted with the approach to a thing Elliot was still circling. What I did not understand, yet, was the particular shape of what was opening up inside me as he spoke. I had categories. I had always had categories for what I wanted. I was beginning to have the unsettling apprehension that the categories were not going to survive the evening.

I made a decision. Not the decision to act—that wasn't mine to make, not here. The decision to be slow. To come toward what he was saying rather than away from it or through it.

"How long have you been thinking about it?" I asked.

"Since the first shoot." He turned back to the developing tray. The figure in the water looked back at both of us. "Maybe longer. But the camera does something. Makes me look." He lifted the print carefully with the tongs and moved it to the stop bath. His hands were steady. Mine were doing something I chose not to examine. "My sister keeps saying it's because I've been in art school and art school makes people weird about themselves, which is probably also true."

"Does she know you're—"

"Figuring it out?" He set the tongs down. "No. I'm not good at—" He gestured, somewhat helplessly, at the entire room, the red light, the chemical smell, the conversation. "This. Usually."

---

What I know about myself, or what I thought I knew: I am attracted to presence. To people who know what they are and move through the world accordingly. I had built an entire theory of my own desire on top of this premise—the kind of person I went toward, why, what it said about me that I went there. The theory was neat and it had held up through a significant amount of evidence.

I was standing in a red-lit room with someone who was, by his own account and in his own words, in the middle of not knowing, and my whole body was oriented toward him like a compass needle that has just found the thing compasses are for.

I wanted to take charge of this. That is my natural mode—take charge, name the thing, move efficiently toward what I want. I opened my mouth to do exactly that.

What came out was: "I don't know what I'm attracted to either. I thought I had a very complete account of it. And then I spent a semester noticing your boots."

The silence that followed had a different density than the silences before it.

Elliot had gone very still.

"The buckled ones," he said, finally.

"And the cardigan."

"It was my grandmother's."

"I know. You said in your artist statement."

He turned to look at me fully. "You read my artist statement."

"I read everyone's."

"You remembered mine specifically."

There was a long moment during which I became acquainted with the particular sensation of having entirely lost control of something while simultaneously suspecting that losing control of it was the point. Elliot was looking at me with an expression I couldn't fully decode under the red light—or maybe the red light was not the reason I couldn't decode it. Maybe it was that I had spent a semester cataloging the details of this person from a safe observational distance and had not prepared any material for what came after the distance closed.

I looked at the developing tray. The figure in the water looked back. Backlit, ambiguous, completely itself.

"I think your photograph knows who it is," I said.

---

Elliot was quiet for a moment. Then he looked at the print—really looked at it, the way he looked at prints in critique when he was preparing the one thing he would say. His hands in the red light were careful and unhurried.

"Maybe," he said. He lifted the print with the tongs and transferred it to the stop bath with the even movements of someone who has done this many times and still gives it full attention. "Or maybe it just doesn't mind not knowing yet."

I thought about that. It seemed important in a way I wasn't finished with.

"Can I see your contact sheets?" he asked. "When they're out of the fixer."

"Yeah," I said. "Of course."

He nodded. Looked back at the tray where another sheet of paper was waiting to begin. And then, very softly, he started to hum again—the same shape as before, but this time he took it further, let it resolve a little, not all the way but further than he had before. He rocked the developer tray. The paper waited. Outside, the semester was ending, and there were people with deadlines and futures that were already decided, and none of that was in here.

I reached for the next sheet of paper.

We started another print.
