# The Lighthouse Keeper

---

*14th October*

Mum,

Got here fine. The ferry was late but that's nothing new and I wasn't in any rush given what was waiting at the end of it. You'd like the boat actually — old thing, smells of diesel and fish and the man who runs it doesn't say much which suits me. His name's Connell and he comes Tuesdays and Fridays unless the weather makes him feel otherwise, in which case it's whenever God sees fit, apparently.

The lighthouse is good. Better than I thought. Three floors, which sounds grand but the floors are about the size of our kitchen so don't go imagining. The mechanism's something though — I spent the first afternoon just watching the rotation, the way the whole thing moves on a mercury float, smooth as you like, barely a sound. They briefed me on it in Oban but seeing it work is different. It's satisfying. That's the word. You wind it and it goes and it does what it's supposed to do and the ships don't hit the rocks. Simple enough job when you put it like that.

The pay's come through to the account already, I checked before I lost signal.

How's Biscuit? Is he still getting up on the sofa when nobody's looking? Don't let him. It was his back that was the problem last time and he's no lighter.

Did McNeil finally fix the fence or is he still talking about fixing it? I give it till spring.

Tell Moira congratulations again from me properly. Assistant manager is nothing to sneeze at, I don't care what she says about the company.

I'm fine here. Genuinely. Tell nobody I said this but the quiet is all right.

Al

---

*22nd October*

Mum,

Right, what is this.

I'm looking at the parcel Connell brought over — he handed it to me with a face like he'd been asked to carry a live animal — and I've opened it and I'm sitting here genuinely asking myself what I'm supposed to do with this.

A barometer.

Mum.

It's broken.

I can see it's broken, there's a crack across the face and the needle's stuck at *Stormy* which, fine, prophetic maybe, but not what you'd call useful. I read the note, yes. I know it was Grandda's. I know he carried it on the fishing boats. I'm not disputing any of that. What I'm questioning is the logic of sending broken navigational equipment to a lighthouse, as a gift, to a man whose entire job is to be aware of weather conditions. You've sent me broken weather.

I've put it on the shelf above the lamp table where I can see it from the chair.

The shortbread was good. Tell me when you want the tin back, I'll send it on the ferry.

Apart from the barometer, which I've already mentioned, things are fine here. Found where the weird noise was coming from — loose bracket on the external ladder, nothing to do with the mechanism, I've tightened it. Connell says November and December can be rough crossings so stock up on whatever you're planning to send because there might be a gap. I'm well-supplied. Don't worry.

Biscuit still on the sofa?

Al

---

*3rd November*

Mum,

Sorry this one's late. Time does a strange thing out here — not in any mystical way, nothing like that, just the days are shorter and they blur a bit. I lost track of what day Connell was coming and missed him, had to wait for the Friday run.

The lamp mechanism started making a sound it wasn't making before, a kind of low harmonic when the rotation hits a certain point. I've checked everything I can check and it's not in the float and it's not in the drive, I think it might be a resonance in the lens housing. I've logged it and I'll have the engineer look when he comes in December. In the meantime it's doing what it's supposed to do so no emergency.

There's a gannet that keeps landing on the lantern gallery railing. Same bird, or at least I assume same bird — same notch out of the beak. Stupid animal, it just sits there looking at me through the glass. I've started calling it Rab because it looks like somebody who's made a series of poor decisions. Rab Gannet. I'm telling you this so you know the isolation hasn't got to me. I've named the bird ironically. That's a good sign.

The barometer's still on the shelf. Still says Stormy. I've started finding it funny actually.

Al

P.S. I found what's still packed in the tin.

That's not shortbread, Mum, that's a fruit cake. You know I don't eat fruit cake. Who told you to put it in a shortbread tin? Actually I know who told you. Tell Moira I said no.

P.P.S. I'm eating the fruit cake.

---

*19th November*

Mum,

I'll tell you what happened last Tuesday. I'm not making anything of it, I just want to tell you because there's nobody here to tell and it feels like the kind of thing that needs telling or it'll just rattle around.

The light in the afternoon at this time of year comes in low and sideways and it hits the lens housing and throws this pattern on the floor, all these overlapping arcs. I've seen it every day. It's not new. But last Tuesday it was raining as well, a particular kind of rain, fine and even and quiet, and the sound of it and the light doing what it was doing — I don't know. I sat down on the floor. Not because anything was wrong. Nothing was wrong. I just sat down.

I sat there for probably twenty minutes and I wasn't thinking about anything specific. I was just there. And then I wasn't, and I got up and made tea and wound the mechanism and that was that.

I don't know what I want you to do with that. Nothing, probably. I just wanted to say it to someone who knows me.

Could you send more tea? The stuff they stocked here is like drinking cardboard. PG Tips, the big box if you can get it. And if you're sending a parcel anyway, the brown jumper that's in the wardrobe in the spare room — not the grey one, the brown one. Top shelf. I think the grey one might actually be Dad's.

The gannet's back. Rab. He seems unbothered by the rain.

Love,
Al

---

*December — I've lost the specific date*

Mum —

Haven't had a letter in three weeks. I know that's not anything, Connell said the crossings have been rough, I believe him, I've seen the crossing, I just —

It's fine. It's fine, I know that.

The mechanism engineer was supposed to come on the fourth. He hasn't come. I called through on the radio and they said weather delay, he'll come in the new year. In the meantime the harmonic is louder and I've done everything I can do which is not much.

The heating element in the oven went last week. I've been managing on the ring burners.

I can't tell if it's bad out there this year or if this is just what it's like and I had no reference point before. The waves at high tide are coming up the rock further than they were. Not a danger, the lighthouse is built for this, I know it's built for this. But the sound of it at night is — it's a sound. It's a large sound.

I'm tired and I think maybe behind on sleep though I keep the watches so it's not that I'm not sleeping, it's more like the sleep isn't —

Rab hasn't been here in a while. Presumably has more sense than to be out in this.

The barometer on the shelf is cracked down the face and the needle says Stormy and that's the only one in the building working and it's not working and I don't know why I'm writing that down.

I keep it where I can see it from the chair.

Al

---

*23rd December*

My Al,

Your letter came yesterday. Connell rang me — yes, he rang me, he has my number from when I called to ask about the service and I'm glad now I did — to tell me it would be on the Friday run so I was ready and I'm writing this tonight so it goes back with him tomorrow.

I'm going to say one thing first which is: I know you're all right. I know that because I know you, and because the letter you sent is still you — it's still your voice, still you noticing things, still you writing things down because they need saying. That's not a man who isn't all right. That's a man who's cold and tired and hasn't had post in three weeks and has a broken oven, which any sensible person would find difficult.

But I want to tell you something now.

You were five or six — your dad had been away working, the Grangemouth job, the long one, nearly four months. I was in that house with you children and the money was tight and the house had a smell at that time that I've never been able to name, slightly damp, slightly not quite warm enough, and one afternoon in November — it's always November, I don't know what November has against people — one afternoon I'd got you settled and Moira was at school and I sat down in the kitchen, right down on the floor with my back against the cupboard door, and I didn't move for a long time. I wasn't crying. Nothing was wrong that hadn't been wrong before. I just sat there until it was time to get up again.

I've never told you that before. I don't know why. Maybe because you don't tell people that, or you don't think you do.

You did tell me.

Now. I've already spoken to Connell about the January run and I've asked can he time it to bring me over on a Tuesday and bring me back on a Friday, so three days. He says he can if the weather gives him any room.

Don't argue with me. I've had the conversation with myself on your behalf and your side lost.

The oven element — find the make and model plate on the back of the appliance and read me the numbers in your next letter and I'll look into whether the part can be sent ahead of me so it's there when I arrive. I'd like to cook something in that kitchen. A person should be able to cook something in a kitchen.

The barometer was your grandfather's and he carried it on three different boats and none of them sank, so the needle being where it is is neither here nor there. Keep it where you can see it.

I love you. Sleep when you can.

Mum

---

*11th January*

Mum,

You don't need to read this one carefully, nothing's wrong, everything's fine.

Connell got you over in one piece which I wasn't confident about but there you were standing on the jetty with two bags and a look on your face like you were personally affronted by the size of the waves, and by the time you left on Friday the oven was fixed and there were six meals in the freezer and you'd somehow established a rapport with Rab who appeared on the Thursday morning and in thirty years being your son I've never seen anything take to you that fast.

Do not tell me you fed him. I know you fed him.

The harmonic in the lens housing turned out to be a screw loose in the casing bracket — I'd checked the bracket twice and it wasn't the bracket, it was the screw in the bracket, which is so stupid I can't even be annoyed about it. Gone now. The mechanism runs quiet.

I've been sleeping better. The nights are still long but February will be better and after February March, and the light in March at this latitude is supposed to be something. I'll send you a letter when I see it and tell you what it's like.

The barometer's still on the shelf above the lamp table. The crack's the same and the needle hasn't moved. I can see it from the chair where I sit in the evenings, just past the lamp, and it still says what it says. I don't know. I've stopped minding that.

How's Biscuit? How's the fence?

Love,
Al
