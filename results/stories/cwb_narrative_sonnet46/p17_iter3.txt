# Court of the Dreaming: Territorial Dream Dispute

---

The bailiff's voice was the kind that had once announced the deaths of kings, and before that, the deaths of things older than kings, and before that, matters that did not have names in any living language. It carried. It was meant to carry.

"OYEZ, OYEZ. THE COURT OF THE DREAMING NOW CONVENES IN THE MATTER OF—"

He paused. He consulted the scroll. The scroll was long. The scroll had been long since before paper existed and made its current manifestation feel slightly ironic.

"—FURST LURVE, PLAINTIFF—"

In the gallery, something with the approximate shape of a hummingbird and the approximate size of a carriage began to laugh and suppressed it.

"—VERSUS MORRA—"

"Mora," said a voice from the defendant's table.

"—MORRA, DEFENDANT, IN THE MATTER OF ONE CONTESTED DREAMSCAPE, SPECIFICALLY THE RECURRING DREAM OF THE MINOR DESIGNATED—" the bailiff's voice achieved its most perfectly solemn register— "MAX."

He had gotten *Max* right. Say what you would about the bailiff.

The courtroom of the Dreaming was not a place that rewarded description, which was either a property of its nature or a defense mechanism it had developed over the millennia to discourage documentation. It was vast in the way that made vastness feel like a personal affront. It was old in the way that made age feel like a philosophical position. The stone—if it was stone—remembered things. That was the problem with stone in the Dreaming. It remembered everything and told none of it in any useful order.

Dream presided at the high bench.

He was not, technically speaking, doing anything. He was sitting. He had been sitting with the particular quality of stillness that belonged to very ancient things that could, if they chose, stop being still in ways that would require everyone present to substantially revise their understanding of the word *movement*. He had not yet chosen. He was giving the impression that he might never choose. His expression was the expression it always was, which was to say it was the expression of someone who had heard everything before and had formed, over several thousand years of hearing everything, a very precise opinion of what everything was worth.

The gallery was modest. Courts of the Dreaming did not typically attract spectators; the cases tended either toward the monumentally cosmic, which was exhausting, or the minutely personal, which was embarrassing, and today's fell into the latter category with considerable force. There were two personified anxieties seated in the back, attending unrelated matters that had been rescheduled three times and counting. There was a small cluster of minor oneiric functionaries who had come because they had heard there would be a ruling and rulings occasionally produced interpretable precedent and interpretable precedent occasionally meant they could stop doing their jobs in the particular way they'd been doing their jobs since the Bronze Age. And there was something in the corner that had too many eyes and appeared by its demeanor to not be entirely certain how it had gotten there, but was committed now.

They were all, in their various ways, waiting.

---

First Love came to the stand.

She was, at first glance, precisely what she was, which was to say she was roseate and determined and had the bearing of a master craftsperson summoned to defend her finest commission before a jury she suspected was not qualified to evaluate it. She carried herself like a grievance held at a dignified angle. She wore the expression of someone who had asked to speak with the manager of the cosmos and had, to their own slightly unsettled surprise, been granted the appointment.

She was also, and this the gallery noted with varying degrees of interest, genuinely upset. Not performed upset. *Upset* upset.

She arranged herself at the stand.

She arranged her notes.

She arranged her sense of professional dignity, which took the longest.

"I will begin," she said, with the tone of someone who has prepared remarks and intends to deliver them regardless, "with the dreamscape itself, because I believe it is essential to understanding the nature of the violation that the court appreciate the craftsmanship involved."

Dream said nothing.

First Love took this as permission, which it may or may not have been.

"The dreamscape in question was commissioned—in the organic sense, the psychic sense—by the subject's own developmental needs, as they always are. I designed the execution. The field component underwent eleven iterations before I was satisfied. *Eleven*. The first three were too pastoral—meadow, proper meadow, which reads as theatrical rather than accidental. A meadow announces itself. A *field* simply exists. There is a difference and it is not a small one." She paused to allow this to be appreciated. The gallery attempted appreciation. "The wildflowers were selected individually. Not a wildflower type. Individual flowers, placed with the studied carelessness of actual nature, because anything too arranged reads as wish fulfillment rather than memory-in-formation, and wish fulfillment produces the wrong kind of imprinting." She consulted her notes, though she did not need to. "The light was specified as late afternoon, specifically the quality of late afternoon in early autumn, the quality in which the shadows are long without being melancholy and the gold is warm without being saccharine. The distinction matters."

"The girl," Dream said. Just that.

First Love paused fractionally. "I style the figures, not name them. She is accessible but idealized. Brown hair. Something kind in the eyes. She smells of something the boy will spend twenty years trying to identify and never quite manage." A faint professional pride in this. "The scene builds toward an almost-kiss. The *approach*. The breath caught. The world contracting to the size of two faces and the space between them." First Love pressed her hands flat against the stand. "The *almost* is the entire architecture. Not the kiss. The *almost*. Because the kiss would resolve it, and what I am trying to produce is not resolution. What I am trying to produce is *longing*. Generative longing. The kind that opens rather than closes. The boy wakes from this dream at the correct moment, with the correct quality of—" she searched— "piercing sweetness. He does not know what he wants. He knows only that he wants. That is the *product*, your Lordship. That is what I made."

Silence in the court.

And then, into the silence, First Love's voice dropped from its professional register into something more unguarded. "It's good work," she said, quietly. "It's genuinely good work. I've been doing this for a very long time, and the Max dreamscape is good work."

Dream was quiet for a moment that seemed to have more interior space than moments usually contained.

"The defendant's modifications," he said.

First Love's composure underwent a rapid series of internal negotiations.

"The defendant," she said, with the precision of someone choosing words for structural rather than communicative purposes, "has introduced into the dreamscape, at the moment of the almost-kiss—in the moment of approach, when the figures have drawn close enough that the breath mingles—an alteration in the girl's behavior, whereby she—" First Love paused. She restarted. "The girl takes a position of—dominance—she *mounts*—she sits astride the boy's—"

"The court takes the testimony," Dream said.

"And then," First Love continued, because she had come this far, "the face changes. Not entirely. Enough. Enough to be wrong in a way that is also, and I find this—specifically this—to be the most egregious element, simultaneously *attractive*. The face becomes something that should not be wanted and *is wanted*. And the girl—the defendant, wearing the girl—bends to the boy's mouth and does not kiss him. She *feeds on his breath*. She takes the breath from him slowly. And the boy does not pull away. And the boy wakes up confused and exhausted and *hungry*, and the *hunger* is the wrong kind—it is not the hunger I built—it is not generative, it is not *opening*—it is—" she stopped. "He's eleven," she said. "He's *eleven years old*."

This last sentence she delivered not to the court but to some point slightly above the court, as though making an appeal to whatever administered things above and beyond courts.

"Your testimony reflects your position," Dream said. "You may be seated."

---

Mora did not so much take the stand as relocate to it. There was a distinction. Taking the stand implied effort, and Mora did not appear to be a creature for whom effort was a relevant category. She moved with the ease of something that had been entirely comfortable in its own existence for several hundred years and had not, in that span, encountered any compelling argument against continuing to be comfortable.

She was, the gallery noted with varying degrees of surprise, not particularly remarkable to look at. This was either the most natural thing about her or the most unnatural. She had the quality of something that fit in everywhere and nowhere, the way a stone fits in a river—present, smooth, entirely itself, entirely unsurprising until you tried to move it.

She faced the court. She did not adjust anything. She had brought nothing to adjust.

"You acknowledge the modifications to the dreamscape," Dream said. It was not phrased as a question, which was either because he already knew the answer or because he found certain performances of due process dispensable.

"I acknowledge I was there," Mora said. "If modifications occurred, I acknowledge they were mine."

"You find this formulation preferable."

"I find precision preferable," Mora said, pleasantly. "The dreamscape was not the thing I went to. The boy was the thing I went to. The dreamscape is incidental."

From the plaintiff's table, First Love made a sound that was extremely controlled.

Mora did not look at her. She addressed herself to the room in the manner of someone who had a defensible position and was content for it to stand on its own merits.

"I'm old," she said. "Not as a complaint. As context. I've been doing this—" she paused as though identifying the most accurate term— "since before most of the oneiric infrastructure currently in this courtroom was laid down. The practice is old. I am old. The relationship between a creature like me and the breath of sleeping humans is not"—she appeared to search very briefly for the right word—"*devised*. It simply is. I do not feed on harm. I feed on breath. The breath returns. The sleeper is tired and"—a mild concession—"somewhat disoriented, but the sleeper is *fine*. I've been doing this for a very long time, and I can say with confidence that the boy is fine."

"You entered a protected developmental dreamscape," First Love said from her table, not quite contain herself.

"I entered a field where a boy was sleeping," Mora said, with the patience of geological processes. "The wildflowers were a nice touch," she added, and it was entirely unclear, and would remain so, whether this was sincere.

"The modifications you introduced—"

"The things that happen when I feed are the things that happen when I feed," Mora said. "I don't modify myself for convenience. I can't be asked to not be what I am in order to eat my dinner in a space that someone else has decided serves a different developmental function." She paused. "With respect."

"The boy is experiencing confusion," First Love said. "Not the right confusion. Wrong confusion. *Complicated* confusion—"

"Good," Mora said.

"—he's *eleven*—"

"I didn't pick his age," Mora said, which was accurate and therefore, in its accuracy, enraging. "A girl's gotta eat, you know? I'm not harming him. I'm leaving him confused in a way that might—and I'll grant this, I'll give you this—not perfectly align with your intentions. But intentions aren't the only thing operating here. I have a nature. My nature has requirements. The boy breathes and I eat and in the morning he's confused and hungry in a way he can't name." She seemed, if anything, mild about this. "Character-building, honestly."

One of the personified anxieties in the back made a sympathetic sound. The other one looked at it.

"I would like," Mora said, to Dream directly, for the first time, "to be clear that I am not arguing for unlimited access. I understand territorial claims. I understand the logic of protected spaces. I am arguing that I have a legitimate need and a legitimate nature and that need and nature do not become illegitimate simply because someone else was there first with wildflowers." She held his gaze with the equanimity of something that had been patient for centuries. "That's my case. I'll sit down now."

She sat down.

In the gallery, the entity with too many eyes leaned fractionally in the direction of the defendant's table. Nobody mentioned this.

---

Dream looked at the space between the two tables for a while.

It was the kind of looking that happening inside as much as outside. The gallery recognized it—those of the gallery who had been present for rulings before, which was most of them—as the specific quality of attention Dream brought to a matter when he had, privately, reached a conclusion and was now in the process of examining that conclusion from several angles before announcing it. The examination was not for their benefit. He was not, the more experienced observers noted, consulting precedent. He was not reviewing testimony. He was doing something that looked, very briefly, like genuine interest.

"The boy," he said. "Max."

Both parties looked at him.

"He is eleven."

"Eleven," First Love confirmed.

"Eleven." Dream said it again, not as confirmation. He said it as though the number was interesting to him, which was not an expression that crossed his face often. Something moved through his expression briefly—something from very far back, like a shape seen through deep water—and then his face was unreadable again.

"THE QUESTION OF PRECEDENT—" the bailiff began, with the enthusiasm of someone for whom precedent was a genuine passion.

"The court has sufficient basis for ruling," Dream said.

The bailiff subsided. He did this with the practiced acceptance of someone who had been subsiding on command for longer than some civilizations had managed to persist.

Dream was quiet for a moment more. Then he stood, and the room stood with him, not because they had been instructed to but because rooms tend to do that when something very old and very certain rises from its seat.

"The court finds as follows," he said.

His voice was not loud. It didn't need to be.

"The defendant entered a designated and protected formative dreamscape without authorization and altered its function. The court finds for the plaintiff on this point. The dreamscape is to be restored to its original specifications. The wildflowers. The light. The approach. The almost-kiss." He looked, briefly, in the direction of First Love, who had allowed herself a small, taut exhale. "Whatever you built there was built well. It will be rebuilt."

First Love said nothing. She was wise enough to wait.

"However," Dream said, and the however arrived with the weight of having been considered, "the defendant's nature is not a defense to be dismissed. She is what she is. She does what she does. She has done what she does in this world since before the dreamscape in question existed, since before the mechanisms for constructing such dreamscapes existed, since before, arguably, the philosophical preconditions for either."

Mora inclined her head.

"The defendant cannot reasonably be prohibited from feeding. A prohibition of that kind is not within the court's scope and would not, if attempted, survive her compliance." There was something in the way he said this that suggested he was not displeased by this fact. "The hunger is legitimate. The method is legitimate. The location presents the problem, and location can be addressed."

First Love was still waiting. She had the expression of someone maintaining calm through technical effort. In her hands, her notes were very still.

"The dreamscape is therefore restored, as specified," Dream said. "And the defendant is granted access."

"*Your Lordship—*" First Love began.

"Every seventh recurrence," Dream said.

Silence.

A specific silence, with dimensions.

"Every—" First Love's voice was doing something careful. "Every seventh—"

"The boy is eleven," Dream said, and now there was something in his voice that the gallery could not precisely classify—something that was not quite warmth and not quite amusement and was perhaps best described as private satisfaction at the specific shape of a thing. "He has years ahead of him. Six dreams built as you built them: the field, the light, the approach, the carefully calibrated almost. Six such dreams, intact, every cycle. Formative, as you intend. On the seventh—" he paused— "the defendant will feed. As she requires."

"The imprinting will be—the effect on his development will be—"

"*Complicated*," Dream said. Not unkindly. Not kindly, either. Precisely.

He gathered his robes. He turned from the bench.
