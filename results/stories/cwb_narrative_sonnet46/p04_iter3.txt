# My Apocalypse Buddy

---

The map was wrong, which was fine, because Midas had drawn it himself three weeks ago and had been wrong about most things for considerably longer than that.

He spread it across the kitchen table—a folding card table, technically, one leg replaced with a stack of hardback encyclopedias that had proved more useful as furniture than as reference material, given that the world they referenced no longer applied. The map showed the six blocks between their building and the old distribution warehouse on Fenner Street. It showed, in red marker, the things to avoid: the sinkhole on Cascade, the block that the Alderman crew considered theirs by virtue of having crayoned a border on the sidewalk in what appeared to be, based on smell, actual blood, and the dog situation on Marrow Lane, which Midas had annotated simply as *dog situation* because he lacked the vocabulary to be more specific.

Chewy stood beside him. Chewy stood beside him in the way that a telephone booth stands beside things: present, rectangular, and fundamentally designed for a world that was no longer operating.

Chewy had been a retail assistance unit, originally. The sticker on his chassis said so, though someone had scraped away RETAIL and written REAL over it in what appeared to be nail polish. His screen, chest-height on Midas, displayed a rolling loop of expressions that approximated human affect the way a plastic fruit approximates nutrition. Currently he was displaying Attentive Interest, which looked like two arched lines above a horizontal one, which looked like a surprised cauliflower.

"Alright," said Midas. He put his finger on the map. "Fenner Street warehouse. They were running packaged goods out of there before the Collapse, which means there's a nonzero chance that—"

"*Every pantry tells a story,*" said Chewy, in the warm brand-friendly baritone he'd been given by engineers who could not have imagined this particular use case. "*What's yours?*"

"Yes," said Midas. "Exactly."

---

The plan had three components.

First: approach via Cascade Street, skirting the sinkhole's eastern edge, because the western edge had a lean to it that Midas had noticed and respected.

Second: enter the warehouse through the loading bay on the south side, which he had scouted from a distance and which appeared unoccupied, the door secured by a padlock that he had the right tool for, specifically a length of rebar that he'd been carrying for six months because you never knew.

Third: prioritize canned goods, medical supplies, and anything with a high caloric density, avoid anything requiring refrigeration, and be back before the light started to go weird, which happened earlier than actual dark and was its own kind of warning system.

"Three steps," Midas said. "Simple. Achievable."

He had been saying this about plans for approximately two years. He had not yet fully absorbed what this track record implied.

"*Your journey starts with a single step,*" said Chewy. "*And ends with savings you can feel.*"

"Motivating," said Midas. "Let's go."

---

The sinkhole's eastern edge had a lean to it now too.

Midas stood at the lip of it and considered this development with the expression of a man who has been surprised by Tuesday. The sinkhole had been, three weeks ago, roughly the circumference of a dining table. It was now closer to the circumference of a dining room. The eastern edge, which had been firm and scornable, had incorporated itself into the general project of the hole.

"I noted the western edge," Midas said, to no one in particular. "I made a specific note about the western edge."

Chewy was looking into the hole with what his screen suggested was Engaged Curiosity.

"*Going down?*" said Chewy. "*Basement levels offer unbeatable value.*"

"We're not going down," said Midas.

They went around. The around route took them through a narrow corridor between two collapsed storefronts where the rubble had been arranged—by weather or intention, it was impossible to say—into something that functioned as a chute. Midas went through it sideways, breathed in, thought briefly about all the infrastructure decisions that had led to this moment, and emerged on the other side missing a button from his jacket.

He noted this in the way that a person notes the loss of a button when the jacket itself still works. Which is to say: he noted it, and continued.

Chewy, being rectangular and approximately the width of the chute, did not fit through sideways. He went through front-first, which left a white scrape on both walls like a chalk rubbing of the word *persistence*.

---

The Alderman crew had moved their border.

Midas discovered this at the point where Cascade met Fenner, which was also the point where three men in matching yellow rain ponchos stepped out from behind a van that had been there so long it had begun to seem geological.

The man in the front poncho—which bore a large hand-drawn eye on the back, though Midas could not see this—crossed his arms and looked at Midas with the evaluative expression of someone who had recently decided they owned something.

"This is Alderman territory," said the front poncho. "Has been since First Frost."

"First Frost," Midas repeated. He thought about this. "Is that a date? Is that a named period? Are we doing named periods now?"

"Everyone's doing named periods," said the poncho, with some dignity. "What's that?" He pointed at Chewy.

"My buddy," said Midas.

Chewy's screen was displaying Warm Greeting, which was a circle with lines radiating from it that was meant to suggest a smile and achieved instead the impression of a small sun having an emotion.

"*Hi there,*" said Chewy. "*We value each and every one of our customers.*"

There was a pause.

"Is it broken?" the poncho asked.

"Somewhat," said Midas.

"*Our team is always happy to help,*" said Chewy. "*Please rate your experience: one to five.*"

The front poncho looked at Midas. Midas looked at the front poncho. Somewhere behind both of them, one of the other ponchos started what might have been a laugh, suppressed it, failed to suppress it, and tried to turn it into a cough.

"You can't go to the warehouse," said the front poncho, rallying.

"Why not?"

"It's ours. Since First Frost."

"Is there anything in it?"

A fractional pause. "No."

"Then," said Midas, with the patience of a man who has had this exact conversation in various forms for two years, "we're not in competition."

The poncho considered this. He uncrossed his arms, recrossed them, and looked at Chewy.

"Does it do that? The rating thing? All the time?"

"No," said Midas. "Sometimes it does slogans."

"What kind?"

"The unhelpful kind. Mostly."

"*Our family brands are your family brands,*" said Chewy, with great sincerity.

The poncho looked at Midas for a long moment, made a decision internal to himself, and stepped aside.

"Warehouse is empty," he said. "I'm telling you now."

"Thanks," said Midas, and he meant it, which was the thing about the post-collapse world that still caught him off-guard sometimes: the genuine gratitude you felt for people simply letting you pass.

---

The warehouse had been empty.

Midas stood in the middle of it and understood, with the calm clarity that comes from practice, that it was comprehensively empty. Not raided-recently empty. Not stripped-but-overlooked empty. It was empty in the way of a place that had been empty for long enough that something had come in and eaten the rats, and then left. The loading bay door he'd planned to pry open was already open, had been open, was essentially philosophically open, the padlock on the ground in two pieces connected by a memory.

The shelves ran the length of the building and held: dust; a single high-visibility vest; a laminated safety notice whose instructions were now approximately as useful as directions to Atlantis; and a cardboard box, on the lowest shelf, which Midas crossed the warehouse to reach with the optimism of a man who has not yet looked inside it.

Inside it were thirty-seven individually wrapped plastic spoons.

Midas picked one up. He looked at it. He put it back.

"Well," he said.

He thought about the three components of the plan.

"*Expect more. Pay less,*" said Chewy, from the doorway.

"That's—" Midas started. He stopped. "Yeah," he said.

He picked up the high-visibility vest. He put it on. It was two sizes too large and fluorescent orange and would make him visible from approximately the distance at which visibility became dangerous rather than useful, but it was the only thing in the warehouse with any structural integrity, and he had learned—slowly, empirically, through multiple occasions of being wrong—to take what presented itself.

He took eleven of the plastic spoons as well.

You never knew.

---

The route back was not the route they'd come.

This was partially intentional—the poncho situation made the Cascade route feel socially complicated in the way that post-collapse social complications had a tendency to become logistical complications without warning—and partially the result of Midas misreading his own map, which continued to bear only an abstract relationship with the physical world it described.

They went through the area around Marrow Lane.

The dog situation had developed.

It was not one dog now. It was unclear, exactly, how many dogs it was, because the dogs were operating as a unified entity with apparently collective decision-making, and by the time Midas had understood what was happening he was already running, the high-visibility vest making him large and orange and exactly as visible as he'd feared.

Chewy ran alongside him. Chewy ran in the way that something not designed for running runs—with complete commitment and absolutely no adaptation, so that his arms did not move and his expression did not change and his legs, which were not legs so much as articulated segments that approximated legs, went at a fixed pace that was slightly too fast for dignity and slightly too slow for actual escape.

"*Safety is our highest priority,*" said Chewy, over the sound of the dogs. "*Please remain calm.*"

Midas did not remain calm. He turned left, went over a fence that he would have said was unclearable until the moment he cleared it, and landed in a narrow side lot where a rusting delivery bike had been locked to a signpost for so long that the signpost had grown around the lock.

The dogs did not follow.

Midas lay on the ground of the side lot and breathed.

After a moment, Chewy came around the side, having apparently found the gate, and looked down at him with what his screen suggested was Gentle Concern.

"I'm fine," said Midas.

"*We care about your wellbeing,*" said Chewy. "*How are you feeling today: great, good, or just okay?*"

Midas stared at the sky, which was the color of old pewter and smelled faintly of something burning three miles away.

"Just okay," he said.

"*That's okay,*" said Chewy. "*We're here for you.*"

Midas closed his eyes for five seconds. He allowed himself those five seconds. Then he got up.

---

They arrived back at the building with the light already going weird.

The building was a six-story walk-up that had lost its walk-up quality on the fourth floor—the staircase there had become structural opinion rather than structural fact—and for this reason Midas lived on the third floor, in a two-bedroom apartment whose second bedroom housed, variously: supplies, the parts of Chewy that had detached and been recovered, a hand-drawn map that was still wrong, and three encyclopedias that were not stair leg, which he kept meaning to do something with.

He sat at the card table and put the high-visibility vest on it.

He put the eleven plastic spoons in a mug that held, now, twenty-two plastic spoons.

He looked at the wall. The wall had a water stain on it that had, over time, come to resemble a running figure, which Midas had spent an unreasonable number of evenings looking at.

Chewy stood to his left, doing what Chewy did when there was nothing to do: display a slow cycle of stock expressions, optimistic and unearned, against his chest screen's dim glow.

"So," said Midas.

He catalogued the day in the way that you catalogue things that have already happened: warehouse empty, route wrong, vest acquired, spoons acquired, one button lost, thirty-seven possible spoons abandoned, dignity condition unchanged from baseline which was already low.

"Same tomorrow," he said, meaning the world. Meaning the trying.

Chewy's screen cycled. It landed on something new, something Midas hadn't seen before, which after two years felt like a small surprise, the way small surprises had become the only kind of surprise that meant anything.

The screen said: *Thank you for choosing us.*

And then, underneath:

*We'll see you again soon.*

Midas looked at it for a long moment.

Outside, the last of the weird light finished going weird and became dark, which was simpler, which was honest, which was a thing you could navigate by.

"Yeah," said Midas. "I'll be here."

He wasn't talking to anyone in particular.

He was talking to both of them.
