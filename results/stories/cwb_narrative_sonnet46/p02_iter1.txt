# The Lighthouse Keeper

---

*Rubha Mòr Light Station, Isle of Garvain*
*14th October*

Dear Mum,

Arrived Thursday on the relief boat, weather permitting being an optimistic phrase given it pissed down the whole crossing. The station is in decent order. Previous keeper left the log books tidy enough and the brass in the lamp room is fair, though I've already given it a proper go-over because there's fair and there's how I'd have it, and these aren't the same thing.

The headland here is called Rubha Mòr, which just means Big Point, not very imaginative, but accurate. You can see from the lamp room a skerry about two hundred yards out called Am Bodach — the Old Man — which is a stack of three flat-topped rocks that does look, in certain lights, like a figure sitting with its back to you. There's a particular ledge formation on the south face which catches spray in a way I've not seen before, throws it up in a fan shape. I expect I'll stop noticing it.

The machinery is Stevenson-built, 1897, and whoever serviced it last knew what they were doing. The clockwork mechanism for the rotation is original and wants watching but it's not beyond me. Fog signal compressor has a gasket I'll need to order.

Food situation is manageable. I've enough tinned goods for six weeks. Please don't send anything, I've enough.

Good signal on the radio in the evenings, mostly.

I'm well.

Your son,
Douglas

---

*14 Salen Terrace, Craigbeg*
*21st October*

Douglas,

Well. Six weeks of tinned goods sounds like six weeks of misery but I suppose you know what you're doing. I've posted a box anyway. There's a dundee cake wrapped in foil at the bottom and two pairs of the thick socks, the ones your Aunt Bettie knitted, she made them in the autumn colours as if that means anything to socks but she was pleased with herself so I didn't say.

Nothing much here. The Patersons' dog got out again and was in Morag MacLeod's garden eating her cabbages, the usual drama. Your uncle Rab is the same, his leg giving him grief in the cold. The number fourteen bus no longer stops outside the Co-op which means I'm walking the extra bit with the messages and I've plenty to say about that.

The box also has the mustard powder. I thought it was worth sending.

You didn't say what the view's like. I always wondered about the view from those places, whether it's grand or whether you stop seeing it.

Write when you can.

Mum

---

*Rubha Mòr Light Station*
*3rd November*

Mum,

Thank you for the box. The cake is excellent. The socks are too large but I'm wearing two pairs of thinner ones inside them which works fine.

The view is — the view is the sea, whichever direction. You do keep seeing it, actually. The Old Man out on the skerry, Am Bodach, is visible from the gallery as long as the weather holds, and the weather here has opinions. Nothing about the view is what you'd call convenient. It doesn't arrange itself for looking at. It's just there and then sometimes it isn't and either way the light needs attending.

Routine is good. I'm up at 0500, lamp off at first light, log, breakfast, maintenance rounds. Afternoon is equipment, records, whatever the day calls for. Lamp on at dusk, watch through until 0200, then four hours. Repeat. It suits me. I'm not someone who needs a lot of conversation.

Tell Rab I was asking for him. The number fourteen thing sounds like a disgrace.

D.

P.S. The fog signal gasket arrived and I've fitted it. Works properly now.

---

*14 Salen Terrace, Craigbeg*
*9th November*

Son,

Good about the gasket. I don't know what a fog signal gasket is but I'm glad it's sorted.

Four hours' sleep doesn't sound a lot. Is that what they give you?

Your dad's cousin Effie had a baby boy, they're calling him Marcus which I think is ambitious for Craigbeg but it's not my business. The Co-op has moved the tinned soup to a different aisle entirely and I stood looking for it for five minutes like I'd never been in the place before. Thirty years going in there.

You're not someone who needs a lot of conversation, no, that's true, but there's a difference between choosing your own quiet and having no choice in the matter. I'm just saying.

Write when you can.

Mum

---

*Rubha Mòr Light Station*
*29th November*

Mum,

Sorry for the gap. There was weather.

I'll try to describe it but I'm not sure I've got the words for it, which isn't like me. It started the 18th, the barometer dropping faster than I've seen outside of a training scenario, and by the evening of the 19th we had force eleven, which is not a thing you read off a gauge and carry on normally. I carried on normally because that's the job and also because I didn't have another option. Kept the light going. Logged every hour. The machinery is solid, nothing failed.

But the noise, Mum. You can't — there's no way to have a sense of it without being inside it. The tower moves. It's built to move, it's supposed to, I know this theoretically, but knowing and knowing are different things. The rock itself moves. You can feel it through the soles of your boots. The lantern glass was fine, a Stevenson tower is built for this, but the sound of the wind in the ventilation and the sea coming up over the base of the tower, which it did, water across the gallery glass, you're talking about water at sixty feet.

It went on for two days.

On the second morning, when it was dropping off, I went out on the gallery. I don't know why, there was no reason to go out at that point. I just needed to see that Am Bodach was still out there. It was. Just sitting the same as always with its back to me.

I went back inside and had my tea.

I'm well and the station is in good order.

D.

---

*14 Salen Terrace, Craigbeg*
*5th December*

Douglas,

That was the longest letter you've written since you were away at college and needed money.

I'm not going to pretend I wasn't thinking about you in that storm because I was looking at the weather on the television and I know where Garvain is on the map, I looked it up when they posted you there. I didn't ring anyone because there's nothing to ring anyone about when a man is doing his job, and anyway you'd have hated it.

But son. Are you alright? And I'm asking directly because your last letter was not quite the same as your other letters and I'm your mother and I notice.

The dundee cake is almost gone I'd imagine. There's another box coming, I've put in more socks and the oatcakes from the good baker and a bottle of the dressing for your shoulder, if it's bad in the cold.

Write and tell me how you are. Tell me the truth of it.

Mum

---

*Rubha Mòr Light Station*
*12th December*

Mum,

I'm fine. The shoulder's not too bad. Thank you for the oatcakes, they're good.

I'm managing fine. The routine is — routine is good for a person, it's something I've always believed and the posting backs it up, in my view. You know where you are with a routine. Up at five, lamp off, log, and so on, it works for me. I'm not someone who mopes, you know that, it's not my nature.

The only thing, and this is not a complaint, the only thing is that the quality of the dark here is different from anywhere I've been. I don't mean darker, I mean — different in texture, somehow. There's no light source between here and the Hebrides and on a clear night without the moon you can see things you didn't know were there, looking up. Which is interesting. And the sounds the station makes, at night, the mechanisms and the tower settling and the sea doing what it does, you get to know them all individually. You get so you'd notice a new one. I notice all the sounds now, I know them all, each one.

And time — this is the only other thing, and I'm not sure why I'm mentioning it — time goes differently here. I don't mean it drags. I mean it's not structured the same way by people, by the sounds of cars and voices and the bin lorry on a Thursday and whatever, and so it feels thicker, I suppose, or unattached to itself. This isn't a problem. I'm just noting it because you asked.

I'm managing fine. Tell me about Rab's leg. Anything about the bus situation.

D.

---

*Rubha Mòr Light Station*
*21st December*

Mum,

I wrote you a longer letter than this and I've put it aside because I didn't like how it read.

I want to tell you something but I'm not quite sure how to put it in a way that doesn't sound like something it isn't, which is a cry for help or cause for worry, because it's neither, I think. I think it's just what it is.

There was a night — the 17th, a Monday — where the light was doing its rotations and the log was written up and I'd done the rounds and there was simply nothing left to do and I sat in the watch room and I thought: no one on this earth knows exactly where I am right now. You know I'm at Rubha Mòr, but that's just a name, you can't see me sitting here. No one can see me sitting here. And the thought, Mum — I couldn't tell you if it was the worst thought I've ever had or the best, which shouldn't be the same thing, probably. It was both at the same time, quite intensely, for about an hour.

Then I did the 0100 log and went to bed.

I went out to check on Am Bodach this morning. The top rock has a crack in it I hadn't seen before, or hadn't noticed, running across the whole of the flat face. I don't know how new it is. The rock looks different now. Like it's a thing that can be broken, which obviously it always was, I'm not saying it's surprising, I'm just saying it looks different. It's still sitting there the same as always. Same angle. Back to me.

I'm alright. I'm sorry if the last few letters have been unusual.

D.

---

*14 Salen Terrace, Craigbeg*
*28th December*

Douglas,

I've read your letter several times now.

There's something I haven't told you, and I'm going to tell you now because I think it's time and because you're my son and because I think you need to know it's not just you.

Your father had a winter, 1979 it was, before you were born, when he was working the supply boats and the contracts dried up in February and there was nothing. Four months we had almost nothing. He went very quiet in a way he wasn't usually quiet — your father wasn't quiet, as you know — and I would watch him at the kitchen table in the evenings and his face had a look on it I'd never seen on him before. Not sad, not exactly. More like he was listening for something and it wasn't coming.

I asked him about it, not straight, I wouldn't have then, I went around the side of it the way we all do. Eventually I got it out of him, or most of it. What he said was: I don't know how to be a man who can't provide. I don't know what I am otherwise. That's what he said.

He came through it, and the contracts came back in June, and he never spoke of it again and neither did I, not until now.

I'm not saying your situation is the same. You've got your work, I know that, you take your work seriously and it means something to you. But the thing underneath it — that question of what you are without anyone to prove it to — I think there might be some of your father in you on that front. And I think he'd have wanted you to know he had the same question. He'd have wanted you to know it's a thing that passes, or not passes, but changes, becomes something you can carry.

You're not alone in this particular type of hard.

I made a braised lamb on Christmas Day and thought of you the whole time, since you love it so and aren't here. The Patersons' dog came into the garden again. I gave up and gave it a bit of the lamb. Don't tell Morag MacLeod.

I love you very much son, in case that wasn't plain.

Mum

---

*Rubha Mòr Light Station*
*14th March*

Mum,

I let some time go so that I could write this letter properly, or what passes for properly with me.

There was a morning last week, a Tuesday, very clear, the first of the real clear mornings we've had since October. I was up at five as usual and the lamp off and the log done and I stood on the gallery to check the weather and the sun was still coming up behind the headland to the east and the light was doing the thing it does when there's no cloud, everything very distinct and with a bit of edge to it.

Am Bodach was sitting out on the skerry the same as always. I could see the crack across the top rock, catching the light, as clear as a line drawn on paper. It's been there all winter and it hasn't got worse and as far as I can observe the rock is stable. It's just cracked. It still holds.

I thought about what you wrote. About Dad and the winter of '79. I didn't write back then because I didn't know what to say to it and I think I needed it to settle in me before I could respond, which is apparently how I work. I'll try to be quicker about it in future.

I'm alright, I think. I don't mean that in the way I meant it in December, which was mostly performance. I mean it in the way of: this is what things are, and they're manageable, and the lamp goes on at dusk and off at dawn and the log gets written and I know all the sounds the tower makes in all weathers now, and some mornings the light is extraordinary and there's no one to point it out to, but I know it anyway.

Thank you for telling me about Dad. It was a decent thing to do.

I'll be wanting that braised lamb when I'm back on leave. The relief boat is due 2nd April, weather permitting — and yes, this is still an optimistic phrase.

What's the situation with the number fourteen bus? Has it been resolved? I've been thinking about it.

Your son,
Douglas
