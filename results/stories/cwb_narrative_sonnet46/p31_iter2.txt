# Gone South
### Chapter One: First Day Down

I've been standing on the corner of Canal and something-I-can't-pronounce for eleven minutes, which I know because I've been watching the battery percentage and it was at 14% when the guy dropped me off and now it's at 12%, and that's how bad things are: I'm measuring time in battery life.

The city smells like beer and flowers and something rotting sweetly underneath both. It's barely nine in the morning and already the heat is a physical thing, not weather but *pressure*, like the air itself has weight and opinion. Tennessee is hot but Tennessee-hot is at least honest about it—it comes from the sun, it makes sense, it goes somewhere at night. This is different. This is the whole atmosphere turned against you. This is tropical. This is the planet trying to digest you slowly and with some enjoyment.

I can't think about home yet. I notice that I can't think about home yet and I let myself not, because the city keeps interrupting: a streetcar grinding past on its rails, a man arguing on a phone in what might be French, a woman in a yellow dress who moves through the crowd like she has a specific appointment with a specific square of sidewalk and everyone else is lucky to witness the route. The smells keep shifting—coffee, exhaust, something frying, the ghost of last night's crowd still hanging in the soupy air. It's too much and I think that's why I'm still standing here on this corner, because moving would require picking one thing to move toward, and everything is equally loud, equally vivid, equally real in a way that is making my body understand that I have actually done this. I have actually left.

Tennessee is two states and forty-eight hours away.

*Don't.*

I pull up the settings. Low power mode. The screen dims and I feel it like a small betrayal, like the phone is already starting to leave me too.

Okay. Inventory. I have forty-three dollars, cash, from the emergency twenty I kept in my sketchbook and the extra twenty-three I had in my back pocket from last week's shift. I have the sketchbook. I have a change of clothes, socks, the charger that is currently useless. I have half a granola bar I can't imagine eating. I have 11% now, because that's what looking at settings costs, apparently.

I pick a direction and walk.

---

The French Quarter is what I know from TV and that's both helpful and not, because it looks right—the wrought iron, the balconies with their hanging plants, the particular swagger of streets that have been around long enough to stop caring whether anyone approves of them—but looking right isn't the same as knowing how to move through it. I move through it badly. I can tell I'm moving through it badly because I keep almost-stopping when things catch my eye, and real people don't do that, real people have somewhere to be and the city is just the reel playing behind their lives. I'm watching it like a movie. I have nowhere to be.

I find a bench near a courtyard fountain and sit with my backpack in my lap, which I understand is a sign, which I can't help. A woman with a cart is selling coffee from paper cups and I look at the money in my phone case for a long time before I spend four dollars I shouldn't on a cup I need, and she says *there you go baby* in a way that almost cracks something open in my sternum, some chamber I've been keeping sealed since the kitchen two nights ago, so I look at my shoes until the feeling passes, until it goes back down.

I look at my phone.

The notifications have been stacking since last night—I turned the sound off somewhere around Hattiesburg—and I've been not-looking at them the way you don't look at a car crash while you're driving past it, which is to say I've been looking at them constantly in my peripheral vision, knowing exactly how many there are without opening them. Eleven. Twelve now.

Mom. Mom. Mom. A number I don't recognize, which might be my stepdad calling from the work phone, which means he's still too furious to call from his own. Dana. Mom. Aunt Cara, which means Mom called Aunt Cara, which means this has moved from *incident* to *family situation*. Mom.

I open Mom's thread.

*Taylor, please just pick up. I'm not angry I promise. Please.*

*It's 1am baby please just tell me you're okay.*

*I talked to Matt. I want you to know that I talked to him and I need you to come home so we can all sit down and—*

I stop reading there.

The thing about that sentence is that it contains an entire architecture of misunderstanding, which is that *sitting down* is not the problem. The problem is not a seating arrangement. The problem is not even Matt's face when I said it, though his face is something I've been trying not to replay and keep replaying anyway, the way he looked at me like I'd admitted to shoplifting, like I was something he'd bought that turned out to be defective. The problem is older than that and larger than that and I have not slept in thirty-six hours and I am sitting on a bench in New Orleans with eleven dollars less than I started with and I cannot, I physically cannot, think about the problem in its full size right now or I will not be able to get up off this bench.

What I can think about: I am safer here. That's true. That is an actually true thing that I can hold onto. In the house I know what the air feels like when Matt is in the next room. I know what it costs to move through that air. I know what Monday would have looked like at school, the particular geography of a small town's attention, the way a secret stops being a secret once the wrong person opens their mouth. Here, no one knows a single thing about me. Not my name, not where I'm from, not anything that happened two nights ago. I am as unknown as it is possible for a person to be, and underneath the fear—deeper than it, almost hidden by it—there is something that functions like oxygen. Like a window opening.

I type: *I'm okay. I'm safe. Please don't call the police I just need some time.*

I look at it for a second. Then I send it before I can think about what it costs.

10%.

---

"You're doing the thing."

I look up. There's someone my age sitting at the other end of the bench—I don't know when they sat down, which is already information about how in my head I've been. Dark hair cut short on one side, a nose ring, wearing a flannel long-sleeve in this heat like a philosophical position.

I say: "What thing."

"The thing where you're trying so hard to look like you belong here that it reads from twenty feet away as someone who absolutely doesn't." They say it less like an insult and more like a diagnosis, like they're reading an x-ray. "How long you been in the city?"

"Long enough," I say, which is nothing, which they understand is nothing by the way one side of their mouth moves.

"Okay." They look back out at the street. "I've been here six weeks."

I wait.

"I'm Reyna."

"Taylor."

"Where you from?"

"Tennessee." There's no point lying about the accent, which has already answered the question before I could.

Reyna nods slowly, like that fills something in. "I'm technically from outside Baton Rouge. People think that basically makes me local. It doesn't." They reach into their bag—a big bag, I notice now, bigger than a day bag, the kind of bag that's living out of something—and produce a water bottle, which they offer without preamble. I take it. I don't perform reluctance about taking it because I am in fact very thirsty and performing reluctance would be stupid.

"You have somewhere to stay?" Reyna asks.

"I'm working on that."

"That means no."

I don't say anything.

"There's a hostel on Dauphine. It's cheap. Cash only. The woman who runs it doesn't ask a lot of questions, which is either a good quality or a bad one depending on who else has found it." A pause. "I can show you where it is."

I run the calculation, because I have to—this person is either exactly what they look like, which is someone who has been exactly where I am and is offering something because they remember what they needed, or this is the part of the story where I make a mistake that turns out to matter. The problem is I don't know how to read this city. I've got a whole set of instincts that were calibrated for a different place, different signals, a different set of what things mean, and those instincts are probably worse than useless here. I'm running Tennessee radar in a New Orleans situation. That's not navigation. That's noise.

"What are you running from?" Reyna asks. Just like that, conversational, like they ask this every morning.

"What makes you think—"

"Because I was sitting on this exact bench six weeks ago, and the woman who sat down next to me asked how long I'd been in the city, and I said *long enough.*"

The laugh gets out before I can close it down. It just escapes, takes me with it for a second. And then it's gone and we're sitting there in the heat, and down the street someone has started playing trumpet—a slow, sad, open-throated thing that moves through the air differently than the other noise, that seems to organize everything else around it.

I look at my shoes. "I came out to my family."

Just that. Nothing else. Not *as what,* not *what happened,* just the fact, the precipitating event, stripped down to the bones of it.

Reyna doesn't make a sound for a moment. When I look up they're not looking at me with pity, which I was braced for, and they're not looking at me with the quick, careful blankness people use when they're trying to seem like they don't have feelings about something. They're just looking, the way you look at someone when you're actually listening.

"That explains a lot," they say, and something about the way they say it—not *I'm sorry* or *that's awful* or *were they bad about it,* just *that explains it*—loosens something in my chest. Like they've simply placed a fact into the correct category rather than making me perform the shape of my suffering for them.

My phone buzzes. I look at the screen. Mom.

I turn it face-down on my knee.

"The hostel's about a ten-minute walk," Reyna says. They stand up, and I notice how they move with their bag—practiced, unconscious, the weight distributed evenly across their shoulders like they've learned where it wants to sit. "There's also a place on Magazine Street I can show you. Best po'boys you've eaten. Which may or may not be a high bar from Tennessee, I don't know your life."

"I've never had a po'boy," I say.

Reyna looks at me. "Then we have a full day ahead of us."

I stand up. My backpack pulls at my shoulders and my shoes are wrong for this heat and the granola bar in the front pocket has probably melted into the wrapper. I've got 10% and forty-three dollars and no plan past this moment, no plan past this block, no plan past this strange and cautious morning that is somehow still going.

But the trumpet is still playing, and the street is opening up ahead of us into something golden and indifferent and enormous, and Reyna is already moving, already three steps ahead, not looking back in a way that gives me the choice about whether to follow, which is the right way to do it, which is maybe something they learned on that bench six weeks ago.

I follow.

The city is loud around us, loud and various and completely uninterested in what I left behind, and I walk into it with 10% and nowhere to sleep and the name of a street I can't pronounce, and what I feel—under everything, smaller than everything else but real, the first real thing in two days—is something I won't let myself call hope yet but that moves the way hope moves, that takes up the same space.

The trumpet follows us for half a block.

Then it doesn't.

Then there's just the street, and the heat, and the sound of two people walking who haven't decided yet what they are to each other—and the whole long uncertain afternoon still ahead, waiting to find out.
