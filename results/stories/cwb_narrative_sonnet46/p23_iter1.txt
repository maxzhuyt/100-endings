## I. Initial Discovery

The drawer was locked. I want to establish this before anything else, because I know how the rest of this will read, and I need the locked drawer on record at the front.

I lock that drawer every morning when I arrive. The key lives on my badge lanyard. At 09:14 on [REDACTED], I unlocked it to retrieve the Holmgren intake forms, and the note was there, on top of them, on standard Foundation memo stock. Gray border. The watermark.

I did not put it there.

The handwriting was mine. I mean by this: not similar to mine, not plausibly mine, not approximately mine. Mine. The particular way I close my lowercase *e*, the way my sevens have a crossbar because I learned to write in Germany until I was nine—it was mine.

It read:

---

*East corridor, B-level. Shift change. Cell 7 will use the transition window. Seventeen people. Please listen to me.*

*— D. Voss*
*[Date: three weeks from initial discovery]*

---

I photographed it with my phone before I touched it. Then I bagged it according to standard evidence protocol. Then I stood in my office for approximately four minutes without moving, which I'm documenting here because the timestamp gap in my keycard activity log will appear anomalous otherwise and I would prefer to account for it myself.

Four minutes is how long it took me to decide this was a containment situation and not a personal one.

I filed Form 7-B (Anomalous Materials Discovery) and went back to work.

## II. Accumulation Log

**Note 2** — Found in the break room, tucked between the lid and the body of the community coffee canister. Same paper. Same handwriting.

*East corridor, B-level. Shift change, 18:00. Cell 7 uses the maintenance panel—the one Facilities hasn't updated in the system yet. Fourteen people. The number matters. Please keep track of the number.*

The maintenance panel referenced is not in any current schematic I can access at my clearance level. I have submitted a separate request to Facilities to confirm whether such a panel exists. As of this filing, no response.

**Note 3** — Found tucked inside a Report 9-F that I personally submitted four days prior. The staple had been removed and replaced. The paper was between pages three and four.

*They find the panel at 18:07. It takes seven minutes. You have a window if you start the lockdown at 18:00. Eleven people. I keep trying.*

The casualty count has changed. Fourteen to eleven to—in the first note—seventeen. I have a theory about this that I'll return to. I am trying to present these in order.

**Note 4** — Found inside a sealed evidence bag in the secondary storage room. The bag's tamper indicator was intact. I documented the intact indicator before opening it. I have photographs.

*I know you're logging these. Good. That's the right instinct. The logs matter.*

*Lockdown at 18:00. Don't wait for authorization. Nine people.*

I began annotating the notes here. Beside *Don't wait for authorization*, I wrote: *This is where it loses credibility. I would wait. I always wait.*

Beside *Nine people* I wrote: *Decreasing. Variable.*

By Note 7 my annotations were single words.

By Note 10 they were checkmarks.

The theory about the changing numbers: if the notes are temporal in origin—if I am genuinely writing them at some future point and transmitting them backward through a mechanism I don't yet understand—then the changes reflect successful interference. Each note I receive changes something. The numbers drop. This is the optimistic interpretation.

The other interpretation is that something is calibrating.

I have not been able to rule out either. This is documented.

## III. Contradiction and Rupture

**Note 14** mentioned Sena Abiodun.

I want to be precise here because I need this report to be read carefully. Note 14 referenced "Abiodun's team" handling secondary lockdown response. It described her using the radio code she preferred, the one she had used since we were junior researchers together, the one she made up herself because she thought the standard ones were ugly.

Sena died in November. Traffic accident, surface roads near Site-17. I attended the service. I wrote part of the eulogy, which I delivered badly.

Note 14 is dated three weeks from now. In the future it describes, Sena Abiodun is alive and working a containment response.

I sat with this for a long time. I have not documented how long.

The implications split into two directions and I cannot get them to merge back. Either the future described in the notes is not *this* timeline's future—it is a future in which Sena did not die in November, which means the notes do not describe my future—or something that has access to my memories is constructing details it knows will land.

Both of these are bad. I understand that they're bad in different ways.

**Note 15** used the word *pellucid*.

I have never written that word. I've searched my sent reports, my personal correspondence, my journals. I don't use *pellucid*. I know what it means. I've simply never written it.

I heard it once. Approximately four months ago, during a routine observation shift. Cell 7. The entity there does not communicate in language—this is documented in all relevant intake files—but on that shift it produced what I logged as "phonemic output, non-standard, duration 4 seconds." I transcribed the sounds as best I could.

One of them was *pellucid*.

I did not include this in my initial log because I did not think it was relevant. I'm including it now. I understand that including it now makes things worse. I'm including it anyway.

## IV. The Investigation

They sent four people. A graphologist from Research, a temporal anomaly specialist whose name I was not given, an agent from Security whose face I had seen before but whose name I also was not given, and someone from Medical who sat in the corner and did not speak and whom I have listed here because I noticed her and I want the noticing documented.

The graphologist confirmed the handwriting without hesitation. She seemed uncomfortable doing so. She said *this is consistent with* rather than *this is*, which is a distinction that matters in evidence but not, I think, in practice.

The temporal anomaly specialist ran two analyses. He sat in front of his equipment for twenty minutes afterward. He filed both analyses. I have been told they contradict each other and that both have been accepted into the record. I asked whether that was normal procedure. He said it was becoming more normal.

The Security agent asked me to describe Cell 7.

I described Cell 7. I described the containment parameters. I described the observation window and the particular quality of the sound insulation and the way the air in the corridor outside smells different from the rest of B-level—metallic, slightly sweet, which Facilities has never been able to explain and which I have logged four times.

Then I described the observation sessions. Seventeen of them over eight months. I described them in order. I described what I had noticed each time. I described the phonemic incident.

I described Sena. I don't know why. The agent hadn't asked.

She always thought Cell 7 was the most interesting thing in the building. She used to stop by my office after my shifts and ask what I'd observed, and she'd turn the descriptions over in her hands like objects. I described this.

I described how she laughed at the intake form's description of Cell 7's containment class—she thought whoever had written it was being evasive, which she considered a form of cowardice she found charming in institutional documents.

The agent stopped taking notes at some point. I noticed but I didn't stop.

Eventually he said: "Thank you, Dr. Voss."

I had been talking for forty minutes. I know because I looked at the clock when I finally stopped.

Something has changed. I'm aware of it. I don't know the name of what has changed.

The investigation team left. The Medical observer said, on her way out, "Get some sleep." It was the first thing she'd said. I'm including it here.

The two analyses are in the archive. They contradict each other. The Foundation has filed them both. I find this either very reassuring or very frightening and I have not been able to determine which.

## V. Final Note

At 16:43 today, I printed this report. Pages one through nine. I stapled them and set them on my desk to review before I submitted. At 17:30, I picked them up to read through them.

Between pages nine and ten—between the page that ended with *I find this either very reassuring or very frightening* and the blank page I had not yet written—there was a note.

Same paper. Same handwriting.

*#4471-V.*

*18:00.*

*Don't go to the east corridor at shift change.*

---

It is currently 17:42. I am writing this. The note is in the evidence bag beside my keyboard. The bag's chain of custody is documented.

The east corridor is four minutes from my office. Shift change begins at 18:00. I have been placed on administrative leave, which means I have no sanctioned reason to be in the east corridor at shift change. I had no plans to be there.

I should say that. I had no plans to be there.

The note is telling me not to do something I was not going to do.

This is the most frightening thing about it. That it had to tell me anyway.

I am aware that this report is the anomaly. I noted this earlier in the filing and I am noting it again. The report the reader is reading is the document in which the final note was found. This cannot be processed out. I have tried. It cannot be processed out.

I keep thinking about the numbers. Seventeen, then fourteen, then eleven, then nine. Decreasing. Variable. I keep thinking that if the source of the notes is me—if I am writing them from a future in which I have information I don't yet have—then each note I write brings the number down. The notes are doing something. The writing is doing something.

I keep thinking about Sena turning descriptions over in her hands like objects.

I keep thinking that filed reports are how the Foundation processes the unresolvable, and that this may be enough, and that this may be all I can do, and that this may be why I'm still writing.

It is 17:51.

I am going to submit this report and then I am going to remain at my desk. I have no plans to be in the east corridor. I had no plans to be in the east corridor.

The note said *don't go.*

I'm not going.

I'm writing this down so it's on record.

*Researcher D. Voss, Personnel #4471-V*
