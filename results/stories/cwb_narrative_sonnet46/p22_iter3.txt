# Prison Strings

The facility doesn't have a name on any public record.

It sits three levels underground, beneath what was once a federal correctional annex in Florida, and its walls are reinforced with something that smells faintly of disinfectant and older things—salt, maybe, or iron. The overhead lights give off the particular yellow that makes everyone look sick, that makes skin look like something recently unburied. Jolyne Cujoh has been in enough facilities to know that yellow. She stopped trusting it a long time ago.

She's not here as an inmate. That's technically an improvement.

She's here because a guard at Green Dolphin recognized something in one of the holding cells that nobody with a normal set of eyes should have been able to see, and called the one number they had for people like that, and ultimately the information reached Jolyne through three intermediaries and a cocktail napkin with cramped handwriting on it. She'd come because the guard described the thing in the cell as having yellow eyes—ringed like a target, layered like something that had never needed to pretend to be human. Eyes like that don't belong to anything that means well. She's learned that the hard way.

The guard who meets her at the checkpoint has the look she's learned to recognize in people who work with things they don't understand: the slight over-steadiness, the way they hold their face a beat longer than it needs to be held. He walks her through intake like she's fragile. She isn't. She signs nothing that she reads as binding and follows him into the corridor without speaking.

Stone Free hums just beneath her skin. The string coils and settles, coils and settles, reading the air.

The corridor is long. The doors are wrong—too heavy for what a standard prison requires, reinforced past any practical need for containing humans. Whatever they're containing here isn't always human. Jolyne keeps her steps even and lets her strings extend by two feet in every direction, invisible, feeling. The air has a pressure to it, the way air gets before lightning.

She catalogs: the dead-end corridor to her left, sealed with what smells like older materials underneath the antiseptic. The guard station with its cluster of monitors, each showing a different cell block angle. The way the other guard at the station doesn't quite look at her, looks slightly past her, like he's been told not to. The faint sound, from somewhere further down, that could be a person and could be something worn like one.

Everything here is a data point. She has learned not to let data points go unread.

---

The cameras start dying three seconds before the woman appears.

Jolyne notices because she always notices—it's trained into her now, automatic as breathing. Corner of her eye: the red recording lights die one after another down the corridor, sequenced, like dominoes falling in reverse. Whatever is moving ahead of the woman moves quickly, clearing the way. And then the woman herself comes around the corridor bend, and she looks like someone on a pleasant errand, someone who stepped off a downtown elevator and found herself here and couldn't imagine why that would be strange.

Auburn hair. Neat clothes, undecorated. A smile that arrives a half-second too fast—not caught mid-expression, not warming into place the way smiles actually work on human faces, but already there, held in position, waiting for an occasion to land.

"You must be Jolyne," she says. Soft voice. Genuinely warm. "I've heard interesting things."

*You first,* Jolyne doesn't say. What she says is: "Who are you?"

"Makima." She offers it without hesitation, without the slight protective delay people have around their own names in places like this. Either she has nothing to hide or she's moved past caring about concealment. Her eyes travel—just briefly, calibrated—to the place where Stone Free's strings are coiled under Jolyne's sleeve. Not visible. Not to any normal eye. Makima looks there anyway, directly, with the accuracy of someone who already knows where to look. "Your ability. I've been curious about it."

*There it is,* Jolyne thinks. *Already.*

"Cool," she says. "Mine too."

---

Makima falls into step beside her without asking permission, and Jolyne lets it happen because she wants to watch. She catalogs: the dead cameras behind them, the way a guard they pass shifts his weight toward Makima without seeming to notice he's making the choice, the quality of Makima's pleasantness. That quality is specific. It's frictionless—like a surface polished past the point where anything can get purchase, past the point where grip is physically possible.

Jolyne has met charming people. She's met manipulative people. She's met people who use warmth like a tool, who deploy it consciously the way you deploy a key. Makima's warmth doesn't feel deployed. It feels ambient. Structural. Like she couldn't turn it off because she's never had to think about it as something separate from herself.

That's the thing that raises the hair on Jolyne's arms. Conscious manipulation she can read and counter. This is something else.

Makima asks questions. About Stone Free's range—how far can the strings extend before sensation degrades? About whether the strings retain information after retraction, whether Jolyne has ever tried running them through solid walls, through water, through other people. The questions are good. Whoever has briefed her briefed her well, and whatever she doesn't know she's assembling fast, building a model, testing and refining it in real time.

She asks the way someone enquires about a dog they're considering acquiring: genuine interest, clinical warmth, no acknowledgment that the dog is in the room and listening.

Jolyne answers nothing directly. She's had practice with this—with people who think charm is a lever, who apply it to a specific point and wait for something to give. She matches the pleasantness, fills the space with noise that sounds like answers and contains none, and watches. What she watches: Makima doesn't blink at the non-answers. She doesn't press, doesn't recalibrate visibly, doesn't show the micro-frustration that people show when they expect a lever to work and it doesn't move. She simply adjusts. Reframes the question. Approaches from a different direction. Patient. *Genuinely* patient, in the way that implies she has never found patience difficult because she has never felt the cost of waiting.

Jolyne doesn't let it show—what that patience does to her. She keeps her face easy and her stride even and her strings quietly reading everything in range.

---

The disturbance is in a cell block two corridors further down: two inmates, and something between them that shreds the air. Jolyne feels it before she sees it—the strings telegraph the wrongness in the atmosphere, the place where the pressure of the space has been disrupted, where something that doesn't belong in the physical world has been forced into it anyway.

She moves before she's consciously decided to. Stone Free's strings are already flying when she comes through the door, reading angles, finding the connections, understanding the structure of what she's looking at. It takes forty seconds. She handles it the way she handles anything—efficiently, with the part of her brain that processes crisis without ceremony, that has learned to identify the fastest line between problem and solution. The inmates are separated. The wrongness is contained. The disrupted air settles.

Her strings retract.

She turns.

Makima is standing back from the door. She hasn't moved to assist, hasn't moved away—she's positioned herself at exactly the angle that provides the clearest view of everything Jolyne just did. Her expression is an expression Jolyne has seen on the faces of people at auctions: the moment when a piece they've suspected is authentic gets confirmed.

*She staged it.*

Jolyne has no proof. She doesn't process it as something that needs proof. The look on Makima's face, and her own gut—her gut that has never, in a genuine crisis, been wrong—tell her with the flatness of established fact. The disturbance was real. The timing was not.

"Impressive," Makima says. "You adapt very quickly."

"Yeah," Jolyne says. "I do."

---

The guard's name, she's established, is Reyes. He's been stationed in this block for six months. He has a daughter somewhere. He's the kind of person who, in most contexts, would be unremarkable—steady, professional, doing his job without drama. He keeps ending up near Makima. Not following her, not watching her openly, just finding reasons to be in the same corridor, the same room, orienting in her direction the way a compass needle orients, driven by something he couldn't name and wouldn't think to try.

Jolyne sends a string toward him, fine as thread. What it finds is *pressure.* Not physical. Something like a connection running between the man and the woman across the room—taut, invisible, the particular tautness of something under constant tension. He's not being forced. That's the specific horror of it. There's no resistance in him, no awareness of something outside him pulling, no sense of a current he's swimming against. He thinks it's his own idea to stand there. He thinks the warmth he feels when he looks toward her is something he generated himself.

She's found it. The actual thing.

Makima is watching Jolyne's face as the strings return the information to her. She doesn't explain. She doesn't tense, doesn't move to interrupt, doesn't show any sign that she's been discovered. She smiles—slightly, privately—and says nothing. And the nothing is more specific than anything she could have said. The nothing says: *yes, and?* The nothing says: *what exactly are you going to do about it?*

Jolyne files the string back. She keeps her face even. The two of them stand in the humming quiet of the cell block corridor, and Jolyne understands now what she's standing next to. The shape of it. The nature of it. Not the full picture—she doubts anyone has the full picture—but enough. Enough to know what the offer is going to be before it's made.

---

They end up in a corridor, just the two of them. Makima stops walking, turns to face her, and the smile adjusts—softens, becomes something that in another face would look like genuine feeling. Maybe it is. That might be the worst version.

"I could look into your case," she says. "The original one. The murder charge."

Jolyne doesn't answer.

"The conviction rested on evidence that wasn't properly handled. There are people who could revisit it, if someone with the right access made the right requests." A pause, calibrated. "And I've had contact, recently, with people who've spoken to Jotaro Kujo. Who could arrange—"

"Stop."

Makima stops.

The name still hits sideways. It always does, through whatever she's built up, through the walls she's constructed since the last time she had to think about what it means that she doesn't know where he is, what condition he's in, whether the distance between them is the kind that might still be closed. It hits through all of that and lands in the part of her that is not a fighter, not a prisoner, not someone who handles crises without ceremony—the part that is just a person who misses her father and doesn't know how to stop.

She keeps her face even.

"That's," she says, and stops, and breathes, and starts again. "That's a very convenient thing to offer."

"It's a genuine one," Makima says. Gently. Without pressure, which is its own kind of pressure.

And the thing is—Jolyne believes her. That's the actual sophistication of it. The offer isn't fabricated. Something real is being extended, here, in this fluorescent-yellow corridor that smells of disinfectant and older things, in the hand of a woman who has invisible strings running to every person in a fifty-foot radius. The offer is real. The hand holding it is real. The leash attached to accepting it is also real, and Makima is watching—precisely, patiently, with the attention of someone who has done this hundreds of times—to see whether wanting the real thing badly enough will make Jolyne stop looking at the hand.

Four seconds of silence.

She lets herself want it. Fully, without the usual suppression—the information about the case, her father's name said out loud in a corridor, the shape of a door that might be a door. She lets herself feel the specific gravity of it, the pull. Lets herself notice how real the pull is, and how well-aimed, and what it means that it's that well-aimed.

And then she exhales.

---

"Okay," Jolyne says. "I'm going to be straight with you, because I think it's been a while since someone has." She faces Makima fully—not as a challenge, just as a fact, the way you face something you're not going to look away from. "I don't know exactly what you are. But you've been running plays on me since you came around that corner. You made those cameras die. You're doing something to Reyes that he doesn't know is being done to him." She tilts her head slightly. "And whatever you just offered me—however real the pieces of it are—it comes attached to something that you're hoping I want too badly to look at directly."

Makima's expression doesn't change. The smile stays in position.

"You're very perceptive," she says.

"I know," Jolyne says. "So, no."

One of the corridor cameras, which had come back online sometime in the last few minutes, flickers. It makes a small sound—electrical, faint—and dies. The red light blinks out.

Makima looks at Jolyne with an expression that might be curiosity or might be the thing that lives where curiosity would be in a face that's learned to produce it. There's something in the look that isn't disappointed—something more patient than disappointment, something that treats *no* as a data point the same way it treats anything else. She'll account for it. She'll adjust. She'll come back to this from a different angle, because she has time, and access, and invisible strings running to half the personnel in this building, and she has never once found patience difficult because she has never felt the cost of waiting.

Jolyne looks back.

She doesn't move. She doesn't need to win this conversation—there's no winning it, no resolution available in the space of a corridor encounter, no punch she can throw that will fix whatever Makima is and put it behind her. She knows this. She knows the facility will still be here tomorrow, and Makima's consulting contract will still be in whatever file it's in, and Reyes will still stand in her gravity without knowing he's doing it. She knows she's refusing one offered leash and that no one is handing her an exit.

She stays. She refuses. She makes the staying mean something by knowing exactly what she's staying in the middle of and refusing it anyway.

The lights hum. Somewhere further down, something that might be a person makes a sound that carries through the reinforced walls, muffled and then gone. Makima smiles. Jolyne doesn't.

Neither of them moves.

That's where it stays.
