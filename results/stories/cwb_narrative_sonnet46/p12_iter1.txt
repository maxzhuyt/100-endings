# Rehearsing Rejection

The shampoo is the kind that smells like something botanical and vague, eucalyptus or tea tree or the idea of a forest, and she stands under the water longer than she needs to because the pressure is good and the evening is not yet a problem. It is just an evening. Maya is coming at seven. They will probably order from the Thai place on Clement and watch something neither of them will remember in two weeks and that is fine, that is the specific texture of what they have, and she is not thinking about what they have because she is thinking about conditioner and whether the grey shirt or the blue one and the particular quality of the light coming through the frosted window, which is the color light goes in October, which is amber and a little elegiac, and she decides that's just October, not an omen.

She towels off. She selects the blue shirt and then puts it back and takes the grey. She checks her phone.

Twenty-three percent.

*She said she'd text when she left.*

She sets the phone face-down on the bathroom counter and goes to find the charger. She finds it under the couch cushion and holds it for a moment, thinking she'll plug in the phone when she's back in the bathroom, and then she sets the charger on the kitchen table and forgets about it, or doesn't forget exactly but allows it to become a thing she'll deal with in a moment, a moment she will keep creating and deferring.

*She hasn't texted.*

There is a version of this where Maya is still at home, finishing something, running a few minutes late the way she always runs, and the world is what it is, which is fine, which is Tuesday.

She decides she's hungry and goes to the kitchen.

---

She's standing at the counter with a mug of tea she didn't want but made anyway when the scene arrives. It arrives the way these things do, not gradually, not announced, but already in progress, already mid-breath, Maya already sitting across from her in the scene's preferred staging — not here, somewhere neutral, a coffee shop or the front seat of Maya's car with the engine off — and Maya is choosing her words, which is the tell, which is how you know, because Maya in ordinary life does not choose her words, Maya in ordinary life talks the way water runs, but in the scene Maya is careful.

*I've been thinking about us,* scene-Maya says. Not angrily. Not even sadly, quite. With the specific exhaustion of someone who has already done the sad part privately and is now delivering the administrative conclusion.

*Okay,* she hears herself answer. Her voice in the scene is controlled in a way she has practiced.

*I feel like we've been—* scene-Maya pauses. She sighs, and the sigh is exactly right, it has Maya's specific quality of exhalation, the way she tilts her chin slightly when she's gathering something. *I feel like you've been somewhere I can't quite reach.*

*You could have said something.*

*I know. I should have. I think I was hoping—*

*You were hoping I'd figure it out.* Her own voice, flat. *That's what you do.*

Maya looks at the table. Scene-Maya looks at the table in the way real Maya looks at tables when something is true and she knows she can't contest it, and this detail — its precision — is the thing that makes the scene so difficult to dismiss, because it isn't a stranger she's imagining, it's Maya, the actual structural grammar of how Maya occupies space, transposed into this terrible context. And here is the odd seduction of it: everything is visible. The scene has an ending. She knows where she is in it. The mug in her hand is at room temperature now, the tea a degree past its best, the kitchen around her muted and unresolved and asking nothing of her, and the scene is *better* than the kitchen, more organized, more honest, the kind of honest that costs something, and so she is *there* and not here, the mug cooling in her hand, October light going to dark at the window.

---

She puts the mug in the sink.

She opens her phone. Nineteen percent. She opens and closes Messages without reading anything.

The thing about last Tuesday is that three hours is not nothing. They had been texting the way they text, steadily, a current between two points, and then for three hours there was nothing, and when Maya came back she said *sorry I got caught up*, which is what you say when a thing is true and also when a thing is not. She had said *no worries* and meant it, mostly, and then spent the following two hours composing and deleting responses to the next text, trying to recalibrate to whatever frequency they were now on.

The dinner two weeks ago: Maya had been somewhere else. Not visibly, not complainably, but in the way that you can tell the difference between someone who is present and someone who is in the room, and Maya that night was in the room.

*The reservation almost got cancelled.* This is true. Maya had texted the day before: *work thing might run late, might have to reschedule*, and she had typed and deleted four responses and finally sent *totally fine whatever you need* and Maya had written back *no no, I want to come, I'll make it work*, and she had wanted to believe this was conclusive. She had chosen to believe this was conclusive.

The compliment Maya gave her at the restaurant had the structure of a compliment but something else at its edge — *you seem really at home with yourself lately*, which could mean anything, which she had logged and is now retrieving.

She arranges the items in a line. The gap. The dinner. The compliment. The plans that almost weren't. They are not individually damning. She knows they are not individually damning. But they form something, laid end to end, the way stones form a path you only recognize as a path once you're already partway down it.

Sixteen percent.

---

She's going to be fine when it happens. That's what she decides, moving to the couch, sitting with her feet tucked under her. When Maya says whatever she's come to say, she is not going to perform devastation. She is going to be *understanding* in the tone that costs the other person more than tears do. She will say: *no, I get it.* She will mean it in the register that makes meaning land like something cold. Maya will leave and she will clean the apartment, methodically, and she will text no one.

She constructs this and it feels like armor. She can feel herself inside it, upright, impervious. There will be a moment afterward where she calls her sister, but only to confirm that she is fine, and she will be fine, and her sister will say *you're always so—* and she'll say *I know*, and she will not cry while saying *I know*, which will be the real victory.

She checks the phone. Fourteen percent.

She does not get up for the charger.

She is thinking, in the abstract, about how she will not cry, and there is something almost pleasant about this, a kind of controlled preview of the anticipated loss, a way of owning it, and—

Maya's voice from one morning in August, saying her name in the specific way Maya had said it that particular morning, which was: *quietly*, which was *like it meant something*, which was in the context of Maya lifting her wrist and holding it for a moment, not kissing it, just holding it the way you hold something you want to remember holding, and she had said *what*, and Maya had said *nothing, just*, and hadn't finished.

She pushes this down.

She pushes it with the specific effort of someone who has learned that the pushing itself is a workout, that it will leave her tired, and this is fine.

She pushes it and it stays pushed.

---

Seven-ten. The knock is exactly Maya, which is to say it is two beats and then a third, like a signature, like something she knows without having catalogued when she catalogued it. She opens the door.

Maya in the actual doorway, not the scene's doorway. Maya in the real coat, the brown one. Her hair doing the thing it does in cold air. Her face doing something that is immediately, specifically, *her* — the slight asymmetry of her smile, the way her eyes are already in the middle of saying something her mouth hasn't started yet.

*Hi.* Maya says this and steps forward and hugs her, and the hug is the kind where you can feel the person's decision to be there, the weight of arrival, and she feels something release in her chest, something she had been holding at a tension she had stopped noticing, and she breathes out and—

Maya pulls back and holds her by both arms, looks at her face. *I missed you,* she says. *I kept thinking about you this week.* She means this in the way bodies mean things, in her hands on both arms and the particular directness of looking. *Come on. I'm starving.*

She feels it arrive. She feels it go to the center, which is what real things do. She needs it. She almost reaches for it with both hands, the way you reach for water—

*She's saying that because she's about to—*

—and she is already drinking it and already converting it, already placing it in the ledger, already thinking: *is this the warm thing before the thing*, *is this what you do when you've already decided*, *is this the last Tuesday with you, do you know*, because the interpretive system does not require new data, the interpretive system was already running when Maya knocked, and the knock was translated before she got to the door.

She smiles. She says: *I'm glad you're here.*

She means it. Both things are true.

---

She's in the bathroom. They've eaten. Maya is in the other room, a laugh-track from her phone, the sound of someone happy in an adjacent space. She ran out with the excuse of water and went to the bathroom instead.

She looks at herself in the mirror.

*I'm doing the thing again.*

She says it to her own face, which looks tired, which looks like someone who has been somewhere difficult. She says it with the relief of diagnosis, the click of a thing named.

*I'm doing the thing*, she thinks, *which means I can see myself doing it, which means I'm being critical and clear, which means I'm not just catastrophizing, which means when I identify real evidence it's because I'm seeing correctly, which means the Tuesday gap is real, which means—*

She turns on the water so Maya can't hear her standing there in silence.

The phone is on the edge of the sink where she set it after checking, as a reflex, when she came in. Nine percent.

She could ask Maya to charge it. Maya has the same cable. She could go back out with the phone and say *mine's dying* and it would be easy, it would be five seconds, it would be nothing. Maya would say *of course* and plug it in with the charger she keeps in her bag, the charger she always has, and then there would be signal, and if she needed to reach Maya later she could, and there would be no cause for—

She leaves the phone on the edge of the sink.

She goes back out. Maya pats the couch beside her without looking up, already pointing at the screen to show her something, and she sits.

---

At nine-forty-five Maya puts on her coat. Tuesday, work early, the familiar liturgy of endings. At the door she turns back, and there is something in the turning back that is hers, compositionally, the particular angle of return. She touches her face, briefly, two fingers against her cheek.

*You good?* Maya asks.

*Yeah,* she says. *Really good.*

Maya looks at her another moment. Not like she's checking; like she's staying an extra second because she wants to. *Okay,* she says. *Text me when you're up.*

And she's gone.

---

The apartment has the specific quality of a place recently held by someone who has said *I missed you* and meant it. She can smell Maya's coat, a little, or thinks she can. The couch is warm where Maya was.

She sits.

Her phone is at four percent and dark, and she turns it over in her hands. She could get the charger from the kitchen table. She sets the phone face-down on the cushion.

She is thinking: *it was a good evening.* This thought arrives and is true and she does not argue with it.

She is thinking: *she touched my face at the door.* This is true. This happened. This was specifically Maya, choosing to do a specific thing.

She is thinking: *I kept thinking about you this week.* Maya's voice in the room, which is the room the actual Maya just left, and the words were in the room too, were real sound waves, were real—

*You say that when you've decided,* the other thought says. *You're generous when you've decided. I know how this goes.*

Both thoughts hold the room with equal weight.

She sits with them. She does not move to charge the phone. She does not move to sleep. The dead phone in her hand and the warm couch and the smell that's almost Maya and the Tuesday gap and the hug at the door and scene-Maya in the coffee shop and real Maya turning back, real Maya's two fingers, real Maya's *text me when you're up* — she holds all of it without knowing which is the story she's living in.

In her mind, tomorrow is already beginning to take shape.
