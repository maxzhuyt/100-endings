# Lost & Found in Osaka

She'd been looking at the same Korg module for eleven minutes.

She knew it was eleven minutes because she'd checked her phone at the start, some anxious reflex, and the timestamp was right there every time she glanced at the display to avoid making eye contact with the shop owner. The module wasn't even what she needed. She had the specs memorized—filter range, patch points, the particular warmth of its oscillator in the low-mid frequencies—and she'd had them memorized for six weeks. She was standing in front of it because it was a place to stand.

Den Den Town was always like this: too loud, too bright, the smell of old plastic and new circuitry and someone's takoyaki drifting over from three stalls down. But she'd take it over school hallways any day. Here she could be invisible in a useful way. An eccentric, a hobbyist, a girl with strong opinions about voltage-controlled filters. Nobody expected conversation. Nobody expected anything from her at all, which was the arrangement she preferred.

Then the guitar started.

It came through the shop entrance like something with opinions of its own—cutting across the ambient beeping, the half-powered hum of demo units cycling through preset sounds, the low refrigerator-drone of a synthesizer nobody had turned off. She felt it before she identified it: a particular *bite* in the tone, the sustain, the way the player sat back on the beat like they owned the tempo and the tempo owed them money. She looked up from the Korg. She took three steps toward the door without deciding to.

Outside, near the arcade entrance, a girl about her age—seventeen, maybe eighteen—was playing a white Stratocaster through a battery amp the size of a lunchbox, working through the opening theme from *Neon Genesis Evangelion*. Not slowed down. Not simplified. *Shredded*—running the melody through the upper register with the kind of controlled aggression that meant years of practice and something beyond practice, something that practice could inform but not manufacture. She had ink-dark hair pulled half up and she was wearing a jacket covered in patches, and she played with her eyes partly closed, which was either very cool or very confident or, infuriatingly and probably, both.

A small crowd had gathered the way small crowds do—the people who were already stopped, plus the people who stopped because others had stopped, plus two children who were there solely for the amp, pointing at it like a discovery. She threaded through the outer edge and stayed there.

She told herself she was evaluating the playing technically. She was cataloguing the harmonic implications, the arrangement choices, the decision to voice the melody up an octave in the third phrase. She was doing exactly what she did with every piece of music she encountered, which was to take it apart in her head the way she took apart synthesizer patches—carefully, with respect, to understand what was happening and why.

She was not doing that at all. She was just listening. She hadn't just listened to anything in a long time.

The song ended. A few people clapped. The player opened her eyes—and immediately looked directly at her. Not at the crowd's center, not at the taller people in the front. Directly at her, as though she'd known exactly where she was standing. Both eyebrows went up, a quarter-inch, a question.

She thought about leaving. She calculated the geometry of it: turn, three steps, gone, no one's feelings hurt, no record of this. But the girl had already seen her watching. Leaving now would be its own visible thing—a statement, a flinch—and somehow that was worse. She took two steps forward. Her bag strap dug into her shoulder. She was very aware of her hands.

"That was—um." She stopped. Looked briefly at the pavement. Started over. "The way you voiced the main melody in the second phrase. Most people play it straight but you were doing this thing with—sorry, that's a weird thing to lead with—"

The busker tilted her head. Something in her expression settled, like a question being answered before it was asked. "No. Keep going."

"You were implying the harmony underneath without actually playing it. The negative space. Like the melody was an outline of the chord instead of—separate from it. It made the whole progression feel heavier. Fuller." She stopped. "I don't know if that was intentional."

The busker was quiet for a moment, looking at her in a way that was not unkind. Then she grinned—a sudden thing, like a light going on in a room. "Nobody has ever said that to me. And I have been playing that song for three years." She shifted the guitar to her left hand and stuck out her right. "Mia."

She shook it. "Sora."

"You play guitar?"

"No. Synthesizers, mostly. And I—" She hesitated. "I listen. I listen a lot."

"I can tell." Mia crouched to wind the cable from her signal pedal, moving with the easy efficiency of someone who'd done it a thousand times. "Synthesizers like ambient stuff, or—"

"Everything. I started with ambient but mostly soundtracks now. Film scores. Anime. I've been deconstructing the *Evangelion* OST for almost a year, which is probably part of why I recognized the—" She heard herself and stopped. "This is probably not interesting."

"Sora." Mia looked up at her from where she was crouched. Her expression was patient in a way that wasn't performance. "I just played *Cruel Angel's Thesis* on a street corner for coins. I am genuinely in no position to judge."

Something in her chest unknotted, slightly, like a thread pulled loose. "Fair."

"You know Sagisu's other work? Shiro Sagisu?"

"Obviously." The word came out before she could moderate it. "He's—*Bleach* is criminally underrated and the work he did scoring the *Berserk* films is—"

"*Is phenomenal, yes*—" Mia stood up fast, pointing at her, guitar dangling from her other hand at an angle that should have been precarious but wasn't. "I have this exact argument constantly and no one knows what I'm talking about—"

"The way he constructs those orchestral textures and then cuts to completely sparse piano with no warning, it shouldn't work as well as it—"

"The *restraint*, right—"

"Yes—"

"Most composers would fill that space—"

"—and it would be fine, it would be totally competent, but he just—*doesn't*—"

They both stopped. Mia was smiling in a way that reached the corners of her eyes. Sora became aware, in a delayed and slightly distant way, that she had been speaking in complete sentences for nearly two minutes without reviewing them first. Without reviewing them at all.

"Okay," Mia said, with the tone of someone reaching a conclusion. "I'm getting sushi. Come with me."

Sora blinked. "You're—what?"

"Kaitenzushi, two blocks up from here. You can keep arguing with me about the *Berserk* films while we eat."

"I wasn't arguing. I was agreeing with you."

"You were agreeing with me *and* implying I hadn't gone far enough, which is a very specific kind of argument." Mia slung her guitar case over one shoulder and picked up the amp by its handle. It was genuinely the size of a lunchbox; it fit in her hand like one. "Come on."

"I—" Sora started. She was already walking.

---

The restaurant was small and warm and smelled like vinegared rice and good dashi stock, with just enough crowd noise to make silence between two people feel natural rather than pointed. They took stools at the counter. The conveyor belt moved past them with its slow, democratic procession—salmon, tamago, a roll with four distinct toppings stacked in a way that seemed structurally optimistic, cucumber rolls, more salmon, a small bowl of miso that revolved past twice before someone took it.

"You're here alone?" Mia asked, plucking a plate of fatty tuna from the belt with the decisive speed of someone who knew what she wanted.

"I come most months." Sora watched a plate of salmon roe approach and then pass. She let it go. "Browsing, usually. I save for one component at a time. It's—it takes a while, but I like having a reason to come."

"Do you actually make things with them? The synthesizers? Or is it more of a—" Mia gestured loosely with her chopsticks.

"Collecting thing." Sora looked at her. "Both. But mostly making. I've got a few pieces I've—" She paused. "I work on things regularly. It's how I spend most of my time outside school."

"Do you play them for people?"

The salmon roe came around again. She took it this time, for something to do with her hands. "No."

"How come?"

She thought about it genuinely, rather than reaching for the deflection she kept near the surface for exactly this question. The restaurant hummed around them. A plate of shrimp went by.

"Because when I play something for myself, it sounds like what I meant it to sound like. What I was trying to say." She set her chopsticks down. "I'm afraid that if I played it for someone else it would just sound like—texture. Background. Noise that nearly amounts to something."

Mia was quiet for a moment, chewing. "What if it sounds like what you meant *and* also like noise," she said, "and that's just—fine? What if those two things aren't in competition?"

"That's an easy thing to say."

"Completely agree." Mia didn't sound defensive. She reached past Sora for a plate of tamago. "I still mean it. Could I hear something sometime?"

Sora looked at the belt. She watched a plate of yellowtail move past, then another plate of salmon. She reached for the salmon.

"Maybe," she said.

It was closer to *yes* than she'd said to anything in longer than she could easily remember. She noticed that, and didn't say it, and Mia didn't ask her to.

"Good enough," Mia said, and bumped her shoulder lightly with her own, and turned back to the conveyor to see what was coming next.

Outside, through the restaurant's fogged window, the September light had turned that particular amber that Osaka did in the early evening—low and warm and slightly unreal, the kind of light that made the familiar look provisional, like a version of a place rather than the place itself. Den Den Town hummed on behind it. She could hear it from here: the beeping, the hum, the half-powered machines cycling through their demonstrations for no one.

She thought she might come back next month. She thought she might have someone to come back with.
