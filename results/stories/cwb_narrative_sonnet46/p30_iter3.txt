# Útiseta

I should tell you that I'm not superstitious. I want that on record, for whatever record this is. I grew up in Stockholm, in a third-floor flat with a father who read engineering manuals for pleasure and a mother who taught middle-school biology and called homeopathy a civic embarrassment. I didn't believe in anything that didn't leave evidence.

I still don't, mostly.

It was Astrid who found me first, which I've thought about since. She was waiting at the bus stop the morning after we arrived, watching me carry boxes from the removal van with the particular patience of someone who has decided something and is simply waiting for events to confirm it. She had the kind of face that northern villages produce—broad, pale, economical with expression—and she was looking at me the way you look at a problem you've already begun to solve.

"You'll need a friend here," she said. Not *do you want* or *I could be*. Just the flat assessment.

She was right. Björkhagen had twelve hundred people and no cinema and a school where everyone had known everyone since infancy, and I was from nowhere they recognized. The other girls had a social architecture so load-bearing and so old that inserting yourself into it meant dismantling something someone else was standing on, and nobody was willing, and I understood that. I didn't blame them. I just ate my lunch beside the window and watched the pine forest at the edge of the playing fields and felt the particular loneliness of being visible to people who have collectively decided not to see you.

So when Astrid offered friendship, I accepted the terms without reading them. That's what loneliness does. It makes you careless with agreements.

---

The midnight games started in September. Small things at first—leaving a red thread on a classmate's doorstep, filling a jar with pins and river water and burying it near the girl who'd been spreading stories about Astrid's cousin. Whether any of it worked was impossible to say. Bad luck happens to everyone eventually, and Astrid was patient about attribution. She wasn't trying to impress me, which was itself impressive. She was simply continuing a practice that had been there before I arrived and would be there after I left, and she was generous enough to let me participate.

The notebooks were her grandmother's. Astrid brought them out sometimes on the kitchen table, handling them with a care that wasn't reverence exactly—more like precision, the way you handle something whose damage would have permanent consequences. They were written in a hand that grew more compressed toward the end of each entry, as though the writer had been unexpectedly overtaken by urgency. Astrid read them with the brisk efficiency of someone checking a recipe and finding it confirmed what they already knew.

"My grandmother learned from her mother," Astrid said once, not looking up. "And she from hers. The women in my family have always known this valley."

I asked what that meant. She turned a page.

"It means we know what it is," she said. "We know what it wants to do with itself. We know which parts of it are listening."

In October she told me about útiseta.

---

We were in her kitchen, the good kind of Swedish dark outside, the wind doing its pine-tree thing against the window. Her grandmother's notebook lay open between us to a page dense with writing and small diagrams I couldn't quite parse, though I could tell they were diagrams of something spatial—positions, distances, directions.

"Sitting-out," Astrid said. "Old practice. You go to a liminal place at midnight. A burial mound is best—somewhere between states, somewhere that's been used for passage. You sit until the hour ends, and if you do it correctly, knowledge comes to you."

"Knowledge of what?"

"Whatever you need. My grandmother did it twice." She paused, reading. "The first time she went alone. The second time she brought her sister." She looked up. "I've been waiting to have someone to bring."

I understood I was being asked something, and I understood I should feel chosen. I did feel chosen. That's the thing I've had to sit with since—that the flattery was real even if it was also a mechanism. Astrid genuinely wanted company. That's not in question. What I've thought about since is whether the wanting of company and the needing of a second person for the ritual were, for Astrid, distinguishable things.

"What do we do?" I said.

She turned the notebook toward me and began to explain.

No iron. No cross. No borrowed clothes—wear things that know you, that have some of your presence worked into the fabric. Leave your phone. Bring nothing with a battery, nothing that mediates. A small candle, unlit—she tapped the page, specific about this, the candle was not for lighting, she didn't explain further. A handful of soil from your own threshold. I had to think for a moment about what my threshold was in a house where I'd lived for six weeks, and I must have paused too long because Astrid said, quietly, "The place you cross to come and go. It knows you by now."

She ran through the prohibitions with the same tone she used to read back her chemistry homework: flat, thorough, already in command of the material.

You must remain still once positioned. You must not speak unless something speaks to you first, and if something speaks, you answer honestly. You must not stand up. You must not leave before the hour ends—

"Regardless," she said.

"Regardless of what?"

She looked at me steadily. "Regardless."

Then she said we should get some sleep, and that we'd leave at eleven-thirty on Saturday, and that I should eat beforehand because it helped to be grounded, and she offered me a biscuit from the tin on the counter with the same manner she'd been using throughout, which was the manner of a person organizing a hiking trip. Practical. Slightly impatient to get on with it.

I took the biscuit. I said I'd be there.

In retrospect, the moment I said yes was not when I agreed to the ritual. The moment I agreed to the ritual was weeks earlier, when I chose Astrid's company over the loneliness that was the alternative. Everything after that was already in motion.

---

We left her house at eleven-forty. I had worn my oldest jeans and a sweater I'd had since I was fifteen, soft from washing and shaped to me in the way things get after years of contact, and I'd put the soil in my coat pocket in a small cloth bag she'd given me, and my phone was charging on my desk at home, and I felt its absence like a missing tooth—present in the space where it wasn't. Astrid wore dark wool and moved quietly, as though she'd done this before, which she hadn't, but which her grandmother had and perhaps that counted for something in her body.

We didn't use torches. She'd been clear about this. You need to arrive with your eyes already adjusted to the dark; you need to have let the night work on you for the full walk, so that by the time you reach the mound you are continuous with the darkness rather than a bright thing that has just had the brightness removed.

This is accurate, as it happens. There's a difference between darkness your eyes have negotiated and darkness you've been suddenly abandoned to, and I noticed it—the way shapes became available gradually, the way the path through the forest asserted itself in increments, the pale thread of it through the undergrowth.

The cold was immediate and serious. October in that part of Sweden is not performing cold; it is cold without apology or end, and the forest holds it differently than open ground, conserves it, keeps it in pockets you walk into and through. My breath came out in front of me and dispersed. I watched it.

The pines were loud. I'd forgotten how loud they are at night when the wind moves through them—it's not the sound of wind in leaves, which is gentle and variable, but something passed through and sharpened and continuous, the consonantal quality of it, the way it almost resolves into something. I told myself this. I knew what wind sounded like. I told myself this several times over the next twenty minutes with lessening conviction each time, because the telling had to do more and more work, and the work showed.

Astrid walked just ahead of me, not fast. She was not anxious. This was, I realized, its own kind of information: that she was apprehensive would have reassured me, because apprehension is a response to danger that assumes the danger is contingent, might not materialize, can still fail to arrive. She was not apprehensive. She was attentive in the way of someone performing a task that requires care. The confidence was not the confidence of someone who thought nothing would happen. It was the confidence of someone prepared for what would.

I felt the mound before I saw it clearly—a shift in the quality of the dark, a long low shape that the treeline opened to reveal with the feeling of something disclosed rather than encountered. It was lower than I'd expected. I don't know what I'd expected. Something taller, more assertively present. This was modest and very old, overgrown, patient in the way old earthworks are patient, which is to say without impatience or any other quality I could name, which is to say I was already attributing qualities to a hill before we'd sat down, and I noticed this about myself and it didn't help.

The grass on top of it was dead and lay flat. The air above it was still while the air around us was not.

Astrid stopped at the base and stood for a moment with her eyes closed. Then she walked up the low slope and I followed, and she positioned us: facing north, cross-legged, perhaps two meters apart. She said something before she settled—not to me, and not, I thought, to herself—something short and low that I didn't catch the words of, and then she arranged herself and was still.

The cold came up through the ground immediately. It has texture, that kind of cold—it finds the gaps between things, the places your clothing doesn't cover continuously, and it occupies them with a thoroughness that feels deliberate. I focused on this. The cold was real. The cold was explicable and I could think about it and thinking about it gave me somewhere to put my attention.

I thought about the cold for approximately ten minutes.

Then I ran out of elaborations and I was just there, on the mound, in the dark, in October, waiting I didn't know what.

---

The sounds came in layers.

First the wind in the trees—I knew this. I knew what this was. The near-consonants, the gathering and dispersal of sound as the pressure changed, I'd been listening to it for twenty minutes on the walk and I had a working account of it. Further: two owls calling out of phase with each other, one closer, one somewhere to the east, repetitive and unconcerned with us. Further still: something small moving through the undergrowth at the tree line, periodic, unhurried. I catalogued these. I found them reassuring in the way that specificity is reassuring, the way that naming keeps things in their proper dimensions.

The things I found harder to catalogue came later.

Maybe thirty minutes in—I had no way to check the time, couldn't check it, didn't have my phone—Astrid's posture changed. Not dramatically. She'd been still with the stillness of someone who has chosen stillness, and then she was still with a different quality, the quality of someone who has stopped choosing and simply is, who has been overtaken by something that happens to manifest as motionlessness. It was the difference between a person sleeping and a person pretending to sleep, and it frightened me more than it should have, more than I could justify.

I watched her from the side. Her face was tipped slightly upward. Her hands were open on her knees.

Then the wind changed.

I know what I heard and I know what wind sounds like and I know that these two statements are in tension with each other and I have been in that tension for years now. What I can say is that what I heard was not dispersing—it was gathering. It did not pass through. It arrived and remained and it seemed to be directed, which is not a quality wind has, which is why I'm still in this particular difficulty. It gathered and it turned toward us, but it turned toward me specifically, or it seemed to—the distinction between seeming and being is precisely what I can't get purchase on here, what I've never been able to get purchase on—and when it turned there was a quality to it that my body registered before my mind could argue with it, which was the quality of attention.

I did not stand up. I had agreed not to.

I sat and the attention continued and I want to be accurate about what this was like: it was not frightening in the way sudden things are frightening, the galvanic shock of it. It was frightening the way being watched for a long time is frightening, the slow understanding that you have been observed without knowing it and that the observation has produced a conclusion you cannot see. I felt exposed in a way that had nothing to do with the dark or the cold or the open ground. I felt looked at from the inside of something.

And I listened.

I don't know what I was listening for. I fulfilled the terms. I sat still and didn't speak, and whatever was happening—and I can say *whatever was happening* and mean it without irony, I genuinely don't know—I let it happen, because I'd agreed to let it happen, because I'd agreed to the terms of the hour without knowing what the hour would contain, and because Astrid was two meters away and she had not moved and she was not afraid and her grandmother had done this twice and come home both times.

The attention peaked—that's the word, it peaked—and then it began to recede, and I had the distinct sense of something that had come close, examined, and withdrawn, which I know is anthropomorphizing and I know is not a reliable account of anything and I know is what I felt.

Astrid exhaled.

She turned to look at me and her eyes caught what little light there was, the faint ambient light of a deep Swedish October sky. She was not surprised. She was not afraid. She had the face of someone who has received a report that confirms an expectation, and she was looking at me with something I can only call care, which was itself clarifying and terrifying in equal measure—the thought that she had brought me here knowing this might happen to me, and that the bringing was an act of affection.

We did not speak. We sat for some time longer, though the active quality of the air had changed, had quieted, and then Astrid said—very softly, conversationally, as though I'd asked a question she was finishing answering—"It's done. We can go."

---

At the tree line she stopped and turned to face the mound once more. She said the same brief thing she'd said at the beginning, and I didn't ask what it was, and then she turned back to the forest and we walked.

I was very cold. My hands were not working properly. I focused on walking, on placing my feet on the path, on the owls that were still calling somewhere in the trees, still unconcerned and repetitive. The forest was the same forest it had been. The path home was the same path. I counted these facts.

After a while she said, "It acknowledged you."

I waited.

"What does that mean?" I said.

She considered this with real care. I saw it in the pause, the particular quality of the pause, the sense of someone genuinely weighing what they know against what they can responsibly say.

"I'm not entirely sure," she said. "My grandmother's notes describe it differently each time. I think it depends on what you brought. Not what you carried." She glanced at me. "What you were, when you sat down there."

"I didn't bring anything," I said. "I didn't intend anything. I didn't know what to intend."

"I know," she said. And then: "That might be why."

She didn't elaborate. I didn't ask her to. We walked the rest of the way home in the particular silence of two people who have been through something together that they don't yet have language for, which is a specific silence, weighted and companionable and frightening in its own way—comforting, almost, the way shared incomprehension is comforting, until you remember that you are both equally lost.

At my door she said: "Sleep. Don't think about it tonight. Let it settle."

She said this the way her grandmother's notebooks were written—with the confidence of prior knowledge, of transmission. Don't think about it tonight. Like she'd been told the same thing, once, and had found it good advice.

---
