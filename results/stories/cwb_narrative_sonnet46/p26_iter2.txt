# Offworlder

The cloak was a mistake. Yaren had known it before she'd gone three steps from the delegation quarters, had felt the knowledge settle into her shoulders like the weight of the fabric itself, but by then the streets had already opened around her and she could not think clearly enough to go back.

The market ran along what had once been, the briefings said, a transit corridor—a tube for moving bodies between work sectors. Now it was roofed with translucent panels through which the local star arrived diminished and silver, and below that light the stalls ran in crooked rows, adapting to the original tunnel's curve. The smell reached her before anything else: something fermented and green, then cooking fat, then a cleaner smell she could not name, mineral and faint, which she would later understand was the smell of the recycled air itself, distinct from pressurized station air in a way she would not have been able to articulate.

She walked.

The briefings had used the word *subspecies* with a precision that had seemed, at the time, clarifying. Here it did not clarify anything. The people around her were people—were argumentative and hurried and bored and curious—and when she noticed the ways they diverged from the baseline template she had carried her whole life, she noticed it the way you notice anything real: partially, in pieces, out of order.

A woman negotiating loudly over a bin of preserved something, her hands moving in rapid emphasis. Her fingers were longer than Yaren would have expected. Not grotesquely—just longer, the proportions slightly reweighted, and when she gestured, the gesture had a reach and a delicacy that the same gesture in baseline hands would not have had. Yaren watched the hands and then felt she had been rude and looked away.

Two men carrying a long panel between them, navigating the crowd with the particular cooperative silence of people who had done this many times. Both were built narrowly—narrow through the chest, deep through the torso, a proportion the briefings had mentioned and attributed to something about oxygen efficiency, though Yaren could not remember the details. They moved through the crowd with a patience that was not passivity. The crowd made room. It was not deference. It was a different thing, some practiced mutual adjustment she did not have a word for.

A child, turning from a stall, collided with Yaren directly.

The child's eyes were what she noticed first—not in the moment of collision, which was brief and ordinary, but immediately after, when they both stopped and the child looked up. Wide pupils. Not alarming. Just wide, taking in more of the silver light in a way that seemed entirely natural to the face around them. The child was perhaps eight, perhaps ten, and stared at Yaren with the frank assessment that children extend to anything unusual.

Then the child reached out and touched the cloak.

Not grabbed. Just touched the hem with two fingers, with the gesture of someone checking a texture, solving a question. The fabric was a deep blue—mission blue, technically—woven to a density and warmth that had been appropriate for the transport vessel and was not appropriate for here, where the temperature was maintained with a consistency that made heavy clothing not just unnecessary but conspicuous. Yaren understood this better now than she had when she'd put the cloak on. She had put it on because it was familiar and because departure in a new place is frightening and because no one in the delegation quarters had told her not to.

The child said something. Yaren's translation implant processed it with a half-second delay: *Is that for ceremony?*

"Not today," Yaren said. The implant would convert this, she knew, into something locally idiomatic, though she did not know what. She did not know what *today* would sound like when rendered in the colonial Creole that had evolved here over the hundred and twelve years since the second generation ships.

The child let go of the hem and slipped back into the crowd.

Yaren stood in the market for another twenty minutes before she went back, and in those twenty minutes she tried to notice things in order to understand them and did not much succeed, but she noticed them.

---

The message was waiting, not a superior in person—she registered this as small mercy and then recognized the registration as its own kind of information about what she'd been expecting.

*Environmental Protocol B applies to all delegation personnel outside sanctioned zones. Your movement was logged leaving Corridor 7 at 1430. The market district is not sanctioned. Please confirm receipt and availability for debrief.*

She confirmed receipt.

The debrief was conducted by Liaison Ostrove, who was not her superior in rank but who had been with the delegation for three years and spoke with the authority of institutional time. He sat across from her at the narrow table in the common room and did not raise his voice. His voice in its un-raised state was already doing something that raised voices could not have done. It was being careful with her.

"It's not a safety question," he said, though it was positioned where a safety question would have been. "Or not only. We have working relationships here. Those relationships are calibrated. When a delegation member moves through the colony unaccompanied, unregistered, in mission dress—" He did not finish this. He let the unfinished sentence do the thing he wanted it to do, which was to make her complete it in terms that implicated her.

"I wasn't in mission dress," Yaren said. "I had the cloak."

"The cloak is mission dress."

She had not known this. She said: "I didn't know that."

"That it would be read that way?"

"That it would be read at all."

Ostrove looked at her for a moment with an expression she could not fully decode, which was itself interesting, because she could usually decode Ostrove. "They know what we wear," he said. "They know what we do. They have been watching delegations come and go for sixty years." He folded his hands on the table. "The Minister of Science has requested to meet with you specifically. This afternoon."

Yaren waited.

"I am inclined," Ostrove said, "to read this as connected to your walk. Whether that's good or bad depends on things I don't know yet." He paused again. "You're to go. What Command wants from this meeting is information about the succession projections and whether the Science Ministry is positioned to influence them. What the Minister will want is something I'd like you to figure out before you give her anything."

He slid a folder across the table. She did not pick it up.

"I've read the briefings," she said.

"Read them again," he said, not unkindly. "They'll look different now."

---

The Ministry of Science occupied a building that had started as something else—they all had, here, where the original infrastructure had been built for a smaller population with different assumptions and everything since had been accommodation and invention. The corridor to the Minister's anteroom was high-ceilinged and cool, and the walls were covered not with decorative work but with what appeared to be working materials—charts, diagrams, a long sequence of numbers with handwritten notation. Yaren walked slowly enough to read some of this and could not. It was not the language. It was the field of reference.

She waited in the anteroom with a low seat and a view of a courtyard through glass that had a faint green tint. In the courtyard, two people were doing something with a large device she did not recognize. They worked with concentration, unhurried.

She thought about what she was walking into.

The Minister—her name was Hassun, Ciara Hassun, forty-four years in the Science Ministry, currently its head—was, the briefings said, a scientist by training, an administrator by practice, and a political figure by necessity. *By necessity* was Yaren's addition. The briefings said *by design*, which told her something about how Command understood the relationship between knowledge and power here, and how that understanding might be wrong.

She thought about the child's hand on the hem of the cloak.

*Is that for ceremony?*

The question had not been hostile. It had been genuinely curious. Yaren had been, in that moment, a thing worth asking about—not a representative of the home-worlds, not a diplomatic instrument, not a potential security incident logged at 1430 in Corridor 7. Just a person in an unusual garment who might, if asked directly, explain herself.

She had not explained herself. She'd said *not today* and let the implant smooth it over.

She thought about what she had actually wanted to say. She wasn't sure. Something truer, probably. Something that would have required more language than she had in the colonial Creole and more understanding than she had of what was safe to offer.

The door opened.

---

The Minister was shorter than her official images suggested. This was not unusual—official images were taken with certain intentions and height was one of the variables they manipulated—but it was still a brief recalibration. She had the narrow-deep build Yaren had seen on the men in the market, less pronounced but present, and she wore a long wrap in a grey that managed to be neither official nor casual. Her face was attentive in a way that was native to her, Yaren thought. Not performed. She had the face of someone for whom attention was a form of pleasure.

"Envoy Yaren," she said, in standard, without an accent that Yaren could precisely locate. "I'm glad you came."

"Thank you for the invitation, Minister."

The office was not large. It had a window that looked out on a different courtyard from the anteroom's, this one with something growing up the walls—not decorative planting, or not only, but something that seemed to be part of the wall's function, something thick-leaved and slow. They sat in chairs angled toward a low table on which there was tea, already poured, and a small plate of something Yaren did not recognize.

"Try one," the Minister said. "They're made with a grain we developed here. Took about four generations to stabilize. They don't export well—something in the transport conditions—so you won't have had them."

Yaren took one. It was dense and slightly sweet and had a quality she couldn't name, something in the texture that required a different attention than she usually brought to eating.

"It's good," she said, meaning it.

"Most visitors are surprised at that." Hassun poured from the second vessel into her own cup. "I think they expect it to be difficult. To eat here, to be comfortable here." She held the cup without drinking from it. "How was the market?"

A silence, but a brief one.

Yaren said: "More organized than I expected."

"In what sense?"

"The official materials made it sound—" She stopped. Started again. "There was a quality of mutual arrangement. People moving together through a shared space in a way that seemed practiced. Not crowded, even where it was crowded." She paused. "I couldn't tell if that was culture or smaller-scale than I was used to or something else."

Hassun was watching her with the pleasure-attention again. "What else did you see?"

"Adaptation," Yaren said cautiously. "Differences. The briefings described them taxonomically. Being there was different from reading descriptions."

"Different how?"

Yaren thought about the hands of the woman negotiating over the bins. "The differences were—inside other things. Inside people doing ordinary tasks. You don't see the adaptation. You see a person and then you notice, as a secondary thing, that they are adapted. The briefings made it sound the other way around."

"Yes," the Minister said. "That's the central problem with your briefings." She said this without emphasis but also without apology. "They were written by people who visited here and saw adaptation. We live here and see people." She finally drank from her cup. "This is not a complaint. It's more that—the difference in perspective has consequences. For how your delegations interact with us. For what your Command understands about what we are."

"What are you?" Yaren asked, and heard immediately how it sounded, and did not try to take it back because it had been honest.

The Minister smiled—surprised, Yaren thought, into genuine warmth. "A civilization," she said. "But not one that plots against your home-worlds, if that's what's behind the question."

"That wasn't the question behind the question."

"No? What was?"

Yaren considered. "I think I was asking what you think you are. As opposed to what we think you are."

Hassun set down her cup. She looked at Yaren for a moment with an expression that was not readable, that was doing several things at once, none of which Yaren could fully account for. Then she said: "You walked through the market alone this afternoon in mission dress, which our people would have noticed and which your delegation would have logged and for which you've presumably been spoken to." She paused. "How much trouble are you willing to be in?"

Yaren did not know the right answer to this. She suspected that the right answer was also not the safe answer, and that the Minister knew this about the question she was asking, and that the Minister was waiting to see which answer Yaren would choose.

"I don't know what trouble looks like here yet," Yaren said. "I've been here less than two days."

"That's candid."

"I'm trying to be."

Hassun nodded slowly, as if this were data rather than a social nicety. She was silent for a moment. "Tomorrow afternoon, there is a review of a long-range atmospheric project—forty years into a hundred-year model. A number of the community's citizens will be present, including several who are not scientists, as a form of civic integration. It is not a sanctioned event for delegation personnel." She paused. "I am not officially inviting you. I am telling you that it exists and that I will be there, and that the man who stands near the door in the grey coat tends to look the other way more reliable than you might expect."

Yaren held this.

She could not determine if it was an offer, a warning, or a test. She could not determine if the Minister's warmth was native or strategic or both simultaneously, which were not, she was beginning to understand, mutually exclusive here in the way they might have been at home. She could not determine what the Minister had made of her—whether she had passed something or failed something or whether the Minister's category for her was more interesting and less binary than either.

What she knew was this: the child had touched the hem of the cloak and asked if it was for ceremony. The question had an answer she had not given. She was not yet sure what the answer was, but she thought she might find it somewhere in the gap between what the briefings said this colony was and what it was actually becoming, in the dark under silver light, in real time, at a pace that the home-worlds were not tracking carefully enough to name.

The tea cooled in her cup. Outside, the thick-leaved thing moved slightly against the wall—a breath of circulated air, nothing more. The Minister waited with the patience of someone accustomed to waiting for things that take a long time.

Yaren said: "What time does it start?"
