# Polymerase Chain Reaction

The thermocycler was lying about its temperature again.

Sable could tell by the smell—a faint scorched-plastic undertone beneath the ethanol and the mildew that lived in the walls of the converted shipping container she called a lab. The unit's display read 72°C, the extension phase, perfectly nominal, but 72°C didn't smell like that. 72°C smelled like nothing. She reached past the tangle of alligator-clipped wires connecting the thermocycler to the voltage regulator and tapped the casing. The display flickered. Dropped to 68. Corrected itself.

"There you are," she said.

Outside, the solar panel array on the container roof was doing what it always did in late afternoon: negotiating. The panels were salvaged from three different manufacturers across two decades, wired in a series that made electricians weep, and the regulator—a gutted UPS unit with its internals replaced by components from a 2031 Honda Civic's battery management system—converted their argument into something approximately usable. Approximately. The overhead strip light pulsed every forty seconds, a long slow breath. Sable no longer noticed it except in her peripheral vision, where it registered as something biological. A heartbeat. A tide.

She noted the temperature deviation in the log and ate four ibuprofen dry.

Her knuckles had been swollen for six weeks—a side effect of the joint-density modification she'd run on herself the previous winter, a practical decision made when the settlement's only external ladder had collapsed under her and she'd understood, mid-fall, that her hands were her most critical infrastructure. The collagen overexpression had worked. She could hang from a pipe by three fingers now without structural consequence. She also could not fully close her left hand in cold weather, and the ibuprofen was less for pain than for the low-grade inflammatory response that accompanied it, the body conducting an ongoing confused inquiry into what she'd done to it. There were other things. Her night vision was better than it had been, which she hadn't anticipated from that particular modification and still couldn't explain mechanistically. She had an intermittent sensitivity to barometric pressure that had twice given her advance warning of incoming storms, enough time to get the panels down before the wind. She wrote everything down. Negative results were still results. Unexpected results were their own category, which she was slowly populating.

She held her left hand under the light and opened it as far as it would go. She noted the range of motion in the log. She moved on.

The contract was on the table beside the centrifuge.

She hadn't looked at it directly in two hours. You didn't look at a thing you were going to do anyway because looking made you think about it, and thinking took time she didn't have because the samples were degrading and the window in which the amplified template would hold fidelity was not a metaphor—it was a number, and the number was getting smaller, and no amount of thinking about the contract would slow it.

This was the fundamental chemistry of the collapsed world. The agribusiness conglomerates had solved the seed-piracy problem with elegant brutality: they'd engineered terminator sequences into everything, crops that produced sterile offspring, forcing annual repurchase from the only entities licensed to issue the reactivation signal. Then the grid went, and the supply chains went, and the servers that held the reactivation keys went with them, and now every engineered cultivar on Earth was running down like a clock no one could wind. The wild types had been outcompeted into near-extinction over the preceding three decades, the old varieties squeezed out market by market until what remained was a generation of seeds away from nothing. Possibly less.

Sable's work was to steal time from that arithmetic.

She pulled the sample tube from the thermocycler and held it to the light. Inside, forty nanoliters of cloudy liquid: the amplified remnants of a tomato variety that had not grown in open soil in eleven years. She'd sourced the original template from a sealed vault sample, traded for it with three months of lab work for the Halverson settlement's medical officer—a thyroid hormone synthesis protocol, legally ambiguous in the way that legality had become a local and negotiable concept, binding roughly within a day's walk. The tomato's genome was a patchwork. Two proprietary sequences, one of which she'd partially reverse-engineered through comparative analysis and intuition and a great deal of failed inference, one of which she was working around with a flanking strategy that was theoretically sound and experimentally untested because she did not have the equipment to test theories before she needed their results. The splice she needed to complete would excise the terminator sequence entirely and replace it with a natural promoter region recovered from an heirloom database that had gone down in 2029 and been manually reconstructed from academic papers by a team in what used to be Manitoba. Restoring inheritance. Returning the tomato to time.

The contract wanted something different.

She knew the contractor as Orin, which was almost certainly not his name, and Orin represented a buyer in the lower valley settlements who wanted the tomato modified not for seed viability but for shelf life—extended post-harvest preservation through modified ethylene response, the kind of modification the agribusinesses had run routinely and that she could execute with the materials she had. They'd eat it but not grow it. They wanted a product with a longer window, not a crop with a future. The distinction had seemed abstract when he'd explained it and felt increasingly specific the longer she sat with it. The payment was a working autoclave and sixty liters of isopropyl alcohol, both of which would extend her lab's functional life by years and the settlement's medical capacity by more.

She thought about the autoclave the way she thought about her left hand—as a structure she needed to be able to rely on, and currently couldn't.

The strip light pulsed. The voltage regulator made its small clicking complaint.

There was also the question of what the buyer intended to do with a shelf-stable tomato that produced no viable seed, which was not a question Orin had invited and which she had not asked because she understood the etiquette of not asking and because she had been afraid of the answer. The lower valley settlements had resources. Resources implied leverage. Shelf-stable produce that only you controlled the supply of was a different kind of leverage than seed stock everyone could eventually propagate. She thought around this the way you thought around a bad tooth, approaching it and retreating, cataloging the shape of the absence without touching the center.

The sample was degrading.

She had perhaps ninety minutes before the amplified template lost fidelity beyond rescue. The edit had to begin now. She had the reagents for both procedures prepped and racked in order on the bench—she'd done this that morning, before the theoretical had solidified into the procedural, perhaps as a way of preserving the illusion of choice a little longer. She looked at both sets. She drew the syringe for Orin's modification. She drew the syringe for hers.

She set them on the bench.

The strip light breathed. Outside the container, someone dropped something metal, and the settlement's dog barked three times and went quiet.

The voltage dropped.

Not to zero. It never went to zero; it went to insufficient—the regulator's clicking becoming a stutter, the thermocycler display going to dashes, the strip light dimming to a brown that was almost nothing. In that dimness the two syringes were the same color, the same size, two small identical claims on the future. The capacitor buffer had maybe four seconds before it gave out entirely, and if the temperature hold went, the cycle collapsed, and the sample was lost, and the autoclave was lost, and the three months she'd spent working toward this particular combination of decisions was just time consumed by a world that consumed everything.

She injected.

Both. The syringes were small and her hands were steadier than they deserved to be and it was not a considered decision so much as a refusal to keep deferring one—a practical heresy, a splice of the splice, the two modifications layered into the same template in a combination she hadn't modeled because there had been only two options and this was not one of them. The reagents went in. She withdrew both needles. She did not know what she'd made.

The voltage recovered. The strip light came back. The thermocycler's display resolved out of dashes into numbers, the cycle resuming from where it had interrupted, the machine completing its work without knowing what it was completing.

She stood with the empty syringes and thought about what grew in the middle of things—between what a buyer wanted and what a settlement needed, between a product and a crop, between the genome she'd been hired to produce and the one she'd been working toward for months. Some recombinant compromise. A tomato that might store well and might produce viable seed and would more likely do neither cleanly, a thing optimized for a situation that didn't exist yet, for a negotiation still in progress between the world that had ended and whatever was organizing itself in the ruins. Which was, she thought, not entirely a failure condition. She had written that down somewhere. She couldn't remember where.

She labeled the tube. A question mark. A date. The settlement's name, abbreviated to three letters, because she'd made it in their name and she wanted it on record that she had.

The log entry took four minutes. She was precise about the parameters—temperatures, volumes, timing, the unexpected intervening variable of the voltage drop and its duration—because imprecision was how knowledge died and she was not willing to let knowledge die even when she wasn't sure what she knew. Especially then. She noted the dual-reagent introduction and flagged it for follow-up. She wrote *theoretical basis unclear, results pending* and felt the inadequacy of that and left it anyway, because inadequacy on the record was better than a clean story that was false.

Outside, the cook fires were starting. The smell of them came through the ventilation gap she'd cut in the container wall with an angle grinder in the fall—smoke, and beneath it something green, something someone had actually managed to grow in the experimental plots to the east of the main container block. She didn't know what it was. She made a note to ask.

She packaged the sample: a sealed tube in a foam-lined case, labeled, logged, chain of custody documented. She put Orin's name on the transfer form and then looked at the name for a moment and thought about what he would make of a result that only partially matched the specification, and what he would tell the buyer, and what the buyer would do with a shelf-stable tomato that might or might not produce seed that was or was not viable. She thought about the autoclave. She sealed the transfer case.

Then she began to clean the bench, because tomorrow there would be more samples, and they would also be degrading, and the work did not resolve into conclusions—it resolved into more work, which was a different thing and in some ways better, because conclusions closed and the work kept the question open, kept the window viable a little longer, kept the cycle running through another iteration toward a result that might, eventually, if the voltage held, mean something.

The strip light pulsed.

She noted it in the log.
