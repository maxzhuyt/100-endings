# Rehearsing Rejection

The zipper sticks at the hip, the way it always does, and she works it free with two fingers without looking down. Mirror check: lipstick even, hair pulled back the way she does it when she wants to seem unbothered. The shoulders are wrong. She turns away from that.

Her phone says 3:47. She said four. Twenty minutes on the train, which means leaving now, which means she is leaving now, coat already on, keys already in hand, moving through the apartment in the practiced way of someone who has decided not to think yet.

She decides not to think yet.

---

The text had come at 11:14 in the morning.

*Still on for today?*

She had written it at 9, the first time she woke up, and then again when she actually woke up, and had made herself wait until a reasonable hour, which she had decided was 9, which was apparently not reasonable, because two hours and fourteen minutes passed before the reply arrived and by then she had already moved through several stages of something she did not have a clean word for.

The reply, when it came, was: *Yes! Can't wait:)*

The colon-parenthesis smiley. Not the emoji. She uses the emoji when she means it.

She is aware that this distinction sounds insane. She holds the awareness gently, at arm's length, the way you hold a thing that might be hot.

The colon-parenthesis smiley could mean anything. It could mean the phone autocorrected. It could mean she typed fast. It could mean she was in a meeting, half-distracted, firing off the reply with one thumb, the way people do when they mean fine, yes, of course, I'll see you soon. The colon-parenthesis smiley is almost certainly nothing.

Two hours and fourteen minutes to send *Yes! Can't wait:)*

She locks the apartment door. The hallway smells like someone's dinner from two nights ago, that particular ghost of cumin and onion that lives in old buildings. She does not think about the text. She is not thinking about the text. She is counting steps to the elevator, which takes a long time to arrive, which gives her nothing to do but stand there with her hands in her pockets, perfectly still.

---

In her head, which she cannot help, the conversation goes like this:

She walks in. Sarah is already there, at their usual table, the one by the window. Sarah looks up. There is something careful in the way she looks up — not cold, but managed, the look of someone who has prepared what they are about to say. She knows that look. She has worn it herself.

*Hey,* Sarah says.

*Hey,* she says back. And then, because she has decided to be brave, because in the rehearsal she is always brave: *Is everything okay? With us?*

A pause. Not a long one. A specific length.

*I've been meaning to talk to you about something.*

And then her own voice, which comes out steadier than expected, which she is proud of even now, even in the imagining: *Okay.*

*I just feel like — and I don't want this to be a big thing — I feel like lately it's been kind of uneven. Like I'm always the one reaching out, and when I do you're either busy or kind of — I don't know. Elsewhere.*

And there it is. *Elsewhere.* The word she would use. She's always had that quality, Sarah, of finding the word that's true and then saying it plainly, without apology, a quality she has always admired and is admiring now even as it takes her apart.

*I'm sorry,* she says, in the rehearsal. *You're right. I know you're right.*

*I'm not trying to make you feel bad.*

*I know.*

*I just — I think I need a little more from you. Or maybe just — different.*

She watches, in the imagining, her own face do the thing it does. The face that tries to stay composed while the rest of her goes somewhere else entirely. She watches herself nod. She watches herself say of course, of course, that's fair, I understand, all the correct things, the things that make her easy to exit graciously.

The elevator arrives. She gets in.

---

The anger comes on the train, which she had not planned for, although she should have.

She is thinking about the way Sarah will say *I need a little more from you* — still future tense, technically, still imaginary — and feeling, in her chest, something that is distinctly not imaginary. Something that has mass and heat. She is angry. She is looking at the dark window of the subway car, at her own reflection moving through it, and she is angry at Sarah for what Sarah has not said yet and may never say.

Because she has tried. She would like that noted. She has shown up, she has answered, she has remembered — the sister's wedding, the promotion that didn't come through, the specific brand of tea that helps when Sarah has migraines. She has made herself available in the particular way that people who are afraid of being too much learn to make themselves available: quietly, carefully, always ready to say *no, it's fine, don't worry about me.* She has made herself easy.

And it will not be enough.

She knows this because — and she is aware, watching herself know this, that something is happening — because she has been here before. Not with Sarah specifically but with the version of events in which she does everything right and it is still, somehow, not sufficient. Not the right shape. Not what was wanted.

She is rehearsing her own abandonment on the F train and she has begun, in some real sense, to resent the person she is going to see.

The train stops. The doors open. A man with a stroller gets on and she shifts to make room. None of this interrupts anything.

---

She surfaces, briefly, somewhere around Bergen Street.

The specific thought she has is: *you're doing it again.*

It arrives in her own voice, flat and familiar, the tone of someone pointing out a recurring mistake — not unkindly, but without much hope either. She has done this before. She knows the shape of this. The small evidence that becomes the aperture through which catastrophe pours. The imagined confrontation that becomes more real than the real one. The anger at someone who does not yet know they are supposed to have said something terrible.

She can see the machinery. She can describe each gear.

She is describing them to herself, calmly, in the part of her mind that has read the right books and sat in enough offices and learned the language for this, when the imagined scene reasserts itself with a vividness that is almost physical — the table by the window, the afternoon light, Sarah's face doing the careful thing it does — and the machinery keeps turning regardless.

*I know I'm doing it,* she thinks.

She gets off at the right stop.

---

Sarah is already there.

She stands up when she sees her. That's the first thing — the standing up, the reaching, the hug that goes on a beat longer than it needs to. Sarah smells like her shampoo, that particular green-herb smell she has used for years, and she is warm, physically warm, and she is squeezing once before she lets go.

*You look good,* Sarah says. *I like your hair like that.*

She sits down. The relief is — she does not have a size for it. It moves through her like something unclenching, like a sound that has been playing for so long she stopped hearing it and has now, suddenly, stopped. Her eyes do something embarrassing and she covers it by looking at the menu, which she does not need because she always gets the same thing.

*I've been thinking about you,* Sarah says. *I feel like I haven't seen you in forever.*

*I know. I've missed you.*

She means it. She can feel that she means it, the truth of it sitting in her chest like something warm. She missed her. She missed this. This specific thing, the table by the window, the menu she doesn't need, Sarah across from her being easy the way she has always been easy.

*I'm sorry I was slow to reply this morning,* Sarah says. *I got cornered by my manager before I even had coffee. It was a whole thing.*

*No, it's totally—*

*You know how I am in the morning.*

*I know. I know you are.*

---

Sarah ordered the soup. She is talking about her manager, this long, complicated thing about a deliverable and a timeline and someone who cc'd the whole department on an email that should not have been sent to the whole department. She is animated. She does the voice. She touches her own collarbone when she laughs, which is a thing she has always done, and the afternoon light is doing exactly what afternoon light does in this particular window at this particular hour.

She is listening. She is here, at the table, actually here.

*You're quiet,* Sarah says, not unkindly. *You okay?*

*Sorry. Yes. Long week.*

*Tell me.*

And she almost does. There is a moment — the soup arriving, the spoon against the bowl, the window with its particular light — where she almost says: *I convinced myself on the train that you were going to break up with me. That's where I'm coming from, just so you know. I rehearsed it the whole way here. I was actually angry at you for about ten minutes for something you never said.* She almost says it. She can feel it, the relief it might offer.

Instead she says: *You know how it goes.*

*I do,* Sarah says. *I really do.*

She reaches across the table and puts her hand over hers. Just for a second. The weight of it is very particular and very real.

---

*You know how it goes.*

She is still at the table. Sarah is talking. The soup bowl has been cleared. The afternoon light has shifted to that later, flatter thing it becomes.

She is still at the table, and she is thinking: *why did she reach across like that?*

Because Sarah does not do that, usually. Sarah is affectionate but in a specific way, verbal, exclamatory — the *I love you* at the end of a call, the *you're my person* sent at midnight apropos of nothing. Not the hand. Not the hand over hers that way, the particular pressure of it.

Unless.

Unless it was compensation. The way you are careful with someone when you know something is coming. The way you give love in advance, make deposits, so that what you are about to withdraw from does not seem so sudden.

The soup bowl is gone. The light is flat. Sarah is talking about her sister's new apartment and whether the neighborhood is actually up-and-coming or if that's what they always say.

From somewhere very close to herself, she nods. She says: *That neighborhood's supposed to be good.* She says: *I've heard good things.*

In her head, which she cannot help, the table by the window assembles itself again. The careful look. The pause of a specific length.

Sarah's hands move when she talks. She is watching them.

She is watching them and she is elsewhere, she is already elsewhere, and from somewhere behind her own eyes she watches this happen, watches the warm thing that just passed between them begin, already, to be reconstructed as evidence, and she cannot stop watching, and Sarah says something that makes her laugh, an actual laugh, surprised out of her, and for a second she is back, she is right here, and then the second passes.

Sarah is still talking. The afternoon holds.

She is somewhere in the middle of what comes next.
