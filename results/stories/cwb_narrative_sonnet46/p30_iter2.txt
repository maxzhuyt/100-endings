# Útiseta

I should say, before anything else, that I knew better. I want that on the record. I was sixteen and I had grown up in Stockholm and I had read enough to know that girls who follow other girls into the woods at midnight are the girls the story happens to. I knew that. I went anyway.

---

We parked Astrid's mother's bicycle against the fence at the edge of the Lindström property and walked the rest in silence, as she had instructed. It was September and the cold came in waves off the ground, off the standing water in the ditches, off the mist that had settled over everything below the treeline. The mist glowed. That's what I remember first—not the mound, not Astrid, but the mist having light in it that didn't behave the way reflected moonlight should. Lateral, somehow. As if something below the surface was faintly warm.

I had been in Norrland for five months by then and the landscape had not opened to me the way I had expected it to. In Stockholm I had always felt the city understood what I was doing in it. Here the fields watched. The treelines held their positions like arguments that had already been made and didn't require restatement. The distances were wrong. You would look at a barn and think: ten minutes, and walk for twenty-five. The land did not correct for you. It waited to see if you would correct for it.

I had not corrected for it. I was still a girl from somewhere else, walking through someone else's dark.

The mound appeared at the field's far end as we crossed the second drainage ditch, and I had a moment—just a moment—of wanting to say: oh, it's smaller than I thought. But it wasn't smaller. It was exactly the size it had seemed from a distance, which was somehow worse, because things at a distance are supposed to diminish. It sat in the flat dark of the hayfield like a word in the wrong language, like a shape the eye makes grammatical adjustments to and cannot. Older than the village. Older than the church, the road, the names on the mailboxes. You understood that immediately, without inference. It simply pushed that information against you.

"We sit at the top," Astrid said.

She wasn't whispering. That was the first thing that had frightened me that night—that she used her full voice, her ordinary voice, the same voice she used to complain about her history teacher or ask if I wanted the last piece of bread. She felt no need to lower it. The night did not alter her register at all.

---

I have tried, since, to explain Astrid to people who didn't know her, and I have always failed. She was sixteen, same as me. She wore the same jeans as everyone, drank the same bad cider at the same parties, laughed at the same things. She had a poster of a pop duo on her bedroom wall—I had seen it once—and she owned three flavors of lip balm and kept them in a specific pocket of her jacket by muscle memory. She was ordinary. She was completely ordinary. And then there would be a moment, and in the moment there was something behind her eyes that had not been assembled from the same materials as everything else about her, and you hadn't been looking for it and you found it and then you could not find the seam where it had been hidden.

Her grandmother had been the same way, I was told later. The village had its own word for the quality. A compound word I never learned to pronounce correctly, something between gift and burden, something that meant the old things had decided to remain in certain bloodlines the way damp remains in old timber.

We climbed. The wet grass soaked immediately through the knees of my jeans and I put my hands against the slope to keep my balance. At the top, Astrid knelt and opened her bag with the focused efficiency of someone setting a table, and laid things out in an order I could only partly see: something smaller than a fist, wrapped in dark cloth. Something in a glass jar, pale and thick. Two flat stones she placed in the grass a measured distance apart, by feel rather than by looking.

"Face north," she said. "Don't speak. Don't move unless I say. If something comes toward the edge of your thinking—" She paused, not because she was searching for words but because she was deciding how many to use. "Don't reach for it. Let it come."

"What do you mean, something?"

She looked at me. Her patience was more unsettling than impatience would have been. "You'll understand when it happens," she said. And then, because she was also just a sixteen-year-old girl from Norrland: "It's not dramatic. Don't expect dramatic."

She turned north. I turned north. We sat.

---

The first thing I did, sitting on the wet top of a burial mound in a field in rural Norrland at half past eleven on a Thursday in September, was think: this is stupid. I thought it clearly and held it up like a lantern. I was a sensible person sitting in wet grass doing a thing with no mechanism for producing any result, participating in a theater that deserved no more credulity than a horoscope or a Ouija board or any of the other assembled debris of human wishful thinking. I was here because Astrid had asked me, and I had said yes because I wanted her to find me interesting, and I had not yet figured out how to want things less badly than I wanted them. These were the facts. I held them and tried to find comfort in them.

My hands were cold. I pressed them between my thighs and felt the damp of the grass through my jeans and felt the cold of the ground coming up through the grass regardless of the press of my thighs against my hands.

The mist moved below us, below the mound's slope. I watched it or tried not to watch it. There was no wind—I had registered the absence of wind early, the way you register anything that should be present and isn't—but the mist moved anyway. Slowly, and with what my brain was already labeling as directionality before I had endorsed labeling it as anything. Wind in the trees beyond the field, maybe. Wind I couldn't feel from here. A thermal gradient off the ground. I supplied these to myself and they were reasonable and I held them and then looked away from the mist.

Astrid sat two meters to my left. I was not to turn my head.

A truck on the N-road, three kilometers back toward the village. Then not. The silence that followed had mass to it. That's the best I can do. It had mass and it had a direction and the direction was north, which was the direction I had been told to face, and I sat inside that and tried to think about something else.

I thought about Stockholm in September—the light, the way autumn arrived sensibly and in order, the way you could watch leaves turn and know from experience what that meant. I thought about my mother's apartment, the particular creak of the third step. I thought about nothing, then nothing, then the sound that came from the field's edge to the northeast, one low sound with no category available to it that was also probably a fox. Foxes made sounds like that. Foxes existed.

And then, without warning, without transition, without any sensation of having reached or been led:

Emma Söderström. Her wrist, which she turned inward at gym class. The sound—specific, domestic—of a car door in a driveway at a specific late hour. Her mother's hands, which I had never seen. Light from a window at a time when the window should have been dark.

The thought arrived the way remembered things arrive: whole, and strangely unshocking, with the flat affect of fact rather than the sharp edge of discovery. I did not gasp. I did not feel my heart do anything dramatic. I just knew it, suddenly and without antecedent, and what was terrible was not the content but the texture—the texture of certainty. The same specific weight.

I had been in this village for five months. I had spoken to Emma Söderström perhaps four times. I did not know her address. I had never seen her mother.

I sat with the knowledge I could not have. I turned it over. I searched myself for the seam where I might have assembled it from overheard conversations, from context clues, from the particular kind of social reading that girls do automatically and catalog without knowing. I searched for the seam and it wasn't there, which told me nothing except that either I had no seam to find or I was too frightened to find it.

My hands had stopped hurting from cold.

At the edge of my vision—at the mist's upper edge, where the slope of the mound ran down to met it—something arranged itself into a shape. Not a shape I would name. A shape that would have required looking at directly to resolve, so I did not look at it directly. I stared north. I stared at the darkness north and I felt, in my peripheral vision, the shape hold itself there at the threshold of being a thing, and then it did not.

Astrid had not moved. Astrid had not flinched, had not turned, had not made any sound. Her stillness was its own category of frightening because I could not decode it. Either she was unmoved because she had done this enough times to be unmoved—familiarity with the unnameable—or she was unmoved because she knew something I didn't that made stillness the correct posture, or she was simply braver than me, substantially and without exception, or she was sixteen and credulous and had talked herself into a performance she couldn't now abandon. I ran through all four and each of them frightened me in a different direction.

"All right," she said.

Her voice was so ordinary. So entirely herself. I nearly reached for her and stopped myself because it would have been embarrassing, and I am still, even now, a person who edits for embarrassment even at the necessary moment.

---

We walked down. My legs were wrong. Not weak, precisely—just wrong, as if they had been briefed separately about the distance and were processing it differently. The wet slope, the grass releasing the cold it had stored, the mist closing briefly over us as we passed through its top layer before we were below it on the field.

At the fence Astrid took the bicycle from me without asking and walked it to the edge of the road where the gravel started.

"What did you get?" she asked. Not looking at me. Adjusting the kickstand.

I said nothing. She waited, and the wait was not unkind. Then she stood the bicycle on the road and pushed off and I followed.

I watched her hands on the handlebars on the ride back. White-knuckled with cold, steady as anything. And I thought about what it meant to grow up with the old things as furniture. To learn that certain practices had weight before you learned that other people found them strange. To have a grandmother who had been a particular way, and to have the particular way arrive in you at sixteen the way brown eyes arrive, expressed from somewhere further back than memory.

I thought about Emma Söderström's wrist.

---

I didn't tell anyone. Not for a long time—long enough for it to become something I could hold at one remove, the way you hold a story about someone you used to be. Astrid moved away the following spring. A city, I think. I don't know which one.

What I eventually did with what I knew, or thought I knew, or had assembled on a cold hillside from materials I couldn't account for was very small. A word to a counselor, framed carefully as concern, framed as something I had noticed rather than received. Something perhaps helpful. Something perhaps not. The causal chain between that word and any outcome that followed it is not something I have ever been able to trace cleanly back to a burial mound in a field in September.

I cannot tell you what happened on that mound. I cannot tell you whether anything happened on that mound. What I have is a girl sitting in wet grass facing north, and a quality of silence with direction, and a knowledge I arrived home with that I had not left the house with, and the absence of any available explanation that doesn't require me to believe something I was not raised to believe and do not, on most days, choose to.

What I can tell you is that I still don't look directly at things at the edge of my vision. Old habit. Old caution. Some adjustment made in the body that the mind has not successfully argued away in the years since.

Some doors, once—

But I knew that going in. I want that on the record.
