# The Last Outpost

## Chapter One

The thing about The Last Outpost is that it smells like every bar I've ever needed. Recycled air carrying a top note of engine grease, something brewing in the back that Ollo insists is traditional Keplan ale and that I've come to believe is mostly stubbornness and questionable judgment. Beneath that: the warm animal smell of people who've been in transit too long and have stopped pretending otherwise. People who needed somewhere to be and found this. I understand them completely.

I wipe the counter in a slow oval. It's habit by now, the gesture as automatic as breathing.

Tuesday by Outpost reckoning, which means medium-busy—the dart corner occupied by three Hessari rig workers whose argument sounds indistinguishable from celebration, because with Hessari it often is. The booths along the ore-panel walls hold the usual rotation: Dex and Comayo from sublevel four conducting their nightly renegotiation of a debt everyone including them understands will not be repaid, the ritual more important than the arithmetic. Priya Soh on her third lemon-water, tablet propped in front of her, not reading the shipping manifests on its screen. Waiting for a message or waiting for the courage not to need one—I've never asked.

I know these people. That's the arrangement. I learn them well enough to be useful, and in return they don't ask me to account for myself. It functions. Most arrangements do, if you're careful about what you ask of them.

I came here to disappear. That's the part I can say plainly now, to myself, in the middle of a busy Tuesday with the hum of the ventilation and the low argument from the dart corner. I came here because no one arrives at a station sitting sideways off every major transit lane without meaning to travel sideways. The Outpost was the furthest point I could reach without planning a next point, and so I stopped. Ollo gave me a shift and then more shifts and eventually I was just here, which is what disappearing looks like when it's working.

I know what I traded for it. The counter-wiping and the careful memory for faces, the patient ear, the particular neutrality that keeps people talking without feeling interrogated. I offer equanimity as a service and the Outpost offers me walls and a purpose that doesn't have edges I might fall off. I don't examine this bargain too closely. It holds.

I'm mid-wipe when the door sighs open on its pneumatic seal.

The figure who drops onto the far stool does so with the particular weight of someone who has been making decisions for hours and has finally, temporarily, stopped. Tentacled dreads built for colder climates, three of them worked loose from their binding and falling across one eye. The coat is well-made—good construction, good material—and there's a split along the left shoulder sealed with tape rather than mended, which means they either left somewhere fast or arrived at bad circumstances gradually. Hands flat on the bar top. Not gripping it. Just landing.

I let them sit. Sit and breathe and remember that they've arrived somewhere.

Then I drift over.

"What can I get you?"

They look up. The eyes are the kind that have been running calculations for a long stretch and have not liked the results. Sharp still, but with the particular weariness of sustained alertness—someone who has been watching their own perimeter.

"Vega whisky, neat." A pause. "Better make it a double."

I reach the bottle down from the shelf. "Long haul?"

"Something like that."

I pour and set the glass in front of them, and they have half of it down before I've returned the bottle to its place. I wipe the counter nearby and say nothing. Silence offered carefully is often the first useful thing.

"You permanent staff here?" they ask, after a while.

"I'm Nets. I'm the bar."

Something happens in their face that might be a laugh finding insufficient room. "Nets. Okay. I'm Farrow."

I let the name rest where they've put it. "Passing through, Farrow?"

They think about this longer than the question should require. "That was the plan."

I wipe. They turn the glass between their palms, watching the amber catch the low light, and we let a minute go by in which the Hessari in the corner escalate and subside again and Priya Soh turns a page she is not reading.

"Quiet place," Farrow says.

"Relative to what?"

"Relative to—" They stop. Begin again, differently. "You're off the main transit corridors. Close enough to Reach Point to be useful, but nothing connecting directly."

"That's right."

"People out here. They keep to themselves?"

I look at them. Level and even, which is different from blank—blank closes people off, and I don't want to close them off. "People out here are generally people who needed somewhere that would have them. So yes. More or less."

Farrow nods once. Slow. Drinks. Sets the glass down carefully, like it matters where it lands.

"I was working a claim," they say. Not looking at me, looking at the glass. "Belt section seven-nine, past the Dullan markers. Solo survey contract with a Meridian sub-contractor. Six weeks. Standard scope—nothing glamorous."

"Okay," I say. Same inflection. Flat and open, a surface they can keep building on.

"I found something in the ice shelf of a C-type." They say *found something* the way people say it when the word *found* is doing a great deal of work, covering things they don't have language for yet. "Not minerals. Not what the contract was scoped for." A pause that has pressure in it. "I ran the standard alert protocol. Filed it with the sub-contractor. Documented everything and filed it."

"And?"

"They didn't respond to the alert." Their hands have stopped turning the glass. They're just holding it now. "They responded to *me*. Stay put, don't move the ship, someone would be out within forty-eight hours."

I wipe the counter in slow circles.

"But you moved the ship," I say.

Their eyes come to me fast. "How did you—"

"Because you're here instead of there."

A breath. Then a nod, conceding this.

"What did you find, Farrow?"

The Hessari corner flares briefly. Subsides. The ventilation hums. Priya Soh does not look up from her tablet, and Dex says something to Comayo that Comayo answers with a long exhale, and the Outpost breathes its slow recycled breath around us while Farrow decides how much to give.

"I don't know what I found," they say finally. "I know it was alive. I know what I was looking at was—" They press their mouth closed, then open it again. "I know it was in distress. I couldn't tell you the specifics. But I know what I was looking at."

"And you know that your sub-contractor's response felt like you'd done something wrong by finding it."

Farrow goes still. The specific stillness of someone recognizing the shape of their own situation from the outside.

"So you ran," I say. "To coordinates that weren't on any direct route." I stop wiping. "Which is here."

They exhale. It's ragged and slow, the sound of pressure that has been contained for hours finally finding a margin.

"I think I was followed." Their eyes move to the door—just a flick, quick and practiced, like they've been doing it since they left whatever they left. "Or I think I will be. I think I have six hours. Maybe less." They push the loose dreads from their face with a hand that isn't quite steady. "I didn't know where else to go. I just needed somewhere that would—" They stop.

"Have you," I say.

They look at me.

I pick up the bottle of Vega whisky.

I refill their glass.

"Six hours is a while," I tell them. It isn't a promise. It isn't nothing. Farrow seems to understand it as what it is—the Outpost recognizing them, for now, the way it recognized me once and has not yet rescinded—and something in their shoulders drops a fraction of an inch, the posture of someone who has briefly, conditionally, stopped running.

I go back to wiping the counter.

The whisky isn't gone. The Outpost breathes. Outside its walls, the belt stretches dark and cold in every direction, and somewhere in that dark something is either coming or it isn't, and there is nothing to do about that yet except stay present and keep the bar.

I wipe the counter in slow, even circles.

Tonight feels like every night until it doesn't.
