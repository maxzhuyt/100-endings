# Chapter Two

The others were already back when JT came in through the tree line. He saw the lodge lights through the pines—orange and particular, the copper warmth of the kitchen windows—and he stood at the edge of the yard for a moment without going in. Just stood. The cold worked into the seams of his jacket and the clearing was still behind his eyes the way a camera flash is still behind your eyes, white and total and not diminishing the way it should.

*The buck spooked them.* That was all. A noise, a shape in the snow, and his father had turned the way any man turns toward sudden movement. JT breathed the cold down into his chest until it hurt, until the hurt was something solid and reliable, and then he crossed the yard and went inside.

The lodge smelled like it always smelled. Gun oil and woodsmoke and the sour-woolly smell of damp gear hung too close to a heat source. Mike was at the table without his boots on, holding a beer with both hands and looking at his phone with the face of a man who has not checked out of the world so much as been asked to leave it temporarily. Garnet, their guide, was at the far end of the table with a cup of something, not speaking—but Garnet was often quiet before dinner, JT reminded himself. That was not unusual. That was fine.

Ray was in the kitchen.

JT watched his father's back for a moment before Ray heard him come in and turned. The smile arrived correctly—the familiar one, the one JT had been reading his whole life the way you read a word you've known so long you've stopped seeing the letters. But the eyes came a half-beat after. Just a half-beat. As though the smile had been dispatched in advance and the rest of Ray was still catching up to it.

"Thought we'd have to send Garnet back for you," Ray said.

"I'm here," JT said.

Ray turned back to the counter. There was a bird there, half-dressed; he went back to work with the particular slow care of a man who likes doing things correctly with his hands. JT sat down across from Mike and didn't say anything and neither did Mike and that was normal. That was them. The fire was good and the room was warm and somewhere under the floorboards the furnace was running a low and steady note, and JT put his cold hands on the table and made himself feel each of those things in sequence: the fire, the warmth, the sound.

He almost managed it.

---

Garnet ate with his eyes down.

That was what JT noticed—not at first, because he was watching his father, watching for something he hadn't named to himself yet—but in the lull after the food was on the table, when everyone had settled. Garnet was at the corner of the table, working through his plate with a focused efficiency that had nothing cheerful in it, nothing of the man who'd spent forty minutes last year explaining the particular grammar of moose behavior in early frost, who had opinions on precipitation and acted on them conversationally. The same man who had told JT's uncle, unsolicited but not unloved, the precise historical reason why one stretch of trail was better for tracking in afternoon light than morning.

He ate. He didn't look up. When Ray asked him about the eastern cut he'd be running tomorrow he said *probably fine* without elaborating and went back to his plate.

Mike glanced at JT. A quick glance, the kind that could mean anything, and then Mike was looking at his food again, and Ray was talking about something else—the weather coming in from the northwest, what it might mean for the morning—and Garnet said nothing, and JT said nothing, and the meal closed around the silence the way water closes over a stone.

---

After dinner JT found him on the porch.

Garnet was at the rail with a cigarette, the ember going orange and small against all that dark. He didn't turn when JT came out. He pulled on the cigarette and looked at the tree line, which was black and still and twenty yards from the porch steps, and he held the smoke in for a moment before letting it go.

"Going to be clear tomorrow," JT said.

"Maybe," Garnet said.

JT stood beside him at the rail. Close enough that the silence between them was something specific, something with edges. The yard was pewter-colored under a moon that was nearly full, and the timber beyond the yard was the kind of dark that seems solid, seems to have a surface.

"You want to tell me what's going on?" JT said.

Garnet turned the cigarette in his fingers. He didn't look at JT. He looked at the moon for a moment, through the pines, the way a man looks at a thing he has already decided about.

"How long you planning to stay," Garnet said.

"Full week. Same as always."

Garnet nodded once.

"You should go home," he said. He said it the way he would have said *you should take the east fork* or *you should glass that ridge before you move*—matter-of-factly, without drama. "All of you. Before three days. Before the moon comes full." He drew on the cigarette one more time and then dropped it to the porch boards and toed it out without looking down. "That's what I'd do."

JT felt the cold on his face, his hands. The particular stillness of the woods out past the yard. He made himself speak slowly. "Why."

Garnet looked at him then. His face in the porch light had the quality of a door someone has recently closed: the impression of something, but the thing itself already gone. He held JT's eyes for just long enough to communicate that what JT was seeing was a decision, not a vacancy, and then he turned and went back inside without another word.

The screen door swung shut behind him.

JT stood at the rail. The tree line stood back at him—twenty yards of open ground and then the dark, and the dark went as far as anything went in this country, which was very far, which was as far as you could imagine and then further. The not-quite-full moon put a cold silver light on the yard that didn't reach far enough to matter.

His hands were going numb.

He went inside.

---

He lay in the narrow bunk and ran through it methodically, the way he'd always done—turned it over like an object, looked at its underside, tried the weight of it. Garnet had worked these bush lines for thirty years. Garnet had, as far as JT knew, never been dramatic about anything in his life. He said what he meant and meant what he said and did not say things he didn't mean. That was the kind of man he was—the whole reputation of the man was predicated on exactly that quality.

*And men like that have bad years*, JT thought. *Private things. Drink, or grief, or some falling-out with someone that turned the whole season sour.* His uncle had gone strange for a winter once, and it had turned out to be his heart, and nothing more.

And the clearing—

He'd been cold, and the light through heavy overcast was flat and peculiar, and his father was sixty-one and had been struggling with the rifle for two seasons at least, though no one said so, and when a man struggles he compensates in other ways, he builds rituals, he stands in fields in cold and silence and you don't know what that means to him, you only know what you see from thirty yards through frosted timber.

*You think too much*, Mike had told him since they were boys. *You take a thing and you sit on it until it hatches something wrong.* Mike had said this as a criticism but also, JT sometimes thought, as a kind of envy—Mike, who could put things down, who had always been able to just put things down and walk away from them.

JT stared at a crack in the plank ceiling and was almost inside that explanation, almost folded into it, when the woods went quiet.

It was not a total quiet. The wind was up and there was sound. But the sound that had been coming from the yard-side of the tree line—subtle and continuous and the kind of thing you don't consciously register until it stops—was gone. In its place was a pocket of silence that had a shape to it, a direction. The way silence sounds when something is standing in it.

He didn't decide to get up. He was simply at the window.

The yard was moon-silver and perfectly still. Nothing was there. The tree line stood where it always stood, the pines standing heavy and black against the slightly lighter black of the sky, their edges indistinct, their interior total. Nothing moved. He looked for a long time. Nothing moved.

He was about to go back to the bunk.

Then he saw his father's face.

Ray was on his back in the far bunk, turned toward the window, turned toward the moonlight that came through the glass and lay in a long cold rectangle across the floor between them. He was asleep. His breathing was long and slow and entirely regular, the breathing of a man in deep and untroubled sleep. And he was smiling.

Not the smile JT knew. Not the one that came with the eyes, with the specific weight of Ray behind it, sixty-one years of a particular man's particular humor and warmth and stubbornness. This was something older than that. Something that lived further down. The lips were parted just slightly and the corners were drawn up and the expression on his face was one of anticipation so profound it had passed through excitement into a kind of serenity—the face of a man receiving something he has long been promised.

JT had seen that expression before. He knew where. He put it away immediately and held it away with both hands, or tried to, but it was there: the clearing, his father's face at the clearing, standing in the snow at the edge of the trees with his rifle loose at his side and his face turned up and his eyes already somewhere else.

Like he was expecting something wonderful.

Like it was almost here.

JT stood at the window for a long time. He looked at the tree line. He looked at his father's face. He looked at the tree line again. The cold came through the glass in a long slow pressure against his forehead and he let it come, let it be something real, something that was at least happening now, in the present, in a world with ordinary rules. His father breathed in and out. His father's smile didn't change.

Outside, past the glass, the woods held their silence a little longer than they should have.

Then, gradually, the ordinary sounds came back—the wind in the crowns, the tick of something in the undergrowth—and the tree line was just the tree line, and the moon moved a very small amount, and the night went on.

JT did not go back to bed for a long time. When he did, he lay on his back in the narrow bunk and looked at the ceiling and listened to his father breathe and did not sleep again until the sky outside the window had gone from black to the particular gray that means the dark is almost done.

He was not comforted by the dawn.

He was only glad it had come.
