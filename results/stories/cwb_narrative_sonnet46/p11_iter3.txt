# Overthinking It

The backup to the backup is: *So which part do you want to do?*

It is risk level three out of three, which means it is the one she will almost certainly use, which means she has spent the last four minutes preparing for a conversation she could have had by accident. The lab table smells like isopropyl alcohol and the specific anxiety of assigned seating, and she is already in her chair, has been in her chair, is the kind of person who is always already in her chair when other people arrive, which itself, she is aware, is a data point she cannot control for. She has been watching him from peripheral vision since he sat down, which is not the same as looking at him, a distinction she finds important and suspects no one else would.

His name is Daniel. She knows this because Dr. Ferris read it from a list.

There are four of them at the table: her, Daniel, Marcus, and Priya. Marcus is talking. Marcus is always talking, which she has come to understand is not confidence so much as a different relationship to the space between thoughts—he simply does not experience that space as dangerous. She envies this so specifically it feels like a medical condition.

While Marcus talks, she opens her notebook and draws.

She does this without deciding to, which is the only way she does anything that could be called spontaneous. Her pen moves to the margin of her lab notes and Marcus becomes a sun with seventeen rays, which is too many, which is correct—Marcus radiates in every direction without hierarchy, without prioritization, a thermal catastrophe. She draws him small and puts his initials underneath. Then Priya, who is sitting very straight and has already divided the lab worksheet into four equal sections and written each person's name at the top of their section in print so neat it looks like a font. Priya becomes a grid. A very organized grid, three-by-three, no smudging.

Then her pen moves toward the corner of the page and begins.

She gets as far as the general shape of a face—the jaw, maybe, or what she is imagining the jaw to be from peripheral vision—before she stops and looks at what she has done and covers it with cross-hatching, dense and fast, until it is just texture, until it is just the suggestion that something was there.

Marcus is saying they should split the report by section.

She agrees by nodding at her notebook.

---

Priya says she will do the abstract and the introduction, which everyone accepts because Priya's organizational grid has made it clear this is not a negotiation. Marcus says he will do the methods section because he was the one who actually ran the experiment, by which he means he was the one who held the measuring cylinder while she read the markings and Daniel recorded the numbers, but this is not worth correcting. She raises no objection. She is going to do the results section, she has already decided this, because the results section is mostly just transcribing data and making a graph, which she can do alone, without synchronizing.

Then Daniel looks at her—not at the group, at her—and says, "Do you have the instructions? I can't find mine."

She hears this. She processes this. This is a simple factual question with a known answer—yes, she has the instructions, they are on the second page of her lab notes, she can see them right now—and the answer to this question is *yes, here* and possibly also *I have a copy you can keep*, which is risk level one, which is the good opener, the one she prepared for a different scenario but which applies here—

*Yes, here* is three syllables. She has said three syllables before. She says three syllables constantly, in her room, at home, in her head, she says entire paragraphs—

She looks at the instructions. She looks up. She looks at the instructions.

*Yes, here* becomes *yeah, I—* becomes the decision to say *yeah, I have it, you can—* which fuses mid-arc with *yeah, here, if you—* and what comes out is: "Yeah, I—here."

Grammatically intact. She checks it afterward against the template of normal speech and it passes, technically, if you don't listen to it. Daniel takes the paper and says thanks and she says *yeah* again, which is one syllable, which is achievable, and then she looks back at her notebook.

The instructions are on the second page of her lab notes.

She already told him that, in the sense that she handed him a page, but she did not say it, in the sense that she did not say it, and she is now constructing a probability model for whether *yeah, I—here* is a sentence that a person says or a sentence that a malfunctioning person says and whether the distinction is something Daniel is equipped to notice and whether noticing it would produce pity or confusion or something closer to amusement and whether any of these outcomes is worse than the others—

Marcus is explaining to no one in particular that the burette was definitely miscalibrated.

---

Midway through the lab period she conducts the inventory.

She has been leaning forward. She checks: yes, forward, which is eager, which reads as either attentive or desperate depending on context, and she has been unable to determine the context because the context is her face and she cannot see her own face. She corrects to neutral, then corrects back slightly because neutral from the outside might read as aloof, which is worse than eager in a group context, she has determined this, she has done the research, meaning she has thought about it for a long time. She has smiled five or six times. She believes five or six times is within the normal range but cannot verify this without access to footage of people smiling naturally, which she does not have, which feels like an oversight. She has said *yeah* four times and *mm* twice and has not yet said anything that meaningfully required her larynx, which is below average output for a fifty-minute class period even accounting for the fact that she is introverted, and she knows she is thinking about the word *accounting* because she uses it when she is trying to treat herself like a spreadsheet, which her therapist had something to say about, she cannot remember exactly what.

Her pencil. She has been tapping her pencil. She stops.

The tapping may have been audible. She calculates: at what volume threshold does pencil tapping become a social statement, a thing that gets noticed, a thing that prompts someone to look at her hands, which would then require her to respond to being looked at with some acknowledgment—a shrug, a sorry, anything—which is an unscheduled interaction she has not prepared for. She places the pencil flat on the table.

She looked at him. She has been tracking this. She looked at him when he asked about the instructions, which was necessary and therefore exempt from analysis, and she looked at him again approximately twelve minutes ago when he said something to Marcus and laughed, and she has not looked at him since, which is either appropriate restraint or conspicuous avoidance, which is also visible, absence is visible, she knows this—

She has been looking at her notebook.

Her notebook is ten percent data and eighty percent margin, and in the margin: a sun with too many rays, a grid, a dense block of cross-hatching in the corner. Her notes on the experiment read: *0.1M NaOH, 25mL, endpoint = pink (faint). Check Priya's figures.* This is accurate. This is everything.

She writes *check Priya's figures* again, for something to do.

---

Daniel picks up his copy of the instructions—her copy, still, he has not returned it—and says, at no one specifically, at the air above the lab table: "Who writes these. Genuinely. Who is responsible for this."

He is holding the page up slightly. The instructions are, in fact, incomprehensible; she knows this, she has been reading them for fifteen minutes and building a mental translation in parallel. He is performing exasperation but gently, in the register of a joke, and Marcus laughs from across the table, and she feels the window open.

She knows it is a window. She identifies it in her brain, in real time, with a little flag that says *this is the window*, and she has something to say, she has the words, she has been reading the instructions and could say, *I think section three is actually a different lab* because she believes this, she genuinely believes they have been given instructions with a section that belongs to someone else's experiment, and this is funny, this is a funny and useful thing to say—

The flag is waving.

She thinks: *I think section three is actually a different lab.*

She thinks: *is this a good thing to say or does it sound like I'm showing off that I read it.*

She thinks: *does it sound like I've been sitting here that whole time analyzing the instructions instead of talking.*

She thinks: *I have been sitting here that whole time analyzing the instructions instead of talking.*

She thinks: *say it.*

She thinks: *if I say it right now, this late, does the lag between the joke and the response make it seem like—*

Daniel puts the instructions down. Marcus is already talking about something else. Priya has not looked up from her section.

Four seconds. Forty seconds. She is not sure which one is the real unit.

She writes, in the margin: *section three wrong lab??* She underlines it twice. It sits there in her handwriting, which is very neat, which is Priya-adjacent actually, and she thinks about that for a moment, the neatness, the way neat handwriting is one more thing that happens when she is alone and can take her time.

The writing is correct. The writing always comes out right.

---

Dr. Ferris says five minutes.

She begins transferring her data to the results section template, which is the responsible thing to do, which she is good at. She writes the column headers. She writes the measurements. She makes a small, accurate note about the endpoint color and adds *±0.05 mL* to the burette readings because that is the correct precision for the equipment used, and she finds this satisfying in the specific way that correct precision is always satisfying, which is probably a personality trait she should have mentioned at some point in the last fifty minutes as a way of making conversation but did not.

She is writing the final data point when she reaches the bottom of the page.

There, below the data table, tucked into the corner where the margin meets the white space at the edge: a face. Small. More careful than the others. Not obscured—she looks at it, she is looking at it right now, and it is not obscured—it is just a face, a jaw and two eyes and something approximate to an expression, and she did not decide to draw it, she knows she did not decide to draw it because she has been monitoring her decisions this entire period and this was not among them.

It simply appeared.

She looks at it for a moment that is not very long.

Then she closes the notebook, cover to cover, and puts it in her bag, and when she looks up Daniel is capping his pen and Priya is stapling her section to Marcus's section and Dr. Ferris is saying *same groups next week, don't lose your data sheets*, and she has not lost her data sheet, it is in the notebook, the notebook is in her bag, everything is accounted for.

She puts her bag over her shoulder.

She did not say *I think section three is actually a different lab.*

She said *yeah, I—here*.

She drew his face twice: once covered, once not. The second time she did not decide to. That seems worth something, she thinks, as she stands up from the table—though she could not say exactly what. She doesn't try. She walks to the door.

Next week is Tuesday.
