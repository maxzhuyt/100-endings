# Caribou Season at the Lodge

## One

He came up the drive in the last good light of the afternoon, the truck easing over ruts that hadn't changed since his father had cut the road thirty years ago. The same birch twins at the first bend, grown enormous now. The same frost heave before the final straight. And then the lodge itself resolving out of the treeline—the dark log walls, the green metal roof, the woodpile running twenty feet along the east side in the orderly stacks his father built every August with a precision that bordered on the ceremonial.

JT pulled alongside his father's truck and killed the engine and sat for a moment without getting out.

The lodge was exactly as he'd left it. Every autumn of his life since he was five years old, some version of this image: the porch light on, the smell of cold woodsmoke and spruce resin, the particular quality of northern October light going amber and then quickly gone. He knew this place by heart, the way you knew certain things without having learned them—knew it in the tendons and the blood.

So he couldn't explain what was wrong with it.

He sat a little longer. The light was failing fast and the temperature dropping with it, and there was nothing to see that he hadn't seen five hundred times before. But the air pressed against the truck windows with an unusual density, and the treeline to the north seemed—closer, maybe, or fuller, something. And there was the feeling, low and formless, of eyes.

Patient eyes. Something that had been waiting and was very nearly done with waiting.

*You're tired,* he told himself. *Eleven hours of driving.*

He got out of the truck.

Inside, his father was frying onions and Mike was at the table with a beer, the radio going soft with a French station it always found up here, the accordion faint and slightly wrong at the edges. His brother looked up and said, "There he is," and his father half-turned from the stove and said, "Good drive?" and JT said it was fine, and the lodge folded itself around him the way it always had, and the feeling in the driveway receded until it was only a feeling—which was to say, nothing.

He was just tired. It was nothing.

---

## Two

The next morning they went south along the ridge before first light, three men and three rifles and the cold so sharp it turned breath to smoke. Howard Calley led, as he had always led, moving through the dark timber with an unhurried certainty that JT had spent his childhood trying to imitate. Mike came behind him. JT brought up the rear.

They were two hours out when they found the herd in the long clearing below the granite face—nine or ten animals dispersed across the frost-silvered flats, heads down, moving slowly. His father signaled and they split. Mike and the old man took the north approach. JT circled east on his own.

He came to the edge of the clearing and went still and waited.

The animal that stepped out from the spruce was a mature cow, heavy-bodied, her coat already thickened for winter. She emerged unhurried into the open and then she stopped and turned and looked directly at him, and JT had his rifle up before he thought about it—thirty meters, quarter angle, clean shot, the kind of shot he had made a dozen times—but what stopped him was not a technical problem.

The animal was watching him. This was normal. Animals watched.

But she was watching him the way you watched something you had already accounted for. Her ears were forward. Her body was loose, weight settled, one foreleg canted in a posture that looked almost like repose. In twenty-two years of hunting JT had never seen an animal stand that way in the open with a man in front of it. They bolted or they froze. This animal simply waited.

He heard the shots from the north—his father's rifle, the distinctive double report. Twenty seconds later, Mike's.

The cow did not flinch.

JT lowered his rifle. He couldn't have explained why. The animal remained in the clearing for another moment, still and temperate, and then she turned and walked back into the spruce. Not running. Walking. She didn't look back.

When he came around the far side of the clearing, his father and Mike were standing over two downed cows. His father was smoking, which he had quit doing eight years ago. Mike was crouched by the nearer animal with his knife out, looking at the entry wound.

"Good morning," his father said, and the words came out in smoke on the cold air.

"Where'd you get that?" JT asked.

"What?"

"The cigarette."

His father looked at the cigarette in his hand as though mildly surprised to find it there. "Must've had one in a pocket."

"How were they?" JT asked. "The shots?"

"Both stopped," his father said. "Just stopped and faced up."

His voice was even, conversational. No inflection JT could read. He looked at his father looking back at him, and there was nothing strange in the old man's face—weathered, steady, somewhat remote in the way he had always been—and so JT filed it away without knowing what file he was putting it in.

They tagged the animals and got to work. His father moved efficiently, pulling up his sleeves in the cold. His hands did not tremble.

JT told himself: some animals stopped. It happened. You heard about it. He'd spent too many hours in the truck getting here and not enough of them sleeping.

The herd had moved on, and the clearing was quiet, and there was nothing else.

He almost believed this.

---

## Three

He nearly missed it.

He'd gone back west along the edge of the treeline to retrieve his pack, and the return path took him along the base of the granite face, where a shelf of rock jutted out and created a blind of sorts, and it was from behind this shelf, without choosing to conceal himself, that JT saw them.

His father and his brother stood together thirty feet into the timber at the far side of the clearing. Between them, facing them, was a buck. Not one of the animals from this morning's herd—something larger, an old bull in the particular stillness of something that had decided to stop.

His father's rifle was in the grass at his feet.

The old man stood with his hands at his sides and his face tilted up toward the animal's muzzle, and even at this distance, even in this light, with his father's back half-turned toward him, JT could see the trembling. Not the trembling of cold or age but of something he could not name—the trembling of a thing that could not contain itself, that was pressing against its own edges. His father's right hand lifted, the fingers extended, reaching toward the animal's face.

Mike stood to his left, similarly unmoored, his weight forward and his mouth slightly open. His rifle was shouldered but the barrel was angled at the ground.

The buck was looking at Howard Calley straight on. It was not moving. Its eyes, at this distance, were flat and without visible color. It had the same quality as the cow in the clearing—that deliberate composure, that patience—but amplified now, gathered and intensified, and the trees around the three of them pressed in and the light had a queer thick quality, and his father's extended fingers were close, six inches, four—

JT stepped back behind the rock.

He pressed his back against the cold granite and looked at the sky and breathed.

By the time he came around the long way and found them again, the buck was gone. His father was picking up his rifle from the grass. Mike was wiping his face with the back of his hand, the gesture of a man who had been recently asleep and was sorting himself out. Neither of them looked at JT directly when he came into the clearing, and neither of them said anything about what they'd been doing in the trees, and the silence around this absence was absolute and practiced—the family's oldest skill—and JT did not see how to enter it.

His father looked at him. His eyes were bright. Too bright, the way a man's eyes looked with a fever.

"Ready to head back?" his father said.

"Sure," JT said.

They walked back in single file, his father ahead, Mike behind, and JT between them spent the entire hour trying to decide what he had seen. The hand reaching out. The luminous, private expression—that was the word, and it was the wrong word for his father, a man who kept his face clean of weather—

*Luminous.*

He hiked and said nothing and that was how it went.

---

## Four

That night, supper was venison stew from a previous season's kill, the meat soft and slightly sweet in a way JT had always liked as a boy. He didn't like it now. He pushed it around the bowl while his father and Mike traded observations about the terrain south of the ridge—where the snow tended to compact, where it stayed loose. Practical talk. Hunter's talk. The kind of conversation that had a shape and a purpose and left no room for anything else.

JT waited for an opening.

"Thought we'd push east tomorrow," his father said, not looking up from his bowl. "Weather's going to turn."

"How do you figure?" Mike asked.

"Sky had that look tonight."

Mike nodded. That was how his father communicated weather—through the sky having looks, the wind having moods, the woods telling him things in a private language JT had never been able to fully learn. He'd spent twenty-seven years trying.

"I saw something today," JT said. "In the clearing, before you guys—"

"Pass the bread," his father said.

JT passed the bread.

He watched his father tear a piece and drag it through the stew, slow and methodical, the way he did everything. Howard Calley was sixty-one years old, lean and weathered in the particular way men got when they spent enough autumns in the same cold place. The lines in his face had deepened since JT had last seen him properly, at Easter. His hands, turning the bread, were perfectly steady.

"In the clearing," JT tried again. "There was a buck—"

"Big country up here," his father said pleasantly. "Lot of deer."

Mike glanced at JT from across the table. Not warningly—just a look, mild and unreadable, before he turned back to his bowl. JT felt the familiar closing-off, the family's practiced talent for collectively not-seeing something until not-seeing it became the only reality on offer.

He let it go. Always safer.

---

He couldn't sleep. The lodge settling its bones in the cold woke him twice, and the third time he startled awake, he wasn't sure what had done it. He lay in the narrow bunk—the same one he'd slept in since he was eight years old, the same faded Hudson Bay blanket, the same smell of cedar and gun oil—and listened.

The woods were not quiet. They were never fully quiet up here, but this was different. There was a rhythm to it. Not the stochastic rustle of wind through spruce boughs, not the intermittent creak of cooling timber. Something regular. Patient. A slow percussion at the edge of the treeline—stop, and then again, stop—like something walking a circuit.

He lay still for a long time, telling himself it was a moose. Moose wandered close to structures in the fall. He knew this. He had known this his whole life.

After a while, it stopped.

In the morning he went out before the others were up and walked the perimeter and found the tracks. Big, cloven, sunk deep into the half-frozen mud near the woodpile. They went all the way around the east side of the lodge and partway along the south, then stopped—not as if the animal had turned back, but as if it had simply ceased to exist at that point, the trail ending without any corresponding disturbance in the undergrowth.

He photographed them on his phone. Then he stood in the early grey light, looking at the photograph, and then at the tracks, and then at the photograph again.

He deleted it before he went inside.

---

"You look like hell," Mike said, pouring coffee.

"Bad night."

"Heard you moving around."

JT wrapped both hands around the mug. "Did you hear anything? Outside? Around three, maybe?"

Mike looked at him. He had the same eyes as their father—dark brown, steady, capable of going very still. "Wind," he said. "It picks up off the ridge."

"It wasn't wind."

Mike set the coffee pot down and leaned against the counter. He was quiet for a moment, and in that moment JT thought—hoped—that something might open up between them. Mike was five years older and had always carried himself with the authority that came from being their father's designated inheritor. But there were moments, rare and fleeting since childhood, when the authority slipped and Mike just looked tired.

"JT," he said finally. "What do you want me to tell you?"

"What happened in the clearing yesterday."

Mike looked out the window at the trees. The light was coming up cold and flat, the sky the color of old pewter. "Nothing happened in the clearing," he said. "We spooked a buck. It bolted. We moved on."

"That's not—"

"That's what happened." He picked up his coffee and turned toward the hallway. "Eat something before we head out."

JT sat at the table alone, listening to the lodge settle around him. On the wall across from him hung the same photographs that had always hung there—his grandfather with a bull caribou, his father as a young man, squinting into the same autumn light. Three generations of men who had come to this place and understood it. Who had been understood by it.

He thought about the buck in the clearing. His father's hand, trembling toward the muzzle. That expression—luminous was the word that kept returning to him, which was wrong, which was not a word that applied to his father.

He thought about the tracks that stopped in the mud beside the woodpile. The way the ending felt deliberate.

Something moved at the edge of the treeline. JT went very still. Through the lodge's single east-facing window, across thirty yards of frost-silvered grass, the dark wall of spruce rose up and held its shape. Nothing visible. Nothing he could name.

But the air in the room had changed. It had gone thick and close, like the moment before lightning, like the feeling he'd had pulling up the drive the day before—that sense of weight, of held breath, of something patient that had been waiting for a very long time and had now arrived at the edge of its patience.

JT set down his coffee mug. His hands were shaking.

From somewhere in the back of the lodge, he heard his father rise from bed. The familiar creak of old floorboards, the specific cadence of the old man's step. But there was something different in it this morning—an ease, almost a lightness—as if Howard Calley had slept soundly and woken certain of something.

JT stared at the treeline and waited for whatever came next.

---

## Five

He drew the short trail that morning and went out alone.

The path ran east along the rim of the lower valley, two miles of spruce and birch and then a long descent to a frozen creek bottom where the game liked to water. His father had sent him because the sign down by the creek had been poor the last two seasons—not much point sending two men, he'd said, his voice carrying the particular neutrality of a reasonable statement that was also a dismissal. Mike went south. Howard Calley went north toward the granite face.

JT went east and tried not to think.

He was an hour out and within sight of the creek when he saw the buck.

It was standing in the trail. Not at the edge of it, not browsing across it—standing in it, occupying it, facing him from forty meters out with the deliberate placement of something that had chosen to be there. Enormous. An old bull, his rack spreading wide and dark, his coat the deep tobacco-brown of winter.

JT stopped.

The buck looked at him.

He had never—not once—seen an animal arrange itself this way, in the middle of a path, unmoving, unhidden, with the quality of something that had been waiting for a specific person and had now found him. The woods on both sides were silent. The creek was frozen and silent. Even the wind had gone out of the spruce boughs, and the whole world hung suspended in grey November light, and the animal looked at him with flat brown eyes and did not move.

JT raised his rifle.

His eye found the scope and his finger found the guard and the animal filled the glass—the barrel of the chest, the shoulder, the angled neck, all the geometry of a clean shot presenting itself with an almost courteous clarity—and he stood like that, the rifle steady, his breathing under control, and he could not pull the trigger.

Could not.
