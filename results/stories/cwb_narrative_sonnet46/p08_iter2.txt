# Stolen Moments in Roma

She came out of the Via della Stamperia at forty kilometers an hour and he stepped into the street without looking up from his guidebook, and the only reason the day did not end there—before it had properly begun—was that Alessandra had excellent reflexes and a long history of avoiding the consequences of other people's inattention.

The motorcycle shrieked to a halt. The guidebook went airborne. A cluster of pigeons near the fountain launched themselves skyward as one creature, briefly unanimous in their panic, then scattered into the ordinary Roman afternoon.

"*Madonna!*" She yanked off her helmet. Her hair, dark and aggressively abundant, spilled forward. She was already composing the full legal case against him in Italian—a language that, at volume, sounds like an opera being conducted during a structural emergency—when she registered his expression.

He was not afraid. He was not aggressive. He was retrieving the guidebook from the cobblestones with the particular dignity of a man who knows he is entirely at fault and has chosen not to let that stop him from maintaining adequate posture.

"I am terribly—" he began. Then, clearly having rehearsed this for a scenario he had not expected to deploy quite so immediately, he switched to Italian: "*Mi dispiace enormemente.*"

Alessandra stopped.

His pronunciation was so catastrophically earnest—every syllable landing with the solemnity of a man defusing something—that for a moment she could not locate her anger. She found it, briefly, and then it slipped away again.

"*Enormemente,*" she repeated.

"Yes."

"You chose that word specifically."

"I wanted to be thorough." He smoothed his jacket. He was tall, and fair, and had the slightly rumpled composure of a man who had once owned better luggage and had made a philosophical peace with its loss. An academic, she thought. They were the worst kind of tourist. They read about a place until they believed they understood it, then they arrived and stood in the middle of it looking bereaved.

"Your guidebook," she said, in English, because she could see already that his Italian had exactly one sentence in it, "is from 2009."

He looked at the cover. "Is that—relevant?"

"The trattoria you've circled on page forty." She had seen it as it fell open in the air. "It's a car park now. Has been for six years."

A long pause. He absorbed this with the expression of a man updating an internal document. "Right," he said. "Well."

Something about the way he said *well*—not with embarrassment, not with deflection, but with the soft acceptance of someone receiving a difficult verdict and finding it, upon reflection, entirely fair—undid her in a way she could not immediately account for. She stood in the middle of the street with her helmet in her hand and her motorcycle ticking quietly as it cooled, and she heard herself say: "Come. I'll show you something that actually exists."

He blinked. "I'm sorry—why would you do that?"

"Because you're standing in front of the Trevi Fountain with a guide to a city that no longer exists, and I find it offensive." She pulled the helmet back on and swung her leg over the seat. "Get on."

"I haven't agreed to anything—"

"*Andiamo.*"

He looked at the motorcycle. He looked at the guidebook. He put the guidebook in his coat pocket and got on.

---

She took him first to a church near the Aventine that was closed on Tuesdays—closed, in fact, on most days, its particular schedule being more spiritual than administrative—and accessed it through a side gate with the ease of someone to whom the concept of closure had always seemed loosely advisory. The gate swung open. She walked through without pausing.

Inside, the afternoon light fell through old glass in long amber columns, sectioning the interior into something ancient and geometric. Dust moved in the beams with total tranquility. Ben stopped walking. He didn't say anything, which Alessandra found she actively preferred to the alternative.

She watched him look. Most people looked at old things in Rome the way they looked at photographs of relatives they had never met—with a dutiful acknowledgment of significance. He looked at the light on the floor with the attention of someone trying to memorize it, as though he expected to need it later.

"How did you—" he started.

"I know people," she said, which was not precisely true but was easier than explaining that she had simply never stopped trying doors.

They left before anyone arrived to ask questions.

At the market near Campo de' Fiori, the afternoon had thickened into that particular Roman hour when shade becomes a serious consideration and the vendors' energy shifts from commercial to philosophical. She moved through the stalls without consulting anyone, lifting a tomato here, setting it down, pressing her thumb briefly into the skin of a peach. Ben followed at a respectful distance, pausing at a display of painted ceramics, turning a small bowl over in his hands.

He did not see her lift the dried fig from the edge of an unattended basket. She ate it in two bites, clean and quick. The vendor returned, looked at the basket, frowned at a specific nothing, and Alessandra was already moving toward the far end of the stall.

She did not mention it. She didn't mention it in the way that she didn't mention most things—not from shame but because it occupied a category of information she had long since stopped bothering to justify, even to herself. Things came to her. She kept some of them. The city was large enough to absorb the difference.

"Where next?" Ben asked. He had put the guidebook away and had not retrieved it. She noticed this without comment, as though it were unremarkable, so that he would not feel rewarded and undo it.

"Up," she said.

*Up* proved to be a rooftop accessed through a building on a street without a name she could be bothered to provide, via a staircase that communicated through its steepness alone that they had no permission to be using it. At the third landing Ben said nothing. At the fifth he attempted to ask something and she said "Almost," and on the sixth the door opened onto open air and Rome in every direction—domes and ochre and television antennae and the enormous sky going arterial in the first suggestion of late afternoon.

She watched his face do what the city did to people whose minds were still sufficiently open to receive it. The competent social surface dropped away for a moment. He stood very still.

"Right," he said, quietly. "Alright then."

"You keep saying that."

"It's what I say when I don't have better words." He glanced at her. "Is that a flaw?"

She considered the skyline. "Probably."

"Mm." He didn't seem especially troubled by this. He went back to looking at Rome. Below them, ordinary life continued in complete indifference to their presence—a shutter opening, a Vespa on a distant street, a woman calling something from a window in a tone that required no translation.

Ben looked at it all with the same generous, unhurried attention, and Alessandra caught herself watching him watch it, which was not something she did.

She walked to the other side of the rooftop and looked at a different view.

---

The rain arrived with the theatrical abruptness Rome reserves for afternoons it has decided to dramatically restructure. One moment the sky was passable. The next it was not. There was no meaningful interval.

"Here—" She grabbed his wrist and pulled without thinking. They ran. She knew every awning on this street by its dimensions, and she aimed them at the one belonging to the old gelato shop on the narrow spur off the Via del Governo Vecchio. The owner, visible through the glass, looked up, identified her, and performed a very particular expression—not surprise, not welcome, something between recognition and resignation—before becoming deeply interested in wiping the counter.

They stood under the narrow fabric overhang and the rain hit the street with complete commitment. Ben was soaked down one side, where the awning ended and the open air began. He assessed the damage with an expression that traveled through mild shock and arrived, improbably, at amusement.

"Do you do this to everyone," he said, "or am I specifically selected?"

"You stepped in front of my motorcycle."

"You nearly ran me over."

"And yet here you are." She crossed her arms against the cold the rain had invented. "On a roof you weren't invited to. In a church that was locked."

"Given the door opened—"

"It opened because I opened it. That's a different category of open." She paused. "You should try the *stracciatella.*"

"Is this the part where I go inside?"

"It's the part where I tell you the *stracciatella* is good."

He looked at her with a directness she hadn't entirely prepared for. Whatever he was assessing, he took his time at it. Then he looked back at the street.

"I came here," he said, in a different register—quieter, addressed more or less to the rain—"because I needed to stop understanding everything." He said it plainly, as though it were a simple fact rather than an admission. "I'm very efficient at understanding things. I have a whole career of it. Papers. Frameworks. I can give you six interpretive models for anything you care to name." The rain clattered on the cobblestones. "I thought if I went somewhere where I genuinely didn't understand the grammar—not just the language—it might feel like—"

"A relief," Alessandra said.

He looked at her. "Yes. Exactly that."

The word *exactly* landed in a way she had not anticipated, and she stayed with it for a moment—the sensation of having named something correctly. She was, in general, accurate about people. She was accurate about them quickly and then she moved on, because the accurate version of a person was usually less interesting than the mystery had promised. But this one had apparently told her something true in exchange, which changed the accounting somehow.

She examined the rain. She thought about what to give back and what to keep.

"You understand more than you admit," she said, finally. "The way you looked at that church. That's not a man who doesn't understand."

He was quiet for a moment. "That's different."

"How."

"When I look at something beautiful, I'm not categorizing it. I'm just—" He seemed to run out of language and gesture at the gap where it had been. "Present, I suppose."

Alessandra said nothing. She looked at a point somewhere beyond the edge of the awning where the rain was doing something complicated with the light on the wet stones.

She had been about to say something else. She recognized the specific sensation—a word or several words assembled and moving toward her mouth—and she let them dissolve, quietly, before they arrived. She stepped out from under the awning instead. The rain had thinned to something negotiable.

"Come," she said. "It's passing."

---

They ended near the Pantheon as the evening turned the buildings amber and the stone of the piazza held the day's warmth like something being saved. She had her helmet under her arm. He had his hands in his pockets. The square had quieted into its evening version of itself, and they stood in it without the motion that had carried them all afternoon, and the stillness had a different quality than the rain—more voluntary, and therefore more legible.

Ben looked at the Pantheon's porch. He did not consult anything.

"Tomorrow," Alessandra said, in the tone she used when nothing was yet decided, "there's a place not in any book. Any edition."

"Another inaccessible church?"

"Something better." She did not specify. Specificity was not the point.

He nodded without rushing it. "What's your number?"

She held out her hand for his phone and he gave it over without hesitation, which she found interesting—a man who had handed over his transportation and his afternoon and now his phone, all without substantial negotiation. She typed. Not her name. A name she had used before when she'd been in a mood that she'd since examined and decided not to renounce.

She gave the phone back.

He looked at the screen. Read it. "*Trouble,*" he said.

"For accuracy."

One side of his mouth moved. He pocketed the phone with the same equanimity with which he had pocketed the 2009 guidebook, the same quality of receiving something and carrying it without making a performance of it.

She put the helmet on. She started the motorcycle. She rode out of the piazza and into the web of narrow streets that led everywhere and nowhere she couldn't find her way back from, and she did not look behind her, and her hand went once to her jacket pocket and touched the small smooth coolness of something she had lifted, very casually, from the ceramics stall while Ben was looking at the bowl, and she held it there for a moment against her palm—an ordinary blue bead, almost nothing, the kind of thing that falls from a display and lies unclaimed—before letting it go.

Behind her, Ben stood on the cobblestones of the piazza as the last of the evening light left the top of the columns, holding a phone with a number in it and very little certainty about anything, which was—he realized, adjusting his jacket against the cooling air, in the way that adjusting a jacket is sometimes the only ceremony available—precisely what he had come all this way to find.
