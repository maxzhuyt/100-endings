# The Morska Conspiracy
## Part III: The Underhold

The door did not creak.

Aya noticed it the way she noticed most things that mattered—not as a thought, exactly, but as a cold shift in the weight of the air, a wrongness that settled between her shoulder blades before her mind had caught up to name it. Old iron on older stone should have groaned. Should have given them something: the comfort of a world still behaving according to its obligations. Instead the door came open in silence, moving on hinges that had been oiled recently and deliberately, and the gas lamps in the corridor beyond stirred in air that had no business moving at all.

She crossed the threshold with her hand raised and open at her side. Not reaching. Not yet. But the immateria was there behind her sternum where she always kept it folded, and it pressed back against her awareness the way a sleeping animal presses back against warmth—present, and patient, and ready to wake at very short notice.

"Too quiet," Simeon said. He was good about keeping his voice down, when it counted. One of his better qualities.

"I know."

"Should we—"

"I know, Simeon."

The underhold opened before them in a long corridor of vaulted stone, the ceiling low enough that a tall man would have to mind himself, gas jets mounted at intervals in iron brackets that threw more shadow than light. Long shadows, angled wrong, climbing the walls at degrees that had no clean explanation. The smell was tallow and damp and beneath that something older—stone that had been wet for centuries and had arrived at a kind of peace with it. Katra moved without prompting to Aya's left and put her hand on the short blade at her hip. Not drawing it. Just resting there, fingers curled, the way you hold something you expect to need.

They made it perhaps thirty feet before the shadows arranged themselves into men.

Six of them, positioned in a loose arc that had clearly been assembled with some leisure and care. Heavy men, all of them, wearing guild colours that Aya's eyes catalogued before the rest of her had fully processed the situation. Truncheons. Hand-irons. And at the far end of the arc, one man with a short repeating crossbow already levelled at a height that suggested he had been holding it for some time and did not find the effort remarkable. He had the face of a man who pointed weapons at people professionally and had arrived, over a long career, at a settled indifference toward their responses.

And at the centre of the arc, in a high-backed chair that had no conceivable reason to be in a storage corridor and yet was there with the absolute conviction of furniture that had always belonged precisely where it sat, was Maren Voss.

*Oh,* Aya thought. *Oh. That is very bad.*

The Morska's guildmaster was a small woman, and the first error most people made was stopping there, at the smallness, and drawing the obvious wrong conclusions. The second error—for those who survived long enough to make it—was missing the quality of her stillness. She sat in the chair the way a woman sits in a throne room where she has received petitioners all her adult life, unhurried, immaculate, her grey coat without a crease in it, her iron-coloured hair swept back from a face that gave nothing away for free. Her hands lay folded in her lap and beneath them, resting across her knees with the easy familiarity of a carried thing, was a brass-handled pistol. She did not look like a woman who had, according to all available intelligence, spent the previous night in a gaol cell. She looked like a woman who had built a room around three people and then waited, pleasantly, for them to walk into it.

Her eyes found Aya.

"The little ghost," she said. The vault carried her voice the way vaults carry voices that have learned to use them. "I did wonder whether you'd come down yourself, or send one of your strays."

Aya did not move. She was thinking—quickly, and arriving at conclusions with a precision that would have pleased her, under any other circumstances.

*The raid. Theatre. The arrest—theatre. Every piece of intelligence we acted on, selected for us to act on. We walked through the door they left open and called it a breakthrough.*

"Hands," said the man with the crossbow, in the voice of a man who does not repeat himself.

Katra's blade was on the floor before the syllable had finished echoing. Simeon raised his hands without waiting to be asked—a reflex of good sense that Aya registered and filed away. She raised her own. The gesture bared her wrists above her gloves, the silver rings on her fingers catching amber in the gas-light. The mask she still wore. For another moment, at least.

Maren's gaze moved to it the way a reader's gaze moves to a word they've been waiting to reach.

"Take it off."

"Madame Voss—"

"That was not a request, dear."

The heavy man who stepped forward was not rough about it. He simply lifted it from her face—careful, almost—and stepped back, and held it, and Aya stood in the amber light of the underhold with her face open to the room and all the information coded into it spread out like a ledger on a desk.

Maren looked at her.

The silence lasted long enough to be a statement.

"Carvenay," she said finally, in the tone of someone reading from a document they prepared some time ago. "The youngest." A pause that had weight in it. "The one that takes after her mother." Another pause, smaller. "In more ways than one, it seems."

The cold that moved through Aya's chest was not surprise. She had always known this particular instrument existed and that the world had been holding it with patience above her. The surprise—if it was that—was the actuality of it. The landing. The way knowledge becomes experience without your permission. Her mother's face surfaced for a moment and she put it under. Not here. Not in front of this woman.

"I can make this worth your while." She heard her voice come out level. She was quietly proud of it.

Maren tilted her head a degree. "You'll want to be specific."

Aya kept her chin up. Kept her shoulders square and still. "My name. You know what it carries—what it costs. My father has creditors on three continents and a seat at the parliamentary table and there is nothing on this earth he fears more than the intersection of scandal and personal inconvenience." She held Maren's gaze and let it land. "Take the artifact. Take what you came for. But sell me back to him quietly, Madame Voss, and you will collect more than anything in that hold could bring you. More than I am worth to hang."

The word settled into the air between them and stayed there.

Maren was quiet for a long moment. Then she uncrossed her ankles, smoothed the front of her coat with both hands in a gesture of perfect composure, and rose from the chair with the ease of a woman for whom chairs are formalities.

"Lock them in the counting room," she said, to the middle distance, to the men arranged in the lamplight. "The girl in irons. The others may keep their hands free." She picked up the pistol as she turned, the motion no different from retrieving a dropped glove. "We'll discuss the matter of worth in the morning, Miss Carvenay, when I've had occasion to think carefully about what's been offered."

She walked past them without looking back. Her footsteps were quiet and even and she did not look at Aya again.

The gas lamps stirred as she passed them.

Aya stared at the middle distance of the corridor and felt the immateria pressing against the inside of her ribs in the way she had always imagined a second heartbeat might feel—steady, and listening, and waiting to be needed.

*Think,* she told herself.

The iron closed around her wrists.

*Think.*
