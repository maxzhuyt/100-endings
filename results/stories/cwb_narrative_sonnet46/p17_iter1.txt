# Court of the Dreaming: A Territorial Dispute

The gallery was largely empty. This was typical. The Dreaming's populace, being creatures of some imagination, generally found better things to do than observe procedural hearings. A bailiff—who had the proportions of a grandfather clock and the face of someone trying to remember a dream—rapped the floor once.

Dream took his seat.

The chamber oriented itself accordingly.

Across the aisle from each other at two long tables sat the parties. At the left: First Love, rosy of cheek, nervously arranging a small bundle of wildflowers that had been submitted as exhibit A. At the right: Mora, compact and dark-eyed, with the kind of stillness that suggested she had once sat on a great many things and was prepared to sit on more. She was grinning at nothing in particular.

A cackle escaped her. It traveled the full vertical length of the vaulted ceiling, found the acoustics agreeable, and returned.

Dream's expression did not change. This, in itself, was a form of expression.

"The court," said the bailiff, "convenes to hear the matter of First Love versus Mora, regarding territorial infringement upon a recurrent dreamscape. Claimant holds original architect's rights. Respondent is alleged to have infiltrated and materially altered said dreamscape to the detriment of its intended subject." He paused. "The subject is a schoolboy named Max."

"Thirteen," said First Love helpfully.

"Thank you," said Dream, in the tone of someone for whom being thanked would be redundant.

First Love rose. The wildflowers trembled.

"I'll be brief," First Love said, and was not. The original dreamscape was described in careful, almost reverent detail: a summer field, long grass, the particular quality of afternoon light that only exists in recurring dreams of adolescence. The girl—unnamed, as she was composite—sitting close enough that Max could count her eyelashes, which he never actually did because he was too nervous, which was, First Love explained, rather the point. The almost-kiss. The pleasant, unresolvable confusion of it.

"Years of work," First Love said, voice tightening. "The ratio of longing to hope was calibrated over forty-seven iterations."

Dream steepled his fingers.

"And then," First Love said, "she arrived."

Mora raised one hand in unasked-for acknowledgment.

What followed was a precise account of the corruption. The girl's playful mounting—First Love's voice strained slightly here, working hard to stay clinical—her weight shifting, her face beginning its alteration. The warmth souring into something older and heavier. The paralysis spreading through Max's limbs like cold water. The new face, which was somehow still hers, and yet very much not, settling onto his chest. The breath-taking. The specific, horrible intimacy of it.

First Love sat down.

The wildflowers had shed two petals.

Mora stood, unhurried.

"All accurate," she said. "Charming field. Nice work." She nodded at First Love, collegially. "My issue is this: the infrastructure was already there. A girl on a boy. Weight involved. Breath in close proximity." She spread her hands. "I didn't build the highway. I just took the exit."

"You *ate* him," said First Love.

"Breath," Mora said, precisely. "I ate *breath*. He has more." Another cackle surfaced, involuntary, ricocheting off stone. She pressed her lips together. It echoed anyway.

"And the terror?" said First Love.

Mora considered this. "The confusion," she said carefully, "was already there. I may have *deepened* it. But the boy found the composite girl compelling before and after the transition, which—" she gestured vaguely at First Love "—is arguably a compliment to the original design."

A silence.

"A girl," Mora concluded, "has to eat."

Dream had, throughout this exchange, the aspect of a man doing long division behind his eyes. His gaze moved from one party to the other with the slow deliberation of something astronomical.

It was during this pause that something shifted—fractionally, privately—in the geometry of his expression. Not amusement, exactly. Not cruelty, exactly. The thought passed through him like weather through a place that has no weather.

He spoke.

"The dreamscape," Dream said, "was lawfully constructed and lawfully registered. Incursion without consent of the architect constitutes violation." He looked at Mora. "You are found in breach."

First Love straightened.

"However," Dream continued, and First Love's straightening peaked and held, "the structural confusion introduced by the respondent cannot be cleanly excised without collapsing the original dream's architecture. The forty-seven iterations have been—" a pause "—*complicated*."

"Complicated," First Love repeated.

"The court's remedy is as follows: Mora retains limited access, circumscribed to a single visit per lunar cycle, on the condition that the transition point respects the integrity of the original emotional register for a minimum of—" Dream appeared to calculate, "—thirty seconds."

Mora's eyes brightened. This was considerably more than she'd expected.

First Love opened their mouth.

"The rationale," Dream said, before First Love could, "is developmental. The subject will require, eventually, an understanding that certain feelings contain their opposite. That the beloved can also be frightening. That weight can be tender and terrible at once." A pause that lasted two seconds longer than it needed to. "This court does not prevent that education."

The bailiff rapped the floor. The case was concluded.

First Love gathered the wildflowers with the careful movements of someone not crying.

Mora was already leaving. Another cackle rose unprompted, found the ceiling, enjoyed it.

Dream watched them go.

Somewhere, Max was asleep in his bed, and the field was there, and the girl was there, and the light was the color of something just about to end.

The court adjourned.
