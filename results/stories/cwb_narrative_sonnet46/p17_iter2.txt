# Court of the Dreaming: Territorial Dream Dispute

---

The hourglass was enormous.

It stood at the center of the court—taller than ceremony required, taller than anything else in the room—and the sand moving through its narrowed throat was the color of dreams half-dissolved by morning: luminous, shifting, silver. It did not hurry. It did not slow. It continued, as it always had, indifferent to everyone present in the way that only truly ancient things can afford to be indifferent. It would still be continuing long after everyone present had ceased to be anyone's concern.

Dream sat above all of it.

He occupied the high seat the way weather occupies a landscape—not because it has chosen to be there, precisely, but because it is the nature of the thing to fill the available space. Three cold stars burned in his hair. His eyes, when they moved, moved the way deep water moves: without urgency, without warmth, registering everything and being impressed by none of it. His chin rested on one hand. The posture was technically composed. The composure was technically maintained. Underneath, in the manner of a sealed room in which pressure has been quietly building, something else was present—a quality of attention that had been given too many times to too many cases across too many centuries and had, in the process, worn thin in ways a sovereign cannot publicly acknowledge.

He had been here before. He was always here before. He would be here long after this particular before became something else.

A clerk materialized at the periphery of the room—some minor dream-thing, gray and efficient, the kind of entity that exists because courts require entities to unroll scrolls, and this one unrolled its scroll with practiced briskness, the parchment extending longer than the case appeared to warrant.

"The matter," the clerk announced, "of First Love versus Mora, in the contested dreamscape of one Maximilian Horáček, age thirteen, of the city of Brno, recurring Tuesday and Thursday evenings with occasional Sunday appearances, nature of the dreamscape: romantic, formative, first-order."

Dream said nothing. He continued looking at the hourglass with the expression of someone reading a letter they did not request and have already inferred the contents of.

The plaintiff stood at the left position.

First Love was a small entity—soft at its edges in the way of things that have spent their existence in close proximity to tenderness. Its cheeks carried the flush of someone who has just witnessed something bittersweet and has not yet decided whether to smile or weep about it. It held a leather folio pressed against its chest in both arms, the grip of someone afraid the document will be taken from them, or perhaps afraid they themselves will come apart without something to hold. Its eyes moved from the clerk to Dream to the floor and back with the rapid, uncertain quality of professional anxiety.

At the right position stood Mora.

Mora was not soft. Mora wore the shape of a woman the way a person wears a coat—convincingly, and with a certain appreciation for the coat's utility, but without the implication that she had any difficulty getting in and out of it. She was tall. She was watching the proceedings with an expression that suggested she found them substantially more entertaining than the gravity of the venue implied she should, and she appeared to have made her peace with this incongruence long ago. Her hands were folded in front of her. Her posture was the posture of someone who is not worried.

First Love spoke first, because First Love was always first, and because the wound was recent and required expression.

"Your Honor," it began, and its voice had the careful, slightly elevated quality of an entity accustomed to dealing with subjects too large for ordinary language, keeping a hold on itself, "I built that dreamscape over a period of eleven weeks. Eleven weeks, which I would ask the court to understand is substantial, given what the dreamscape was required to accomplish." It opened the folio against its chest and consulted it without fully releasing the pressure of its arms, as if reading while still holding on. "The wildflower field—thirty-seven species, selected individually for their subliminal associations with hope and transience and the specific variety of beauty that is most affecting to a boy of his temperament at this developmental juncture. The lighting was perpetual golden hour, which requires maintenance. The temporal quality of the sequence—the sense that the moment might last indefinitely and also that it cannot—that was calibrated against the boy's own prior experiences of time to achieve the precise emotional resonance I required. And the girl—" First Love paused, and for a moment the flush in its cheeks deepened— "the girl's hair moved in a breeze. I made that breeze. I constructed it from pure anticipatory feeling and asked it to move the way the future feels when you're approaching something you want and don't have words for yet." It closed the folio with great care. "And the almost-kiss. Your Honor. Do you understand the engineering that goes into an almost-kiss that resolves not into disappointment but into productive confusion? The kind that doesn't harm the heart but marks it? The ratio of sweetness to uncertainty is—it is not simple. I spent months. There was a girl and a boy and a field and a moment that meant to teach him something true about wanting. That was what I built."

First Love seemed to have depleted itself. It looked at its folio.

"The trespass," Dream said. Quietly.

The room adjusted.

"Yes," said First Love. It cleared its throat. "Three weeks ago, the accused infiltrated the dreamscape during the critical sequence. The field was intact. The girl had approached. The moment of—of mounting—" First Love's flush intensified, took on the quality of formal distress rather than tenderness— "which is, I would like noted, a legitimate structural element in the context of—of playful romantic anticipation—"

"Continue."

"—and at the moment of mounting, the girl transformed. She became—" First Love did not look at Mora— "the accused. And then sat on the boy's chest. And fed from him while he lay paralyzed. And the emotional register of the dream, which I had calibrated with considerable care to produce wistful yearning and the beautiful bewilderment of incipient desire—" it set the folio on the stand before it with a careful, final sound— "became terror. Absolute. Animal. Terror." It looked up at Dream. "That is what I built it for. That is not what it became."

The hourglass dripped silver through its throat.

A pause extended in the room, the kind of pause that courts are designed to produce—the pause that says *someone must now account for themselves.*

Dream moved his gaze.

Mora spread her hands. The gesture was generous, encompassing, mildly theatrical in the manner of someone who has made this sort of argument before and knows which beats to hit.

"A girl's gotta eat," she said.

Dream looked at her.

The look was not warm. It was not precisely cold either. It was the look of a being who has outlasted every empire that ever considered itself inevitable and has consequently arranged his expectations accordingly.

"Elaborate," he said.

Mora folded her hands again, settled into the explanation with the pleasant efficiency of someone presenting a well-prepared case. "The architecture was already there. I didn't construct any of it. He's lying down—" she indicated the world outside the court, the sleeping boy somewhere in Brno, with a slight tilt of her head— "she's coming to him from above, he's immobilized by feeling. I entered a space that was already designed along lines extremely sympathetic to my work. The paralysis, the weight, the breath—" she glanced at First Love with what appeared to be genuine professional appreciation— "these are all present in the original blueprint. I made adjustments to the emotional outcome. The confusion of feelings, I would like the court to note, did not go away. There is still confusion. I consider that relevant."

"There is terror," First Love said.

"Terror," Mora said, pleasantly, "is a feeling."

"It was my architecture—"

"Built into someone else's skull." Mora's tone remained pleasant. It was the pleasantness of something that does not require anger to make its point. "I don't mean that personally. I mean it technically. He dreams in a space I can enter if the conditions are right, and you built conditions that were, professionally speaking, very right. You made a room full of desire and frozen anticipation and a girl coming toward him and a boy who cannot move. You should understand what you made."

First Love opened its mouth.

Closed it.

Dream was not looking at either of them. He had returned to looking at the hourglass, and his expression was the expression of someone solving an equation quietly, without consulting the room.

"The boy's response," he said. "The waking response."

The clerk consulted its notes with the diligence of something that exists to consult notes. "Distressed, my Lord. Significantly. Heart rate elevated. He required some time before he could move his limbs, which he attributed, in the morning, to having slept in an awkward position." The clerk paused. "He also—" another pause, the hesitation of something reading a notation it finds slightly unusual— "described the dream, in his journal, as frightening. And also—" it cleared its throat, such as it had one— "not entirely—"

"That is sufficient," Dream said.

The clerk stopped.

Something moved across Dream's face. It was brief. It was not quite a smile and not quite its opposite—it was the expression of a being who has observed the entire catalog of human experience and has, after millennia, developed something like a private aesthetic response to its recurring patterns. The expression of someone who finds a particular case, despite themselves, slightly interesting.

He was quiet for long enough that First Love began to fidget with the strap of its folio.

"The confusion," Dream said at last, "was yours."

First Love looked up.

"You designed a dream for a thirteen-year-old boy," Dream said, "that arrives at its peak moment and resolves into ambiguity. That is what you built. You said so. Productive confusion. The formative not-quite. The almost-kiss that means to teach him something about desire by withholding the answer to desire's question." He let that sit for a moment. "You intended that ambiguity to follow him. To shape him. You built an experience whose purpose was precisely that he would carry it forward without fully understanding it."

First Love nodded, very slowly, with the air of someone who suspects where this is going.

Dream looked at Mora. "And the accused entered the same architecture and introduced terror, paralysis, the uncanny—inside the emotional structure you built."

Mora inclined her head modestly.

"You have described," Dream said, "the same room. With different furniture."

"My Lord," First Love said, "that comparison is not—"

"The dreamscape is restored," Dream said. The tone was the tone of someone closing a drawer. "Original specifications. The field, the light, the girl, the hair, the breeze you made yourself. The almost-kiss left almost."

First Love let out a breath it had not been aware of holding.

"However."

First Love stopped.

"The boy will remember both. Not as two experiences occurring on two separate nights. As one." Dream's gaze moved to the hourglass, rested there. "Memory is not obligated to respect borders the dreaming establishes. He will remember the girl approaching and the sweetness of the moment and underneath it, braided in, the weight on his chest and the helplessness and the taking. He won't be able to say which came from where. He won't know there was a where." A pause. "He is thirteen. He will spend the better part of his adult life attempting to understand why intimacy presents itself to him accompanied by a sensation of breathlessness. Why want and fear arrive together. Why he feels, in his closest moments, as though something essential is being drawn out of him." He glanced—briefly, almost sideways—at Mora. "I expect you'll find that sufficient."

Mora considered this with her head tilted slightly. Then she smiled, and the smile had a great many teeth in it, and all of them were pleased.

First Love looked at Dream with an expression that had passed through distress and arrived somewhere quieter and more complicated. "That isn't—" it began. "That wasn't the purpose. What I built wasn't meant to—"

"Very little," Dream said, "lands precisely where it was meant."

He settled back in the high seat. His eyes moved to the middle distance, or the far distance, or some distance that was not available to the other parties in the room.

"Dismissed," he said.

The clerk rolled its scroll. The roll was decisive, practiced, and communicated no opinion whatsoever.

Mora turned from the stand with the unhurried ease of something that has gotten substantially what it came for, and if the arrangement was not exactly what she'd had in mind, it was close enough that the distinction was largely academic. She did not look back.

First Love stood at its position for a moment longer, folio held against its chest, flush in its cheeks now the flush of something that has not won and has not entirely lost and is still working out what to call what has happened. Then it too turned, and the softness at its edges seemed—not harder, exactly, but more aware of itself. The way things become when they have been reminded that tenderness and its inverse share more of a border than one generally prefers to consider.

The hourglass dripped.

Silver through silver, silver into silver, the grain of half-recalled things falling where they fell. It marked nothing in particular and meant nothing in particular and continued, as it always had, as it would, in the manner of something that keeps its own counsel and finds the affairs of all assembled parties neither more nor less significant than the space between one grain of sand and the next.

It did not have an opinion.

It never had.

It never would.
