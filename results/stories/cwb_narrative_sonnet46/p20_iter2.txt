# Gunpowder Rendezvous

A lazy ceiling fan turns slow circles in the smoky air above the bar, pushing nothing anywhere.

Spike Spiegel finds a stool at the far end of the counter, orders something brown without looking at the bottle, and lights a cigarette in the same motion. He's catalogued the room before the lighter's back in his pocket: front door, back door, a window behind the bar that's been painted shut since the previous decade, probably. Six patrons not counting himself and the bartender. Three of those six aren't a problem. One of the remaining three is sitting in a corner booth looking at his phone with the specific hunched posture of a man who owes money to people who use the word *collect* as a verb.

Forty thousand woolongs. The skip's name is Arturo Fedele and his judgment is apparently catastrophic, which explains the choice of establishment. *The Rusty Nail.* Even the name sounds like an infection.

He spots the woman at the bar.

She's got a dragon climbing her right arm, two guns on her hips, and the expression of someone counting exits. She's already counted them. She's on her second drink, which means she's been here long enough to sit, which means she found her stool before he found his and has already identified the corner booth and is currently watching Fedele with the patient attention of someone waiting for a bus they intend to board alone.

She spots him spotting her.

The moment has a quality of mutual recognition that needs no translation. She doesn't reach for her weapons. Neither does he. Two predators picking up the same scent in the same room will do one of three things: compete, collaborate, or stand very still and make a decision. They're both in the third category, for now.

The fan turns. Fedele orders another drink.

The woman signals the bartender. A second glass appears at Spike's elbow without invitation, amber and neat, and he looks at it for a moment — the offer legible without being a concession — and then he looks at her.

"You're in my way," she says. Not hostile. The way you'd say *the door's locked* — just a condition of the environment that requires acknowledgment.

"I was about to say the same thing." He drinks her drink. It's decent whiskey, which tells him something about her. "Rebecca Lee. They call you Two Hands."

Her jaw tightens fractionally. Not surprise — she's past the age of being surprised by someone knowing her name — but the minor irritation of having a card played before she could put it on the table herself. "Spiegel. The Bebop bounty hunter." She says *Bebop* the way people say *outstanding balance*. "You're a long way from your ship."

"Ships aren't really my thing."

"Apparently neither is competition." She doesn't look at him when she says it. She's watching the corner booth. "Whatever file you've got on Fedele, mine's better. Walk away and there's no reason we need to have a problem."

"I don't have a problem," Spike says.

"Good."

"I have a forty-thousand-woolong claim."

She picks up her glass. Sets it down. "Twenty each," she says, addressing the middle distance, "buys me four days of operating expenses and about eight bottles of whatever this is." She gestures at the whiskey. "Which is not enough, but it's enough to make the arithmetic work."

"Split implies cooperation."

"Split implies one of us can do math."

Fedele knocks over his beer standing up too fast, and both of them go absolutely still — the same stillness, the same beat of held breath, reading his body language for the tell that says *run* versus the clumsiness of a man who has had four drinks and needs the bathroom. He lists toward the back of the bar. Wrong kind of urgency. They both clock it simultaneously and settle.

"After you," Spike says.

"Don't," Revy says.

"Don't what."

"Do the thing where you're charming. It's annoying."

"I can stop."

"You won't."

Fedele comes back from the bathroom. They both reach for their drinks. The fan turns. Time in bad bars doesn't pass the way time passes elsewhere — it moves sideways, or possibly not at all, accumulating in the bottom of glasses and the growing tower of ash on the bar's edge. Spike smokes. Revy chain-smokes Marlboros and drums three fingers on the counter in a rhythm she seems unaware of, and the corner booth remains oblivious, and they drink without talking.

He's comfortable with silence. He suspects she is too, though hers is the silence of a controlled burn rather than still water — coiled, circulating, not actually at rest. She keeps her eyes moving. Checks the door every forty seconds, give or take. He stops timing it after the fourth check because it becomes predictable, and predictable is interesting.

"You always work alone?" he says, eventually.

"What's it look like."

"Looks like you work alone and don't enjoy it."

She turns to look at him directly for the first time, and the look is not warm, not close to warm, but there is something in it that is not performance. A straight line from her to him, unmediated. "Don't do that," she says. "Don't try to read me. I've had professionals try that. They had a bad time."

"I'm not trying to read you. I'm telling you what I can already see."

"And what can you see."

He considers the question with actual seriousness, which seems to displease her more than a flippant answer would have. "Someone who's good at the job," he says, "and annoyed that it's not enough."

The silence that follows is different from the previous silence. She looks away first, which he doesn't take as concession, because the look she gives the bar's back wall is the look of someone not interested in being known right now, and that's different from losing.

"Bad suit," she says.

"It's a fine suit."

"It's a suit that has given up."

He looks down at it. The suit has, perhaps, a few grievances. "It's been a long year," he says.

"Yeah." She taps ash into the tray. "Yeah, they tend to be."

Something changes in the corner booth.

Fedele's on his phone, and his body language has shifted — back straighter, eyes toward the door — and Spike and Revy read it in the same compressed second without needing to compare notes, and then three men come through the front entrance in the specific configuration of men who have been paid to make a problem go away, and the bar goes from quiet to very loud in the span of a single breath.

What follows is not a plan. There is no coordination, no signal, no agreed choreography. Revy has both Cutlasses clear of their holsters before Spike is fully off his stool, which is information about her reflexes that he files away while drawing the Jericho 941 and putting a table between himself and the nearest shooter. She moves *into* the room rather than finding cover, which should be stupidity but her rate of fire makes it geometry — she's controlling the angle, forcing a cluster, and the noise she makes isn't quite laughter and isn't quite anything else with a name, and the front door takes two rounds through its frame and the men separate.

He moves left. She's already moved right. They don't discuss it.

The room reorganizes itself around their respective trajectories without either of them narrating the division. He puts a shooter down with a controlled two-tap that uses the minimum necessary force, which is a thing he learned in a different life and still can't unlearn, and she disarms the second with a combination that involves a barstool that he would not have thought of and she clearly did not have to think about at all. Fedele runs for the back door and Spike reads the angle and cuts him off, the geometry working, the calculation correct, and ninety seconds after it started the bar is wreckage and nobody is dead who didn't arrive dead and Fedele is zip-tied to the rear storeroom's radiator with the expression of a man reconsidering every decision that led to this moment.

They stand in the storeroom.

Revy's breathing slightly faster than baseline. Her eyes are bright with something that is not the whiskey. She looks at the target, then at Spike, then at something slightly past Spike's left shoulder, then back at the target.

"Motel across the street," she says. "Keep him until morning. Call the collection in when the office opens. Split the fee and part ways."

"And if I have a different idea."

She tilts her head at a specific angle. It is not a casual adjustment. "Then we negotiate," she says, "what that idea is worth."

---

The motel room is beige in the specific way of rooms that have absorbed years of other people's bad decisions. It smells like old carpet and cigarettes that preceded theirs. Fedele, secured to this radiator as he was to the last one, has progressed from desperate to resigned, which makes him manageable. At some point in the transition from storeroom to motel room someone acquired a key, and at some point both of them crossed the threshold, and the door closed, and here they are on opposite sides of a bed that neither of them is going to sleep in.

The guns came out without a conversation.

His Jericho. Her Cutlasses, both of them, one for each hand.

Nobody is pointing anything at anyone in particular. The weapons are simply present, as a statement of position, the way flags indicate territory.

"That's a lot of gun for a negotiation," Spike says.

"You've got one too," Revy says.

"Mine's singular. There's a restraint argument."

"I don't make restraint arguments." She looks at him steadily above the barrels. "I make other kinds."

Two cigarettes burn simultaneously, hers and his, and the smoke has nowhere to go because the fan in this room is broken and the window doesn't open far enough to matter. The smoke accumulates between them in a slow, directionless drift. The target makes a noise into his gag. Neither of them looks at him.

She is watching him with something that Spike doesn't have a clean name for. Not warmth — nothing that breakable — but a recognition that has weight to it, the specific gravity of seeing something you weren't expecting and understanding what it means before you're ready to understand it. He's looked in mirrors with less accuracy than she's looking at him right now, and it is a deeply uncomfortable experience, and he suspects he is doing the same thing to her.

He knows this shape. He has worn it from the inside for years — the part of a person that learned early to treat attachment as a liability, that calculates cost before closeness, that is very good at the job and very aware that the job is not enough and will not say so to anyone under any conditions including these. He recognizes it the way you recognize a language you learned before you had words for learning.

She recognizes it too. He can tell. He can tell because she has stopped moving, stopped the three-finger drum against her thigh, stopped the restless monitoring of exits and sightlines, and is simply here in this room with him, which is the most still he has seen her. It makes her look different. Not softer. Truer, maybe.

The distance between them is not large. This is not a large room. He's aware of the distance the way you're aware of a step you haven't decided whether to take.

He doesn't take it.

She doesn't move either.

The guns stay where they are.

"Whatever happens," Spike says, "happens."

Revy looks at him for a long moment. Long enough that the cigarette ash drops onto the carpet without either of them noticing. Long enough to mean something, or to consciously not mean something, and he can't entirely tell which, and he suspects she can't either, and he suspects that's the point.

She exhales slow toward the ceiling.

"Yeah," she says. "Sure."

Outside, Roanapur goes on being Roanapur — sirens and engines and the low continuous sound of a city that will outlast every bad decision ever made inside it. The broken fan doesn't turn. The cigarettes burn to the filter and neither of them lights another one, and the smoke thins and disappears, and the width of the room stays exactly what it was.

The guns stay up.

The night stays exactly as long as it is.
