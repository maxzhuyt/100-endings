# Darkroom Revelations

Here is what I know about myself: I make the first move. I always have. In eighth grade I passed Tyler Okonkwo a note that said *I like you, do you like me, circle one,* and yes, I drew the circles myself, because why leave that kind of thing to chance. In tenth grade I told Priya Mehta she had beautiful hands while we were waiting for the bus and she looked at me like I'd spoken a foreign language and two weeks later she kissed me behind the gym, which meant I was right and I'd been patient about it for fourteen whole days, which was, for me, practically archaeological patience. I know what I want. I decide. I act. I have a reputation, which I have cultivated, which I deserve.

I have also been watching Elliot Marsh for four months.

Not in any way I would have admitted—not in any way I have admitted, to anyone, including myself until approximately right now, which is me getting ahead of the story. But there it is. His boots first, actually, those broken-in brown leather things he wears with everything regardless of weather logic, and then his name on the sign-up sheet, and then the boots again, and then the way he loaded film into a camera with his back to the room like the task required privacy. His schedule: Tuesday/Thursday studio time, Mondays he ate lunch in the east stairwell, he was always slightly early to class but sat in the back, which is a combination that interested me for reasons I couldn't have articulated. He bought coffee from the cart outside the science building. He wore a burnt-orange scarf that was clearly thrifted and clearly not ironic.

I know his schedule because I paid attention. I paid attention because something about him caught in me like a splinter—small, specific, inexplicably persistent.

I did not approach him for four months because I was being strategic, I told myself. Gathering information.

This is a very sophisticated way of saying I was nervous, which I will acknowledge only here, in this telling, and which I would dispute vigorously in any other context.

---

The darkroom smells like chemicals and like time. That's the only way I can put it—like something being counted down or counted up, like the air itself is in the middle of a process. The red safelight makes everything look like the inside of a memory, and when Elliot turned to set his tray down on the counter next to mine, I couldn't quite read his expression, which was strange, because reading expressions is another thing I'm good at.

"Your negatives or mine first," he said. Not a question, just an opening.

"Yours," I said. "I want to see the light ratios you were talking about in class."

This was true. It was also a reason to watch him work, which I was aware of and did not examine.

We moved around each other in the small space with the careful precision of people who are conscious of the other person's body without wanting to appear conscious of it. His elbow once, briefly, and I felt it in a way that was disproportionate to an elbow. The chemicals made my eyes water slightly. The red light softened the edges of everything—the counter, my hands, his profile when he leaned over the developing tray.

Outside was February. In here it was something else, some suspended middle season that only existed between these walls.

---

We talked about composition for a while. Safe and genuine—I actually cared about his framing choices, the way he'd learned to find negative space, how his eye worked. Then we talked about exposure times and I told him he was pulling his prints too fast and he said he knew, he liked them slightly underexposed, and I said *why* and he was quiet for a moment in a way that meant he was deciding something.

"There's this one," he said. He'd printed it already, earlier in the week, and he had the contact sheet. He showed me. It was a self-portrait—him in three-quarter profile against a pale background, wearing what I recognized, now that I looked, as a women's blouse. Cream-colored, draped differently than anything else I'd seen him in. His expression in the photograph was doing something complicated. Not performing. Thinking.

"It's for the series," he said. "I told Marchand it was about thrift culture. Which is also true."

He said *also true,* and I noticed the *also.*

"The light's good," I said. "The drape of it. The fabric does something interesting in that contrast."

He looked at the contact sheet rather than at me. "I found it at the bins on Fletcher Street. I just—" A pause. "It felt right in a way I didn't know how to explain to anyone. So I photographed it instead."

There was a thing I could have said. It gathered in my mouth the way things do when you're about to be very direct with someone, when you're about to name the thing and move toward it, which is how I operate, which is my mode, my reputation, my self.

I looked at his print instead.

"That makes sense," I said.

He looked up at me, briefly, and something in his face settled, like a print going into the fixer.

We went back to work.

---

My phone buzzed in my pocket. I registered it the way you register weather: a piece of information about the outside world that currently had nothing to do with you.

Marcus, probably. We'd been texting about a party Saturday and I was supposed to let him know, and the phone buzzing was that version of my life arriving at the door of this one asking to be let in. Marcus, or Morgan from my bio study group, or my mother with one of her check-in questions that required a check-in answer. Someone who expected me to be the version of myself that knew what she wanted and moved toward it without complication.

The chemicals smelled sharp and specific. Elliot was sliding a print gently into the stop bath.

I left the phone in my pocket.

We had, I estimated, forty-five minutes left.

---

His language, when he got there, was careful. He picked up each word and set it down with both hands, testing the surface first.

"I've been thinking a lot lately," he said, "about—I don't know how to say this exactly."

"You don't have to say it exactly."

He looked at the developing tray. Something was coming up slowly in the chemical bath, a print he'd shot outside the library, bare tree branches against a pale sky. We both watched it for a moment.

"About whether the way I present myself is—" A pause. "Whether it's the whole thing. Whether there's more that I haven't figured out how to—" He stopped. "I don't know yet. I think I don't know yet. Maybe that's the thing I know most clearly. The not-knowing."

The print was getting darker by degrees, the branches thickening, whole where they had been ghost-pale.

Inside me, two things happened simultaneously. The first was the part of me that wanted to lean toward him and say something decisive—to name what he was describing so he didn't have to, to make it easier with my certainty, to be useful in that particular way. This is what I do. This is the instinct.

The second was something quieter. A recognition that arrived without announcement: if I reached over and made this easier for him, I would be doing it for myself. Because I like moving first. Because I like being the one who sees the thing and says the thing. And this was not mine to see first. He was in the middle of seeing it.

I let the silence sit.

"I've been wearing things," he said. "In private. Or—like with the blouse. And then photographing them, which is the only way I knew how to look at it directly."

"Your work has gotten more interesting this semester," I said.

He made a sound that was possibly a laugh.

"I mean it," I said. "It has. You can tell when someone is making work about something real."

He pulled the print from the stop bath. The branches were dark and clear and precise now, fully themselves.

---

I was watching a print develop—my own this time, a shot I'd taken through a window, the subject obscured by reflected light—when I understood something.

The thing I'd been cataloguing for four months: the thrift-shop boots, the east stairwell lunches, the careful back-turned concentration with the film loader. I had been watching someone who was always slightly in the process of something. Not incomplete—not less than—but in motion, in the middle of becoming whatever he was becoming. There was a quality to him like a question held with patience, and I had found it, from across a photography classroom, privately, secretly, without knowing what I was finding, extraordinarily compelling.

I thought I'd been watching him the way I watched anyone I was interested in. Gathering data.

I had been watching him the way you watch something develop.

And I had—without framing it this way, without owning it—found it beautiful. Found *him* beautiful. Found the uncertainty of him beautiful in a way that I, who had always known what I wanted, who had always moved directly toward it, had not had words for, which was probably why I'd spent four months not having words for it and calling it strategic patience instead.

The image came up in the tray. The window, the reflected light—and the figure visible through both, blurred by the glass, not-quite-resolved, present and in the process of becoming visible.

I pulled it at the right moment this time.

---

We cleaned up in the order we'd made the mess, which established a comfortable rhythm that neither of us commented on. He capped the chemical bottles. I hung the last prints on the line. The red light made them look like they were lit from within.

"Same time next week?" he said.

"I was thinking Wednesday," I said. "I have better negatives to work with by Wednesday."

He considered this. "Wednesday works."

He was putting his coat on—the regular one, the grey canvas one I'd seen him wear since October—and I looked at the print I'd pulled, the blurred figure, the double-exposed light.

"The self-portrait," I said. "The one with the blouse."

He looked up.

"You should put it in the show," I said. "In February. The department one. It's some of your best work this year. Maybe your best."

He held my eyes for a moment in that red-softened dark, and something moved across his face that I didn't try to name. Then he picked up his bag.

"I'll think about it," he said.

"That's all I'm saying," I said.

He almost smiled. "I know."

He held the door. I walked out first, and the ordinary fluorescent light of the hallway was very bright and very cold, and behind me the darkroom door swung shut on everything that had been suspended in it and I thought: Wednesday. I have better negatives by Wednesday.

Which was true.

Also true.
