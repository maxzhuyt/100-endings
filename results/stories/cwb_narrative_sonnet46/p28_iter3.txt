# Seven Minutes Back

The quiz came back with a 54 written in red at the top, and beneath it, in Mr. Okafor's cramped handwriting: *See me.* Dani folded it in half before anyone could see, but Priya had already leaned over from the next desk.

"What'd you get?"

"Fine," Dani said. "I got fine."

This was seventh grade science, period three, and the classroom smelled like the hand sanitizer dispenser by the door that someone had broken so it kept dispensing on its own. Outside the window, the parking lot shimmered. It was the kind of February that couldn't decide whether to be winter anymore, everything gray-wet and halfway to something else.

The day didn't improve. At lunch, Dani went to sit at the table by the window where Marcus and Bea were already set up with their trays, and caught a foot on the chair leg mid-slide, and watched twelve ounces of chocolate milk travel in a clean arc across the table and into Marcus's lap. The sound Marcus made was involuntary and high-pitched. The cafeteria did not go silent—it was too loud for that—but the immediate radius did, that little bubble of attention that was somehow worse than a whole-room silence because it was so *focused*.

"Dani," Marcus said.

"I'm sorry. I'm so—here, use my—" She grabbed napkins and held them out and Marcus took them without looking at her and that was, somehow, the worst part.

She sat down. She ate half her sandwich. She thought about transferring schools.

The package was on the kitchen counter when she got home.

Her grandmother had sent it—the return address was her grandmother's loopy handwriting, the Tampa zip code Dani had memorized from birthday cards. The box was small and dense. Inside, under three layers of bubble wrap, was a watch in a thin cardboard sleeve. Not a phone-watch, not one of the ones that showed notifications. Just a watch. Round face, slim black band, the face itself a dark matte gray. On the bottom of the sleeve, in her grandmother's handwriting on a Post-it: *Won this in a raffle at the senior center!! Thought my favorite middle schooler could use it more than me!! Love Grandma Rosie.*

Dani turned it over. On the back, stamped into the metal: *ChronoFlex Personal Wellness Series. Seven-day trial. See enclosed documentation for warranty.* There was no enclosed documentation.

She put it on. The band was slightly too large, so it sat loosely on her wrist, and the face glowed a faint blue when she tapped it—just the time, 4:17 PM, and beneath the time a small number she didn't look at closely enough to register. She was already thinking about the quiz. Already thinking about Marcus and the napkins and the way he hadn't looked at her.

The watch face flickered.

Just once, just a blink, the numbers going dark and then coming back. 4:17. She figured it needed charging. She figured she'd look for a cable later. She went upstairs to start her homework and forgot about it.

---

Three days later was when Marcus's science fair project fell apart.

Dani had been there when he built it—she'd sat on his basement floor for two weekends helping him hot-glue the circuit board housing while he explained, for the fourth time, how the light sensor was supposed to work. The project had a title on a tri-fold board in Marcus's mother's professional-quality printing. It had a working prototype. It had taken him eleven weeks.

The prototype stopped working twenty minutes before the judging started.

Dani watched him crouch over the table in the gym, pressing the test button, getting nothing. His face went through several expressions quickly. Around them, other projects hummed and blinked. A girl from eighth grade was demonstrating something with a motor that everyone kept watching.

"Marcus," Dani said.

"It worked this morning." He pressed it again. "It *worked this morning.*"

The judges were three tables away. One of them was already writing something on a clipboard.

Dani didn't think about what she was doing. She was just frustrated—for him, and also somehow for herself, for the quiz and the milk, for all the things that had gone wrong this week that shouldn't have—and she jabbed at the watch face with two fingers the way she'd jab at an elevator button she'd already pressed.

The gym disappeared.

Not dramatically. Not like a film cut. More like a blink—her eyes closed and opened and she was still in the gym but the judges were four tables away, and Marcus was standing straight with his hands in his pockets with that specific posture he had when he was trying to look confident, and the prototype was glowing green the way it was supposed to.

Dani stood very still.

The sounds in the gym were slightly flat, like a recording of the gym rather than the gym itself. The fluorescent lights were the same fluorescent lights but something about them was almost a half-shade warmer than she remembered. She looked down at the watch. The number on the face—she looked at it this time, actually looked—was a 6. It had been a 7 before. She was almost certain it had been a 7.

The judges reached Marcus. The prototype did everything it was supposed to do. He got a blue ribbon. An actual physical blue ribbon that he held with both hands afterward and then immediately pretended he wasn't that excited about.

On the drive home—Marcus's mom, minivan, Dani in the back row—Dani sat with her wrist in her lap and tried to understand what had happened. She did not fully try. It was the kind of thing where trying too hard felt dangerous. The watch read 6. The watch had read 7 before. She had jabbed at it and something had gone backward by some number of minutes and the problem had simply not happened.

She looked out the window at the passing street.

Maybe it needed recalibrating, she thought. Maybe the 7 had been a setting.

She didn't think about it after that, the way you don't think about a door that opens when it should have been locked.

---

The next four days were the best days Dani had had in months.

She used it during English, when she said the wrong thing about the poem and everyone laughed—jabbed the watch, said the right thing, got a surprised nod from Ms. Chen that felt extraordinary. She used it in the lunch line when she dropped her tray—jabbed, didn't drop it. She used it during passing period when she waved at Priya and Priya didn't see her and it became the specific misery of a wave that goes unreturned—jabbed, didn't wave, crisis avoided. Then again in gym, when she airballed a free throw so completely that the ball hit the bleachers—jabbed, tried more carefully, got rim, which was near enough to dignity to count.

Each time: the slight flatness of sound, the half-shade of light. Each time: the small correction that made everything smooth.

The number went down. She caught it at 5, then 3. But she was catching it at odd moments, sideways, and she didn't stop to think about what the number was counting. She assumed it was like a pedometer. Some kind of activity tracker. Her grandmother had won it at a senior center; it was probably a wellness thing.

On Thursday she woke up and her pants were too short.

Not by much. A centimeter, maybe. She stood in front of her mirror and looked at her ankles, bare below the hem, and thought: *dryer.* She thought: *I probably shouldn't have put those in the dryer.* She pulled on another pair and went to school.

On Friday something was different with her voice. Not wrong, exactly. Just different. Lower in a way that felt unfamiliar in her throat. She spoke as little as possible.

On Saturday she stood in the bathroom mirror and looked at her jaw and thought: *stress.* She thought: *it's been a weird week.* She thought about the quiz, still a 54 even now, and about Marcus and the blue ribbon, and about the poem and Ms. Chen's nod. She thought the jaw looked like she'd been clenching in her sleep. She got a glass of water and went back to bed.

The watch on her nightstand flickered in the dark.

The number it showed was 2.

---

Monday, Priya caught up with her in the hallway between second and third period and stopped, a step back, and looked at her.

"When did you get so tall?" she said.

"I'm not tallerhell," Dani said. She had started to say *I'm not taller* and then said *hell* because she'd noticed Priya's face doing something she didn't have a name for.

"No, you are. You're like—" Priya tilted her head. "When did that happen."

"Growth spurt," Dani said. "I think. Or something."

Priya was still looking at her the way you look at an optical illusion after you've seen it both ways and can't get it back to the first way. "Yeah," she said. "Yeah, probably." And then the bell rang and they went in opposite directions and Dani stood in the hallway outside Mr. Okafor's room and did not go in immediately.

Third period, Mr. Okafor handed back a worksheet. He called the names alphabetically, the way he always did. He got to Dani's row and looked at her and then looked down at his roster as if checking something. "Daniel—" he started, and then said, "I'm sorry. Dani." He handed over the paper with a formality he did not use with anyone else, a slight nod, and moved on.

She stared at the worksheet. Her name was at the top of it in her own handwriting. *Dani.* He knew her name. He had known her name for six months.

After school, she passed the front office, and the secretary—who always called her *hon* and once gave her a mint from a bowl on the desk when she was waiting to call home about her forgotten lunch—looked up from the computer and said, "Can I help you, young lady?" In a tone that meant: *I don't know who you are.*

Dani walked faster.

She went to the bathroom at the end of the science wing, the one that was usually empty this late in the afternoon. The mirror above the sinks was the long institutional kind, slightly wavy. She made herself look at it.

For a moment she thought the mirror was broken. Then she understood that she was seeing herself.

She was—older was too simple a word. She was *more*. More face, more jaw, more height. Not an adult—she couldn't be an adult—but not twelve either. She looked like herself at seventeen, eighteen, looked like the image she'd sometimes tried to construct when she imagined the future, except the construction was wrong because it wasn't supposed to be here yet, it wasn't supposed to be now. She was wearing her seventh-grade clothes and she was not a seventh grader.

She looked down at the watch.

The face showed a 2. And below the 2, something she hadn't seen before: a reflection. Hers. But not this one—the old one, the real one, the small anxious one with the folded quiz in her pocket. Twelve. As she watched, the image faded. Just the 2 left. And under it, smaller, so small she had to bring the watch close to her face:

*Remaining draws: 2.*

She sat down on the floor.

The tiles were cold. The faucet dripped. She sat there for a long time with the watch on her wrist and tried to understand the accounting.

Seven at the start. Seven chances, seven—*draws*. Whatever the watch had given her. And she had spent five of them: the science fair, the English poem, the lunch tray, the wave at Priya, the free throw. Five moments she had taken back. And between those moments she had grown—not toward something, but away from something. Away from now. The watch had drawn forward from somewhere to do what it did, had taken account of something and applied it to her, and what it had applied was *time*.

She was two chances away from zero.

And somewhere in the last four days, something was wrong. She felt it the way you sometimes feel a mistake before you can name it—a misalignment, a loose gear. She thought of Priya's face. She thought of Mr. Okafor saying *Daniel.* She thought of something on the edge of her memory, something about today, something about—

Marcus.

She hadn't seen Marcus today.

---

She texted him three times walking home and got no answer. She called once and it went to voicemail. She told herself this meant nothing. He sometimes put his phone on silent for homework. He sometimes forgot to text back.

She got home and sat at the kitchen table and thought about it.

She had used the watch during the science fair. She had gone back—how far? Long enough to fix the prototype. Long enough that when the judges got there, everything worked. She had run the memory of that afternoon back and forward, and she couldn't find the place where she'd changed something she shouldn't have. Marcus had gotten his ribbon. Marcus had been fine.

But she'd changed the gym, in some small way she couldn't locate. A sound a half-shade flat. A light a half-shade warm. She had made one moment smooth and the texture had shifted somewhere downstream.

Priya texted her at 7 PM. A short text, the kind that meant the sender had typed and deleted several longer versions: *hey. did you hear about marcus.*

Dani's thumbs went still.

*No*, she typed. *What happened.*

*He was in an accident after school today. On his bike. He's okay,* Priya wrote. Then: *I think. His mom called my mom. He has a broken arm. And like a concussion I think.*

*He's okay though?*

*Yeah. I think yeah.*

Dani set the phone on the table. She looked at the watch. She thought: he was in an accident on his bike. She thought: on his bike. She thought: he always bikes home from school except on Fridays when his mom picks him up, and sometimes, on the days when it's warm enough, he doesn't take the trail route, he takes the road route, the faster one that goes past the lumber yard, except he doesn't take the road route when he's carrying something bulky, something he can't easily manage one-handed on the handlebars, like—

Like a science fair project.

He had taken the road route today because he hadn't needed to carry anything home. He hadn't needed to carry anything home because the project had worked. The project had worked because she had jammed the watch three days before and sent him back to a version of himself standing casually with his hands in his pockets, prototype glowing green, and she had removed from the equation whatever Marcus had been doing before that moment, which must have included—she thought—testing a different route. The trail route. Something slower, something that would have taken him twenty minutes longer to get home, twenty minutes that would have put him somewhere else on the road.

She had done this.

She hadn't meant to. She had meant to give him the blue ribbon. She had meant to fix it.

But the watch face now read 2, and below the 2 the small reflection of herself at twelve was gone, and the number below it read: *Remaining draws: 2.*

She sat in the kitchen for a long time. Her mother called her for dinner and she went and sat at the table and ate something and said the right number of words and went back upstairs. She did the math the way you do terrible math, slowly, trying to find the error.

*One more rewind*: maybe she goes back. Maybe she doesn't. Maybe it takes two. If it takes two, then there are zero left. Zero left and she is standing in a mirror looking at a stranger and Marcus has a broken arm and the world is split wrong and she used her last draw going back and now she's stuck.

Or: she does nothing. She keeps the 2. She keeps whatever she has left. And Marcus has a broken arm and she is a twelve-year-old in a body that doesn't match and Priya looks at her the way you look at something you can't place.

She lay on her bed and looked at the ceiling.

The watch face showed 2, then showed 1, then showed 2 again, as if uncertain.

---

She pressed the button during lunch the next day, in the cafeteria, before she'd thought it through completely.

She chose a specific moment. The previous afternoon, two hours before the accident—she'd felt the watch pulse under her fingers and thought: *there.* Back to a point where Marcus was still in school, still in the building, back far enough that she could correct the thing she'd corrupted. She didn't know exactly how the correction worked. She only knew it had worked five times before and she was trusting the sixth.

The cafeteria disappeared.
