# Blood Debt

The fighter from Jigen-ryū arrived first.

His name was Daisuke, and he came through the northern archway and stopped, letting his eyes do what they needed to do. The temple had been a temple once in the way that a man with no teeth had once chewed meat—the structure was complete enough to name but its purpose had evacuated long ago, leaving rubble, columns with their faces knocked off, a section of roof that had given up and opened itself to the sky. Through the gap, a low grey light came in and fell across the floor at a shallow angle, illuminating nothing generously. Forty, perhaps forty-five people stood pressed against the remaining walls—merchants' sons and dockworkers and at least one man whose clothing suggested he had no business being here at all. They watched Daisuke take stock of the room with the attention of men who had exchanged money over the outcome.

He took stock of the room.

East wall: three columns, two intact, one reduced to a stump at waist height. The collapsed roof section above, which meant compromised footing in the northeast corner where debris had scattered and not been cleared. West wall: cleaner, fewer obstacles, which was where Wei would want to be. South entrance, where Wei would appear. And the floor—pale stone, old, the kind of pale that had never been white but had been trying. Fractured near the center in a diagonal crack running roughly northwest to southeast, and through the crack, a thin movement of water, finger-wide, trekking south in no particular hurry, catching light from the roof gap and doing nothing useful with it.

Daisuke filed the water away.

He moved to the northern end of the floor, turned south, and let his arms hang at his sides.

He did not have to wait long.

---

Wei came through the southern arch without announcement, without pause at the threshold, without any of the gestures that might signal he understood the occasion's weight. He was shorter than Daisuke by a meaningful margin and built the way things are built when someone has decided not to waste anything—dense and economical, the kind of compact that does not look like strength until it is moving toward you. His forearms were corded from repetition, from years of circling wooden posts and hanging bags and the particular kind of bag that eventually tells you its name. He walked to the center of the floor, stopped precisely at the northern edge of the diagonal crack, and rolled each shoulder once, the motion unhurried, the motion of a man reviewing something familiar before returning to it.

He looked at Daisuke from across the floor.

Daisuke looked back at him from across the floor.

Through the walls, the city moved at its ordinary distance. In the crowd, a single voice said a number, and someone answered with a different number, and coins were pressed onto a flat stone and covered with a palm. The sound of it arrived and passed and the room went quiet again in the way that rooms go quiet when everyone in them has decided to stop pretending they are anywhere other than where they are.

Both men settled into their guards.

Daisuke's was stillness. Not the stillness of a man who had nothing to begin but the stillness of a thing that has already decided and is only waiting for the body to receive the instruction. His weight centered, his shoulders dropped, his right hand drifting to his hip with the particular precision of a hand that has rehearsed this position through eight thousand mornings and has stopped thinking about what comes next because what comes next has become reflex, has become cellular, has become the school's entire argument expressed in the location of a palm.

Wei's guard was motion that had not yet gone anywhere. His weight shifted forward and back in a rhythm no one watching could have named, his lead foot testing the floor in small transfers, a man perpetually in the act of arriving.

The space between them measured itself and waited.

---

Daisuke moved.

He crossed the floor in an interval too small to fairly call steps—two of them compressed into something closer to a single committed event—his right hand drawing back to the position his school named the loaded hammer, his hips rotating to put his whole mass behind what came next, his body a single projectile aimed at Wei's floating rib with the doctrine of his school behind it: one blow, unanswerable, the conversation ended before it began. This was Jigen-ryū's central theology, its promise to itself, the thing it had been refining for generations—that the world could be simplified to a single overwhelming instant if you were willing to be that instant completely.

Wei was not there.

He stepped left and fractionally forward, inside the arc, and the strike passed his right ear with enough force to disturb the short hairs at his temple. His left arm came around horizontal, the forearm rigid, the rotation coming from his hip and his shoulder and everywhere below them, and it connected with Daisuke's extended arm above the elbow and leveraged momentum into the stone pillar behind him.

What the pillar contributed to this arrangement was significant.

The impact produced a sound that the crowd felt in their chests before they heard it, and the pillar, which had been standing in this location for approximately two hundred years, shed a fist-sized piece of itself into the dirt and would have more to say on the subject of structural compromise as the night progressed.

Daisuke reset.

He stood with his back to the ruined pillar and looked at Wei and understood, in the cold way that his school had trained him to understand things in the middle of them, that the first exchange had not gone as the first exchange was supposed to go. Jigen-ryū existed for that moment—existed, in a meaningful sense, entirely for that moment—and the moment had refused it. He felt this as information. He allowed it to be information and nothing else.

He reset his guard.

The crowd made a sound that was not quite language.

---

What followed had its own grammar.

Daisuke threw committed diagonal cuts, each one aimed to end, each one a full expenditure of intent—his school did not have modes between decisive and catastrophic, and he moved through the floor with the contained ferocity of something that has accepted it cannot be gradual. His strikes arrived with velocity and they arrived loudly, the air splitting audibly when they missed, a sound like cloth tearing but with more authority. Wei circled. This was the other school's central argument, expressed spatially: that there was no straight line worth taking, that the most direct route between a man and defeat was a curve, that footwork was its own form of violence because it forced your opponent to keep updating his map of where you were.

Wei was never quite where Daisuke aimed.

And from those adjusted angles, those positions between positions, Wei answered with the strikes that were his school's inheritance—the whipping forearm traveling horizontal, the elbow arriving from below a guard's natural field of attention, the heel snapping in a lateral arc at the soft interior of a thigh. They came from directions that were wrong in a way that took Daisuke's body time to report. His thigh received one and the nerve there went quiet for a moment in a way that was not pain but was the conversation that precedes pain.

He took two of these before he adjusted his line.

Wei took a hammer-fist to the shoulder that knocked him sideways along the floor and would express itself as a deep purple bruise by morning and as a mobility complaint for the better part of a week. He absorbed it without going down—absorbed it the way his training had built him to absorb things, rolling with it rather than bracing, giving it the direction it wanted and arriving on the other side of it with his feet under him.

Both men were bleeding.

Daisuke: from a split above his right brow where Wei's elbow had arrived on a lateral exchange, running close enough to the eye that he had to blink the warmth of it clear.

Wei: from inside his left cheek, where his own tooth had done the work when Daisuke's palm-strike rattled his jaw and the soft tissue had been between the tooth and the impact.

The floor recorded their passage: footwork in the grime between the stones, a spatter of blood on the pale surface near the east wall, another near the center. Both men tracking where they were relative to the debris in the northeast corner, relative to the stump of the destroyed pillar, relative to the crowd's line—because the crowd was a wall, in practical terms, and a wall behind you is a wall you cannot step through.

Their breathing had changed.

Daisuke pressed Wei toward the east wall, toward the debris, toward the corner where the footing was uncertain, using his forward momentum the way his school used everything—as a form of argument. Wei circled away from it, a sweeping lateral step that took him back toward the center of the floor, back toward the open space he preferred, back toward the diagonal crack and the thin line of water that had been moving south through it since before either of them arrived and had no opinion about what was happening above it.

His right foot came down on the wet stone.

---

It was not a slip. It was not a fall. It was something smaller than both—a quarter-inch of give, a fraction of a second in which his weight transferred forward and his body had committed before it had finished confirming the ground beneath it. The wet stone communicated what it was a moment too late. The gap was small and the gap was sufficient.

Daisuke's backfist was already moving.

It could not be called an exploit in any martial sense. It could not be called a counter or a read or an expression of superior technique. What it was was a coincidence of physics—the strike that was already in motion arriving at the moment that Wei's structure was interrupted by a finger-width of water that had not been placed there by either fighter and had not been considered by either school. The backfist connected across the left side of Wei's head, the flat of the forearm meeting his temple, and his legs performed their brief editorial on the situation by buckling.

He went to one knee.

His right palm met the dry stone.

His head dipped forward.

The crowd's noise terminated. Not faded—terminated. One moment it existed and in the next moment it did not, replaced by a collective held breath, a swallowed oath someone strangled before it completed, the bright small sound of two coins hitting the floor where nervous hands had released them without noticing.

---

Daisuke stood over Wei and drew his hammer back.

Wei raised his head.

He looked at Daisuke without the mediation of expression—not defiance, not appeal, not the performance of dignity that losing sometimes requires. He looked at Daisuke the way that one fact looks at another fact.

Daisuke looked back.

His fist did not fall.

What occupied the interval between Wei's raised face and Daisuke's lowered arm was not mercy and was not respect and was not the warm story that either school would have preferred to tell. It was recognition—the cold, specific recognition of a man who has just understood exactly what he is looking at. He was looking at something the floor had done. He had not beaten Wei. The geography of the ruin had beaten Wei: the crack that was old before either school existed, the water that moved through it without interest in how this ended, this specific square inch of stone made wet by the particular angle of the crack and the particular amount of water available to move through it on this particular night. He could step forward. He could deliver what the floor had arranged for him to deliver, and his school would receive the account, and the account would be carefully shaped by the time it arrived, and no one who received it would have standing to dispute it.

He understood all of this in the way the body sometimes understands things before the mind has finished drafting its argument.

He lowered his arm.

He stepped back.

---

Wei stood.

He rose from one knee to standing in a single motion and he did not rush it and he did not perform it and he made nothing of it because it was not something he had chosen to make anything of. He stood facing Daisuke across a distance of six feet and looked at him for a moment with something that began as contempt and then became something worse than contempt—something quieter, something that had thought contempt through to the other side of it and arrived at a place that had no name that either school had given it. He looked at Daisuke the way a man looks at something that has declined to be significant.

He straightened his collar.

He turned away.

---

Three sharp whistles came from the street outside, and whatever the source—a school's forward scout, a patrol conducting its rounds, someone who had simply been watching the door and knew when things had reached their conclusion—the effect was immediate. The crowd dissolved. Not slowly, not in stages, but in the way that crowds move when they have suddenly remembered they are crowds in a place where being a crowd is inadvisable. Wagers were disputed in whispered argument between people already moving, the dispute conducted urgently and never resolved because the people conducting it were separating into different exits. Someone's lantern was dropped onto the stone floor and the someone did not stop.

Within two minutes the temple contained no one.

The fighters had gone through separate doors into the separate darkness of their separate neighborhoods, carrying with them their separate and irreconcilable accounts of what had occurred here—accounts that would be refined in the telling, that would accumulate details that served the telling, that would become the version their respective schools would rehearse until the rehearsal began to feel like memory.

The dropped lantern's small flame found nothing to sustain it.

It stretched, considered, went out.

---

The water moved through its crack.

It crossed the pale stone south without urgency, without interest, moving across the place where both men had stood and one man had briefly kneeled, moving through the precise inch where the night had pivoted, moving toward the south wall and through some passage in the stone beneath it and into the earth beyond that. It had been moving before either school was founded. It would be moving when the floor gave out, which it would, which the widening crack was already in the slow process of arranging.

The broken column held its new shape.

The roof gap let in the grey light of the city at night, which was not much, which was enough to see by if you already knew what you were looking at.

The ruin resumed its business.
