# Stolen Moments in Roma

The scratch on Alessandra's bike ran hip-to-fender in a long diagonal stripe — three colors of paint overlaid like geological strata, each layer someone else's bad day. She never explained it to anyone. It was simply there, part of the bike's vocabulary, the way a scar is part of a face.

She was doing forty through the Trevi crowd — technically illegal, practically Roman — when the man stepped off the pavement with his nose in a guidebook.

The brake was pure instinct. The front wheel stopped six inches from his knee.

He looked up. She looked down. The guidebook, still open to *Fontana di Trevi: constructed 1762, architect Nicola Salvi*, dangled from one hand like evidence.

"*Madonna,*" Alessandra said, which was not a compliment.

"I'm — God — terribly sorry —" the man said, in an English that had clearly never left England. "I was reading, which was obviously — I should have —"

Around them, Rome did not pause. A tour group broke and reformed like water around a stone. A man selling roses laughed at something Alessandra couldn't hear. Two pigeons disputed a crust near the fountain's base with considerably more urgency than the near-death of this tourist warranted.

"*Stai bene?*" she asked. Are you well.

"*Sì, sì,*" he said, with the confidence of a man who had learned exactly two words of Italian and was deploying both of them immediately. "*Grazie. Mi dispiace.* I'm — I've run through my Italian, I'm afraid."

She stared at him. He was tall, slightly rumpled, wearing a blazer in June that said everything about him. He had the look of a man who made lists and kept them and derived real satisfaction from keeping them. His hair was doing something unauthorized on the left side that he was apparently unaware of. His shoes were good but not Roman — not built for cobblestones, not built for anything that wasn't a library floor.

She almost rode away. The gap between a tour bus and a gelato cart was still there, still navigable, and she had four deliveries before nine.

Then he said, in what he clearly believed was Italian: "*Il mio errore. Sono uno stupido turista.*"

*I am a stupid tourist.* Grammatically impeccable. Accent a disaster of the highest order, as if every Italian sound had been routed through a particularly cautious suburb of London before emerging.

Alessandra laughed. It came out before she could stop it, which annoyed her considerably.

"Get on," she said, in English, jerking her head toward the backseat.

"I beg your — I'm sorry?"

"The bike. Get on." She revved the engine once, lightly, as punctuation.

"I don't — we haven't —" He stopped. Restarted. "I don't know your name," he said, which struck her as a very English objection to getting on a motorcycle with a stranger in a foreign city.

"Alessandra."

"Ben."

"Good. Now you know my name. *Andiamo.*"

He hesitated for exactly three seconds — she counted, without meaning to — then folded himself onto the seat behind her with approximately the grace of a man attempting origami for the first time. She didn't wait for him to fully settle. The bike moved, and his hands found the grab rail with a sound that might have been a small, startled word that she preferred not to hear clearly.

They joined the traffic on the Via del Tritone and she took the corner hard, feeling him shift his weight behind her a half-second late, and Rome opened up ahead of them in the full, indifferent splendor of a Tuesday afternoon in June.

---

She took him to Trastevere, because tourists always went to Trastevere but never went to the right parts of it. She parked the bike illegally beneath an orange tree — the shade was adequate, the sign prohibiting parking was faded past the threshold of her concern — and walked him into the neighborhood at a pace that required him to actually move rather than observe.

He was, she noticed, a looker. Not in the way she usually meant the word, the way she applied it to men who regarded Rome as a backdrop for their own photographs. He looked *at* things. A rusted bracket on a wall where a lamp had once hung. The pattern worn into a doorstep by three centuries of feet. He catalogued without quite realizing he was cataloguing, which she supposed was what historians did when they couldn't help themselves.

"Your Italian," she said, as they cut through a courtyard — *privato*, the sign said, a fact she had decided did not apply to her — "it is from a book, yes?"

"Books, actually. Several." He ducked under a low archway without being told to. "I'm a historian. Medieval urban infrastructure. Roman water systems, aqueducts, the evolution of the *domus* into the Renaissance palazzo. I thought I'd learn the language before I came." A pause. "I learned the grammar."

"But not the feeling."

He stopped walking for just a moment. "Yes. That's exactly it."

She looked at him sideways and said nothing, because she hadn't meant it as a compliment and was mildly annoyed that he'd received it as something true.

At a market stall bright with scarves and postcards and small ceramic saints and the kind of cheap trinkets that somehow looked beautiful in Roman light, she paused to examine a silk square — deep blue, the color of earliest evening above the Gianicolo — while the vendor argued prices with a woman in a yellow hat. Alessandra's hand did not move quickly. It moved *unhurriedly*, which was a different thing entirely, a thing that looked like browsing, like consideration, like someone deciding against a purchase and moving on. When they walked on, Ben glanced at her jacket pocket and then looked up at the street ahead, and she said nothing, and he said nothing, and Rome went on being Rome around them, loud and old and entirely uninterested in the minor moral calculations of two people cutting through its afternoon.

---

By two o'clock she had him on a rooftop terrace accessible only through a stairwell whose door she opened with a key she had acquired some years ago under circumstances she had never fully explained even to herself. The key worked. The roof was empty. The city spread below them in every direction, terracotta and stone, television aerials and ancient campanili sharing the skyline with the cheerful egalitarianism of a place that has long since made its peace with contradiction.

"We shouldn't be up here," Ben said, though he was already walking to the railing.

"No."

"Is this — whose is this?"

"The building's." She leaned against the parapet and lit a cigarette. "Nobody comes up. The chair is there, you see — the ashtray — I think someone used to, but not anymore. Things get abandoned in this city." She exhaled. "Then someone else finds them."

He stood at the railing and looked out, and she watched him doing what she had found all historically-minded people eventually did when confronted with Rome from height: he began to read it. She could almost see his eyes moving through time, subtracting the modern, tracing the lines of the ancient beneath. It was, she thought, a particular kind of hunger — to always be looking for what used to be there instead of what was.

"You've done this before," he said.

"Brought strangers to rooftops?"

"Shown people the city. The un-approved version."

She considered the question through a long pull of the cigarette. "I had a friend," she said. "He was very good at finding the places nobody had bothered to close off yet. Before he —" She stopped. Drew again. Let the sentence go. "I remember them. The places."

Ben turned from the railing and looked at her. She redirected before the look could settle into anything.

"The *vietato* swimming at Cala Azzurra — you would need to come back for that, it's three hours south." She dropped the cigarette and stepped on it. "But there is something at the Aventine I want to show you while the light is right. Come."

---

He stopped protesting being somewhere he shouldn't be sometime around three-thirty, when she took him through the private courtyard of a palazzo whose owners had left for the coast in May and where the roses had consequently rioted, unpruned, over every surface, and he stood in the middle of it making no sound for a long moment, and she watched his relationship with the word *vietato* undergo a quiet revision.

After the courtyard, the resistance was largely ceremonial — a polite note of objection registered for the record before he followed her anyway. She noticed that he had also stopped apologizing to people, a habit she had initially found exhausting and now found herself having to consciously register was gone. He was still himself — still the blazer, still the careful attention, still the quality of looking that was actually seeing — but the man descending a narrow staircase behind her at four o'clock moved differently than the man she'd nearly destroyed at the Trevi. Lighter. Like someone who had set down something heavy without quite noticing the moment of setting it down.

She didn't mention this. It wasn't her observation to make.

---

The Aventine Hill in late afternoon had the quality of a city holding its breath. The Knights of Malta gate was, as always, surrounded by a small crowd of people waiting their turn to look through the keyhole — this, at least, was an approved attraction, documented in every guidebook including, she presumed, Ben's — and Alessandra hung back while he approached and bent and put his eye to the bronze.

She watched him go still.

Through the keyhole, the long corridor of hedged garden terminated in the precise, luminous circle of Saint Peter's dome, framed as though by a camera, or a prayer. She had looked through it perhaps thirty times in her life and it had never failed to do something to her composure that she preferred to manage privately.

"Everyone walks past it," she said, when he straightened. "Every day. All these people who live here. Nobody looks."

It came out with more weight than she'd intended.

She was looking at the dome. She was aware, without confirming it, that he was looking at her. There was a quality to the silence that she recognized — she had, after all, created it before, in other places, with other people who'd looked at her in late-afternoon light and arrived at mistaken conclusions about what the day had meant — but this silence had something different in its texture, something she found she didn't want to categorize quickly.

So she checked her phone.

"I have a delivery," she said. Partly true. "The last one I can still make tonight."

"Of course." He was already arranging his expression back into something appropriate, which required no time at all, which was possibly his least endearing quality and also his most. "Alessandra — this was —"

"*Prego.*"

"No, I mean —" He paused. Laughed briefly at himself. "I was going to say *I don't have words for this*. The historian without words. You'd think I'd have planned for that."

She pulled on her helmet. Paused with her hands on the chin strap.

She reached into her jacket pocket and took out the blue silk square from the Trastevere market. She held it toward him over the bike's frame. He took it; looked at it; looked at her, and something moved through his face that was more complicated than the question forming behind it.

"Did you —" he began.

"For your pocket," she said. "You are a man who should have a pocket square."

"Alessandra —"

"*Ciao, Ben.*"

She pulled into traffic without looking back. She knew, with the specific certainty of long practice, what she would see if she looked: a tall man in a June blazer standing at the end of a Roman street, holding a square of blue silk, a stolen dome behind him in the failing light, looking like a man who has briefly inhabited someone else's life and has not yet decided how to feel about returning to his own.

She kept her eyes on the road.

She made the turn.

The bike merged with the city and the city took her in the way it always did — without ceremony, without comment — and the scratch along the fender caught the last red light and held it for just a moment, three colors deep, and then the darkness came and there was only the road ahead, and Rome, and whatever she was going back to, which was hers.
