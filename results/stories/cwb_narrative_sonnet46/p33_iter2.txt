# The Last Outpost

The gravity hiccups at 9:14, same as always, and I watch six drinks wobble in unison across the bar like they're sharing a private joke. Vega whisky goes amber-left. Cressian pale goes translucent-right. The fermented kelp situation in front of Old Marro does something geometrically improbable before settling back into its glass, dignified and unspilled. Twenty-two months at The Last Outpost and I still find this charming. That probably says something about me.

"Nets." Old Marro taps the bar twice, his four-jointed fingers producing a sound like a small xylophone. "This been watered."

"It hasn't."

"Tastes watered."

"Marro, I have watched you drink six of those tonight. Your ability to assess—"

"Watered," he says, satisfied, and goes back to his fermented kelp.

I let him have it. That's maybe forty percent of bartending, right there.

The Outpost is doing its usual Thursday thing. Dock workers from the Cassini haul clustered near the viewport, still in their pressure suits, arguing about claim margins. Two Belarian merchants playing cards at the corner table with an intensity that suggests the stakes are well above recreational. Somebody's kid asleep in the booth nearest the heat exchanger, using a coiled tether as a pillow while their parent—a single dad I recognize from the ice-mining cooperative—sends frantic messages on his comm and tries not to look desperate. I put a bowl of complimentary salted yura nuts near the kid without being asked. The dad looks up. I look away. That's the other forty percent.

The last twenty is memory. Every regular, every preference, every running tab of grievance and affection. Sixteen standard drinks strong, Priya from the co-op wants the lights dimmed when she's working through something. Jared—two syllables, he'll correct you—takes his coffee with a rim of sulfur salt that I special-order and don't advertise. The gravity fluctuation always makes the Belarians nervous, something cultural I've never asked about, so I've learned to make a small joke right after it happens. *She's just reminding us she's in charge.* They like that. I like that they like it.

It's a good life. I chose it carefully, which is to say I fled to it. But I chose to stay, and that's the thing that matters now.

I'm good at reading rooms because I spent three years in rooms that would kill you if you misread them. I'm good at keeping secrets because I have several of my own that I tend like a private garden, watering them just enough to keep them from dying, never enough to let them grow. I'm good at making people feel seen without making them feel watched—those are different skills, and learning the distance between them cost me things I don't think about on Thursdays. Thursdays are good. The gravity hiccups and the drinks wobble and Old Marro accuses me of watering his kelp and somewhere in the belt the ice keeps calving off into the dark and getting scooped up by people like the cooperative dad, and it all continues. The hum of it. The Outpost earns its keep by being the last place before nothing, and I earn mine by making the last place feel like somewhere worth staying.

This is armor. I know it's armor. But it fits well and I've worn it long enough that some mornings it's hard to tell where the armor stops and I begin, which is maybe the goal, which is maybe also the problem. I don't think about that on Thursdays either.

At 9:31, the stranger comes in.

I clock the askew tentacled dreads first—dark, iridescent, the kind that usually move with deliberate intention, but these are listing slightly left like a plant that's been kept from light too long. Then the eyes: not making contact with anything. Scanning the room but seeing *through* it. She moves through the Thursday crowd like someone navigating by memory of a different room and reaches the bar stool at the far end, away from the dock workers, and sits down with a kind of controlled exhaustion that I recognize from somewhere deep in my own chest.

I give her a moment. Then I cross to her end.

She speaks first.

"Vega whisky, neat." A pause. "Better make that a double."

I pour it. The gravity twitches while I'm setting it down and the whisky leans briefly toward her, curious, then rights itself. She stares at it.

"First time in a wobble zone?" I ask.

"No." She wraps both hands around the glass. Close up, I can see the dreads are slightly tangled, which takes real effort—they're self-organizing by nature. "Been a long transit."

"From where?"

She takes a long drink before answering. "Does it matter?"

"Not to me." I wipe down the bar near her, not retreating entirely. "I'm Nets. I run this stretch of the universe."

That gets a small sound that might be the beginning of a laugh. "Nets."

"Short for Nettle."

"Is that earned or given?"

"Bit of both." I tilt my head. "You?"

She looks at me for the first time. Actually looks. Her eyes are the pale gold of Vega whisky, which I suspect is not a coincidence. "Sable."

"What brings you out this far, Sable?"

Another long drink. The glass is almost empty and she's contemplating its bottom like there's scripture in there. "Work."

"What kind?"

"The kind that doesn't work out." She sets the glass down and I refill it without being asked. She notices this, and something in her posture loosens a fraction. "You hear anything lately? Out this way. Movement. Ships routing off-corridor."

I stop wiping. Keep my face easy. "I hear lots of things."

"Cortex ships," she says, quiet enough that only I can hear it. "Running dark."

The fermented kelp situation wobbles again, though the gravity hasn't moved.

"Hey, Nets!" The cooperative dad is waving from the booth. "Can I get another of whatever this is? The red one?"

"Anderi sunset," I call back. "One minute."

I hold Sable's gaze while I answer him. She's watching me with the careful attention of someone who has learned to read exits.

"Why are you asking me?" I say.

"Because bartenders know." She traces the rim of her glass. "Because this is the last stop before the rim. Because—" She stops.

"Because?"

Her dreads shift slightly, almost involuntarily, curling toward each other like something seeking warmth. "Because I don't have anywhere else to ask."

I go make the Anderi sunset. I take slightly longer than necessary, measuring the grenadite with more precision than it requires. The dock workers are laughing about something. The kid is still asleep. The heat exchanger hums its steady, faithful hum.

Cortex ships running dark on the outer rim would mean one thing, and it's a thing I haven't heard in twenty-two months, and the reason I know what it means is the same reason I'm standing behind this bar on the thinning edge of populated space instead of somewhere with a sky that's actually blue.

I bring the dad his drink. He smiles the tired smile of someone holding on by increments. I smile back and it is not performed.

I come back to Sable.

"Finish that," I say. "Then tell me what you actually know."

The gravity hiccups. Her whisky leans toward me.

Neither of us says anything.

---

She talks for forty minutes. I listen the way I always listen—with the part of my attention that doesn't show on my face—and by the end of it I've polished the same section of bar top to a brightness it probably hasn't seen since installation. The dock workers have gone. The Belarians have packed up their cards. Old Marro is asleep on his stool, breathing the deep oceanic breath of a man who has achieved his nightly goal.

Sable keeps her voice low, but her hands do the heavier telling. The way they tighten on the glass when she says *Station Kerev.* The way the left one goes still entirely—goes careful, deliberately still—when she says *the archive.*

"How many ships?" I ask.

"Three confirmed when I left. Possibly five by now." She finally releases the glass. Flexes her fingers like she's checking they still work. "They were moving in formation. Dark running means they've suppressed their corridor registration. Traffic control can't see them. The locals can't report them."

"Who's the locals at Kerev these days?"

"Contractors. Same as everywhere on the rim. Paper authority, no real teeth." She looks at me steadily. "You know how Cortex operates."

I do. The reason I know is the same reason I'm standing here instead of sitting wherever she's sitting, which is the ruined side of something. "You said you came here for work. What work."

"I need someone who knows the Kerev approach corridors." She turns the empty glass slowly. "The old ones. Before they were decommissioned."

Something in my chest does a thing it hasn't done in twenty-two months. Like a door, very far away, opening. I don't look at it directly.

"Those charts have been sealed," I say.

"I know."

"Sealed means not accessible through standard registry."

"I know what sealed means."

I look at her. She looks at me. The Outpost hums around us, indifferent and familiar, and I am aware of the cooperative dad in the booth doing the math of his life on his comm screen, of his kid asleep with a tether for a pillow, of the six bar stools that will have regulars on them again tomorrow night requiring my particular and cultivated care.

"Why Kerev?" I say.

And this is where something in her face changes. It doesn't fall—it's not that dramatic—but it realigns, like tectonic plates making a small, necessary adjustment.

"There's a population there," she says. "The contractors brought people out. Families. They're—" She presses her lips together. "The ships aren't there for the station."

"What's in the archive."

"Names." Her dreads have gone very still. Even the tangled ones. "A full census. Three generations of people who came out on cooperative passage and never got incorporated into the main registry. Who don't technically exist, on paper, except in that archive." She pauses. "Cortex has been acquiring the land-title claims on the belt's third corridor. You acquire the claims, you control transit permissions. You control transit permissions, you can legally—" She stops again.

"Define *legally,*" I say.

"I mean *paperwork,*" she says. "I mean the kind of violence that doesn't look like violence. Displacement orders. Resource reclassification. If the archive is gone, there are three hundred people out there who have no documented right to be where they are." She picks up the empty glass and puts it down. "I worked courier for the cooperative commission for six years. Before that—" A small, economical gesture. *Before that doesn't matter.* "I know what's in that archive because I helped transcribe it. Twelve years ago. For a cooperative rep named Davan Pell."

The glass I am holding doesn't break. I am careful about that.

"Davan Pell," I say, which is not a question.

Sable watches me. "You know the name."

"I know the name."

"Were you—"

"I worked the filing commission." I set the glass down slowly. "Not under my own name. I was twenty-four. I didn't stay long." I look at the bar top. The bright, polished section. "Long enough."

She's very quiet for a moment.

"The archive is still there," she says finally. "Or it was eight days ago. A contact at Kerev got a message out before—" She stops. "Before they stopped being able to send messages." She looks at her hands. "I have a ship. I'm a decent pilot in a corridor I know. But I don't know the old approaches. If Cortex is running dark, the registered corridors will be watched. I need another way in."

"Getting in," I say, "is only half of it."

"I know."

"And there are three hundred people you'd need to—what. Evacuate? Redirect? You can't evaporate three hundred people."

"I know that too." She looks up. "I'm not asking for a plan. I'm asking for a chart."

I think about this. I think about Davan Pell, who was a cooperative representative with ink-stained fingers and a laugh that made you feel the room had gotten warmer, who used to bring two coffees to the filing office and put one on my desk without asking and who called me by my real name, my whole name, in a way that nobody here does because nobody here knows it. I think about the last time I saw the name Cortex written anywhere and what happened directly after. I think about twenty-two months of a carefully constructed and genuinely beloved hum.

Then I think about the cooperative dad and his kid asleep on a tether, and I think about what *displacement order* means when it lands on a family that has no entry in the primary registry, and I think about the specific texture of paperwork violence, which I know from architecture of its walls.

"Those charts aren't in the registry," I say.

"I know."

"So you'd need someone who had them from before. Personally."

"Yes."

I look at her for a long moment. Her tentacled dreads have loosened slightly, the tangled ones unknotting themselves by small degrees, the way they do when the worst anticipation of a thing has partially passed.

"Finish your drink," I say again. Because I say things twice when I'm thinking.

She does.

I turn and I look at the Outpost—at the carved marks in the third bar stool where Old Marro has tapped out approximately ten thousand opinions, at the scuff on the heat exchanger from when Priya backed into it during the supply dispute of month four and we laughed for five minutes while the temperature alarm went off, at the cooperative kid asleep in the booth with total faith in the structural integrity of this specific corner of the universe.

The gravity fluctuates. Every glass on the bar leans gently in a single direction, as though the station itself is trying to point at something.

I reach under the bar. Behind the locked panel behind the cleaning supplies behind the thing I call the dead zone, where I keep the two items I brought with me when I fled. One is a photograph, physical, printed on actual paper, of three people standing in front of a registry office on a station that no longer exists under that name. One is a data chip so old it needs an adaptor to read.

I hold the chip in my palm for a moment. It's warm from being stored near the heat exchanger. It has been warm for twenty-two months.

"I'll need a couple of hours to prep the adaptor read," I say. "The format's old. I'll have to remap the coordinates to current corridor notation or they'll be useless to you." I slide it onto the bar between us. "I'll also need to call in a favor for a transit permit through the secondary junction at Cass-7. That'll give you cover for the first leg."

Sable looks at the chip. Then at me.

"You don't have to do this," she says. Which is the most interesting thing she's said all night, because she's right, and because she's saying it instead of just taking the chip, which tells me something.

"I know," I say. "That's not usually the deciding factor."

"What is?"

I look at the booth. At the kid's sleeping face, entirely trusting and entirely unaware. At the bowl of complimentary yura nuts I put there without being asked.

"Memory," I say. "Mostly."

Sable looks at the booth too. Her dreads have settled back to something approaching upright. They won't be quite right until she sleeps, and I doubt she's slept properly in days, and that's also a kind of information.
