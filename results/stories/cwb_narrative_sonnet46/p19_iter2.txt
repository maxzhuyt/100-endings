# High Seas and Low Vices

The bell in the harbourmaster's tower struck seven as I was finishing my buttons. I counted them by habit—the bells, not the buttons, though I counted those too. Six buttons on the coat, nine on the waistcoat beneath, and each one an argument: I am still this man. I am still the man these buttons belong to. The argument had become less persuasive over eighteen months, but I had not yet lost the habit of making it.

My quarters looked onto the inner harbour, and the harbour smelled that morning of tar and salt and the particular brine of fish being gutted somewhere below my window. I had learned to ignore it. I had learned, more precisely, to layer it beneath other smells—the wool of my coat, the cedar of the chest, the remnant of last night's lamp oil—so that it became background rather than fact. This was a skill I had developed without noticing I was developing it.

Ensign Whitmore was on the step when I came out, holding a ledger and the expression of a man who has been waiting long enough to be anxious but not long enough to say so. He was nineteen and eager in the way that made me feel ancient, though I was thirty-one.

"Sir. The morning inspection—"

"I'll be missed this morning." I adjusted my cuff. The motion was automatic, the kind of thing hands do when a man has been an officer long enough. "Company offices. Can't be moved." I let a trace of reluctance into my voice, professional regret at civilian obligation. "You'll manage the inspection capably. You always do."

He straightened slightly at the compliment. I had learned precisely how much compliment was required to foreclose further questions. Whitmore was a good ensign and would make a middling lieutenant and might, with fortune and the avoidance of places like this one, eventually command something modest and decent. I thought this without contempt, which perhaps surprised me. The contempt had gone somewhere. I didn't know where it had gone.

"Of course, sir. Should I note your absence as—"

"Company business," I said. "That's sufficient." 

I walked away at the pace of a man late for something important, which is a pace I had perfected. It differs from the pace of a man who is simply hurrying, and it differs from the pace of a man who does not wish to be followed. It is brisk but directional, purposeful but unhurried. It says: I know where I am going and it matters. 

I walked it in the wrong direction for two hundred yards before the corner of the chandlery allowed me to turn and head toward the city.

---

The British district in the mornings had the quality of a theatre before the audience arrived—everything positioned, every surface suggesting the performance that was about to begin. Men in good coats moved with the deliberate legibility of people who wished to be read at a distance: *I am conducting commerce. I am conducting empire. I am exactly what I appear to be.* I had once found this reassuring. Now it struck me as effortful in a way that made me tired.

I kept my eyes on the middle distance and walked at the officer's pace and crossed the district without incident. There were two men I recognised—Hargreaves from the *Sunderland*, talking with a Company factor outside the post office, and a naval surgeon whose name I could not recall who was coming the other way with a leather bag. I had my routes. I had been mapping them for a year. The surgeon looked up a moment too late and I was already presenting the back of my coat to him, very busy, clearly elsewhere. He did not call after me. Nobody ever did. This still managed to surprise me, dimly, each time.

Past the boundary of the district the city changed the way a river changes below a weir—same water, different character. The streets narrowed first, then compacted. The signage in English fell away. The smells complicated: fish sauce layering over incense layering over something burnt and syrupy, a background sweetness that organised the other smells around it. And then, past a bend that brought me into shadow, the mangoes.

I don't know who grew them or carried them into this part of Canton. They appeared, cyclically, in the gutters and at the edges of the walkways—split and oozing in the heat, their skins going black, the flesh subsiding into something between solid and liquid. The smell was not simply decay. It was fermentation: a sweetness past its peak, overripe and abundant and specific enough to be, for me, a location rather than a sensation. That smell meant I was nearly there. My body knew it before I did. By the time I could detect it clearly, I was already walking faster, and I noticed this only because I was always trying to notice it, which did not, as yet, prevent it from happening.

---

"Lieutenant."

Fong was leaning against a wall that had no particular reason to be leaned against, in a posture suggesting he had been there so long he had become a feature of it. He was a small man and very still—the kind of stillness that is not patience but efficiency, an absence of wasted motion. He worked, as far as I understood, for three separate principals in this part of the district. I had never met any of them. I suspected this was by design on all sides.

I didn't stop walking. He fell in beside me without reconfiguring his manner at all, which I found I had always disliked about him.

"Mr Sung," he said. "Forty dollars. Fourteen days beyond."

"By the end of the week."

"He would prefer the money to the schedule."

"He may present his preference to me at week's end when I deliver both." I looked at him briefly. He had a face that gave nothing. I had looked at it enough times to have given up looking for things. "Is there another arrangement to discuss, or is this an errand?"

He considered. Not performing consideration—actually considering, which was one of the things about Fong that I found workable, in the way that I found certain things about the drug workable and not others.

"A letter," he said. "It needs to move through the naval post without examination."

I walked for a moment.

"Commercial," he said. "You would not be asked to do anything with it except carry it."

"I am already not being asked about its contents."

"Forty dollars offset against the Sung debt. And a consideration for the following month—in advance."

I stopped. Not because the decision required stopping, but because the body sometimes registers a thing while the mind is still pretending to deliberate. I looked at the alley that opened beyond him, which went toward the tea house and the rooms behind it. The mango smell was a weight against my face. I thought, briefly and without conviction, about what I was being asked to carry. A commercial letter. Entirely. There was a captain named Forsythe who had died in the spring and whose death I had been positioned to question, had raised no question, and whose name I had not spoken since. The letters were not unrelated to that. I understood this without having confirmed it, which appeared to be satisfactory for all parties.

"This evening," I said. "At the usual place."

He was already gone.

I stood for a moment in the shadow of the alley. This was the thing I sometimes did—stood briefly with what I'd agreed to before the agreement became simply history. It had seemed important, once, not to allow each decision to pass unremarked. I wasn't sure it had achieved anything. The decisions passed anyway. What remained was the brief standing, which had become, I supposed, its own kind of performance, staged for an audience of one who was not watching very closely. I walked on.

---

The tea house was legitimate. This was something I had checked once, out of the remnant of some old professional instinct, and confirmed: a functioning establishment, with paying customers and a woman who ran it with the close attention of a person for whom this was a livelihood. The rooms behind were a different business conducted by the same woman, and the two businesses did not acknowledge each other, which was an arrangement common enough in this part of Canton to require no explanation and receive none.

Madame Chen—no one called her this; I thought of her this way because I had no other name for her—looked at me when I came through the low door and said something brief to the boy who stood near the inner curtain. He brought the pipe. This was the transaction. There was no other transaction required of me here. Whatever I had traded away in the alley had no currency in this room.

Four other men were distributed across the low platforms. A Dutch merchant named Van Hoorn whose operations I knew something of professionally and with whom I had exchanged certain information twice—never in here, always elsewhere, the encounters governed by a mutual pretence that we did not recognise each other in this room. A young Chinese man I had seen six or seven times and whose connection to the trade I had not determined and had stopped trying to determine. Two others I didn't know. We maintained the courtesy of not observing. It was not unfriendliness. It was respect for a shared project.

I settled onto the platform. Removed my coat with more care than it deserved, folded it, set it aside. Without the coat I was just a man. I sometimes thought this during the only moment when it was possible to think it cleanly.

The first draw extinguished something.

Not the morning—the morning was long past and I was past being in it. What extinguished was the sensation that had been present since waking, the one beneath the uniform and the buttons and the paced walk and the precisely calibrated compliment to the ensign. It was not pain—it was the absence of ease, and its removal was the restoration of a state I could not achieve by any other means currently available to me. My hands lay still. The sounds of the port, which had been pressing at me, became peripheral and then merely present and then, after some interval I could not measure, almost companionable.

I thought about a lake.

This was something that happened with reliable peculiarity during the early stage—the mind went somewhere specific, somewhere I would not have chosen, somewhere that appeared without invitation and with unreasonable vividness. The lake was in Cumberland, where my uncle had taken me fishing at an age I no longer remembered, eleven or twelve, a September that smelled of cold water and bracken and the rubber of wet boots. I could smell it now, which was impossible and simply true. Cold English lake water. The specific cold of autumn in a place where water goes grey before the sky does. I had not thought about that lake in years when I was in any other state. In this state, I thought about it almost every time, with a fidelity of detail that made me wonder what else I was holding that I had no access to.

After a while, I came back.

Van Hoorn was gone. The young Chinese man lay on his side watching the ceiling with the complete absorption of a man who has found something there worth watching. The light through the paper screens was the colour of weak tea. Afternoon light. I sat up and put on my coat and noted that I had been here considerably longer than I'd intended, which was the case almost every time, and was never not a problem.

---

The streets were the same streets. The mangoes were still there. The difference was the light, which had gone lower and more horizontal, throwing shadows that changed the angles of everything I'd passed through on the way down. The district conducted its evening business around me. I moved through it at the officer's pace, which required some maintenance now—the pace was not natural and the body wanted a different pace, slower or more erratic, and I was arbitrating between them constantly with the portion of my attention I could spare from watching for faces I recognised.

Two sailors from my own ship came around a corner. Stevens and Hargreaves—I knew them, not well but sufficiently. They were off-duty, in their loose shore clothes, and they looked at me with the sudden alertness of men who have not expected to encounter their commanding hierarchy and are calculating whether the occasion requires formality.

I gave them the expression first. I had learned to do this—to lead with the expression, so that by the time they were looking at my eyes the expression was already there to meet them. Composed, familiar, an officer briefly encountered in the city, nothing notable about it.

"Stevens. Hargreaves."

"Sir," Stevens said. The taller one, with the edge of something uncertain in the way he held himself. He was looking at my face with the blankness of a man carefully not registering what he was registering.

"Evening."

I walked past them at the pace. I did not look back. Not looking back was important—looking back gave the moment weight, suggested that it was a moment worth weighting. I walked as though the encounter had already passed from memory, which was the performance I needed the performance to achieve. I counted in my head, which was something I did. By thirty I had rounded the corner. By fifty I was on a different street.

What Stevens had seen in my eyes, or thought he had seen, I could not know with certainty. I had caught, in the fraction of a second before the expression arrived, his face doing the thing faces do when something doesn't align. A small displacement of the neutral. The recalibration. He was a competent sailor with the ordinary intelligence of a man who has spent years reading the ocean for information. He would have seen something. What he would do with what he had seen remained to be determined.

I walked back to the ship with the particular effort of walking as though walking required no effort.

---

My quarters at night. The small desk. The lamp.

On the desk was a piece of paper on which I had, four months ago, begun a letter to my brother in Bristol. The letter had not progressed. On it, in my own writing, which still looked like the writing of a capable officer and not like the writing of anything else, were the words *I am well and the posting is, on the whole—*

I sat with these words for a while. The posting was, on the whole. I had sat with this sentence for four months and had not improved it. The difficulty was that I knew what came next and could not write it, and also did not know what came next and could imagine nothing, and these two problems occupied exactly the same space.

I folded the letter and put it in the drawer with the five others.

Through the porthole, the harbour worked. Ships moving in the dark, lanterns tracking slow courses. The creak of ropes under load, which was the sound I had fallen asleep to for four years before it became audible to me again, which had happened sometime in the last year, which I took for a sign of something without specifying what. The sound of men still working, still maintaining the apparatus of passage.

Tomorrow there was an inspection I would attend. A report I was behind on and would file, competently, by midday. A letter I would collect from Fong at the usual place and carry through the naval post without examining its contents, because I was a man of my word, which was a formulation that had meant one thing for the first thirty years of my life and now meant whatever it meant now, which I had not lately examined.

I had been good at this job. I thought about this sometimes—not with grief, or not primarily, but with the same attention I had given to the lake, the same fidelity to a detail that no longer applied. I had been a good officer. The logbooks from my first posting bore this out in the handwriting of a man who believed it. Competence, probity, a genuine aptitude for navigation and an ability to hold a crew together in difficult weather. These had not disappeared. They had simply become the scaffolding of something that required them as cover.

I could smell the mangoes. I had carried the smell back in my coat, or the smell had followed me, or I simply could not any longer tell the difference between a smell that was present and a smell that my body had decided to remember. The harbourmaster's lamp swung on its post on the dock and threw moving light across the water.

I went to sleep. 

In the morning I would put on my coat.
