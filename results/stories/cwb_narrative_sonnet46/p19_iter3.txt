# High Seas and Low Vices

The supply manifest for the *Persistence* ran to eleven pages, and I had read the first four with something approaching attention before setting it aside. This was, I told myself, not neglect but efficiency. Hargrove was more than capable with figures. The boy had been trained precisely so that a senior officer's mind might be freed for larger concerns—strategy, positioning, the diplomatic textures of a posting to Canton that demanded more than arithmetic. I had larger concerns. I folded the manifest and left it on my desk with an air of deliberate authority, the way a man leaves something he intends to return to.

The cough came as I reached the companionway. Brief, productive, copper behind the teeth. River air, I thought. The Pearl River at this hour produced a particular quality of mist that sat in the chest—any man posted here long enough would tell you the same. I had been told it myself, on arrival, by men who had since rotated home. I cleared my throat and continued up toward the light.

The dock at Whampoa was already loud with the business of morning. I noted it with the accustomed eye: the lighters moving between our anchorage and the factories downriver, the bare backs of the coolies at the bales, the precise and necessary chaos of a trading post at work. I had a meeting with Superintendent Elliot's man at nine. The clock in the harbourmaster's building, visible from where I stood, read twenty past eight.

I went below again to find my coat.

---

Hargrove intercepted me at the gangway. He was twenty-three and had the earnest, slightly persecuted look of a man who understood that he was being used as a buffer between his superior and the superior's responsibilities, and who had not yet decided whether this was an opportunity or an indignity.

"Captain Merrick, sir. The Superintendent's office sent a runner. The nine o'clock—they've asked if it might be extended, to cover the Jardine matter as well. They're hoping for an hour, at least."

I looked past him at the dock. The morning had that particular soft quality of light over the Pearl before the haze burned off—everything held at a distance, even the near things, even the sharp prow of the *Persistence* where she sat at anchor, as though the world were being shown to you through gauze.

"Tell them I'm coming directly," I said. "And Hargrove."

"Sir."

"The manifest. See to it yourself. Whatever wants correcting, correct it."

Something moved in his face that he was too disciplined to complete. "Of course, sir."

I walked down the gangway and along the dock and then, where the dock road bent left toward the Superintendent's compound, I continued straight. The smell of the river was already giving way to something else—charcoal smoke, and under it something fermented, and under that something I had no English word for, something organic and centuries deep that Canton produced from itself the way a living thing produces heat. The sound changed too. The clatter of western commerce behind me, and ahead something more complex—voices carrying the tonal patterns I had spent twenty months learning to hear as language rather than music, the creak of handcarts, the specific knock of wooden sandals on stone, a woman calling from an upper storey into a narrow street below, and the street answering.

I walked into it. The mist closed behind me like water.

---

Fordyce had been in Canton longer than almost anyone. He had come out as a supercargo for a tea firm whose name I'd forgotten, married a sequence of women whose nationalities I'd also forgotten, and arrived at some point at a permanent condition of apparent contentment that struck everyone who met him as either admirable or obscene, depending on their own condition. He was large and carefully dressed and sat every morning at a particular tea house table on the Thirteen Factories street as reliably as a piece of furniture.

He was there now, lifting a cup with the unhurried motion of a man who had nowhere further to be.

"Merrick," he said, without surprise.

I sat across from him. A girl brought tea without being asked. This was Fordyce's effect on his surroundings—things arrived.

"You look well," he said.

"I am well."

He drank. He had a way of not looking at a thing that was more attentive than looking would have been. "I heard you had the Jardine business today. Complicated position, that."

"Hargrove will manage it."

"Of course." He set down his cup. "You're going to see Wei."

It wasn't a question. I found that I had turned my own cup in a slow circle on the table and stopped doing it. "I have some time this morning."

"He was asking after you. Two days ago—or three. He mentioned a complication." Fordyce's tone was perfectly even, the tone of a man discussing weather or freight rates, which was the only tone anyone in this street ever used for anything. "Something about a balance."

"I'll square it."

"I'm sure." He looked out at the street. A pair of men passed with a shoulder-pole between them, carrying something wrapped in oil cloth. "He mentioned you might be in a position to help with something. In your official capacity."

"Did he."

"He was vague. You know how Wei is."

I knew how Wei was. "Thank you for the tea," I said.

"Stay." He said it mildly. "Finish the cup."

I finished the cup. Fordyce talked about a shipment of Canton crêpe he was arranging, and I listened in the way that requires no actual attention, and once I coughed—one short convulsion that I ended by pressing two fingers to my lips—and Fordyce said nothing and I said *the river air* and he said *yes*, and looked out at the street again, and we sat together for another few minutes like two men who were simply acquainted.

---

Wei Linzhi operated from a room above a medicine shop on a lane behind the western factories. This was not a secret. In the same way that the Superintendent's compound was known to be where official British business happened, this room was known to be where another kind of business happened, and the two kinds of business were known to depend on each other in ways that no official document would record. I had been coming here for fourteen months.

The room smelled of camphor and sawdust and the particular sweetness I no longer registered as anything but neutral—the smell of interior space, of a specific ceiling above a specific floor. Wei was seated on a low stool, going through an accounts book with a brush. He looked up when I entered with the expression of a craftsman noting the arrival of a regular job.

"Captain Merrick." His English was better than he let on, which was itself a matter of craft. "You are two days early."

"I know."

"And short." He had not looked at my hands or my face or any of me in any obvious way, and yet he knew. "By how much."

"A week's worth. Perhaps ten days." I held the number steady in my voice. "I can cover it by the fifteenth."

"The fifteenth," Wei repeated. He returned to his book for a moment, made a notation. Set the brush down. "I have a difficulty," he said. "Also a solution to your difficulty. These two things are connected."

I waited.

"There is a letter. It requires to be carried aboard the *Persistence* and placed among the ship's official papers. Not permanently. For two days—perhaps three. Then it will be retrieved." He said this as he said everything, with the measured affect of a man specifying a delivery window. "The letter has a character that benefits from naval association. The impression that it has passed through official channels."

"What kind of letter."

"The kind that satisfies certain questions before they are asked." He folded his hands in his lap. "Your man on the night watch—Fletcher, I believe. He is one of mine. He will tell you when retrieval is complete."

Fletcher. I had promoted Fletcher eight months ago. I had written the recommendation myself.

"And the balance," I said.

"Cleared. And your usual supply, early." He looked at me with the clear, professional attention of someone assessing a tool's condition against the work required of it. "Also a small addition. For the inconvenience."

I thought about saying something. I have tried to remember what I might have said, in the years since—what objection was available to me that I was not in the process of foreclosing. The ceiling of Wei's room was a particular shade of brown, the colour of old tea, with one beam running crosswise that I had stared at so many times I knew its grain. I looked at it now.

"When do you need it placed."

"This afternoon would be convenient."

---

He made me wait twenty minutes. A problem with the supply, he said—his own supplier had been delayed, a matter of an hour, he was sorry for the inconvenience. I sat in the outer room on a bench below a window that looked into the lane.

I had received a commendation from Admiral Holland in the spring of my third year. This was 1831, before China, before Canton, before Fordyce and Wei and the particular colour of Wei's ceiling. The ceremony was aboard the *Meridian* at Portsmouth, and afterward Holland had shown me—with the unguarded pleasure of a man who had waited years for the right audience—a collection of charts he had commissioned of the South Atlantic, drawn by a surveyor named Carrick who was also, improbably, gifted with watercolours. The charts were beautiful. They were the most beautiful thing I had seen to that point that a man had made, precise and expressive at once, the ocean rendered in gradients of blue that somehow conveyed depth, current, the specific coldness of that water in that latitude. Holland had spread them on the table in his cabin and said *look at this coast*, pointing to Tristan da Cunha, and I had looked, and we had stood there in companionable silence for what might have been five minutes, two naval men looking at what someone had managed to make of the world's southern edge.

I had written to my wife about those charts. She had written back that she would like to see them someday. I had written back that I would arrange it. Neither of us had.

In the lane below the window, a child was hitting a stone against a drainpipe, rhythmically, for no apparent purpose. The sound came up as a dull knock, patient and repetitive.

Wei's assistant appeared in the doorway.

---

The letter fit inside my coat. It was sealed with plain wax, addressed in characters I didn't read, smaller than I'd expected—no thicker than three or four pages folded together. I was aware of its weight in a way that had nothing to do with weight.

I walked back to Whampoa in the early afternoon. The haze had burned off and the Pearl was a flat, worked silver under the sun. At the gangway I nodded to the watch—Coates, not Fletcher; Fletcher was off-shift—and went below to my cabin and locked the door.

I opened the manifest. I read the first line. I opened the drawer of my desk and took out the ship's administrative file—the accordion of forms and correspondence and official documents that I had accumulated and Hargrove organized and that represented, in its modest way, the administrative existence of Her Majesty's ship *Persistence* and by extension the Admiralty's presence on this station. I opened it. I placed the letter inside, two-thirds of the way back, between a copy of Elliot's trade regulations and a letter from the harbourmaster about anchorage fees.

I closed the file. I put it back in the drawer.

I sat at my desk for perhaps a minute. Then I put on my coat again and went back out.

---

The house was on Guangyuan Street, which did not appear on any British map of Canton I had seen, and which was reached by a sequence of turnings that had taken me three visits to learn and that I could now complete without thought, the way a man navigates the interiors of his own home in darkness. The door was opened before I knocked by a woman called Siu-Wan, who had never, in fourteen months, addressed me by name or rank, and whose particular quality of not-quite-expression I had come to think of as the most honest face I saw in Canton.

She showed me to the back room. I settled into the chair—it was a large wooden chair, too formal for the space, incongruous and thus inevitable—and she prepared the pipe with the focused, unhurried attention of someone doing skilled work.

The pipe was heavier than most people imagined. I had thought, the first time, that it would be light—a delicate thing for a delicate activity. It was not. It had heft. It required two hands and a quality of stillness.

The smoke was particular. Not sweet, despite what was written about it—that was a literary idea, sweetness; the reality was closer to something mineral, something that tasted of the earth it came from, of the distance it had traveled. My lungs accepted it without protest. This was the relief—not pleasure, which was elsewhere and earlier and already gone, but relief: the particular corporeal gratitude of a thing returning to its accustomed state.

The ceiling here was white plaster, water-stained in one corner, a brown aureole spreading from the join of wall and ceiling like a continent seen from distance. I had spent many hours studying its coastline.

In the lane outside, something—a cart, or a pair of men, hard to say—passed at the slow pace of the afternoon. The shadow moved in the window's lower pane and was gone.

I breathed out.

The child was somewhere else now, or had stopped, or I had ceased to hear the knocking. The copper had left my mouth. The afternoon light coming through the window was the colour of the Pearl before the mist burned off—gauze-filtered, everything held at its proper distance, even the near things near, even the sharp improbable fact of where I was and what I had done today, all of it present and correctly positioned and requiring nothing further of me.

I breathed in.

The stain on the ceiling had the precise shape of Tristan da Cunha.
