# The Last Outpost
## Chapter One

The *Outpost* has a sound for every kind of night.

I learned to read them the way you learn anything out here—slowly, through repetition, through small disasters that teach you what the normal ones look like. A slow night hums low and even, like something breathing in its sleep. A good night has a pitch to it, a tightness, voices climbing over each other and the clatter of glasses keeping time. Tonight sits right in the middle: warm, busy, a little loud. Comfortable.

I know comfortable. I've spent three years building it.

"Marisol, your order's up." I set the glass at the near end of the bar without breaking stride, already moving toward the Deltan couple in the middle who've been trying to flag me down for four minutes. I know they've been trying because I've watched them try, cataloguing their patience the same way I catalogue everything in here—the slow draining of Drix's second Calloway Dark in booth three, the particular angle at which Captain Ferosh holds his shoulders when he's losing at cards, the smell of whatever Yennick is cooking in the back that's been drifting into the bar all evening and improving everyone's mood without their knowing why.

This is what I do. This is what I've made.

"Sparkling or still?" I ask the couple.

"Sparkling," she says.

"Still," he says.

They look at each other.

I leave them to it and pour both. Three years has taught me most disagreements resolve themselves if you give them thirty seconds and something to do with their hands.

The station groans—just slightly, just briefly—as a cluster of debris from the outer belt ticks against the hull. It's a familiar percussion. Half the new arrivals flinch the first time they hear it, and you can always spot them by that flinch. Everyone else barely blinks. I don't blink at all.

I hand over the sparkling and the still, pocket the tip, and move back toward the center of the bar just in time to catch Ferosh jabbing a finger at the table.

"That's not what we agreed—"

"Ferosh." I say it without volume, which is the only way to say it. He looks up. I give him a look that communicates, without requiring elaboration, that we have done this before, we both know how it ends, and I have better things to do with the next ten minutes than supervise his temper at the card table. "Take a breath."

He takes a breath.

"Yennick's got that spiced flatbread coming out soon," I add. "You want a round sent over, or are we doing this again?"

A long pause. Ferosh leans back. "Round. Fine." He waves a hand in the direction of his opponent that is technically an apology, if you squint.

I go to put the order in. This is the shape of an evening. I know its proportions by heart.

---

The glass breaks somewhere around the tenth hour.

I don't hear it break so much as feel it—a shift in the room's texture, a new hole in the sound where something whole used to be. Behind me, toward the eastern cluster of tables, followed immediately by a whoop of laughter and a scrape of chairs.

"That's coming out of your tab," I call out, without turning.

"Wasn't my glass!" someone protests.

"Whose was it?"

Silence is its own kind of answer. I finish wiping down the section of bar in front of me—slow, even strokes, the same motion a thousand times over—and turn to survey the damage. Lorvik, one of Ferosh's regulars, is already on his knees picking up pieces. I catch his eye and nod once, and he nods back. He knows where the dustcloth is. He's used it before.

"Nets." Marisol-Seven materializes at the bar, half-empty glass in hand, picking up her monologue exactly where she left off. I don't know precisely where she left off because I only follow fifty percent of Marisol-Seven's monologues, which is the right amount. "—and anyway I told him, I said, the manifest is the manifest, you can't just add cargo after filing—"

"You were right to say so." I refill her glass.

"Of course I was. I'm *always* right about manifests." She squints at me. "Nets. Are you listening?"

"Cargo manifests," I say. "Your ex-husband. Unambiguously your fault in no way."

She points at me. "Exactly." But she's studying me now with that look, the slightly too-careful one she gets when she's decided to try again. "You know, if you ever wanted to get off-station for a run—just a short one, I do the Calloway circuit every six weeks or so—"

"I'm happy here."

"Nobody's *happy* on an asteroid outpost."

"I contain multitudes." I smile at her. It's a real smile. That's important—the real ones close conversations without closing doors. "Let me know if you need anything."

I move before she can build up momentum again. It's not cruelty. Marisol-Seven will ask again next month; I'll deflect then too. This is the rhythm we've found. Most things out here have a rhythm, if you're patient enough to wait for it.

The broken glass is swept up. The card table settles. Yennick's flatbread arrives and improves three separate moods instantly.

I stand at the center of my bar and let the familiar hum of it move through me.

Then something changes.

---

I don't look up right away.

The room tells me first, the way it always does. A ripple in the pattern, small and unmistakable—Drix glancing toward the door from booth three, his card hand momentarily forgotten. The Deltan couple pausing in whatever minor argument they've found to replace the sparkling-versus-still one. A half-second of collective recalibration, so quick that no one in the room would consciously notice it, that I have consciously noticed roughly four hundred times over three years.

A new arrival.

I finish pouring. I complete the transaction I'm in the middle of. Then I look toward the door.

They're already moving toward the far end of the bar by the time I place them—or fail to place them, which is its own information. Humanoid arrangement, but the details go wrong in ways I can't immediately catalogue. The coloring is off, a pale grey that sits outside the range I'm used to, like something has been adjusted. Eyes a shade too wide, a fraction too present. They're wearing a jacket that has encountered a great many places and had very little time to recover between them, and their dreads—the bioluminescent tentacled kind, the ones you see on Cephali traders in the inner charts—hang slightly askew. Not fashionably. Genuinely.

Regulars in here are worn-in. This person is worn-out.

They settle onto the stool at the far end of the bar. The hum of the room absorbs them, slowly. I walk over.

"What can I get you?"

They look at the shelves behind me, but their eyes aren't really moving across the bottles. They're calculating something else. "Vega whisky, neat." Then, before I've even turned: "Better make that a double."

I reach for the Vega without commenting. You don't comment on the second order. The second order is a confidence told in the language of drink; you receive it and you keep it.

I pour the double and set it down. They wrap both hands around it—six fingers each, an extra joint below the knuckle—and don't drink. Just hold it. The way you hold something warm when you've been cold for a long time.

"Rough transit?" I ask.

"You could say."

I lean against the back counter. Keep my posture easy. I'm not in a hurry; I'm never in a hurry in a way that the customer can detect. "You passing through, or are you looking to dock awhile?"

A slight pause. "Passing through." A beat too long. "Maybe docking awhile. I haven't worked that out yet."

*Haven't worked it out, or don't have the option of working it out.* I file it. Don't touch it.

"Hatch Master Odova handles the logistics," I say. "He's fair, he's quick, and he doesn't ask unnecessary questions as long as you're polite and tip in hard currency. He had a situation with cryptocurrency."

The wide eyes shift to me for the first time with something that might be actual attention. "What kind of situation?"

"The kind nobody talks about, which is why we all just call it *the situation*." I tilt my head. "He's still not over it."

The stranger almost smiles. The dreads catch the bar light as they shift—blue, then a deep amber, then blue again. Bioluminescence. I've always liked the effect.

"I'm Nets," I say, and put my hand across the bar.

They look at it. Not rudely—more like they're reading it for information. Then: "Sable." They shake. The grip is careful, deliberate, the grip of someone who has decided in the last three seconds to make a choice and is living with that decision while it's still new.

"You're far from home, Sable." I say it gently. No accusation. Just a shape I'm laying on the bar for them to look at if they want to.

The almost-smile goes. They take their first actual sip of whisky.

"What makes you say that?"

"The Vega. It's an acquired taste—you have to have grown up near enough to the inner systems for it to feel like home before you can drink it without making a face. New drinkers always flinch." I pause. "You didn't flinch."

They look at me again. That same considering quality. "You didn't, either."

"I order it," I say, and reach for a glass to polish. "Because it's good, and because sometimes people need a taste of somewhere else."

*And because I used to know what it tasted like before it tasted like relief.* But I don't say that part.

---

The bar moves around us, indifferent and alive. A fresh round at Ferosh's table. Lorvik telling a story I've heard before with a new ending. The hull percussion, distant and familiar.

Sable and I talk around the edges of something neither of us has named.

They ask me small things. How long has the *Outpost* been here. Whether the supply runs come reliably. Whether there are any authority presences on the station—they say it casually, but there's nothing casual about the asking. I give them the true answers, which are five years, usually, and no, not since the sector re-zoning pushed the transit lanes further north.

I don't ask much. I ask about the inner systems, because I said what I said about the Vega and it's out there now. I ask about the Cephali traders, because the dreads are a fact that sits between us and it's easier to name it than to leave it hovering.

"Not Cephali," they say. "The dreads are—" A pause. A decision. "Cultural. Courtesy. The people I was traveling with."

*Were traveling with.* Past tense.

"How long ago?" I ask.

Their hands shift on the glass. "Long enough."

---

"Someone told me," Sable says, quiet enough that only I'd catch it under the noise of the room, "that the *Outpost* was the kind of place where people don't dig."

I set down the glass I'm polishing. "Who told you that?"

They don't answer.

I consider them across the bar—this worn-out stranger with their carefully held glass and their too-wide eyes and their past-tense companions—and think about the particular arithmetic of whether someone is safe. I know that arithmetic. I've worked this problem from the other side.

"The *Outpost* doesn't dig," I say. "That's accurate." I hold their gaze. "I make my own calculations."

A long moment.

"Is that a threat?"

"No." I don't look away. "It's a distinction. One I think you're equipped to understand."

Sable looks down at their glass. The dreads shift. When they look up again, something in the expression has moved—barely, like the difference between a locked door and an unlocked one that's still closed.

"There's something in my ship," they say.

The bar hums around us. No one is listening.

"Something I need to not have found."

The sentence lands between us with a weight that isn't volume, isn't drama. Just the plain gravity of a real thing. I can hear, in the gaps of it, a shape I don't have words for yet: a *not have found* that belongs to someone specific. Someone looking.

I should ask. I know I should ask.

I reach for the Vega instead.

Sable's glass is empty. I pour without being asked, and set it down, and let the silence between us be the comfortable kind—just for one more moment, just until they're ready, just until I've decided what I'm going to do with the weight of that sentence.

"Third one," I say, "is on the house."
