# Darkroom Revelations

Here is what Dani knows about herself: she asks for things. In seminar, when Professor Meade pauses and lets the silence pool, she is the one who speaks into it—not to fill space, not from nerves, but because she has something to say and she says it. In relationships, she has been the one who texted first, kissed first, said *I like you* with her eyes open. She takes up the room she needs. She has been told this is a lot. She has been told it with admiration and with something that was not admiration, and she has learned to tell the difference, and to decide accordingly.

She is not used to waiting. She is not good at waiting. She has, for one semester, been waiting.

---

Elliot Vasquez sits two rows ahead of her in Intermediate Film Photography, which means she has had sixteen weeks to study the back of his head, the collar of the coat he rotates through three versions of—all thrifted, all too large, all somehow exactly right—and the particular stillness that comes over him before he speaks in class. Most people's silences are filled with preparation. His silence is different. It's consultative. He seems to be quietly asking himself whether the thing he's about to say is the thing he actually means.

She has watched him look at photographs.

Not take them—look at them. The way he holds a print, at a slight distance, with his head tipped, as though he's listening for something. He does this in critique, when everyone else is performing commentary. He is the only person in the room who seems to have forgotten there's a room.

This is the inventory she has assembled: three coats. The pause before speaking. The way he holds prints. The fact that he once stayed after class to ask Meade about silver grain structure and then stood by the door for twenty minutes after Meade left, looking at the example photographs pinned to the wall.

She has catalogued all of this. She has been waiting for an occasion to do something with it.

When Meade announces darkroom pairings, she hears her name and Elliot's in the same sentence and writes the date of their first session in her planner before she's processed what she's feeling.

---

The darkroom is at the end of the Visual Arts building, past the painting studios that always smell of linseed oil and quiet crisis. There is a changing room, then a door, then the room itself: long and narrow, lined with trays and enlargers, the red safelight making everything the color of something borrowed from a dream. Elliot holds the door for her. She walks in first.

"The red makes it feel like a submarine," she says. "That's my opener. I've been workshopping it."

"It's good," he says, setting his bag down. He looks around the room with something she might call recognition.

"You've used this one before?"

"A few times. With the advanced class last year."

"I didn't know you took it."

"I audited it."

She files that. He didn't have to be here. He chose to learn this. She says: "I always thought it was strange that photographers are afraid of light."

"We're not afraid of it. We're precise about it." He's already looking at the enlarger, adjusting something with one hand. "Too much, too soon—" He shrugs. "You wreck the whole thing."

She knows this. She has known this for weeks, has read the technical materials, understands the photochemistry. She knows that the red safelight works because photographic paper is insensitive to those wavelengths, that it's a mercy, a controlled exception. She knows that what looks red in here looks like everything else once it's fixed. She's known all of this since the unit started. But standing in the red room with Elliot explaining it to the enlarger, she lets him tell her anyway.

There is something about the light in here. It flattens his features slightly, softens the edges of him, makes his face harder to read. She notices that he doesn't seem to mind. In the fluorescent seminar room, he tends to sit near the wall. Here, in the red dark, his posture is different. Looser. He moves around the room like he knows where things are.

She notes this. She does not yet have a name for what she's noting.

---

Their project is a collaborative portrait series—each of them will photograph the other, and they'll develop the prints together. Simple enough. Tonight they're beginning with Dani's self-portraits from an earlier assignment; Elliot suggested they start with something already shot, learn each other's process before they turn the cameras on each other.

They work. The room is not quiet—there is the sound of chemistry, of trays being adjusted, of the timer's small ticks. Dani is good at this part, the technical part, the sequence of steps that must happen in order. She finds that doing something with her hands makes her better at conversation. She's making a note of this, thinking about what it means for how she usually does things, when Elliot says:

"There's something about this one." He's looking at a self-portrait of hers—she'd taken it in her apartment, pointing the camera at the bathroom mirror. "The way you're not—" He stops. He looks at it differently. "Sorry. Never mind."

"What?"

"It's not—" He turns back to the developer tray, adjusts the print's submersion with a pair of tongs. "I was going to say something that's actually about me, not your photograph. Which is bad critique."

She looks at him. In the red light, she cannot quite make out whether he's flushed. She wants to press—*what about you, what were you going to say, say it*—feels the question shaped and ready in her mouth.

She doesn't ask it.

She doesn't know why, exactly. It's effortful, not asking. She is aware of the effort in a physical way, a clenching somewhere in her chest. She turns back to the tray and they watch the image come up together in silence, and she keeps replaying the unfinished thing—*not* and then the redirect, not and then nothing—until the timer goes.

---

The photo paper is in a stack on the counter beside the enlarger, and it's Dani who reaches past Elliot for the lens settings and she's not sure which of them actually touches the stack first—her arm moving past, his elbow at the wrong angle—but the paper slides, the edge catches the safelight filter in a way that lets in a brief, catastrophic slant of white from the hallway, and then it's over, and the silence that follows is very specific.

Twenty sheets, maybe. Enough for the week's work. She stares at the stack.

"It's light-struck," she says. "The whole—"

"I know."

"We can't—"

"I know." He picks up a corner of the paper, sets it back down. "It's okay. Meade keeps a reserve stock. I know where it is. We can get more next session."

"The timeline—"

"Dani." She looks at him. "It's okay. It was an accident."

She wants to assign it. Her mind keeps trying to reconstruct the sequence—her arm, his elbow, the angle of the stack—and she knows this is not useful and she cannot stop doing it. She is aware of a specific frustration, granular and hot, not quite at him and not quite at herself.

Then it passes. Or not passes—it subsides, moves to the side.

The ruined paper floats in the developer tray where Elliot has put it, already beginning to fog. The enlarger is still humming. They have nothing left to expose.

"We have time," Elliot says. He sits down on the low stool near the fixer tray and puts his hands on his knees. "If you wanted to stay. We don't have to do anything with it. We could just—" He looks at his hands. "I've been thinking about something. I don't know if this is the right time."

"There's no paper," she says. "And you have my attention."

He takes a breath. The red light sits on everything like a mood.

"I've been thinking about myself," he says. "About—some things. That I've been—" He pauses. The pause is different from his classroom pauses. This one is asking something harder. "I've been trying to figure out what's accurate. When I look at pictures of myself. I keep—it doesn't—" He stops. Starts differently. "I don't know how to say this yet. I want to say it the right way and I don't have the right way yet."

"You don't have to have the right way," she says.

He nods. He is looking at the wall above the fixer tray.

"I think I've been understanding myself wrong," he says. "Or—not wrong. Incompletely. Like I've been working from a proof that was missing a step." He pauses. "Does that make sense or does that sound insane."

"It makes sense," she says, and she means it, and she is simultaneously, in the part of herself that runs faster than her speaking self, doing something that feels like urgent excavation. She is going through her own history—the people she's wanted, the shapes of them, what specifically she was responding to—and she is doing this not to locate herself but to locate something she didn't know she was carrying: a preference for a particular kind of readability. She had always known, roughly, what she was working with. She has been good at knowing. She has liked knowing.

She misses something he says. She catches the tail of it—*—since probably high school, honestly*—and she doesn't ask him to repeat it. She adjusts. She listens harder.

She is going through her own files. She is doing it because she doesn't want to impose her categories on him—doesn't want to already know what she thinks he is before he tells her who he's finding himself to be. The reckoning is not about her. But it has to happen, she understands, quickly and honestly, so that she can be present for him without her own unexamined assuming landing on him like a weight. She is doing this for him. It does not feel comfortable. It is supposed to be for him.

He is talking about a photograph of himself. From when he was fourteen. How he'd looked at it recently and felt—

"Corrected," he says. "Like I'd been going in a direction, and the photograph from before I knew that direction, it looked more—"

He doesn't finish.

"Accurate?" she offers.

He looks at her. In the red light, she reads him as moved and also frightened. She does not try to name what he has not named.

"Yes," he says. "Accurate."

---

The ruined paper floats in the tray. The enlarger hums. Dani says:

"I don't have a full picture of you. I've been—I've been watching you all semester and I have details, but they don't—they don't resolve into something I completely understand." She looks at the paper, the dissolving potential of it. "I think that's probably why I can't stop looking."

There is a silence that is not empty.

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me in a long time," Elliot says.

"I wasn't trying to be honest. I was trying to tell you something about your photographs."

"You were doing both." He is looking at her now, not at the wall. The red light does its thing—softens, flattens, takes the sharp edges away. She can see him anyway. "I have some photographs. Of myself. That I've taken in here. At night, when no one else was using the room." He looks at his hands again. "I haven't shown anyone."

"What are they like?"

"I don't know yet," he says. "I'm still figuring out what they're of."

---

They stay past when they needed to.

Dani helps Elliot put the trays in order. She writes their names on the masking tape labels for the chemical bottles they'll use next time. The ruined paper is still in the developer, fully black now, fully exposed, all its potential spent. She leaves it. She doesn't know whose fault it was and she has stopped trying to determine this and she finds, to her own mild surprise, that she means it.

At the door, Elliot collects his bag. She reaches for the safelight switch.

She turns it off.

For one second the room is completely dark—the particular complete dark of a room sealed against light, the dark of a place that was designed for this. She stands in it. She is aware of Elliot somewhere to her right. She is aware of the chemical smell and the sound of the fixer dripping and the sound of her own breathing. She is aware that she walked into this room knowing, with some confidence, who she was in relation to other people: the one who moves forward, the one who decides, the one for whom attraction has always resolved cleanly into a known quantity.

She does not have access to that certainty right now. She notes its absence. She does not try to reconstruct it.

Then the door opens, and the fluorescent light of the hallway comes in, and Elliot holds it for her the way he held the outer door, and she walks out into the ordinary corridor with its ordinary institutional light and its smell of linseed oil and her face is just her face again, fully visible, nothing softened.

They both stand there for a moment. Down the hall, someone is doing something in one of the studios—there is the sound of a chair, a movement, the particular noise of a building being ordinarily inhabited.

"Same time Thursday?" Elliot says.

"I'll text you," she says.

He nods. He starts down the hall, and after a few steps he does the thing she has watched him do in class—he slows slightly, as though confirming something—and then continues. She watches him until he rounds the corner.

She stands in the fluorescent hallway with her bag over one shoulder. The darkroom door is closed behind her. She knows she should walk to her car. She knows the shapes of what happened in there, can name most of them, can put them in order. She does not know yet what develops from them.

She thinks: *too much, too soon, you wreck the whole thing*.

She thinks: not yet. Wait. Let it come up on its own.

She walks to her car. The night is cold and ordinary and she is in it, whoever she is now, someone in process, someone whose most familiar room has just been flooded with a different kind of light—not ruined, she decides. Not ruined.

She does not yet know what to call this.

She starts the car. She gives herself that: the true thing of not yet knowing.
