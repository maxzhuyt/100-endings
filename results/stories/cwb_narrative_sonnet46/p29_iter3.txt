# Polymerase Chain Reaction

The mushrooms are brighter tonight.

Maren notices this the way she notices most things now—sideways, through the modified rods clustered in her left eye's peripheral field, which render low-light spectra as a greenish wash her brain has spent the better part of a year learning to call *seeing*. The fungi trace the tile cracks in slow foxfire pulses, blue-white, bioluminescent, descendants of a contamination event that happened in this room before this room was hers, before she had collapsed the word *laboratory* and the word *home* into a single load-bearing noun. They glow along the baseboards and up one corner of the wall beside the fume hood. They have learned the room's geometry. She has learned theirs.

She does not yet think about why they are brighter.

Her hands are inside the laminar flow hood, moving through the negative-pressure field with the deliberate, wrist-led economy of someone who has learned not to disturb the air she cannot afford to contaminate. The hood is custom—built from a decommissioned bathroom exhaust fan, a HEPA filter she'd traded a month's worth of wound-sealant compound to obtain, and a plexiglass shell she'd heat-formed herself over a wooden mold she then burned for fuel. It functions. It functions well enough, which is the only category of judgment she applies to most things now. The HEPA seal along the left side weeps slightly when the wind comes from the north. She has packed it with silicone she rendered from salvaged caulk. She monitors it.

The thermocycler on the bench beside her is running cycle eleven of thirty-five. She built it in February from a commercial sous-vide immersion circulator, a PID controller stripped from a ceramics kiln, and an aluminum heating block she spent nine weeks machining by hand with equipment that was itself improvised from equipment that had been improvised before her. It holds temperature within plus-or-minus 0.4 degrees Celsius. She wanted 0.3. There are nights she still feels the failure of 0.4 the way she feels the north-wind leak in the hood seal—a small, specific wrongness she carries in the front of her attention, careful not to set it down.

Inside the cycler sits the last viable genetic material from what was, in the world before the Unraveling, designated *Triticum aestivum*, cultivar GoldStrand-12. A wheat. Engineered by Castellan BioSystems for drought tolerance and nitrogen-efficient growth, GoldStrand-12 had fed approximately forty million people across three continents for seven years. Engineered also—elegantly, irreversibly—with a second-generation terminator system that Castellan's IP attorneys had branded a *Germline Stability Covenant*, which was the most expensive lie Maren had ever encountered written in codon sequences. The covenant worked by expressing a recombinase enzyme at seed maturation, one that excised a small but load-bearing section of the embryo's development-initiation pathway. Not enough to make the seed obviously inert. Enough to ensure it never germinated. You could collect the grain, mill it, eat it, hold it in your hand in autumn sunlight and feel its weight and smell its starch-smell and believe, in every sensory particular, that you held next year's planting in your palm. You held nothing. There was no next year's planting. There was only Castellan's spring catalogue and Castellan's pricing structure and Castellan's regional distribution representatives, and Maren had spent three years of her post-Unraveling life hating this with a white and technical fury before she understood that hatred was not a substrate she could work with and redirected her attention to the restriction enzymes.

The question is whether anything viable remains in the template. The source material is fourteen months old, stored in a residential freezer that lost power eight times during that period. Degradation is the default state of biological material; preservation is the intervention, and interventions fail. She has extracted what she can extract. The cycler will amplify what the template still carries—and also, necessarily, amplify the gaps, the breaks, the corrupted regions where adenine should follow cytosine and instead there is noise, there is nothing, there is the molecular record of every freezer failure and every handling error and every degree of temperature fluctuation across fourteen months of imperfect cold chain. The PCR process does not distinguish between signal and corrupted signal. It faithfully reproduces both.

She has thought about this for eleven months. She thinks about it now, waiting, the cycler's heating block cycling through its programmed temperatures with the rhythm that has become, for her, the sound of concentrated hope: *denature, anneal, extend. Denature, anneal, extend.* Heat to breaking. Cool to binding. Extend into something new. The machine's internal fan produces a low whirring undertone. The mushrooms pulse. She turns these rhythms over in the part of her mind that is always running a parallel process, the part that was not made for molecular biology specifically but that took to it the way water takes to a channel—finding the available path, moving through.

She thinks: the corporation understood what they were doing. This is not a point she reaches through anger anymore; anger was an early-stage processing error she has largely corrected. They understood that if you engineer degradation into the substrate—into the seed, into the genome, into the foundational biological material—you do not need to maintain coercive infrastructure. The coercion is delegated to biology itself. The terminator sequence does not require enforcement. It simply expresses. And after the networks collapsed and the regional reps stopped returning messages and the catalogues stopped coming and the spring planting season arrived anyway, indifferent to the dissolution of corporate distribution systems—the terminator sequences kept expressing. In fields across several thousand kilometers of former agricultural land, billions of seeds performed their engineered futility with perfect, posthumous corporate fidelity.

She considers the phrase *posthumous corporate fidelity*. Files it in the same category as *0.4 degrees Celsius*—a specific wrongness she carries carefully.

The cycler chimes. Cycle twelve.

She checks her right hand before she moves to the gel rig. The tremor begins at the base of her ring finger and propagates toward the wrist in a movement she has charted on a progression curve now encompassing nineteen data points over twenty-six days. The curve is comprehensible. She dislikes comprehensible curves with negative slopes. She dislikes them with particular intensity when the dependent variable is her own peripheral nervous system, but she understood this risk when she accepted the modification, or understood it as well as the documentation allowed—which is to say she understood the mechanism, the probability range, and the basic biological logic of the tradeoff, and she had decided, as she decides most things, that operational necessity takes precedence over personal outcome when one's operational necessity involves keeping three hundred people alive through a second consecutive crop failure.

What the documentation had not mentioned—what she suspects no documentation for this modification yet reflects, because the trials were necessarily short and the Unraveling had scattered whatever longitudinal follow-up might have caught it—is the immune response. The same modification that had reorganized her rod-cell expression had apparently altered something upstream in her inflammatory pathway. She'd had a metacarpal fracture in January. It was not comfortable. It was clinically unremarkable in its recovery, which should have taken six to eight weeks and instead took three. She'd had a respiratory infection in March that she documented carefully because she expected to be seriously ill and intended to manage her workload accordingly. The infection resolved in four days without intervention. She keeps a separate log. She does not discuss this log with the settlement council, not because she distrusts them specifically but because she cannot yet explain what she is observing in terms that would be useful to them, and she has found that offering data you cannot yet interpret to people under existential pressure produces emotional rather than analytical responses, which helps no one.

The low-light vision has saved her twice in the dark between the annex and the water collection station, once from a structural collapse she saw coming as a greenish shimmer of displaced air and once from something she doesn't categorize except as a reminder that the dark between buildings now has more inhabitants than it used to. She considers these things part of the cost structure. She adjusts the cost structure when new information arrives. She has a threshold below which she has decided not to grieve losses, and she acknowledges, privately, that the threshold has moved.

The contract arrived three weeks ago through her supplier contact—a person of indeterminate background and practical ethics who facilitated trade between settlements and independent operators without troubling any party with information the other parties hadn't specifically requested. The offer was concrete: one viable, self-replicating cereal grain cultivar, delivered before first planting season, capable of producing seed-true offspring. Payment on delivery: a twelve-month supply of broad-spectrum antibiotic compound sufficient to address the respiratory infection cluster killing the settlement's elderly population. Four confirmed deaths in the last six weeks. More coming.

The timeline was eleven weeks. Maren had said yes.

She'd said it before the contact finished the terms, in fact, which she thinks about now—the speed of it, the absence of deliberation. She does not think the speed was careless. She thinks the speed was accurate: she had already been running this calculation for months, and the contact's arrival had simply provided the variable she'd been waiting to enter. The trap closed and she had already decided to be inside it.

She is inside it. The cycler runs. The mushrooms glow.

She begins preparing the gel.

The electrophoresis tank is illuminated from below by mushroom-light coming up through a crack in the bench's laminate surface, where the underlying particleboard has softened and separated and the fungi have found their way through. The UV transilluminator burned out in December. She had grieved this loss slightly above her threshold level and then adapted. The mushrooms' bioluminescence falls in a spectrum that does not precisely match ethidium bromide's excitation wavelength, but it falls close enough, transmitted through the gel at the right angles, to produce a reading she can interpret if she is careful and if she has calibrated her left eye's modified field to account for the blue-white shift. She has done both of these things.

The gel tonight is a problem. The bands from the test sample are present—she can see them, faint ghosts of sequence where she needs sequence, the primers she synthesized binding to what remains of the wheat's usable template—but they are diffuse in a way she doesn't like. Smearing. The degradation is faster than her model predicted at this temperature, in this humidity, in this room where she cannot fully control environmental conditions because the room is a former office in a former annex building with three windows she's covered in six-mil poly sheeting that does not seal to the wall with any reliability.

She adjusts the primer concentration. She records the adjustment. She prepares a second gel with a modified buffer ratio and runs it in parallel.

At cycle nineteen, something goes wrong with the cycler's extension-phase timer. She notices it on the PID controller display: a two-second deviation, consistent, repeating, which means the extension phase is truncating and the polymerase is not completing its run on longer fragments. She opens the controller's interface—a laminated instruction sheet she wrote herself, because the kiln's original manual was in a language she reads only approximately and covered only the kiln—and recalibrates the extension time manually, adding three seconds of buffer. This will add forty minutes to the total run. She adjusts her schedule and does not think about the antibiotic payment and does not think about the four confirmed deaths and does not think about eleven weeks, now ten weeks, and works.

At cycle twenty-seven, the second gel shows her what she did not design.

She sees it and does not immediately allow herself to understand it. This is a discipline she has developed—the deliberate lag between observation and interpretation, the insistence on looking longer before deciding what you are looking at. She looks. The bands she wanted are present: the flanking sequences, the terminator region cut cleanly on both sides, the evidence that her synthesized restriction enzymes found their targets and performed their function. This is what she asked for. This is what she spent eleven months working toward.

Below the expected bands—faint, consistent, replicable in both parallel gels, which means it is not artifact—there is a second cluster she did not introduce.

She runs a sequence prediction on the annex's surviving laptop. The laptop runs hot; the fan has a crack in one blade that produces a rhythmic clicking she has learned to hear as neutral, the way she's learned to hear the cycler's whirr as neutral, as simply the sound of the work being done. While it processes, she sits. The mushrooms pulse blue-white. They are definitely brighter than they were when she started tonight; she can see this now clearly enough to admit she's been seeing it for an hour.

The prediction resolves.

The insertion sits in a heat-stress response region. It came from the contamination background of the room—from the lab's former life, from the decades of biological work done in this annex before the Unraveling redistributed the building's population and purpose, from the ambient DNA that persists in a research space the way grief persists in a house. Some fragment of it found its way into the degraded template during an early cycle, when the template was already breaking down and the molecular context was, functionally, an open door.

The insertion codes for upregulated growth expression under sustained thermal stress. A plant carrying this sequence would respond to prolonged heat by increasing rather than suppressing its growth-phase expression. Under the conditions the cultivar was originally designed for—the average temperatures of a world that no longer exists—this trait would be neutral at best, a metabolic cost at worst. Under the conditions of the world that does exist, which runs four degrees warmer than the baseline model by conservative measurement and warmer than that in the east field where the settlement's primary planting happens, where the black soil holds heat past dusk well into the growing season—

She sits with this for a long time.

The mushrooms pulse. She finds herself watching them with a clarity that has something of fear in it and something else she doesn't immediately name.

The contract specifies viable, self-replicating cultivar. It does not specify this. She has no obligation to disclose an unintended trait that arrived out of noise, out of contamination, out of the Unraveling's long settling of biological debris into new configurations. She could package what she has made and deliver it and accept the antibiotic payment and two months from now the settlement would have a wheat that grows—actually grows, seed-true, persistent, uncovenanted—and she would not have lied, technically, about anything.

The contact would learn eventually. Whoever planted the grain would see eventually. A wheat that responds to heat by growing harder, by investing more energy in seed development when the temperature climbs past a threshold that the current climate ensures it hits every July, that hits it reliably for six weeks running—someone would notice. Someone would come back to ask where it came from. Someone would want to know what else came out of Maren's annex.

She picks up her pen. She opens the log that is not the council's log. She writes a description of the insertion, its predicted function, its likely origin. She writes it in the technical register she uses for things she is not yet ready to think about emotionally—precise, thorough, annotated. She gives it a sample designation. She notes the possibility that it is heritable, that it will express consistently in daughter plants, that the trait could propagate through a population of planted grain across multiple seasons.

She closes the log.

She begins disassembling the gel rig. Her right hand trembles on the glass plate and she slows, compensates, uses the left hand to stabilize. She notes the worsening in the other log—the one that measures things happening to her body—and then puts both logs away and moves to the packaging materials.

What she makes of the insertion, she has not yet decided. She knows the shape of the decision without knowing its content: it is a decision about who is owed what, about what a person is permitted to keep when they find something in the wreckage, about whether an accident that benefits many people is the same as a gift, or a theft, or something that has no existing category and requires one to be built.

She has time. She has ten weeks. She can build a category.
