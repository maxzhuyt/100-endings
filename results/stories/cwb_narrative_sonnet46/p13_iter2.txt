# Blood Debt

The temple had no roof left to speak of. What remained were three walls and their suggestions—stone faces webbed with cracks, moss grown thick along every seam where winter had entered over the decades and simply stayed. Someone had wedged torches into the rubble at uneven intervals, and the light they threw was the color of old rust, unsteady enough that the shadows along the walls appeared to shift and breathe as though something inside the stone were still alive and restless. The floor was the worst of it. The flagstones had buckled across their whole expanse—roots had come up from beneath, frost had worked in from the sides, and the result was a broken terrain of ridges and sudden drops and cracked seams wide enough to catch a heel. Anyone who fought here would need to read that floor. Anyone who did not would pay for the ignorance.

They had arrived in the way that careful people arrive at things they are not supposed to be doing. Small groups, through the treeline, twenty minutes between each cluster. Now they pressed themselves against the standing walls in a dense and quiet mass, two dozen bodies breathing fog into the cold air. A few held paper lanterns that did little but soften the darkness immediately around their faces. Most did not bother with light. These were people accustomed to business conducted without it—money moved from palm to palm without ceremony, odds whispered, nods given that meant *yes* and meant *no* and meant *if this goes wrong, I was never here*. They pressed closer as the moment approached, and their collective weight and hunger changed the quality of the air inside those three walls. They were not spectators. They were the fourth wall the temple was missing, and without them this would have been two men alone in a ruin, which would have meant something different entirely.

The two fighters stood at opposite ends of the floor.

Takeda Rōichi wore black, his school's armband—red edged in black—knotted at his left bicep. He was twenty-three years old and had not moved since he took his position. He was still in a way that was almost architecturally deliberate, his right hand raised to chest height, his weight loaded deep into his rear leg, his eyes fixed on the man across the floor with an expression that was not anger, precisely, but something colder than anger and more total—the expression of a fire that has already decided what it will consume and is waiting only on opportunity. His sensei had told him, beginning when he was seven years old and could barely hold a bokken, that the first strike was everything. That if the first strike was perfect there would be no second. Takeda had heard this across so many years and in so many postures that it had stopped being instruction long ago. It had become something closer to a belief in God—not a thought he repeated but a shape his body had taken on.

Wei Chunming moved. He had not stopped moving since he stepped onto the floor, and the movement was not nerves—it was the opposite of nerves. His feet shifted in small rolling arcs, his shoulders rotated in a loose continuous cycle, his hands drew slow figures at his hips. He was built wide through the chest and narrow at the waist, and his body carried the quality of rope under tension: nothing rigid, nothing wasted, but something stored and available. His armband—red edged in black, like the other, different in the only ways that mattered to the people watching—sat on his right arm. He had learned Piguaquan in his uncle's freight yard, in cold mornings before anyone else was awake, then under three different teachers in three different cities, and then finally alone, which was when he understood it most clearly, because alone there was no one to impress and no form to perform for and the body found what it actually knew. He had been dismissed by men in clean white gi. He had been looked through, talked past, sent to the service entrance of every room he had ever tried to enter correctly. He was here because he was done managing other people's discomfort with his presence. He was here because he had been patient long enough that the word had lost meaning.

Near the east wall, a man raised his hand.

He lowered it.

Takeda came first.

He crossed the distance in two steps that were controlled detonations—his rear foot driving the first explosion, his lead foot accelerating the second—and he brought his right hand down in a vertical hammer-strike aimed at the junction of Wei's neck and shoulder, the Jigen-ryū opener designed not to open a fight but to close one, to drive the opponent into the earth on the first motion and leave them there. It was fast enough that the torchlight seemed to pull away from it.

Wei was no longer where he had been.

His right foot had stepped forty-five degrees offline, one smooth pivot carrying his whole body out of the strike's path, and his left arm rose in a whipping arc that caught Takeda's descending forearm and redirected it—not blocked, *redirected*, the force allowed to continue past his ear and spend itself on air. In the same extension, unpaused, Wei's right arm snapped forward with the elbow leading and the forearm cracking across Takeda's jaw on the return swing. It was not a punch. It was a flail. The contact was brief and very loud.

The crowd inhaled together, one sound.

Takeda took two steps back and reset. His jaw was already reddening along the line of the cheekbone. He had been struck before; it did not unsettle him. What moved through him in the two seconds of resetting was something smaller and sharper than fear—a private and precise recognition that he had not seen the counter coming. Not the strike, not the redirection, nothing after the first motion. He had not seen any of it.

He came again. This time with accumulation, with pressure—two strikes, three, pushing forward because forward was what Jigen-ryū answered with when certainty became uncertain. Wei moved in circles around him—not retreating, never retreating, but rotating, letting each strike arrive at angles that had been vacated half a heartbeat prior. The margin was unreasonably small, deliberately so. The Piguaquan footwork had already found the broken floor and made use of it in ways Takeda's straight-line advances could not accommodate. Wei stepped to the inside of a cracked flagstone ridge that forced Takeda to step around it, and the step around it opened his left side, and a horizontal whipping strike found the floating rib there and drove in.

Takeda felt it.

He catalogued it and kept moving.

They separated. Both men were breathing audibly now, their breath visible in the cold air. The crowd had found its voice in pieces—someone shouting in Japanese near the north wall, someone answering in Mandarin, the two sounds layering into something that stopped being language and became a pressure.

*His master's hands on his wrists at age eight, correcting the grip. His master's voice, which had never once softened: the first strike is everything. If the first strike is perfect there is no second.*

*Every door he had ever been directed away from. Every room in which he had understood, within the first minutes, that he was expected to be grateful merely for being permitted to remain.*

Neither of them was thinking about technique.

Takeda threw a right-hand strike that had nothing formal in it. It came not from the system but from somewhere beneath the system, from an older and less disciplined jurisdiction—fast, wild, traveling in a line that held no particular elegance. It came because holding the form had stopped being possible, because the pressure of this floor and this night and this man's persistent circular refusal to simply receive what was being offered had eroded the layer of training and exposed what lived under it. The strike found Wei's cheekbone as Wei's evasion began precisely half a breath too late.

Wei went down to one knee.

The crowd came apart.

Takeda stood over him. He stood there for one breath, then two, and across his face moved something that was not mercy—he was not sure he felt mercy—but was perhaps its structural neighbor: a sudden acute awareness of being watched, of being present in a moment that would exist in other people's accounts of the night long after the night itself was finished. He was visible. What he did next would not un-happen.

Wei looked up at him.

Then Wei rose.

He rose without ceremony and without any performance of difficulty, and when he had his feet fully under him he did not step back—he stepped forward, into Takeda's space, inside the distance that Jigen-ryū required for its first strike, negating that distance as a weapon. His body began to wind. Hips rotating first, then torso, then shoulder, collecting everything and storing it the way a rope stores the pull before it releases. The strike that came from all of that rotation was the full lateral whip of Piguaquan at its most committed, a forearm moving with the weight of his whole body behind it, nothing reserved. It caught Takeda across the temple.

He went down.

He did not get up.

The torches had burned lower. The crowd was already separating into its factions without being told to, already moving toward the treeline in the same careful intervals by which they had arrived, money changing hands in the dark, the arithmetic of the night being quietly settled. Someone's voice carried a number. Someone else's voice carried a different number. They became indistinguishable from the wind in the trees.

Wei stood in the center of the broken floor and did not move. He was bleeding from the cheekbone where Takeda's strike had landed. His breathing was the breathing of a man who has been very deep underwater and has recently returned. He was not celebrating. There was nothing in his posture that resembled triumph, and if the people dispersing around him noticed this, they did not remark on it.

On the flagstone between the two men, shaken loose in the final exchange, two armbands lay in the dust.

Red edged in black. Both of them the same colors. The torchlight moved across them and moved away.

Both men's masters would know by morning. This was not a question.

Takeda lay still with his eyes open, looking at the ceiling the temple no longer had, and above it the night sky with its cold indifferent arrangement of stars, and he was thinking—not planning, not yet, just thinking—about what his master's face would look like when the story arrived, and whether the shame of losing would outlast the shame of having come here at all, and whether those were even different shames or the same shame folded once.

Wei did not look at him. He looked at the armbands.

The debt had not been paid. A debt paid is discharged, is finished, leaves a space where it used to be. What had happened tonight was different. The debt had changed shape in the way that water changes shape—finding the same weight and volume in a new container, taking on the outline of whatever now held it. Tomorrow they would wake in the same world with the same histories and the same masters who had made those histories mean what they meant, and they would be more damaged than they had been, and that damage would be added to the ledger that had already been accumulating longer than either of them had been alive.

One of the last paper lanterns moved through the trees and disappeared.

The torches burned because torches burn until they don't.

The two armbands lay where they had fallen, in the dust of the broken floor, and the night settled over the roofless temple the way it settles over everything it outlasts.
