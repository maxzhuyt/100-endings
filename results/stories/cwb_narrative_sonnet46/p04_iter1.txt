# My Apocalypse Buddy

---

The former IKEA on the Purley Way had many advantages as a base of operations, not least of which was that it was large enough to get genuinely lost in, which Midas had found useful on three separate occasions and which had possibly saved his life on one of them. The roof had partially collapsed over the display kitchens, which meant there was a sky of sorts in the middle section, and this admitted light and occasionally rain and once a cormorant, none of which IKEA had originally intended. The rest of the structure remained sealed in the particular way of large retail spaces—a sealed quality, a sense of compressed air that had been recirculating since 2019 and had simply given up on the idea of freshness. The emergency lighting, running on some battery system that Midas did not understand and was afraid to investigate, produced a permanent orange twilight that made everything look like it was being reviewed by a committee.

Midas had furnished his corner of the former bedroom display section with some intention and a great deal of accident. He had a mattress that had been a floor model and was consequently named HÖVÅG and had a tag on it that he kept meaning to remove. He had a lamp that did not work. He had three lamps that did not work, arranged around the one lamp that did work, in the way that a court clusters around a monarch. He had a system for his supplies that he was prepared to defend intellectually and which was, by any objective measure, a system for distributing tins of chickpeas across a large area in a way that made them difficult to find.

He had Chewy.

Chewy was a FurReal Friends Cuddles My Giggly Monkey, except that he was not a monkey—he was a koala, and he was named Chewy in the way that survivors name things that are still with them: imprecisely and with deep conviction. He was battery-operated and required three double-As, of which Midas maintained a supply with the kind of religious seriousness that other men had historically reserved for prayer. He had been manufactured to respond to touch with pre-recorded vocalisations—chirpy, Australianly aspirational sentiments about eucalyptus and friendship—and his right eye was glass and his left eye was a slot where glass had been, and his fur was worn to the copper wire beneath it along his left flank in a way that Midas described as a scar and which was, technically, correct. His audio processor had been damaged at some point—Midas did not discuss when—so that his responses were no longer cleanly triggered by touch but emerged on a slight delay, or sometimes without apparent cause, which gave Chewy the quality of someone who had heard what you said and was choosing his moment.

It was seven-forty in the morning by the watch on Midas's wrist, which was accurate to within eleven minutes and which Midas had decided was accurate to within four.

"Right," said Midas. "So. The plan."

Chewy did not respond. The orange light sat on his remaining eye and made it look like he was thinking.

"The plan," Midas said again, pulling his map from under the mattress. The map had been a road atlas of Greater London, and it was now a road atlas of Greater London onto which Midas had drawn additional information in four different colours of pen, none of which he had reliably distinguished from one another in the half-dark, which meant that the map now contained a great deal of information and very little reliable intelligence. "We're going southeast. Staying off the A23 because last time—"

"Fun is what friends are for!" said Chewy.

"—because last time, there were dogs. Bad dogs. Whether they were bad before everything happened or whether they learned bad on the job is a philosophical question I'm leaving for later. So southeast via the side streets, through what used to be Thornton Heath, picking up anything useful on the way—there was a pharmacy that I think is worth revisiting—and then we're going to find this place."

He put his finger on the map at a point where three different colours of ink intersected in a way that suggested either a significant location or a small explosion.

"I found it marked—well, I found a *reference* to it. It will make sense when we get there. The point is: two hours out, two hours back, we're home before dark. Simple."

Chewy said nothing. He did nothing. His remaining eye caught the light.

"It's a *good* plan," said Midas, folding the map incorrectly.

---

The thing about the world, since the thing that had happened to the world, was that it had not ended so much as it had become extremely inconvenient. The mechanisms were all still there—roads, buildings, the underlying geometry of how places related to other places—but they had been subjected to the full force of about eighteen months of things going wrong without anyone fixing them, and the cumulative effect was that you could walk from Croydon to Crystal Palace and the landscape would feel familiar right up until the moment it felt deeply, creatively wrong.

Midas had a particular talent for finding himself in this zone. He moved through the world the way a man moves through a crowded room when he has not yet accepted that he is bad at this—with confidence, with optimism, with a powerful willingness to interpret obstacles as personality.

He carried Chewy in a sling arrangement made from a canvas bag with a hole cut in it, so that Chewy rode against his chest, looking out at the world with his one glass eye and his one dark slot. This was not a tactical arrangement. This was, as Midas would have described it if asked, extremely tactical.

The streets through Thornton Heath were a litany. A bookmakers with its windows staved in, bets uncollected. A nail salon where the nail polish had run in a long streak of coral across the floor and dried there, frozen in the act of reaching the door. A pile of traffic cones arranged in a spiral that was either art or a memorial or both, which had become a category with some overlap. A park where a child's swing moved slightly despite the absence of wind, which Midas did not look at for long. A Greggs that was completely intact, and this was somehow the worst thing Midas had seen all week.

He catalogued these things as he walked, not sentimentally, but with the attention of a man who had learned that the texture of a place is intelligence. Whether a space had been picked over. Whether voices had moved through recently. Whether the quality of the silence was empty-silence or waiting-silence, two things that felt identical right up until they weren't.

In this way, moving through the aftermath of the afternoon the world had become significantly more complicated, Midas came to the wall.

It was the side of what had been a Wetherspoons, and it was heavily graffitied in the usual way of such walls—tags and declarations and the territorial marking of people who needed to mark territory long before anything happened to justify the impulse. But there was one line, recent, the paint still carrying its chemical shine, that had been written differently. Carefully. In block capitals that were actually block capitals, not the rounded approximation of someone unused to them.

**THERE IS STILL A PLACE. THE BLUE HOUSE. IT IS REAL. THE KEY IS THE LOCK.**

Midas read it three times.

"The key is the lock," he said.

He looked at it for another moment. The command of the syntax. The reasonable handwriting. Someone had stood here with purpose.

"It's a code," he said. "Well, it's obviously a code. The key—so, what if the blue house is—" He pulled out the map. Looked at it. The colours were unhelpful. "Or it could be literal. A lock that's also its own key. Self-locking. Or a metaphor. Or—"

"Eucalyptus is my favourite!" said Chewy.

Midas lowered the map and looked at him.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. Southeast. We'll know it when we see it."

He had decided that the graffiti was directing him toward a residential area about a mile and a half further on, on the basis that his map showed three streets forming what was, if you squinted and rotated the map slightly and were prepared to commit, a shape that could be interpreted as a lock. The key would be something—he would determine the key when he found the lock. The blue house would be blue.

*The narrator feels it appropriate to observe, at this juncture, that the human capacity to extract navigational guidance from unrelated information is the same capacity that filled the world with religions, stock markets, and horoscopes. Its track record is mixed. Midas was, in this sense, absolutely representative of his species.*

---

The pharmacy was a Boots, which had once aspired to be a wellbeing destination and now aspired to nothing at all. Midas entered through the door that had been open since last spring, or possibly since a few months after everything happened, and conducted what he thought of as a professional sweep, which was to say he moved through the space quickly and took things that seemed useful and tried not to think too hard about the scale of what was missing.

He found: two rolls of bandage, a tube of antiseptic cream that had been behind something else and was therefore intact, six sachets of a sports electrolyte powder in a flavour called Tropical Edge, which Midas regarded with exactly the suspicion that flavour name deserved, and a first aid manual that he already had in two editions but took anyway because one could always be a spare.

He found, also, a child's plastic step stool that had been used to reach a high shelf and left there, as though someone had planned to come back for it.

He did not take the step stool.

He was halfway back toward the door when the shelving unit against the north wall, which had been structurally uncertain since at least last February and which had been waiting, with geological patience, for a reason, finally found its reason in the combination of Midas walking past it and an imperceptible shift in the building's slowly redistributing load, and came down.

It did not hit Midas. It hit the space Midas had been standing in two seconds earlier, which was better, and it hit Chewy's arm, which was worse. The corner of a metal shelf bracket caught the sling and then caught Chewy and then there was a sound that was brief and unambiguous and then Midas was standing in a drift of moisturisers and paracetamol and silence, holding Chewy, looking at where Chewy's left arm was no longer articulated in the way it had been.

The arm was still there. It was just at a new angle. There was a sound, now, from somewhere in Chewy's chest—a small repetitive clicking that had not been there before, like a clock that had learned the wrong lesson from clocks.

"Okay," Midas said.

He said it in the voice of a man doing the arithmetic of a situation. The quiet accounting of things broken versus things not broken yet versus things that need to stay functional until home.

"Right. Yeah. This is fine. This is—you're fine. We're fine." He tucked Chewy back into the sling. The arm sat wrong. Midas adjusted it as best he could, which was not well. "Adventure in the middle section. Classic structure. We're going to the bit where things turn around now."

Chewy clicked.

"The good bit," Midas said. "We're going to the good bit now."

He had said this before, on previous days, in previous Boots pharmacies and similar venues of bad luck, and he had varied the inflection enough to maintain a working belief in the statement's accuracy.

He did not examine this tendency. Examining it would have required standing still, and standing still in rooms with unstable shelving was something he had learned to stop doing.

---

They found the blue house at twenty past twelve.

It had been blue. It was now a blue that had passed through several stages of weather and catastrophe and had arrived at a shade that a paint company might have called *Recollection of Blue* or *Former Blue* or possibly just *Grey But Remembering*. It was a semi-detached house on what had been an ordinary street, and what made it distinct from the houses on either side of it, both of which were shells, was that it was not a shell. The windows were boarded from inside, not outside. The front door had a new lock, which was the lock of someone who had acquired it after events, not before.

Midas stood in front of it for a moment.

He tried the front door. The front door did not move.

He walked around to the back because he was the kind of man who tried the front door and then walked around to the back. The back gate was padlocked. He looked at the padlock for a long time. He looked at the key that was hanging from a hook on the gatepost beside the padlock.

The key is the lock, he thought. Not metaphor. Instruction.

The key fit. The gate opened. The small back garden, which had been someone's careful project, had become an uncareful project in their absence and then something else—vegetables growing badly but growing, fruit canes unpruned but unpruned from something that had been pruned. An attempt at order becoming, through neglect, a different kind of order.

The back door was unlocked.

Inside: dry. Swept, recently enough to matter. A table with chairs around it, the number of chairs suggesting someone had expected a number of people that had not materialised. Tinned food, not hoarded—shelved, labelled in handwriting that was the same as the graffiti's. A note on the table that said, in the same block capitals: *HELP YOURSELF. THERE IS MORE. COME BACK. OTHERS ARE WELCOME. THE FIRST RULE IS THAT THERE ARE NO RULES. THE SECOND RULE IS CLEAN UP AFTER YOURSELF.*

Midas stood in the kitchen of the blue house and looked at what the graffiti had meant.

It was not nothing. It was genuinely not nothing. Someone had built something here, was building something here, intended something here, future tense, ongoing, alive.

It was also not a refuge. It was not yet. It was the *bones* of a refuge, laid out by someone who was not here today but had been here recently, and would be here again, and had assumed—correctly, as it turned out—that eventually people would follow a painted wall and a hanging key and find their way to a kitchen table with too many chairs.

Midas sat down in one of the chairs.

Chewy clicked steadily from the sling.

"Right," said Midas. He looked at the note. He looked at the food. He looked at the chairs. "Right. Okay. There are—there are *people*. Who did this. Who are continuing to do this." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Somewhere."

"Fun is what friends are for!" said Chewy, on a delay.

Midas looked at him. His remaining eye. His arm at its wrong angle.

"Yeah," said Midas. "Close enough."

He took three tins from the shelf—replaced them with two tins from his own pack, because he could not have said why except that it felt important to leave more than he took—and stood up. He looked around the kitchen one more time, to remember it properly.

On the notepad beside the tins, he wrote, in letters that were not quite block capitals: *CAME. FOUND. WILL RETURN. THERE IS AN IKEA ON THE PURLEY WAY. ALL WELCOME. THE ENTRY IS THE LOADING BAY.*

He looked at what he'd written. He added, below it: *WE HAVE CHICKPEAS.*

---

The route home was longer than the route out, in the way that return journeys are always longer than departure journeys—not in distance but in the quality of distance, the accumulated weight of used-up enthusiasm. The light was different too, or the light was the same and Midas was different inside it, which produced a similar effect.

The swing in the park was still moving.

The coral streak of nail polish was still reaching toward the door.

The Greggs was still inexplicably intact, which had not improved with familiarity.

Midas walked through all of these things with the slower attention of coming back. The architecture of ruin reads differently on the return—less as a series of obstacles and more as a series of facts that had been true all day, that you simply hadn't got to yet.

He talked to Chewy as he walked. This was not unusual. This was, in fact, the primary mode of his interior life, which had long ago decided that an exterior life was a more sustainable format for thought.

"The thing about the chairs," he said. "The extra chairs. That's someone who remembered the number they started with and hasn't updated the count. Or they're keeping them for expectation. Like—expecting to need them. Which is its own thing."
