# Carnival of Dangerous Intentions

She had been standing at the edge of the boardwalk long enough that a child had bumped into her twice without apology, absorbed entirely in a cone of pink spun sugar. The shooting gallery clattered and popped ahead of her — tin ducks skating their mechanical route, a man in suspenders calling out terms that sounded increasingly like conditions. She had not moved toward it. She had not moved away.

*His locks.* There it was again.

She pressed the thought down the way you press a bruise, just to confirm it still hurts. It did. She had not been raised on romantic language, had distrusted it since girlhood, since her mother had used it like currency that always came up short. And yet the phrase had arrived in her mind last Tuesday — when she'd first seen him — fully formed and antique, like something borrowed from another century. She hadn't invited it. She hadn't cultivated it. It had simply installed itself, and the fact of its residence was more alarming than its content.

She suppressed it again and watched the ducks.

The carnival lights were buzzing all the way to life now, yellow and white and a red that pulsed slightly, ambiguously, like a warning that couldn't quite commit. She adjusted her gloves. She was thirty-one years old and possessed of enough good sense, her sister frequently remarked, for two women. She wondered what her sister would make of this. She decided she would not be finding out.

To her left, a few yards along, an old fortune teller's booth stood slightly apart from the noise — a canvas awning printed with palmistry charts, lines of fate and heart and life rendered in fading burgundy ink, as if the hand depicted had spent decades being consulted and had grown philosophical about it. A woman sat within it, neither young nor particularly old, watching the crowd with the mild expression of someone who expected nothing and was therefore surprised by nothing. Their eyes met briefly. The fortune teller looked away first, which felt like a small mercy.

"You came back."

He was simply there. No approach she'd registered, no footsteps on the boards. Just his voice from slightly behind her right shoulder, low and unhurried, as if he'd been standing there for some time and was only now choosing to announce it.

She turned with deliberate composure. "I was in the neighborhood."

He smiled at this — not broadly, not with the vulgarity of a man who's scored a point, but with something quieter, more private, as if she'd confirmed something he'd suspected about her character and found it worthwhile. His eyes were exactly as she remembered them. Darker than they had any right to be in the electric light. Above them, his hair — she did not think the word; she refused the word — caught the carnival red and held it.

"Coney Island," he said. "Nobody is in the neighborhood."

"Then perhaps I was curious."

"About the shooting gallery?"

"About whether you'd be here." She said it evenly, and hated that she could. The composure that usually protected her felt, tonight, like a kind of dishonesty — a locked door that kept out the weather but also kept out the air.

He looked at her for a moment without speaking. The fairground continued its indifferent roar around them. Someone, perhaps fifty feet away, won something that required a bell.

"You could go in there," he said, nodding toward the fortune teller's booth. "Ask her what brings a woman like you to a place like this."

"I know what brings me." She paused. "I'm simply not certain it speaks well of me."

Something shifted in his expression — not softening exactly, more like recognition, the look of a man who has heard many things and knows the difference between a performance and a disclosure. "What's your name?" he asked.

"You first."

"Daniel."

She considered him. Considered the name. It suited him in the way that a correct answer suits its question — not remarkably, but without argument. "Eleanor."

"Eleanor." He said it as if testing the weight of it, or perhaps the weight of the decision to use it. "Are you always this careful?"

"I'm always this sensible," she said. "There's a difference."

He glanced toward the shooting gallery. "Come try your aim."

"I'm not interested in shooting anything."

"Then watch me." He said it without particular inflection, which was somehow worse than if he'd made it an invitation or a flirtation. It was simply a statement of what was going to happen, offered with the same easy confidence as a man observing that it will be dark in an hour. He was not wrong about that either.

*Don't*, she told herself.

She followed him anyway, which she noted, which she did not remark upon, which she filed.

He was a better shot than she expected, which irritated her more than it should have. Three ducks, clean, without showmanship or commentary, with the matter-of-fact efficiency of someone for whom competence was simply the baseline. He handed her the air rifle without instruction, adjusted nothing, stood close enough that she was aware of the particular warmth of him — distinct even in the July heat, distinct in a way that was difficult to categorize and so she categorized it anyway, precisely, and filed that as well.

She shot. Missed.

"Again," he said quietly.

She adjusted. Shot again. The duck spun off its track and clattered down.

"There," he said. Just that.

She handed the rifle back to the man in the suspenders, who looked aggrieved by the whole enterprise. She and Daniel walked back toward the fortune teller's booth without deciding to, the drift of it as natural and unconsidered as the tide going somewhere it was always going to go. She recognized the pattern. She did not address it.

The palmistry chart nearest to her depicted a hand with all its lines named and annotated in that same fading burgundy. *Girdle of Venus*, she read. *Mount of the Moon*. The fortune teller glanced at her once and returned her gaze to the middle distance, serene and entirely uninvested.

"Do you believe in that sort of thing?" she asked.

"Fate?" He studied the chart with apparent seriousness, hands clasped behind him, as if he were in a gallery considering something debatable. "No. I believe in what people do with what's in front of them."

"That's a very convenient philosophy for a man who arranges to meet women he doesn't know."

He turned to look at her directly. Not sharply — there was never anything sharp about the way he looked at her, which was its own kind of problem. "You arranged to meet me."

She opened her mouth and closed it. The fairground noise continued around them. A child shrieked somewhere in uncomplicated joy, so far from where Eleanor was standing it might as well have been another country. She was very aware of standing still. Of having been standing still for some time, in one sense or another.

To her left the fortune teller's booth. The exit, lit in carnival colors, waiting.

She thought: *I could go in there. I could make a joke of it — have my palm read, let the interruption reset the scene, walk away afterward with my ironies intact.* She thought it clearly and completely.

She stayed where she was.

"I don't know what I'm doing here," she said finally. It cost her more than she showed, and she suspected he knew exactly what it cost, which made it cost more.

He didn't fill the silence immediately. This was the most unsettling thing about him, she had decided — not his eyes, not his aim, not even the particular warmth of him in July. It was this. Most men rushed to fill silences, decorated them, defused them, built small noisy structures over the empty space so that no one had to stand in it. He simply let it stand between them like furniture. Like something that belonged there and had a right to take up room.

"Yes, you do," he said at last.

His voice held no triumph. No cruelty, no performance of certainty, none of the things she'd braced for. It held something she was considerably less equipped to manage than any of those. It held patience — the specific patience of a man who was not surprised by her, who had in fact been expecting her, not Eleanor with her gloves and her sister's opinions about her good sense, but *her*, the one who came back, the one who missed the first duck and tried again, the one who knew exactly what she was doing here and had said otherwise because the truth was too much to carry through a sentence without setting it down on the other side and seeing what happened to it in the light.

She looked at the fortune teller, who was gazing elsewhere with complete serenity, as if the scene behind her were none of her assigned business.

*I could walk away*, Eleanor thought. *I am still entirely capable of walking away.*

She did not say this aloud. It seemed, suddenly, like the kind of thing that only a woman trying to convince herself would need to say. It seemed like the word *sensible*, and all the work that word had done, and all the places it had and hadn't gotten her. It seemed like her mother's currency, always coming up short.

The carnival lights buzzed above them. The red one pulsed, ambiguous as ever, not quite committing to its warning.

She did not walk away.
