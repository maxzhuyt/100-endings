# Gunpowder Rendezvous

---

The bar was the kind of place that didn't name itself. A hand-painted sign above the door had once said something, but the paint had blistered off in strips until only a red smear remained, like a mouth that had given up trying. Inside, the light came from fixtures that had yellowed with the smoke of a thousand previous nights, and the floor was the particular sticky that meant the mop had learned to avoid the corners. The waterfront smell came in under the door—salt and diesel and something organic that the tide brought and took away on its own schedule.

Spike Spiegel sat at the bar and ordered whiskey, and the bartender poured it without looking at him, which was the right instinct. He was a man who looked like he'd been assembled from spare parts—too long in the limbs, jacket hanging off shoulders that didn't quite fill it, one eye that caught the light wrong. The whiskey cost him more than he'd estimated, which meant the rest of the week would require some creative arithmetic. He considered the glass for a moment before drinking, a habit left over from something he no longer entirely remembered.

The lighter came out when he reached for a cigarette. It was a simple thing, a zippo, but its bottom edge had a ring of rust that had eaten permanently into the chrome—the kind of oxidation that happened when an object spent time submerged in something it wasn't meant for and survived anyway. He lit the cigarette, returned the lighter to his jacket pocket, and turned slightly on his stool to survey the room with the particular boredom of a man who has spent a long time not being surprised.

The woman at the table in the back had her boots on the table. This was the first thing he clocked—not the boots themselves but the fact that no one had asked her to move them. She was working through a bottle rather than ordering by the glass, which communicated something about her relationship with moderation, and she was reading something pinned to the corkboard beside her table with the focused attention of someone making a calculation. She had guns. The guns were not hidden.

He looked at the corkboard.

He looked back at his whiskey.

He looked at the corkboard again.

There was a bounty sheet pinned there, dog-eared at the bottom left corner, the photograph slightly blurred in the way of photographs that had been taken without the subject's cooperation. He had an identical sheet folded in the interior pocket of his jacket, which he knew because he could feel it there, a rectangle of paper against his ribs.

He finished the whiskey. He ordered another.

---

She noticed him the way she noticed things she might need to shoot—categorically, efficiently, without looking like she was doing it. The man at the bar had a posture that looked like carelessness and wasn't. She'd met enough people who'd trained themselves to take up space that way, like they'd decided a long time ago that being hard to read was a better investment than being fast. His eye was wrong. She filed that away with the same neutral efficiency she'd file a jammed chamber or an exit she'd need later.

He glanced at the bounty sheet.

She took a drink.

He glanced at it again.

She said, "You're going to read it wrong from over there."

He turned on the stool to look at her fully, which she gave him credit for—no studied casual rotation, just the look. "I've got good eyes," he said.

"Good eye," she said, and watched the small thing happen in his face, the quarter-second where something moved and then didn't.

"Mm," he said.

He took his whiskey and came to the table. He didn't ask. She didn't tell him to sit down, but she also didn't tell him not to, and her boots stayed exactly where they were. He took the chair across from her with the ease of a man who had either no social awareness or a very refined version of it, and she couldn't tell yet which, which was annoying.

"You know him?" she said, nodding at the sheet.

"Know of him." He tapped ash onto the floor. "You?"

"Same difference."

"Uh-huh."

She took a drink from the bottle and did not offer it. He did not ask. Around them the bar made its particular noises—the clink of glasses, the low channel of conversation from the booth by the window where three men were systematically losing money at cards, the sound of rain beginning outside.

"You're not from here," she said.

"No."

"Passing through."

"For now."

"For now," she repeated, in a tone that made it a comment without specifying what it was a comment on. "Traveling for business or pleasure?"

"Is there a difference?"

"That's a stupid question."

"Most things are, if you look at them long enough."

She looked at him. He looked at the middle distance in the maddening way he had, like the conversation was slightly less interesting than something happening just outside the frame of it. She'd met men who cultivated that look because they thought it made them seem deep, and men who had it because they'd genuinely stopped being interested in the surface of things, and those were not the same species of man.

"So you do this professionally," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Do what."

"Not answer questions."

"I answered several."

"You made sounds in the direction of questions." She leaned back in her chair. "There's a difference."

"Uh-huh." He looked at her then, directly, and she noticed that his eyes—both of them, the wrong one and the other one—held the specific quality of attention of someone who had learned to look at people the way you look at a door, for hinges and points of failure and whether it swung the way you expected. She had the same quality of attention and recognized it the way you recognize an accent from your hometown in a foreign city. "You always talk this much?" he said.

"You're the one who came over here."

"You invited me."

"I told you that you were misreading something. That's not an invitation."

"What's the difference?"

"That's a stupid question."

The corner of his mouth moved. She wasn't sure she'd have noticed it if she hadn't been watching, and she was slightly annoyed that she'd been watching that closely.

She refilled her glass.

---

The name came out by accident, which was the only way it could have. They'd been circling the bounty without landing on it—she'd said *the contract*, he'd said *the job*, both of them talking around the specific weight of a specific number—and then the rain outside got louder and she said the mark's name because she was thinking about the route to the warehouse district and the name came out with it and he went still.

The stillness was the tell.

She recognized it because she'd done the same thing a breath before, when she'd heard what she'd said and heard the air in the room change.

Neither of them moved for a moment.

"Hm," he said.

"Yeah," she said.

She watched his hand. He watched her hand. Both hands were where they were supposed to be—hers around the glass, his flat on the table—and they stayed there, but there was a quality to the stillness that was about what the hands had decided not to do yet. It was the kind of pause that had a sound if you knew how to hear it.

"That's a problem," he said.

"No," she said, "that's *the* problem."

"Contract specifies sole collection."

"Always does."

A beat. The card players in the booth had gotten louder. The rain made the window run.

"You could walk away," he said.

"So could you."

"I spent my last decent money on two of these whiskies."

"That's a you problem."

"Generally." He looked at her. The look had shed whatever laziness it had been wearing and she found she preferred it this way, direct, calibrated, the look of someone who had adjusted their estimate and was showing her the new number. "I'm not walking away from this contract."

"Then we've got a problem."

"We've established that."

She put the glass down. "So what are you going to do about it?"

He considered this with the infuriating calm of a man who was either genuinely unworried or had decided that worry was someone else's department. "I'm going to finish this cigarette," he said, "and think about it."

She looked at him for a long moment. Her hand had moved and returned to the glass without either of them acknowledging it.

"Fine," she said, and took a drink.

---

It was not the mark who found them. It was three men who worked for the mark and had been watching the bar since before Spike walked in, waiting for exactly the kind of person who goes looking at corkboards with folded paper in their pockets. They were not subtle men. They came in through the front and the side entrance simultaneously, and the first thing that went was the card game in the booth, chairs scraping back, the three men who'd been losing money diving for the floor with the practiced speed of people who'd survived this kind of evening before.

Revy was on her feet before the door finished opening.

She clocked the front entrance—two, one with position on the bartender, one moving toward the table—and was already turning when she heard him move behind her, not away but sideways, and she registered without processing it that he'd taken the angle that covered the side entrance, which left the front to her, which was where she was already pointed.

She didn't think about this. She moved.

The noise in the room became immediate and total and she moved through it the way she always moved through it, which was forward, because going forward was faster and she was better than anyone in a room who was planning to be somewhere else. The first man went down and she was already past him. The second took longer, more cover, and she felt Spike somewhere behind her and to the left—not his footsteps, just the pressure of someone moving in the same direction you're moving, the way you know where someone is in a dark room when you've been in the room long enough.

She had a beat where her angle got complicated and she pulled right to get clear of it, and from her right his arm came into her peripheral as he took the shot she was pulling away from, and she didn't look—there wasn't time to look—but she felt the way the room changed when it connected, the specific adjustment a room makes when a problem in it stops.

There were two more from the side entrance and she came back around and covered him while he moved up, and she kept his left side in her field because she'd noted the eye, the wrong-light eye, and the left was the blind side, obvious once you'd clocked which one it was. She didn't think about this either. It was just geometry.

And then the room was different. The kind of different that settles instead of continuing, the gradual audit of a space after the fact. The rain outside. The bartender slowly emerging from behind the bar. The three card players still on the floor.

She checked her hands. She was fine.

She didn't look at him.

She heard him reload, and she reloaded, and neither of them said anything.

---

She was grazed just below the left shoulder, which she didn't mention and he didn't ask about directly. What he said was, *there's a motel on the block south of the warehouse*, and she said *I know*, and they went separately and arrived at more or less the same time, and she paid because she happened to have cash that was the right denomination and they would settle it later or they wouldn't.

The room was small. The rooms were always small.

He took the bed—simply spread himself across it in that loose-limbed way, still in his jacket, one arm behind his head, cigarette going. She took the chair that faced the door, angled so she could see both the door and the window and the bed, which she would have taken regardless of who was in the room with her, but which also communicated something she was fine with communicating.

Her weapon was on the arm of the chair.

His was under his jacket.

The rain had gotten serious outside, the kind that decided to stay for a while.

He reached into his pocket and took out the rust-ringed lighter and moved it toward her, a slight extension of the arm—not quite an offer, just a direction. She produced her own lighter from somewhere and lit her cigarette with it and set it on the arm of the chair beside the gun. He retracted his arm and returned the lighter to his pocket without comment.

She looked at the door.

He looked at the ceiling.

The silence between them was not comfortable and was not hostile. It had edges and texture. It was the silence of two people in a room that was too small for neutrality, managing the distance with precision, because the distance was the only negotiation they were admitting to.

"Your shoulder," he said.

"It's fine."

"Uh-huh."

"It's grazed."

"I know." He drew on the cigarette. "I wasn't asking."

She looked at him. The ceiling had not taught him anything interesting but he kept looking at it. "Then why'd you bring it up."

"I didn't."

"You said my shoulder."

"As a statement," he said, "not a question."

She took a long drink and looked at the door again. The rain ran the window. Two floors down a door opened and closed, someone coming in wet, their footsteps going away down the corridor.

"The warehouse," she said. "He'll move the operation when he hears about his guys."

"Tonight," Spike said. "He'll move it tonight. Which means our window is about three hours."

"Two," she said.

"Two," he agreed.

---

Sometime in the second hour she said, without preface, "I grew up in a place like this."

He didn't ask what she meant by *this*—the bar, the motel, the city, the particular quality of rain on windows in rooms where you were waiting out a problem. He seemed to take it as a category, which was what she'd meant.

She looked at the door and not at him.

"I know what I am," she said. "I got clear on that a long time ago. I don't lose sleep about it."

The cigarette moved. The silence held.

"I had a woman," he said. "Past tense."

She didn't ask how. She didn't ask when. She didn't look at him.

"Yeah," she said.

He didn't say anything else.

The lighter came out—not toward her this time, just held, turned over once in his fingers, returned. She watched this peripherally and then looked at the window and said, "Two hours is probably already one forty-five."

"Probably."

"We should figure out the split before we go in."

"We should."

"I'm not going in with someone I don't have terms with."

"That's reasonable," he said.

They talked about the split. The split didn't work—it couldn't work, the contract was structured to prevent exactly this kind of accommodation. They both knew it. They talked around the knowledge the way they'd talked around the name earlier, and eventually they stopped talking and the silence came back in, and the silence was specific: not comfortable, not hostile, something with more texture than either, the texture of two people who had said one true thing each and left them where they'd fallen, uncollected, not nothing.

---

She didn't decide to move. Her body made the assessment while her mind was still on the warehouse route—three hours becoming two, two becoming she didn't know how long anymore—and her hand was already in motion when she registered it.

His was already up.

The room was dark. She'd cut the light an hour ago out of habit or insomnia or both, and in the dark the two muzzles found each other with the immediacy of things that had been pointing toward each other for some time, and they stopped there.

Her hand was steady.

His hand was steady.

She could hear his breathing and her own.

Neither of them fired. Neither of them had—the thought, the intention, whatever preceded a trigger pull—they'd reached for weapons the way you reach for something familiar in the dark, reflex preceding reason, and now they were here, on either side of four feet of motel room with weapons level, and the moment had opened into something that was not a threat and was not an accident and was not yet anything else.

The lighter was in his jacket pocket. It held its rust the way it held everything—without complaint, without hiding it, a record of water and survival and the continued motion of a person who had been submerged more than once and had come back anyway. She didn't know this about the lighter specifically. She knew it in the way you know things about people who have the same quality of damage you have, the same particular weariness of continuing, because she'd looked at him the way she looked at doors and she'd found the hinges.

He didn't lower his weapon.

She didn't lower hers.
