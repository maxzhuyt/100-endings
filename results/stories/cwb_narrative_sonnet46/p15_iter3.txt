# Therapeutic Boundaries

The injury has a clinical name I stopped using around week three, because the clinical name implies a clinical outcome, and I have been told, with the particular gentleness reserved for athletes who don't know they're already past tense, that my outcome is not going to be clinical. It is going to be *managed*. The ACL is gone. The meniscus is a memory. The cartilage is doing whatever cartilage does when it decides it has fulfilled its obligations and would like to retire. I am thirty-one years old and I have been managed.

The waiting room smells like rubber matting and eucalyptus oil and the specific humiliation of being somewhere you do not want to be. I fill out the intake form. *On a scale of one to ten, rate your current pain level.* I write seven because seven sounds like someone who is not catastrophizing and also not stoic in a suspicious way. Seven is a credible number. The truth is that I reorganized my relationship with pain sometime in the second month and I am no longer confident I can locate it on a linear scale. The truth is that the pain has become ambient. Background radiation. I have stopped noticing it the way people stop noticing traffic noise outside a window they've lived beside for years, and I am not sure whether this represents progress or a further kind of damage.

The form also asks what I hope to achieve through physiotherapy. I write *return to full function* because I was told to write *return to full function*, but what this means in practice is that I would like to be able to walk down stairs without thinking about it, and I would like to stop doing the mental architecture required to sit in certain chairs, and I would like to stop being the particular kind of man who stands very carefully in a room and knows exactly where all the furniture is. I hand the form to the woman at the desk and she smiles at me with the warmth of someone whose job involves a great deal of managing people exactly like me.

---

She comes through the interior door wearing teal-and-gold compression sleeves that run from wrist to elbow, and I recognize them before I recognize her face.

The sleeves are from the national training camp. Seven years ago. A sponsor had sent boxes of them and we had all worn them ironically at first, with that specific affected contempt that people perform when something is useful and they are not ready to admit it, and then we had just worn them because they worked. The teal is specific—not the generic teal of sports merchandise but a deeper, almost institutional teal, a color chosen by someone in a boardroom who was trying to communicate seriousness, and the gold piping that ran along the seam had a particular weight to it that I can feel in my memory right now in a way that is entirely unreasonable given that I am in a waiting room with a bad knee and a seven on a form.

Her sleeves are faded. The gold has gone the color of old straw. The teal is still mostly holding on. She has worn them, that's the thing. She has worn them enough to fade them, and something about this—the fading, the sustained use, the fact that she had carried a thing forward from that life and kept using it until it wore down to the color of something pastoral and old—lands somewhere under my sternum in a way I am not going to pursue.

She is holding a clipboard and looking at it when she says my name, and when she looks up there is a single second where her expression does something she does not intend, some small seismic event moving through her face, and then she brings the clipboard up against her chest like a modest shield and says, with absolute composure, "Okay. Good. Let's take a look."

Her name is Dara. I knew that. I knew that seven years ago and I know it now from the lanyard around her neck and from whatever it is that the body knows before the mind has finished its deliberations.

---

The treatment table is vinyl-covered foam and it crinkles when I climb onto it with what I hope is a dignified lack of urgency and what is certainly not that. She gives me a pillow for my head without asking and positions my injured leg so the knee is supported at approximately thirty degrees of flexion, heel resting on a foam wedge. Her hands find the joint without hesitation. Thumbs along the joint line, fingers wrapping the distal femur. She begins the passive assessment the way someone begins a sentence they have spoken ten thousand times—phrase length, intonation, no searching for words. Taking the leg through its available range: into flexion until approximately one hundred and ten degrees, where the grinding resistance arrives like a door finding its frame, and then back toward extension, where it terminates six degrees short because it terminates six degrees short and will apparently continue to do so in perpetuity.

"Tell me where your ten is," she says.

"I've been having some trouble with that question."

"Approximate it."

She takes the leg into internal rotation. Something in the lateral compartment objects with great specificity and volume and I say a number and she nods the nod of someone filing information away in a system that does not require you to have feelings about it. She palpates the joint line. She tests the passive stability of the medial and lateral structures with small, precise stresses that are not painful in themselves but that carry the information of damage the way a door tells you through the handle that its hinges are loose. She notes things on the clipboard. She does not look at my face much, or she looks at it the way you check an instrument panel—a brief directed glance, gather data, return attention elsewhere.

Her sleeves are faded to straw and old teal and I notice them when she reaches across me to adjust the foam wedge and they come briefly into my peripheral field and I look away.

"Right," she says, in the tone of someone closing a door. "We have some things to work with."

"That's one way to put it."

She looks at me then. "What's another way?"

"Wreckage," I say. "Managed wreckage."

She writes something on the clipboard. Her face does not react. "I've worked with worse architecture," she says.

I cannot tell if this is clinical reassurance or something else, and I find I am irritated by not being able to tell, which is itself information I am not going to do anything with.

---

There is a stretch she wants. Hip flexor, the iliopsoas specifically—she traces the anatomical route with two fingers against my lower abdomen, not touching, just indicating, the muscle that originates at the lumbar vertebrae and ilium and inserts on the lesser trochanter of the femur, and which is apparently functioning as a ringleader in my particular dysfunction. Tight on the injured side, inhibiting extension, contributing to the compensatory patterns that have now also begun to cause problems in the lumbar spine, the knee being in the habit of borrowing trouble from neighboring jurisdictions.

She lowers the table by a few inches—a brief mechanical interlude that involves a lever and a hydraulic sound and neither of us saying anything—and arranges me in a half-kneeling lunge, injured leg forward, the back knee on the padded surface. She stands behind me and places both hands on the crest of my pelvis, thumbs positioned at the sacrum, and tells me to posteriorly tilt. To tuck under. This is a cue I know. I know this cue the way I know a great number of things that are no longer immediately useful.

I tuck under.

Her thumbs press into the sacrum to give me positional feedback. This is proprioceptive cueing. This is a clinical technique with a name and a rationale and a body of evidence behind it and I know all of this.

She says, "Hold that," and I hold it, and the stretch begins to develop in the anterior hip with the gradual quality of light changing, and I am—

I am thinking about the medical room at the training camp, early in the morning, six a.m. or before. Both of us icing knees from something inadvisable the afternoon before, seated on the treatment tables across from each other with ice packs held on by elastic bandages, the room still dim because whoever was supposed to have turned on the lights had not yet done so and neither of us had moved to fix it. She'd started laughing at something I said—I cannot now retrieve what I said; the joke has gone entirely, leaving only the shape of her laughing—and laughed until she spilled ice water across both our laps, a spectacular and total accident, and we had sat in wet training gear and finished icing our knees and then gone to the morning session damp, and no one had asked and we had not explained.

She had said something to me that morning. Before the ice water or after it. I was trying to—

"You're holding," she says. "Breathe out. Let the hip come down."

I breathe out. The hip drops and the stretch moves into new territory in the anterior hip with a quality that is not quite pain and is not quite not pain. I make a sound I had not authorized. Not large. Not large, but not nothing.

Her grip on my pelvis does not move except that the pressure in her left thumb increases by some fractional amount. This could be guidance. This could be a technique. I am not going to put any weight on this.

"Good," she says, and her voice is exactly level and I am grateful for it. "Hold it there."

I hold it. The stretch continues its work. At ten seconds it becomes productive discomfort; at fourteen seconds it becomes something with a distinct edge, the psoas releasing in a way that is not comfortable but is the kind of uncomfortable that is the point. My quadriceps trembles, the involuntary vibration of a loaded muscle reporting on its own condition. My right hand closes on the edge of the table.

"Twelve," she says. "Fourteen."

My right hand is on the table and her hands are on my hips and we are very far from a dim treatment room in 2017 and so entirely not far from it that the two moments are in some way the same moment.

"Good," she says. "And release."

She guides the pelvis back to neutral and steps around to the front of the table and occupies herself for a moment with resetting the foam wedge. The adjustment does not require much attention. She gives it more than it requires. Then she looks at me and says, "You need to ice tonight. Twenty minutes. Bag of frozen peas is fine."

"Right."

"And I want you to find the end range of extension without forcing it. Just where it stops. Get familiar with where it stops."

"Get familiar with the stopping."

"Yes."

A pause occurs. It is not a long pause. It lasts long enough to contain something.

"You were good at this," she says. "Listening to the body during load."

I say, "I was good at not listening to it. There's a difference."

Something moves through her face that is not professional, that is not the expression of a clinician receiving patient history. It is smaller than that and older than that. "Yeah," she says. "I know." And then, more quietly, one beat after, looking not quite at me: "So was I."

She doesn't say anything else immediately and neither do I and the room holds what she's said with a care that I did not expect the room to be capable of.

Then she uncaps a pen and begins writing on a printed sheet.

---

She hands me the sheet. Exercises typed, sets and reps filled in by hand in a small controlled script: clamshells, three sets of fifteen; prone hip extensions, three sets of twelve; terminal knee extensions with a resistance band—she specifies the resistance band color, an orange one, available at any pharmacy. A note at the bottom: *ice post-exercise, avoid loaded flexion past 90°, return in one week.*

The appointment card is white with the clinic logo. It says *Tuesday* and a time that is the same as the time I arrived today.

She is explaining the terminal knee extensions, demonstrating the movement with her own leg briefly, a clinician's functional demonstration, and I watch her demonstrate it and I look at her compression sleeves, at the place where the gold piping has faded to the color of dried grass, where the teal has softened into something approximating the sky before weather arrives, and I think about the fact that she has worn them until they have reached this color, worn them through whatever the years between then and now contained for her, all the ways her body has also been used and asked and required, and I think about the medical room and the ice water and the thing she said that I cannot retrieve and I think that it must have been something, it must have been a thing worth saying, because I can still feel the shape of having heard it even with the words entirely gone.

I put the appointment card in my jacket pocket.

"Any questions?" she says.

"No," I say. "I don't think so."

She nods, and clicks her pen, and holds the door.
