# Offworlder

---

The reception hall had been carved from the living rock of the plateau, and in this the colony's architects had done what the briefing documents described as *a gesture of cultural continuity*. The envoy, whose name was Solis and whose rank was Third Attaché to the Diplomatic Office of the Terran Continuance, stood near the east wall with a glass of something fermented and pale and watched the colony's delegation arrange itself for photographs across the room.

The briefing documents had used the phrase *cultural continuity* eight times in forty pages. Solis had counted.

Above the delegation, above the photographic fuss of it, the wall carried glyphs in low relief: figures rendered in a style that owed something to Minoan fresco and something to nothing Solis had seen before. A man chained to stone. An eagle, or a bird that had been imagined as eagle-like, its wingspan cracking the frame of the panel. Below this, barely at eye level: a circle consuming itself, the tail inside the mouth, forever.

Prometheus, the briefing said. *The colony maintains strong affiliations with pre-Diaspora mythological tradition, particularly Greek corpus, suggesting continuity of cultural identity and therefore alignment with Terran values and governance frameworks.*

Solis looked at the chained man. His face was turned away from the eagle. He was looking at something outside the panel's edge, something the carver had put beyond the stone's reach.

That was not in the briefing.

The colony's Minister of Development was moving toward Solis through the crowd, and Solis turned from the wall and shifted into the face they had been trained to wear for these: open, attentive, slightly warm.

"Third Attaché," said the Minister of Development. His name was Carrasco and his file described him as *amenable, pragmatic, culturally fluent*. He was taller than Solis had expected. His handshake was calibrated. "We're honored by the Continuance's attention. These are uncertain years for all of us."

—*Uncertain* was their word, Solis noted. Not *transitional*. Not *developmental*. Uncertain.—

"The Continuance shares that feeling," Solis said. "That's why we're here. To understand."

Carrasco smiled like a man who had been smiled at professionally for most of his adult life and had learned to smile back with precisely the same currency. "Of course," he said. "Of course."

Across the room, someone laughed at something. The sound hit the carved walls and came back changed—fuller, with a resonance that seemed to Solis to originate not in the laugh but in the stone itself.

---

The cultural demonstration was held in a long low building near the hall, and it consisted of three musicians, two looms, a table of preserved foods, and a practiced narration about *the synthesis of old-world craft traditions with colonial necessity*. The narrator had clearly given this narration many times. She had found the places where the words gathered momentum and she rode them there, reliable as water.

Solis stayed for eleven minutes. Then they said quietly to no one in particular that they needed to find a washroom, and went through a side door that opened, as they had noticed it would, onto a service passage that ran the length of the building and came out finally into the open air.

The afternoon sky above the plateau was a color that the briefings had called *atmospheric distortion due to particulate content—not optimal but habitable*. It was the deep amber-green of old glass.

Solis walked toward the sound of the market.

---

The stalls began at the base of the government quarter's steps and ran through the narrow carved-stone corridors of the district like a river taking the shape of its bed. Solis moved with the crowd, which was not a crowd in the way that Terran crowds were crowds—it did not move with the anxious purposiveness of people trying to occupy the same space efficiently. It moved the way things moved in water: with eddy and pause, with sudden stillness around certain stalls and easy flow past others, with the ambient pressure of bodies that seemed comfortable in proximity in a manner the briefing documents had noted clinically as *reduced personal-spatial norms, likely environmental adaptation*.

A smell of something frying in fat. The sharp green smell underneath it, mineral and strange. The sound of a transaction happening in a language that was Standard-derived but pitched and inflected in a way that made it feel like Standard heard through the wall of a room you were not in.

Solis stopped at a stall selling cloth.

The cloth was extraordinary. It was deep red with threads of something iridescent running through it in patterns that Solis could not quite resolve into either geometry or figure—they seemed to shift when the eye moved away and rearrange when the eye returned. Solis reached out to touch it before they had decided to reach out.

The woman behind the stall watched this.

She was, Solis estimated, somewhere between forty and sixty, which was not necessarily the same range here, the briefing had noted, as it was elsewhere, due to *metabolic variations associated with the colony's atmospheric and dietary adaptations*. Her eyes were very pale—not gray, not quite blue, but the color of ice when the sun hits it sideways and reveals that it is not white but a particular shade of refracted nothing. They were adapted for something. The briefing had said *low-light environments* and then moved on.

"You can look longer," the woman said. Her Standard was unaccented in any way Solis recognized.

"I'm sorry. I didn't ask."

"You paid," she said. "With attention. It's how we do it here."

Solis looked at the cloth. "How long does it take to make?"

The woman tilted her head very slightly, which Solis read as either thought or amusement or both. "Depends on what you're asking. The thread is our grandmother's work. The pattern—" She paused, as if considering the precision of language. "The pattern comes when it comes."

She folded the cloth with movements that were efficient and almost ritualistic—a specific order of folds, each deliberate. Her hands were not wrong. They were not deformed, or strange, or anything the briefing's clinical vocabulary would have flagged. But they moved differently. The grip was differently organized. There was a second motion at the base of the fingers when she pressed a fold flat, a small additional articulation that served some purpose Solis could not name.

Solis paid for a small length of cloth they did not need.

At the next stall, a man was selling something that looked like a tuber from the wrong angle. He was arguing cheerfully with his neighbor about a price. His neighbor was a head shorter than him, with the broad chest the briefing described as *high-altitude thoracic adaptation*, and when he laughed—he laughed easily, this man, it was his conversational punctuation—the sound came from lower in him than it should have. Not from the chest. From somewhere behind the chest.

It resonated in Solis's sternum. A pleasant dissonance, like a second bell struck while the first one is still moving.

The two men noticed Solis noticing.

"Offworlder," the taller man said, not unkindly.

"Is it that obvious?" Solis said.

"Your shoes," he said. "Nobody wears shoes like that on the plateau. You'd break an ankle first week."

Solis looked down at their shoes. The briefing had said nothing about shoes.

The shorter man—the one with the resonant laugh—was watching Solis with an expression that was not unfriendly but was not simple. He said something in the local inflection to his neighbor, too quickly for Solis to parse.

The taller man answered. They went on past Solis.

Solis stood there a moment, absorbing the market's ongoing weather: a child running between stalls with something wrapped in cloth held against their chest like treasure, or like an animal; an old woman seated on a low stone who seemed to be doing nothing but watching and was perhaps doing exactly that; the smell changing as the wind shifted, bringing something from the far end of the corridor that was sweet and slightly fermented and not unpleasant.

Two stalls down, a woman was being told something by a market inspector—a man in the gray-green of the colony's civil administration. The inspector held a small device and spoke in Standard, precise and unhurried. The woman vendor listened with her arms folded and her eyes on the middle distance, which Solis recognized as the look of someone who had heard this particular thing before and had arranged their face to survive it.

When the inspector was finished, the woman said: "We declared it."

"The classification has been updated," the inspector said.

"We declared it under the old classification."

The inspector consulted his device. "The reclassification was distributed seven weeks ago."

The woman's arms tightened against her chest. She said nothing.

"There's a petition process," the inspector said, less precisely, with a different quality in the word—an edge that might have been embarrassment or might have been something else. "The form is available."

He moved on.

The woman unfolded her arms and began rearranging her stall. Her rearrangement was unnecessary—the stall was organized—but her hands needed to be doing something and this was what they did.

Solis filed this away without writing it down.

---

Command reached Solis that evening on the secure channel.

The duty officer's voice was a woman's voice, and it was very measured. "Third Attaché. Your location at fourteen hundred hours today was not on the approved schedule."

"I needed air," Solis said.

A pause so brief it was almost not a pause. "Your communications device was inactive for one hour and forty-seven minutes."

"I didn't have anything to communicate."

"That's not the protocol, Third Attaché."

Solis sat in the chair in their quarters and looked at the wall, which was, like all the walls here, carved stone. Someone had smoothed it for this purpose but had not erased all of the tool marks. They ran in long arcs, the ghost gestures of someone's labor.

"No," Solis said. "It isn't."

"The senior attaché has noted this formally. You should be aware that it will be logged."

"I understand."

Another pause. This one longer. When the duty officer spoke again, something in the measured quality had adjusted slightly—not warmly, but with the texture of information being delivered rather than consequences. "The Minister of Science has requested a meeting. Tomorrow morning. The senior attaché has approved the assignment, given your—" the briefest inflection, so slight it might not have been deliberate "—your initiative in establishing independent contact with the district."

"I didn't establish contact with anyone."

"The meeting is at nine hundred. Her offices are in the east wing of the administration building. Display has the route."

The line closed.

Solis sat looking at the wall for a while longer.

The route. The approved route, presumably. Through the parts of the building that had been made ready for them to walk through.

The question that Solis turned over in the quiet—that they did not say aloud, did not write, did not send to anyone—was whether the walk through the market had been permitted to happen. Whether the fact of their shoes, so obviously wrong on the plateau, was information that had always been available. Whether the market's weather had been weather at all, or weather that someone had arranged.

The cloth was on the bed. Solis had taken it out of their jacket pocket and spread it flat on the synthetic coverlet, and its iridescent threads moved in a way that seemed to require no light source. Or drew something from the stone. Solis was not sure.

They left it there and went to sleep.

---

In the morning the corridor to the Minister's offices ran for a long time through the administration building's deep interior, where the stone was older and the ceilings were lower and the carved panels were continuous rather than spaced. Solis walked along them and did not stop.

Then stopped.

The panel was in a section where the corridor turned slightly, forced into a modest angle by some geological feature of the plateau's bedrock—some vein or fault that the builders had chosen to accommodate rather than breach. The turn meant that this section had never been on the direct sightline from either approach. You only saw it if you were already in it.

A figure—a man, or a figure given a man's proportions—stood ankle-deep in water. The water was carved in the old way, with overlapping arcs to suggest depth. Below the surface of the water, the figure's feet were different from its hands, different from its standing posture—the lower half had gone elsewhere, into the water's history perhaps, or the water's future. The upper half remained: arms wide, face turned upward. Not to the sky. The carver had not put a sky in the panel. Just the gesture of turning toward something above that the stone could not contain.

Antaeus, Solis thought. Who drew strength from the earth. Who could not be beaten by anything except removal from the ground.

But the water was wrong for Antaeus. The water changed it.

Proteus, then. The shape-changer. Held down until he stopped shifting and told the truth. But Proteus was captive and this figure was not—or did not seem to be. The arms were not bound. The uprising face was not in distress.

Or the distress was something else. The relief of form. The turning toward.

Solis did not know which myth was in the stone. Did not know whom the adapted hands had meant to put there, what meaning the outstretched arms carried in the context of this plateau, this atmosphere, this particular genetic divergence across however many generations under this amber-green sky.

The corridor continued. The stone walls had been here longer than the mission. That was simply true.

Solis walked on.

---

The Minister of Science was a small woman in her late fifties who wore her gray hair close-cropped and her authority like something she had long since stopped needing to perform. Her office had bookshelves. Physical books, bound, in a quantity that represented a decision—a decision about what to carry and what to leave behind and what to reproduce when you arrived. She was standing at the window when Solis was shown in, looking out at the plateau, and she turned without hurry.

"Third Attaché Solis," she said. "Minister Okafor. Sit, please."

Her Standard was perfectly standard. It had no inflection that told Solis anything about where she had been born or schooled or shaped. This was, Solis thought, a choice.

They sat.

"You had a walk yesterday," the Minister said. She sat across from Solis with a cup of something hot and did not offer Solis one. It was not rudeness. It was information. "How was it?"

"Educational," Solis said.

"That's a diplomatic answer."

"I'm a diplomat."

"Mm." She drank from her cup. The sound of it in the stone room was unremarkable. "What did you learn?"

Solis folded their hands on the table surface and thought about the grain of the answer. "That the market district functions as a market district. That the residents seem—comfortable. That the cloth here is beautiful."

"It is," the Minister said, without warmth or its absence, simply as a fact. "Do you know how the thread is made?"

"No."

"It's produced by an organism we cultivated from the native moss. We introduced a particular manipulation in the third generation. The pigment isn't dye—it's a structural property of the filament itself. The light goes into the fiber and comes out at a different angle." She paused. "The Continuance's trade office classifies it as a biological material of uncertain origin and has not assigned it an export category."

Solis waited.

"We applied for classification six years ago," the Minister said. "The form is quite long. We are told it is under review."

The woman in the market, rearranging her stall. *The classification has been updated.* The arms folded tight.

Solis said: "The reclassification process has always been—"

"Slow," the Minister agreed.

"—thorough."

"Yes." She set down her cup. "Do you know why Minister Carrasco uses the word *uncertain* when he talks about these years? Instead of *transitional*, which your briefing documents favor?"

Solis felt the quality of the room change. Not dramatically. Quietly.

"No," Solis said.

"Because *transitional* implies a known destination. A future that has already been designed." She looked at Solis in a way that was direct without being aggressive—the look of someone who was deciding what to offer and had not finished deciding. "Uncertain is honest. The Continuance uses *transitional* because you have, in fact, designed a future for us. The question you are actually here to answer—and that Command has tasked you with determining—is whether we have noticed."

The silence in the room was the silence of stone rooms, which did not amplify or diminish but simply held.

"Have you?" Solis said.
