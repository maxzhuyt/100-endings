# Blood Moon

---

The moon had gone the color of an old wound.

It hung above the treeline like something that had given up pretending — not the silver courtesy of ordinary nights but a deep arterial red, as though the sky had been cut and left to bleed out slowly over the ruins of what had once been a mill. The structure stood at the edge of three roads that came together without resolution, each one arriving from a different darkness, none of them offering anything so useful as a destination. Stone walls remained where the roof had surrendered. An arch. A threshold leading to open sky. The millstone still present, propped at an angle against the east wall, patient as all obsolete things are patient.

The wolves had started an hour ago and had not stopped. But they were not howling in the way wolves howl when they are communicating — when they are laying territorial claim or calling something home. They were howling in the way animals howl when they have encountered the edge of their understanding of the world, and found it insufficient. There was a pause of forty seconds, sometimes fifty, sometimes thirty, the interval wrong in a way that could not be precisely named. Then a fresh voice from a different compass point. Then silence that felt occupied.

The grass at the crossroads did not move. There was no wind. The grass moved anyway.

---

She was working in the arch.

The ritual required the moonlight — required *this* moonlight, which was not a coincidence she had arranged but a conjunction she had located by spending three weeks with texts that smelled of rendered fat and other people's desperation. Yennefer of Vengerberg stood with her arms slightly raised, not in supplication but in concentration, her fingers spread as though she were holding something invisible gently enough not to shatter it and firmly enough not to drop it. Her hair was black. The moon made the black of it into something else — something with depth, something that seemed to continue past the surface into a darkness that had never been hair at all.

The ritual was for someone else. It was always for someone else now. There was a child who was dying from something physicians could not name and sorcerers could only partially name, and a father who had mortgaged everything including his dignity to stand in a receiving room and explain the situation in a voice that kept losing footing. She had taken the case. She was not in the habit of explaining why.

She was seven minutes from the critical threshold of the working when she felt it.

Not heard it. Not saw it. *Felt* it — a change in the quality of the air at the edge of the cleared space, the way a room changes quality when someone enters who has never had to make noise to announce themselves. The sensation was not fear. She had felt fear — she knew its particular shape, the way it began in the stomach instead of the spine — and this was not that shape. This was calculation, rapid and precise: she ran a fast inventory of what she knew and did not know, what the ritual required against what an interruption might cost, whether whatever was at the edge of the ruins was the kind of thing that respected work in progress.

She decided, without turning, that it was not the kind of thing that respected anything in particular.

She finished the sequence she was in. She let the working breathe — suspended, not collapsed, held in a configuration that would last perhaps four minutes. Then she lowered her hands and turned, and her violet eyes found the darkness beyond the arch, and she said, in a tone that had no warmth in it but considerable precision: "I've been aware of you for nearly a minute. If you were going to kill me, you'd have done it while my back was turned. So I'm curious what you want, and I'm becoming less curious the longer you wait."

The scent of lilac moved through the still air, finding no wind to carry it, carrying itself.

---

He stepped out of a shadow that was not, geometrically, large enough to contain him.

This was not a metaphor. The shadow was cast by the angled millstone and fell perhaps four feet across the ground — a narrow triangle, a shadow appropriate to an August midnight. He stepped out of it. He was not small. There was no reasonable physical account of how he had been in that shadow and there was no reasonable account of much else about him either: the red of his coat in the red of the moonlight achieving something too saturated to be a color, more like a statement. The wide-brimmed hat. The orange-tinted glasses, which caught the blood moon and held it in two small burning ovals, so that he appeared to have fire where his eyes should be and nothing else.

He had been there for considerably longer than a minute.

"You finished your sequence," he said. His voice was layered in a way that voices were not layered — not an accent, not a quality of timbre, but something structural, as though more than one thing were using the same voice and had reached an arrangement. "That took discipline. To hold the working and conduct a conversation simultaneously. Most practitioners would have dropped one or the other."

"Most practitioners haven't had four hundred years of practice," Yennefer said. She said it as a correction, not as a claim.

The orange glasses did not move. "I've been watching you work," he said. "The ritual structure is yours — the base is Alzur, but you've inverted the second movement and extended the third in a way that substantially increases the precision at the cost of the safety margins. That's an interesting choice to make alone, at a crossroads, under a blood moon, in a place where three roads argue with each other about where they lead."

"I know where they lead," she said. "One of them leads back. I'll take that one when I'm finished." She looked at him — full assessment, the kind that could be mistaken for coldness by people who did not understand how cold she could actually be when the assessment came back wrong. "The coat is for show," she said. "The hat is for show. The glasses make no sense given the light conditions. Everything about you is a frame but I can't yet find what it's framing."

Something at the corner of his mouth moved. Not quite a smile. The proposal of one.

"You'll find it," he said, and there was a quality in this that had nothing to do with confidence — was not confidence at all — was something that confidence would look like from a very great distance. "Eventually, everyone does. The question that interests me is what you'll do once you have."

A wolf called from the eastern road. Forty seconds of silence. Another voice from the north, ragged and then sustained.

She did not look toward the sound. Neither did he.

---

"You haven't told me what you want," she said. She had shifted her weight — barely, imperceptibly to anything that wasn't paying close attention — so that her right hand was in a position to begin three separate workings and her left was closer to her hip than it had been. "At a haunted crossroads, at this hour, with that moon, the population of things that might appear to a working sorceress is not infinite, and most of them have an agenda they're either terrified to name or delighted to describe in excessive detail. You've avoided both strategies. I'm trying to decide whether to find that interesting or concerning."

"Can it not be both?"

"It can't be both if I'm about to have to kill you. I'd rather not waste the energy on something I find interesting." She paused. "Do you have a name?"

"Several," he said. He moved then — not toward her, not away, but laterally, as though he were examining the ruins from a new angle, as though the stone walls had become fascinating for reasons unrelated to her. The red coat moved with him in the windless air with a sound like something large breathing in and out. "The one I was born with belongs to a different century. The one I earned is more accurate but tedious to use in conversation. The one most people call me is Alucard, which is a small joke I've maintained far longer than the joke deserves."

"I know what it spells backward," she said.

"Everyone does," he said. "No one ever thinks to ask why I chose something so transparent. It is, in its way, the most honest thing about me." He had stopped moving. He was looking at the blood moon, his head tilted at an angle that suggested he was listening to it rather than watching it. "Yennefer of Vengerberg. Formerly of Aretuza. Presently entangled with Geralt of Rivia in a way that neither of you has figured out how to name properly. I know who you are."

"You've done research," she said. The word *research* landed with a slight edge on its last syllable. "That suggests either respect or a preoccupation. I'm curious which."

"At my age," Alucard said, "they become difficult to distinguish."

---

She crossed the threshold of the arch and came into the cleared space with him — not because she was comfortable, but because remaining framed in the arch while he moved freely felt like a concession she wasn't prepared to make. She stopped ten feet from him. Ten feet was close enough to demonstrate that she was not keeping distance out of caution while being far enough that it wasn't an invitation.

"You said *at my age*," she said. "How old are you?"

"Old enough that this moon is not the first I've seen of this color," he said. "Old enough that the village a half-mile east has a church built on the foundation of a temple built on the foundation of something that was there before temples were a concept humans had organized around. Old enough that I have watched fourteen civilizations decide they were the final, correct version of civilization, and watched eleven of them finish the process of being wrong about that." He turned his head from the moon to her, and even through the orange glass she had the sense of something being turned on her that was not eyes in any ordinary sense — something that examined rather than merely saw. "And yet," he said, "you are doing something with that inverted second movement that I have not seen before. That is not common, at my age. New things."

The way he said *new things* — it was quiet, and it was not a compliment in any ordinary register. It was something closer to hunger than admiration, and it was too specifically aimed to be comfortable.

"I don't perform for audiences," she said.

"You're performing right now," he said, pleasantly. "The posture. The hand position. The cold affect. You've decided that if you are colder than the situation, you control it. It's a very effective technique." He paused. "Against most things."

"You're suggesting you're not most things."

"I'm suggesting," he said, "that I recognize a technique because I invented a version of it roughly four centuries before you were born. Two performers in the same space cannot perform for each other indefinitely. Eventually something involuntary happens." He tilted his head again, and there was now something almost gentle in it, which was worse than the hunger had been. "I'm not threatening you. I'm informing you. There's a difference even if it doesn't feel like one."

Yennefer looked at him for a moment that had some weight to it.

"I've met monsters," she said. "I've made agreements with several. I've killed a few. I've made the mistake of underestimating none of them twice — once each, and I've recovered from each once with decreasing patience for the experience." She let that sit. "What I haven't met, in considerable time, is something that looks at me the way you're looking at me."

"How am I looking at you?"

"Like I'm a problem you want to have," she said. "Not solve. *Have.*"

The thing at the corner of his mouth moved again. This time it completed the journey.

---

She released the working.

She hadn't planned to — or rather, she had not decided to, which was a different thing. She released it and brought the ritual's suspended energy down through her hands in a new configuration and did not build toward anything so much as she demonstrated, without preamble or warning, what it looked like when four hundred years of practice and a fundamental attunement to the magical architecture of a blood moon were operating without restraint. The arch blazed — not with light, exactly, but with a quality of darkness becoming visible, the negative image of illumination, the shadows thrown into relief as the light itself receded and became structural. The arch became a door. Not a metaphor. The space within it opened to somewhere the crossroads was not, somewhere the moonlight had an angle impossible from this latitude, a place that smelled of lightning and old stone and the specific silence that exists above cloud lines.

She held the door for six seconds. She let it close.

She turned back to him.

He had gone very still.

Not the stillness of shock — she had expected shock and watched it fail to arrive, which recalibrated her faster than anything in the previous twenty minutes of conversation had. This was the stillness of something that had learned, over a long time, to be calm in the presence of things that astonished it, because the alternative was to be undone by astonishment repeatedly across the centuries and that was not a sustainable way to persist. He was still the way ancient water is still. Moving underneath where the surface couldn't show it.

"The secondary application of the blood moon's resonance to fold the spatial boundary," he said, and his voice was slightly different — not raw, but stripped of something, approaching sincerity the way a tide approaches a shore. "That's not theoretical. You've done that before."

"I've done it in worse conditions," she said. "With worse injuries. While maintaining a conversation that required more of me than this one has."

There was a pause.

Then he moved, and what he did was not a display so much as a concession — a thing given in exchange for a thing received, the logic of it immediate and clear even as the act itself had no logic that the physical world found coherent. He removed the hat. He removed the coat. He was not beneath them what the coat might have suggested — was not diminished by the removal, was not smaller, was in fact harder to look at, because without the frame his nature was not clothed in anything human enough to give her something to rest her eyes on. What he was instead was too much and very old and not in possession of anything that a body does to betray its anxiety. He opened his hands.

The darkness answered.

It gathered the way it should not gather — from corners that didn't exist, from the space between her breath and the next, from the shadows the blazing arch had thrown and then abandoned — and it gathered into him and through him and he was for a moment in the burning red light of the blood moon the complete absence of light that the moon was reflecting, the source the moon had borrowed from, the darkness that everything that was not darkness was defined against.

A wolf howled from all four directions simultaneously, which was not possible, and then went silent all at once, which was also not possible.

He put the darkness back.

He was simply standing in the ruins again, in the red light, in his shirtsleeves. He looked at her.

She had not moved. She had not reached for any of the three workings her right hand was positioned to begin. She had done something she could not immediately account for, which was to file away every survival instinct that had fired in the past fifteen seconds on the grounds that they were interrupting something more important.

"That," she said, and paused, because her vocabulary was arriving slightly behind her thoughts. "That's the actual version. Not the theater."

"Yes," he said.

"How long ago did you stop needing the theater?"

He looked at her for a moment. "I don't remember," he said. "Some time before I started using it again." Something crossed his face — not quite visible, not quite suppressible. "It's useful to be underestimated. It turns out to be useful even by people who are unlikely to underestimate you. The habit is very old."

She almost said something. She stopped herself and watched him instead, and what she was watching was the thing that the hat and coat and the performed volume of him were designed to make un-watchable: the thing underneath that had been alone for an extremely long time and had organized its entire existence around making that fact decorative rather than devastating.

She knew that organization. She had done it differently and with different materials and the result had a different texture, but she knew the engineering.

She said nothing. But she lowered her right hand all the way.

---

She said it by mistake.

That was the only way to account for it afterward — that she had been calculating something about the working she still needed to finish, the child who was dying, the inverted second movement and whether the door she had opened had spent too much of what she needed — and in the process of calculating she had moved through a thought she moved through too quickly to intercept. She said: "The working I'm here to finish won't restore the fertility."

She heard it leave her mouth.

She stopped.

She had not meant to say it. She had meant to say something about the working. She had meant to say it to herself, as a technical note. What she had in fact said was the technical note and its weight simultaneously, in the same sentence, without preparation, in front of something that was older than any secret she had ever kept and was looking at her with those orange glasses that caught the moon and held it in two burning points.

The air changed temperature.

The calculation crossed his face with a speed that she tracked because she was watching for everything now: he understood what the working was *for*, and what *the fertility* meant, and what it cost to say it in that way, in that context
