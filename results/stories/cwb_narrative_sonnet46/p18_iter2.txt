# Sisyphus Gives Advice

The scratch of quill on papyrus is, in Crow's considered opinion, the worst sound in the Underworld, and Crow has heard the sound a soul makes when it remembers everything.

Nevertheless.

"—and if she has not responded to three messages," Sisyphus said, not looking up from the letter in his hand, "then the fourth will not be the one that changes her mind. I know this seems obvious from outside the situation. From inside the situation, nothing is obvious. What you are actually asking, I think, is whether you are allowed to grieve a thing that did not fully begin—and you are. Give yourself a week. Not to feel better. Just to feel it without trying to fix it. Then write to your friend Marcus, the one you keep meaning to write to. He will be glad to hear from you."

Crow finished the line, regarded his ink-stained talon with the expression of someone engaged in detailed arithmetic about their life choices, and indicated, by a small motion of his head, the next letter in the stack.

The stack was considerable.

The boulder sat at a slight angle near the base of the slope, doing nothing in particular, as boulders do when they are between appointments.

---

The Underworld light at this hour—if hours meant anything, which they did if you decided they did, and Sisyphus had decided, some centuries prior, that they did—was the color of old bronze, warm without being warm, like the memory of an afternoon rather than an actual one. It came from nowhere visible and distributed itself without enthusiasm across the operation: the small folding table, the two camp stools, the stack of correspondence that arrived each morning through means Crow had investigated once and then decided not to investigate again.

People had always written letters to the dead. Most of the letters were grief, or accusation, or both. But a certain number—and the number had been growing steadily since the column began, though Sisyphus was not sure how it had begun, only that one day there had been one letter and he had answered it, and then there had been more—a certain number were questions.

The living, it turned out, had an enormous number of questions.

Sisyphus had, it turned out, an enormous amount of time.

---

"This one," Crow said, "is from someone who cannot stop arguing with her mother about a kitchen renovation."

"What kind of renovation?"

"She would like to remove a wall. Her mother believes the wall is structural. The contractor says it is not structural. Her mother believes the contractor is wrong."

Sisyphus set down the letter he was holding and accepted the new one. He read it twice, in his characteristic way, lips moving slightly on the second pass as though testing the weight of each word.

"How old is the house?"

Crow, who had read the letter in full, said, "Forty years."

"And the mother—does she live in the house?"

"No."

Sisyphus nodded to himself, a slow nod that suggested he was organizing things.

"The wall," he said, "is not the argument. The argument is that her mother is afraid the house will stop being something her mother recognizes. The daughter has understood the wall as an obstacle; the mother has understood it as a record." He paused. "Does she say anything about her mother's memory? General health?"

Crow checked the letter. "No."

"Then she should ask. Gently. Not as a challenge—as someone paying attention." He held out his hand for the writing materials. "I will draft it. She should thank the contractor for his patience, delay for a single month, and in that month take her mother to dinner somewhere they used to go together. Not to soften her mother's position. To remember that there is a person inside the position." He began to write. "The wall can wait. Walls are very patient."

Crow said nothing, but the scratch of his quill seemed, briefly, less irritated.

---

"Up," Sisyphus said, in the middle of dictating a response about workplace recognition, "I'll finish this thought in a moment—*the fact that no one has noticed your effort is genuinely painful, and you are not wrong to feel passed over*—Crow, hold that line."

He stood, stretched his arms once in each direction with the thoroughness of a man who has developed good habits about his back, and walked to the boulder.

It went up. The slope received it, and he pushed, and the particular sound of stone on stone mixed briefly with the bronze-colored light and Crow's waiting and the stack of letters and the distant sound of something that might have been water.

When Sisyphus came back, his tunic had acquired a faint line of dust across one shoulder. He settled onto his stool, accepted the papyrus from Crow, and read back the last line to himself.

"—*and you are not wrong to feel passed over,*" he said. "Yes. Continue: the question is not whether your effort has value, because it does. The question is whether you want to spend the next five years being right, or the next five years being recognized. These are different projects, and only one of them depends on Henricus paying attention. I would suggest—"

Crow wrote.

---

The fourth letter was, technically, a letter in the sense that it had once been composed by a human hand and shaped into something like communication. What it was now, following what appeared to be a journey of considerable geographic ambition, was a small rectangle of passionate ambiguity.

Crow placed it on the table between them with the air of a man presenting evidence at a trial he had not agreed to attend.

They both looked at it.

"Water damage," Sisyphus said.

"Extensively."

Sisyphus picked it up and tilted it toward the light, which was not helpful. He tilted it away from the light, which was also not helpful but felt more deliberate. "There's a salutation. *Dear—*"

"Agreed."

"And then—" He moved his finger along a margin of staining. "Something has gone very wrong, or someone has, or there is a feeling about a thing."

"That covers a range."

"It covers almost the entire range, yes." He set it down and looked at it with the focused patience of a man who has played a great deal of chess against himself. "The signature."

Crow, who had already attempted the signature, offered: "It begins with a *W*, or possibly an *M*, or the letters are simply in difficulty."

"What are the chances," Sisyphus said, not rhetorically but as a genuine probabilistic inquiry, "that this concerns work?"

"High."

"And the chance it concerns a relationship?"

"Also high."

"And the chance," Sisyphus continued, "that the work problem and the relationship problem are the same problem wearing different hats?"

Crow considered this with the seriousness it deserved. "Elevated."

Sisyphus picked up the letter again. He could make out, near what he guessed was the third line, two words that were either *cannot stop* or *mountain top*, and one word near the end that was clearly either *exhausting* or *everything*, and a phrase in the middle that he felt fairly confident was *what is even the point*—though point might have been *part*, which changed things, or *post*, which changed them differently.

"All right," he said. He took the writing materials from Crow. "We answer all of it."

The response he composed ran as follows:

*Whatever the specific circumstance that brought you to write—and I believe you have described it faithfully, even if the description has had some difficulty arriving intact—I can see that you are tired in the particular way that comes not from doing too much but from doing the same things repeatedly and feeling unequal returns. This is a real tiredness. It is allowed to exist without justification.*

*If this concerns your work: the point of what you are doing is not always visible from where you are standing. This is structural, not a personal failure. Consider who benefits from your effort even when the benefit is not attributed to you—not to make yourself feel better about being uncredited, but to locate the actual meaning before you decide whether to continue.*

*If this concerns a relationship: the question "what is even the point" often arrives not when things are hopeless but when you have been hoping at full capacity for too long without rest. You are not required to resolve this immediately. You are allowed to set it down briefly—not to abandon it, but to recover enough hope to engage with it honestly.*

*If it concerns both, as I suspect it does, then the above applies in whichever proportion is relevant.*

*You wrote to me. This suggests you are not finished. I think that is the right instinct. Continue, but continue differently—you are the part of this equation most available to revision.*

*Yours, with great respect for the effort of writing at all,*
*S.*

He handed it to Crow, who read it with the expression of someone re-evaluating a prior assessment.

"This is," Crow said, "actually advice."

"It is advice for approximately eleven different people," Sisyphus agreed, "but I believe our correspondent is at least one of them."

---

The fifth letter had a preamble.

The preamble was long. It established, with considerable scaffolding, that the writer was a perfectly ordinary person, a respectable person, a person with a good reputation in their community and strong relationships with their neighbors and absolutely no history of behavior that anyone would consider noteworthy, and that the following question was being asked in a purely hypothetical capacity, and that should Sisyphus feel the question was inappropriate he should feel entirely free to disregard it, and that the writer understood completely if he chose not to respond, and that they were not normally the sort of person who—

Sisyphus skipped to the question.

He read it.

He read it again, not because it required a second reading but because Crow was watching and he felt the second reading looked considered.

"I see," he said.

Crow, who had also read it, offered nothing.

"The answer," Sisyphus said, "is yes, this is a normal thing to want, and no, wanting it doesn't mean what they're afraid it means." He took the papyrus. "The mortification in the preamble suggests they've already spent considerable energy on self-judgment, so I won't add to it."

He wrote:

*You have approached this question as though it requires a defense. It does not require a defense. It requires, at most, a conversation—and those are easier than they appear when you stop waiting for permission you can simply give yourself. The fact that you've been thinking about this for as long as your letter implies suggests the energy spent in avoidance has probably exceeded the energy the actual matter would cost. That seems like an inefficient arrangement.*

*A practical note: the conversation goes better when led with curiosity rather than confession. You are not reporting a crime. You are describing a preference to someone you trust. These are different speeches.*

*I receive a great deal of correspondence. You would not believe—or perhaps you would—how thoroughly ordinary you are. I mean that as the highest comfort I can offer.*

Crow read it over his shoulder and produced, after a moment, a small dry sound that was not quite a laugh but was adjacent to one.

"Yes," Sisyphus said, without looking up.

---

He was halfway through a letter about a man who had been trying to finish the same novel for seven years when he stopped, held up one finger in Crow's direction, and stood.

The boulder had been patient.

He put his shoulder to it and walked, and the slope received him as the slope always did, with complete professional neutrality. Halfway up, he was thinking about the novelist—the particular problem was not the writing, he suspected, but the finishing, which was a different problem with different dimensions, and he was turning an approach over in his mind.

Near the top, he thought, briefly and without particular emphasis, about the specific quality of the moment when the thing was going exactly where it was going, when there was not yet a question about what would happen next, when the effort and the direction were for one moment the same. The satisfaction of that. The way it required no endorsement.

The boulder went over.

The boulder came back.

He walked down behind it with his hands in his pockets—a recent affectation he'd adopted from someone's letter description of how her father used to walk—and settled onto his stool.

"—the problem," he continued, as Crow readied the quill, "is not that you're seven years in. A thing that takes seven years is not a thing that has been failing for seven years; it's a thing that needs seven years. The distinction matters. The question to ask yourself is not *why haven't I finished* but *what would the finished thing require that the current draft doesn't yet have*. That question has an answer. The other question is just a place to stand and feel bad."

Crow wrote.

---

The last letter was written in a careful hand that had been made careful by apparent effort rather than by nature—you could see in the loops and crossings a kind of deliberate attention, the handwriting of someone who had decided to write legibly as an act of respect. The paper was good paper. It had been folded twice and then once again, and then unfolded, and folded differently, and the crease lines made a small map of the writer's decision to send it.

*Dear Sisyphus,*

*I am forty-one years old. I work in my family's mill, which my father worked in before me and his mother before him. I wake at the same time every morning. I do the same work. I eat the same meals, largely. I see the same people. I am not unhappy, exactly. But I have begun to wonder whether this is all there is—whether what I am doing constitutes a life or just the shape a life leaves behind. My son asks me sometimes what I do all day and I find I cannot explain it to him in a way that sounds like it matters. I'm not sure it does. I'm not sure what would.*

*I apologize for the vagueness of this question.*

*Yours,*
*T.*

Sisyphus read it once.

He sat with it for a moment, in the bronze-colored light.

Then he read it again.

Crow did not hurry him.

The Underworld made its various distant sounds: something that might have been water, something that might have been wind if there were wind, the particular quality of quiet that exists in places where questions have been arriving for a very long time.

Sisyphus picked up the quill.

*Dear T.,*

*The vagueness of the question is not a problem. Vague questions are often the accurate ones.*

*You asked whether your life constitutes a life or just the shape one leaves behind. I want to ask you something first: when you do the work you do—the work that is the same every morning, that you cannot explain to your son—is there a version of it that you do well, and a version you do poorly, and do you do the well version on purpose?*

*Because if you do, then something is happening. The shape a life leaves behind does not require that effort. The effort is what is extra. It is what you are adding. That is not nothing; that is the whole point of it.*

*The work does not have to be remarkable to be lived in. You have been in it for forty-one years, which means you know things about flour and grinding and the behavior of grain in different seasons that no one else in the world knows in quite your way. That knowledge is not transferable. Your son cannot be told it; he will only find it if he stays, and he may not stay, and that is all right—it will have been yours.*

*What you are describing—the same morning, the same people, the long view from inside a repetition—is not emptiness. It is a kind of patience most people cannot sustain. The meaning is not at the end of it. It is distributed throughout. You have been living in it all along.*

*You do not need to explain this to your son. You only need to keep doing the well version on purpose. Eventually he will notice that you have, and he will know what it means, or he won't, and it will still have been true.*

*Yours, with great regard,*
*S.*

The quill stopped.

The boulder, somewhere above them on the slope, made the sound that boulders make when they have reached the end of a particular direction.

Sisyphus looked up from the letter. He picked up the blotting cloth. He pressed it evenly across the page, corner to corner, the way you do when the thing is done and you want to do it correctly.

He folded the letter in thirds. He placed it on Crow's side of the table.

The sound from the slope grew closer, and then arrived.

Sisyphus stood, and tucked his stool under the table, and straightened the remaining stack of letters with one hand so that the edges were even, and turned toward the hill.

"Same time," he said.

Crow picked up the letter and said nothing, which was, on this particular afternoon, exactly the right amount.
