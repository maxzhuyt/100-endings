# The Lighthouse Keeper

---

*Rubha Mòr Lighthouse, Isle of Lewis*
*14th October, 1953*

Dear Mum,

Arrived safe on the 11th as planned. The supply boat was late out of Stornoway owing to weather but the crossing was manageable. The posting relief man, MacPhail, showed me the lamp mechanism and the log procedures before he left and I think I have a reasonable handle on things. The light itself is a fourth-order dioptric, which I have not worked with before but it is not complicated once you understand the principle. The station is solid. Two rooms below and the lamp room above. The walls are three feet at the base.

The main difficulty at present is the fog horn compressor. The seal on the pressure valve is deteriorated and wants replacing, but the part has not come. Until it does I must bleed the pressure manually every hour and a half or so, which means sleeping in short runs. I have been setting the clock. It is manageable. I mention it only so you don't worry if my letters are brief—I am tired more than usual, nothing more than that.

The rations are adequate. There is a good store of coal.

Tell me how Agnes is getting on. I hope the cough has settled.

Your son,
Ewan

---

*28 Calder Street, Glasgow*
*22nd October, 1953*

My dear Ewan,

I was so relieved to get your letter. I had been watching for the postman since the 18th and Mrs. Gillies from the floor above caught me at the window and now she will have told everyone I am pining, which I am not, I am simply a mother and a mother looks out the window. You know what she is like. Her daughter married that man from Motherwell and she has never got over it and now she finds other people's business to occupy her instead.

I am glad the walls are thick. I worried the whole time MacPhail was explaining it to your father—when he came here to talk through the posting, you remember—that the sea would come in. I know that is foolish. I know a lighthouse is built for exactly that. But a mother's foolishness is her own business.

Agnes is better, I think. She was at the table for tea on Tuesday which she had not managed the week before. The doctor says it is her chest and has given her something for it. I have been taking her shopping on Thursdays so she doesn't have to manage the hill.

Are you eating properly? Is there a doctor on the island you could get to if you needed?

I have enclosed Mrs. Gillies' own recipe for tablet, which she gave me in a moment of goodwill last Christmas and which I have been meaning to send you. I thought it might be a nice thing to make on a quiet evening. I don't know if you have a pan suitable for it. I suppose you could use whatever pan they've left in the station.

Write when you can.

All my love,
Mum

P.S. I cut out the wee piece from the Herald about the new crossing service. I don't know if it runs near you but I thought you would want to know.

---

*Rubha Mòr Lighthouse*
*3rd November, 1953*

Dear Mum,

Glad Agnes is improved. Tell her from me.

The tablet recipe made me laugh. I will need a heavier pot than what is here but I have made a note of it for when I'm home. I cannot imagine making tablet in this kitchen. The ceiling comes down at a slope on one side and there is not enough counter. But it's a funny thing to have, the recipe, and I appreciate you sending it. Mrs. Gillies would have a fit if she knew.

I have a funny thing to report, which I tell you only because it will give you a laugh. I was doing extended maintenance on the lamp assembly last Tuesday—cleaning the lens panels, which must be done by hand—and I was talking. Aloud. Going through things in my head about the procedure, I thought, but when I stopped to listen to myself I realised I had gone well past the procedure and was talking about I don't know what, various things, the mechanics of it, and other things, and I'd been at it for over an hour without noticing. The lamp is good company, it turns out. Very attentive. Does not interrupt.

I imagine this is what happens to men at these postings. Nothing wrong with it. I just thought you would find it amusing.

The compressor part has still not come. The sleep is manageable.

Is the coal holding? I worry about the price.

Your son,
Ewan

---

*28 Calder Street, Glasgow*
*12th November, 1953*

My dear Ewan,

An hour talking to a lamp. Well. I suppose there are worse listeners. Your father used to talk to the car when it wouldn't start. A full conversation. I always thought it was the most sensible thing about him.

The coal is fine. I have enough laid in for the winter. Mrs. Gillies' nephew helps me with the carrying when it comes, he is a decent enough boy whatever you might think of his aunt.

Agnes had a bad week. I won't go into it at length because the doctor says she is not in danger but she was back in bed from Monday and I have been over there more days than not. She sends her love.

I want to ask you something and you don't have to answer it. Are you sleeping enough? I don't mean the compressor business, I know you said it is manageable. I mean in general. You sound tired in your letters. I don't know how else to put it. You sound like a man who is very tired.

Write soon.

All my love,
Mum

P.S. What is the seabird situation up there? I always think of you with seabirds. I don't know why.

---

*Rubha Mòr Lighthouse*
*28th November, 1953*

Dear Mum,

I am sorry to hear Agnes has been bad again. You must be worn out with it. Please don't take on more than you can manage.

Tell her I am thinking of her.

The compressor part came on the 19th and I fitted it on the 20th. I slept seven hours that first night and woke up not knowing where I was for a moment. It passed.

I have to tell you something and I want you to understand that it is resolved and I am not asking you to worry about it. On the 15th of this month there was a heavy storm out of the northwest, force 8 gusting higher. I was running on about forty minutes of sleep at a stretch by that point and during the storm I made an error. I let the lamp dim—the wick wanted adjusting and I misjudged the timing—and it was eleven minutes before I had it back to full. I do not know if there were ships in the shipping lane that night. I have not been able to find out. Eleven minutes is not a long time in most situations.

I don't know why I'm telling you this. It is resolved. The lamp is right. The compressor is fixed.

I am sleeping now.

Your son,
Ewan

---

*28 Calder Street, Glasgow*
*7th December, 1953*

My dear Ewan,

I have read your letter several times. I am glad the part came and I am glad you are sleeping.

I think about those eleven minutes. I know you said it is resolved.

I am not going to say a lot about it because I know you, and I know that the more I say the less you will tell me next time. So I will only say: I am glad you told me. That is enough.

Agnes is recovering slowly. The doctor says spring will be better for her. Mrs. Gillies has been less insufferable lately, which I think is because her nephew is courting someone in Partick and she has new material.

I am thinking about coming up to see you in the spring. I have been looking at the ferry connections and I think it is possible. The crossing from Ullapool, is that right? I want to see where you are. I want to see the actual building and the lamp and the kitchen with the sloped ceiling. I want to know what you are waking up to.

Write when you can.

All my love,
Mum

---

*Rubha Mòr Lighthouse*
*19th December, 1953*

Dear Mum,

Spring would be fine. Yes, Ullapool.

The lamp is running well. The logs are in order. The weather has been hard this week but nothing unexpected for December here.

I have been eating. There are enough rations.

The seabirds you asked about—they were landing on the gallery rail all through October, resting there. They have stopped now. I don't know if it's the time of year or the cold. They were good company while they came.

Merry Christmas when it gets to you. I have nothing particular to report.

Your son,
Ewan

---

*28 Calder Street, Glasgow*
*29th December, 1953*

My dear Ewan,

Merry Christmas. I had hoped your letter would arrive before the day but it came on the 27th. 

Agnes was well enough to come to me for Christmas dinner which was good. We thought of you.

I have been rereading your last few letters. I want to ask you something directly and I want you to answer me directly: how are you? Not the lamp. Not the rations. Not the weather. How are you?

I am asking because your letters have got short. I know you will say you have been busy and I know that is probably true. But you have always been a man who when he is telling me everything gives me a page and a half, and when he is telling me nothing gives me a page and a half as well, and lately I have been getting half a page. And I know you.

I am planning to come in spring. The Ullapool crossing, as you said.

Write to me.

All my love,
Mum

---

*Rubha Mòr Lighthouse*
*9th January, 1954*

Dear Mum,

I want to tell you something and I don't know how to account for it exactly, so I will just tell you what happened and leave it with you.

There was a clear night last week, very cold, no cloud, exceptional visibility. I was doing the last check of the evening—this is close to three in the morning—and I was looking out at the light as it moved, the way it does, and I started thinking about the distance it carries on a night like that. Clear nights in winter the light reaches considerably further than usual. I know the numbers. I have the specification in the log.

I started thinking about the angle of projection and the distance and I know the calculation is wrong because you need elevation and curvature and the geometry doesn't work, I know that. I know how far Glasgow is from here. I know the coast of Lewis does not face that direction. I know all of that.

But I stood at the glass for—I don't know how long. Several hours. I was watching the light go out and watching it come back and I was thinking: if you were standing at Calder Street right now, at the end of the road, looking west, you might see it. I know you couldnae. I know it cannae reach that far. I know that. I just kept watching.

I am fine. I am only telling you because you asked.

The lamp is in good order.

Your son,
Ewan

---

*28 Calder Street, Glasgow*
*17th January, 1954*

Ewan,

I am coming now. Not spring. I have already written to Mrs. MacAulay in Stornoway who Agnes knows from years ago, she has a room she lets, and I have the information about the ferry. I will write again with the date once it is fixed, which will be soon.

Your father used to say the light looks different from the inside.

Leave the door unlocked for me.

All my love,
Mum
