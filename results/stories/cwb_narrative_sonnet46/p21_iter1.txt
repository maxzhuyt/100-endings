# Blood Moon

The village has been empty for three days.

Yennefer knows this the way she knows most things — by reading what remains rather than what is present. The hearth fires have burned themselves out to cold gray powder. A loaf of bread on a windowsill has gone the color of old bone. The well's rope hangs slack, the bucket still lowered, and no one has thought to bring it up. She moves through the main street slowly, her black skirts trailing over dust that holds no footprints, only the patient accumulation of three days' wind.

Above her, the harvest moon hangs too low and too red — swollen with its own significance, bleeding rust-light across the empty buildings until everything looks like the aftermath of something she hasn't identified yet. She doesn't find this comforting or alarming. She finds it aesthetically consistent. The world tends toward the dramatic when she is in it.

The smell hits her before the sound does.

Not her own scent — not the lilac and gooseberry that clings to her regardless of where she's been or what she's done, the one constant in a life that has rearranged itself around her several times over. Something older than that. Copper at the base of it, and beneath the copper, the deep mineral sweetness of earth that has held things for a very long time. Rot, controlled and ancient. The smell of something that should not still be walking.

She stops in the middle of the street.

Her fingers curl at her sides, not reaching for anything yet, simply acknowledging the possibility.

The shadow between two buildings on her left does not move. It doesn't need to. She has already located it — him — the way you locate a sound by the particular quality of the silence surrounding it. He is very still in the way that things are still when stillness costs them nothing.

"You can come out," she says to the shadow. Her voice is conversational. She has found that conversational is more unsettling than threatening in most situations. "Or you can stay there. We can continue pretending this is subtle."

A pause. Then the darkness simply releases him, the way water releases something that has rested on the bottom long enough.

He is tall — theatrically, almost aggressively tall, as though height were an argument he'd decided to win conclusively. A red coat, long, the kind of red that moonlight makes look like something spilled. A hat with a brim wide enough to cast its own shadow. And the glasses — orange-tinted, at midnight, in a dead village under a blood moon — which tell her, more efficiently than anything else could, exactly what kind of creature she is dealing with. Not the kind that hides.

The kind that announces.

He is grinning. The grin is the first wrong thing about him, which is notable, because several other things are also wrong. It is too wide and too still and it involves a quantity of teeth that the human mouth was not designed to accommodate. But the eyes above it — she catches the edges of them past the glass — are watching her with the precise, cataloguing attention of something that has survived by never looking carelessly at anything.

She looks back.

Her violet eyes hold the moonlight and don't release it.

Neither of them speaks for a moment. She is reading him: the absolute stillness of a chest that does not breathe, the way the shadows seem faintly reluctant to leave him even in the open, the age of him — not visible exactly, but present, the way cold is present, as a quality of the air around an object rather than the object itself. She places him in a category that has very few members. The oldest kind. The kind the stories remembered wrong.

He is reading her too. She can feel it — the weight of assessment, thorough and unhurried, the way a scholar handles something unfamiliar. His grin does not change but it alters somehow in character, acquiring an edge of something that might be interest.

"A sorceress," he says. His voice is the kind that fills space without effort, the way water fills the shape of whatever holds it. It carries amusement the way most voices carry air. "I thought I smelled magic."

"I thought I smelled a corpse," she says. "Turns out we're both right."

He tilts his head. Something in his expression adjusts minutely, recalibrates. He begins to move — a slow arc around her, wide and deliberate, the circling thing. She tracks him without turning, reading him in her peripheral vision. She has known many things that circle. It has never ended the way they imagined.

"You're not afraid," he says, completing half the circle and stopping.

"Should I be?"

"You really should be." The grin widens incrementally. It has architecture now, the grin. It is a thing built for a purpose. "It is, in fact, somewhat rude that you aren't."

"I'll add it to my list of social failings." She glances past him toward the village square, where something dark is folded over something darker, a shape that doesn't resolve cleanly from this distance. "Is that yours?"

He does not look. He looks at her. "Everything in this village is mine. I arrived first."

"You arrived first, did the work, and left the aftermath for whoever came along next." She begins walking toward the square. "An impressive display of dominance."

He falls into step beside her. The proximity is deliberate — closer than conversation requires, engineered to require a response. She does not provide one. She does not alter her pace or angle away, which she suspects is not the response he expected. This seems to please him, which is its own kind of information.

"What are you?" she asks.

"What I appear to be."

She glances at him sidelong. The red coat. The ridiculous hat. The midnight sunglasses. "A man who makes decisions about accessories."

"A vampire." He says it without the weight most creatures give their own name. Simply a category, simply a fact. "The old kind."

"The stories got it wrong."

He looks at her with something that is briefly, cleanly, surprised. "The stories got nearly everything wrong."

"They usually do." She crouches beside the shape in the square.

It was something large. What remains is less than that — the way a fire becomes less than wood, not fully accounted for by what's left. She draws a symbol in the air with two fingers, precise angles, the geometry of inquiry. The sigil flares violet and hangs for a moment like a held breath, reading the residue, the magical signature that violence always leaves, then collapses inward on itself like an ember going out.

She stays crouched, processing.

She is aware, with the part of her mind she keeps for threats, that he is watching her cast. Not the way most creatures watch magic — with fear dressed up as contempt, or contempt dressed up as fascination. He is watching the way she watches him: cataloguing. Reading. The focused attention of something that has remained alive by never looking carelessly at anything curious.

She stands.

"You didn't do this," she says.

"No."

"But you know what did."

"I have theories." A pause. He has moved without appearing to have moved — three feet closer, not threatening, simply proximate. "You're very skilled. That casting. Most magic I encounter is small. Defensive. Afraid of what it is." He pauses again. "Yours isn't."

The compliment arrives without the scaffolding of flattery around it, which makes it difficult to dismiss. A thing offered plainly, that she can take or leave. She registers that this is sophisticated. She registers that he knows she's registered it. She files all of this and moves past it.

"What do you want?" she asks. Not in the village; she understands the village, or is beginning to. What he wants. Why he stepped out of the dark.

He spreads his hands. The gesture is enormous and theatrical, the gesture of a showman, and she could stop reading him there — at the performance of it — except that she has spent a great deal of her life learning to look at what moves beneath the surface of a performance. Beneath the red coat, beneath the impossible grin, beneath the patience of something that has never needed to hurry anywhere — there is a quality she recognizes. Not hunger for blood. She has seen that, in things far lesser than him, and it looks nothing like this.

This is the hunger of something that has consumed everything available to it and found the landscape boring.

"Someone worthy," he says. Simply. Without theater.

The moon has shifted. Or the shadows have. Something in the light between them changes.

"You thought that might be me," she says.

The grin this time is slower. It doesn't show the teeth. It is, briefly, something that looks almost like the real thing — the expression beneath the performance, small and unguarded, there and then not there.

"I think it might be you," he says.

Yennefer looks at him for a long moment. She thinks about all the things that have wanted her diminished, contained, redirected, used. She thinks about all the things that have wanted her power without wanting her — that saw the magic and looked straight through the person holding it. She places this creature in a separate category from all of that. Not safe. The opposite of safe. But oriented toward her in a different way than any of the others. She cannot fully read the way, which is interesting. She will think about the fact that she finds it interesting later, alone.

"I'm not a specimen," she says.

"No." The real grin again, brief and unguarded. "You really aren't." He tilts his head. "What are you called?"

The moon is directly overhead now and the village is so quiet she can hear her own heartbeat. She notices that she cannot hear his.

She straightens her coat. The lilac scent of her lifts briefly through the copper-and-grave of him, and for a moment the two smells occupy the same air without canceling each other out.

She does not answer.

She walks past him toward the far edge of the village, where the road continues south and the darkness is ordinary darkness, the kind that doesn't hold anything.

"That thing in the square," she says without looking back, "is moving northeast. Whatever it is, the magic reads like compulsion — something else is driving it, not instinct."

Behind her: nothing. No footfall, no breath. Just the village silence and within it, his voice, unchanged, carrying its habitual private amusement.

"I know."

"Then you don't need me."

A pause that might be consideration or might be theater, and she finds she cannot determine which, which is also interesting.

"No," he agrees. Another pause. "But I'll find you again."

She does not answer. The road opens ahead of her, pale and bone-colored under the harvest moon, and she walks out of the empty village, down into the long descending dark, and she does not look back at the thing standing perfectly still at the village's edge — the thing in the red coat and the midnight glasses, watching her go, in absolutely no hurry.

It has never needed to hurry.

It has never run out of time.

She is three miles south, on the pale road, with the moon declining behind the tree line, before she understands that this is what she is carrying with her — not the teeth, not the darkness, not the full impossible weight of what he is. Those she has held before, in different forms. Those have names and counters and the kind of menace that operates in straight lines.

What she carries is the patience.

The particular quality of something that could have moved and chose not to. Something that is already calculating the shape of the next encounter with the measured calm of a creature for whom next is simply a matter of preference rather than urgency.

She walks faster. Not fleeing.

She is simply aware, for the first time in a very long time, of the value of a head start.
