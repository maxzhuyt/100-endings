# Rehearsing Rejection

The earring backs are small and the mirror is slightly wrong-angled and she has been standing here, tilting her head, for three minutes longer than necessary. The phone is face-up on the bathroom shelf. She checked it four minutes ago. She checks it again: nothing new, the same last message from Dara sitting in the thread like a held breath—*see you at 6! :)*—sent Wednesday, and it is now Saturday and there has been nothing since, no checking-in, no *so excited*, none of the usual ambient warmth that used to run between them like water through a pipe. Eleven days before that: the call that lasted nine minutes when it usually lasts an hour, Dara saying *I should actually get going* with something careful in her voice, some texture that wasn't quite hurry. She had noticed it then and told herself she hadn't and has been noticing it since.

This is not suspicion. She knows the difference.

Through the wall, the neighbors' television says something she can't quite catch. A drama. Someone is explaining something to someone else with great seriousness—she can hear the register if not the words, the cadence of a difficult conversation being had—and she turns back to her own face in the mirror.

The earring goes in. There. She looks fine. She looks like herself, which is to say she looks exactly as she would if nothing were wrong and also exactly as she would if everything were.

---

Dara will come in and sit across from her—they always take the small table by the window, the one with the uneven leg they've learned to accommodate—and there will be a moment, a distinct and recognizable moment, when the levity drops. When Dara straightens slightly and her expression does the thing it does when she's about to say something she's been carrying. She has a way of beginning these things: *So I've been thinking.* Or sometimes just a breath, a particular breath, that functions as punctuation.

*I've been thinking,* she'll say, *and I don't think I've been great at this—at us, at being what you need—*

*You don't have to—*

*No, let me.* And that's the part that will be worst, the gentle firmness of *let me*, the way Dara is careful even when she's leaving, especially when she's leaving, and there will be something so fundamentally like her in that carefulness that for a moment—

She will say: *I understand.*

She will not say: *I saw this coming.* Though she will say it in her posture, in the way she nods. She will be prepared. She will have the high ground of someone who has already grieved.

*I think I've known for a while,* she might say. Quietly. Looking at the table. And that will be true by then—she will have known, she will have been knowing for weeks, alone with the knowledge while Dara went about her life, unknowing that she was already being prepared for, already being rehearsed against.

She checks the phone again. 5:18.

---

She's doing it again.

She knows she's doing it—she can see herself doing it, like watching someone across a room make a mistake too far away to stop—the whole scaffolding of imagined trouble, the trial she convenes and presides over and loses and survives alone in her own bathroom. The word her last therapist used was *catastrophizing*. Dara herself has said, gently, more than once: *you do this thing where you decide what I'm feeling before you ask me.* She knows what the thing is. She has words for the thing. She can hold it at arm's length and look at it clearly and see that it is a pattern, her pattern, a groove worn into her by years of something she has also put words to, in journals and sessions and late-night conversations that always felt like progress and always dissolved back into this: the bathroom, the mirror, the manufacturing of a wound.

*I'm doing it again.* She thinks it plainly. She lets herself think it plainly.

And then: *but the call was only nine minutes.* And the scaffold goes back up.

Through the wall, something on the television—a laugh, or nearly a laugh, or the shape of one.

---

The pasta place they like is also where she and Dara had their first real conversation, three years ago when *real* still felt like an achievement, when she was still learning that Dara meant things when she said them. She remembers being suspicious of that. Of sincerity. She remembers being sixteen and then twenty-two and then twenty-eight and learning, each time, slightly differently, that warmth has an expiration she just hasn't found yet.

Dara forgot her birthday last year by two days, then remembered and called and was genuinely stricken, and she had said *it's fine, genuinely, it doesn't matter* and had mostly meant it but had also, she now understands, catalogued it. It was small but it was catalogued. She has come to recognize this capacity in herself: the archive, the running file she does not choose to keep but keeps anyway. There are other entries. The time Dara cancelled the trip, though she had good reason. The way she talks about her other friends—with a different quality of enthusiasm, maybe, a higher brightness, though this might also be nothing. The call that was nine minutes.

She takes them out now, the evidence, and does what she does with evidence: arranges it. The picture it makes is coherent. She knows how to make coherent pictures; she has been making them her whole life. The picture says: *you have always known this was temporary, it is embarrassing that you let yourself believe otherwise, but at least you are not surprised, at least you have been building the exit ramp.*

She is pulling on her coat when she thinks: *at least I am not surprised.* And underneath that, very small, some other thought she doesn't look at directly.

5:41. She picks up the phone. She leaves.

---

The restaurant is warm in the specific way of restaurants in November, the warmth that meets you at the door like a correction, and she sees Dara before Dara sees her—Dara at the table by the window, the uneven one, red coat draped over the chair back, pulling her hair out of its thing with one distracted hand, and she is so recognizably herself, so entirely Dara, that something in her chest moves before she has decided to let it.

Then Dara looks up and her face just—opens. That's the word for it. The way her face opens.

"*There* you are," Dara says, and stands up, and hugs her, and the hug is not a polite hug—it lasts a second longer than polite, it has warmth distributed through it, it smells like the cedar thing Dara sprays on her sweaters. She thinks, involuntarily: *this is not a hug someone gives before they say I've been thinking.*

"God," Dara says, "I've been in back-to-back meetings since Monday and I was genuinely afraid I'd forgotten how to be a person."

"You seem like a person."

"The coat is doing a lot of work." She sits. She's already looking at the menu. "Their gnocchi thing—did we do that? We did that. Should we do that again?"

"We should."

"We should." She flags down the server. "Also I need you to tell me everything. The job thing, the—your mom, when did you last talk to her?" She sets down the menu. "Tell me. In order or not, whatever's louder."

She opens her mouth and what comes out is the actual thing, the true version, the one she has not rehearsed—the job that is fine but is just fine, the mom who said a thing on the phone last week that landed wrong and she's been turning it over, the feeling, lately, of waiting for something without knowing what—and Dara listens. She does not do the thing people do where listening means waiting. She just listens. She says *what did she mean, do you think?* and then listens to that too.

The gnocchi comes. They share, like they always share, fork trespassing onto each other's plates without negotiation.

At one point Dara laughs at something she says, a real laugh, and the sound is so familiar it almost hurts.

---

"I'm genuinely so glad you're my person," Dara says, at some point in the second glass of wine.

She says it looking at her own glass, slightly offhand, the way real things sometimes get said—without preparation or ceremony, just offered. And she hears it. She receives it. She holds it for a moment, turning it over, and it is true, she can tell it is true, she knows how to tell the difference between—

*Why now, though.* 

The thought arrives before she's invited it. *Why say that now, specifically—what is the thing she's building up to, is this the cushioning, is this what you do before—*

She breathes. Across the table, Dara is talking about something else now, a colleague, a situation with a colleague, and her voice is animated, slightly indignant, and she is so real she is almost difficult to look at.

*Genuinely so glad you're my person.* 

She holds it. She examines it. She cannot find the place in it that isn't true and this should be evidence too, she knows this should be evidence, but the file is open and the archive is practiced and the lawyer in her is already rearranging—

Through the window, a couple on the street, unintelligible, mid-argument, gesturing. They walk past. They are gone.

"—and I said that to her face, actually," Dara is saying, "I surprised myself," and she is nodding, and some part of her is here, is present, is genuinely charmed by Dara's story about the colleague, and some part of her is already somewhere else, at a table she hasn't arrived at yet, rehearsing.

---

Dara's hug at the end is the same hug as the beginning. She squeezes once, extra, and says "two weeks feels too long—can we do something before that?" and pulls out her phone. They find a date. They agree on a date. It's simple. Two weeks is not long.

She walks to the train and four minutes later her phone lights up: *so needed that. love you. ok goodnight :)* 

She reads it standing on the platform.

She reads it again.

It's a good text. It is an unambiguous text. She has learned enough, over enough years, to know when something is a good thing and this is a good thing—warm and easy and sent without apparent deliberation, the kind of text people send when the evening was what they said it was.

*love you.*

The train comes, she gets on, she sits.

She reads it again.

Dara has seemed tired lately—she'd said so herself, back-to-back meetings, the colleague situation, there has been a lot on her. It would make sense that a person with a lot on them might have less to give for a while, might be slower to reply, might let calls go a little shorter. That would make sense. That is a reasonable thing that happens between people who are fine.

Though.

She turns the phone over. The train moves.

In her ears, through someone else's headphones nearby, something she can't quite make out—a voice, a song, explaining something at length.

She turns the phone back over.

*Two weeks,* she thinks. She will have to buy something to wear. The green thing is still clean but she doesn't know if it's right, if it sends the right—she will want to look like someone who is not worried. She will want to look like herself.

She has exactly two weeks to prepare.
