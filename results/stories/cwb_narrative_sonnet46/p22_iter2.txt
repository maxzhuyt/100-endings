# Prison Strings

The chain-link fence rattled when Jolyne walked past it.

She didn't think anything of it. Wind came through the yard in strange ways—the facility was built into a cliff face on the eastern side, and the geography did something to air pressure, made currents that didn't track with any weather she could parse. She'd been here eleven days and the fences rattled constantly. She'd stopped registering it the way you stop registering the smell of a place after the first forty-eight hours.

What she registered instead was the woman standing near the water station.

Not because of anything the woman did. That was the thing Jolyne kept coming back to, later—there was no single wrong gesture, no obvious tell. The woman was simply standing there with a paper cup of water she wasn't drinking, watching the yard with the patient attention of someone who had nowhere to be and had made peace with that fact in a way that Jolyne hadn't, in eleven days, managed to make peace with.

She was pale, almost formal-looking despite the facility's standard-issue clothing. Dark hair. Yellow eyes, which was unusual enough to clock—though in this place, unusual eyes weren't the strangest feature you'd encounter before lunch. She wore them like they were ordinary. She wore everything like it was ordinary.

The woman saw Jolyne looking and smiled.

It was a good smile. That was the problem. It was warm and measured and it arrived on her face with a slight lag, the way a translation arrives after the original words—correct, but processed. Jolyne looked away.

She walked the perimeter, which was her habit. Eleven days in and she'd already built habits, because habits were how you survived enclosed spaces, how you kept the walls from becoming the whole world. Walk the perimeter. Count the exits. Note who stands where and why. She'd learned this from her father without him ever teaching it to her—absorbed it through the walls of his absence the way you absorb cold from a window without touching the glass.

"You walk like you're measuring something."

Jolyne didn't stop. "Most people do."

"Most people don't look like they'll find it." The woman had fallen into step beside her without Jolyne registering the transition—she'd been at the water station, and then she was here, moving at precisely Jolyne's pace. "I'm Makima."

"Good for you."

"And you're Jolyne Cujoh." She said it without emphasis, which was its own kind of emphasis. "I've heard things about you."

"Then you already know everything you need to."

Makima smiled again. This time Jolyne caught the lag—half a second, maybe less, the smile assembling itself like something built rather than something felt. "I heard you're one of the more interesting arrivals this facility has seen in some time. I heard your ability is unusual even by the standards of this place." She paused. "And I heard you don't trust anyone."

"Batting a thousand so far."

"I'd like to change the last one, if you'd let me."

Jolyne stopped walking. The fence to her left rattled. She looked at Makima straight-on for the first time, cataloguing—the stillness of her hands, the way she stood with her weight perfectly distributed, nothing fidgeting, nothing betraying the small anxieties that leaked out of everyone in confined spaces. Even the hardest people Jolyne had met in the original Green Dolphin had leaked something. A tapping foot. A jaw working.

Makima leaked nothing.

"I don't need protection," Jolyne said. "I don't need information. I don't need a friend." She said the last word with just enough inflection to make it clear she'd identified the play. "I'm fine."

"I know," Makima said. She didn't sound offended. She didn't sound anything. "But the woman in Block C is going to try to take your food ration tomorrow morning, and she has three people who'll back her. The guard rotation shifts at 0600, which leaves a four-minute gap near the east corridor." She paused. "I thought you'd want to know. For your measuring."

She excused herself with a small nod and walked back toward the facility entrance, her paper cup still in her hand, still undrunk.

Jolyne watched her go.

Then she paced the length of the yard twice, checked the strings she'd begun threading along the inside of her left forearm—habit, now, keeping them close—and picked a fight with a large man from the northern block who'd been eyeing her shoes for the better part of an hour. It wasn't her most considered decision and she knew it. She did it anyway, because she needed something to push against, something that would push back.

He pushed back. She appreciated it.

---

The next morning the woman from Block C came for her ration at 0612, during the gap.

Jolyne had been awake since 0545. She'd been ready since 0600.

She didn't use her Stand. She didn't need to—forewarned was everything in a tight space, and the three-on-one odds collapsed completely once the first one went down fast and loud. The other two reassessed. That was the thing about people who relied on numbers: remove the assumption of numbers and they had nothing.

She ate her ration. The food was terrible.

She didn't go looking for Makima to say thank you, because saying thank you would be its own kind of concession. But she noticed, as she walked the perimeter that afternoon, that Makima was already there at the water station, already holding her paper cup, turning to watch Jolyne make her circuit with that assembled smile.

Jolyne nodded, once.

Makima nodded back.

---

The fight broke out on a Thursday, or what passed for Thursday in a place without reliable sunlight. Two of the devil-adjacent prisoners—beings that looked humanoid from certain angles and stopped looking humanoid when they moved—had been circling each other for days, and whatever territorial negotiation they'd been conducting finally collapsed into something physical and loud near the center of the yard.

Jolyne was not involved. She became involved when one of them—a thing with too many joints in its fingers—grabbed a smaller prisoner and held her off the ground by the throat with the apparent intention of using her as a shield or a weapon or both.

Jolyne unraveled her arm.

She did it without thinking, which was the only way she'd ever done it—Stone Free answered to urgency the way it answered to nothing else, the string coming apart from her skin and muscle with a sensation she'd never quite found language for, something between cold water and static electricity, the threads pulling free and extending outward in a net-like spread that caught the too-jointed hand and pulled, sharply, redirecting the grab into empty air. The smaller prisoner dropped. Jolyne's string recoiled and she was already reassembling herself—arm first, then the fingers—before the thing fully registered what had happened.

The fences rattled so hard the sound was almost musical.

She didn't look for Makima. She knew, from some frequency below conscious thought, that Makima was watching. She focused on making sure the thing with the joints understood that trying again would be educational in painful ways, and it did seem to understand—they backed off in that particular fashion that meant the decision was actually a strategic withdrawal—

"Beautiful."

Jolyne turned.

Makima was standing at the perimeter, exactly where she'd been. She hadn't moved. Her hands were loose at her sides—no cup today—and her yellow eyes were still moving, slowly, across the space where Jolyne's strings had been, as if she could see the afterimage of them in the air.

"Your ability," Makima said. She said it simply, without decoration. "It's beautiful."

The word found a place between Jolyne's ribs and sat there.

She thought about the string catching the light as it extended—she'd seen it from the outside only in reflections, in windows, in the rare piece of metal that threw back a surface—and she thought about how it had looked from Makima's position, from the perimeter, from that particular angle of stillness and attention.

She thought: *what did she see when she watched that.*

She thought: *why does that word feel like a hand closing around something.*

"Thanks," she said, which was wrong—too easy, too reciprocal—and walked away before anything else could happen.

---

There was a prisoner in Block D whose name, translated generously, approximated something like "Vessel." Jolyne didn't know the full history. She knew Vessel was devil-adjacent in a way that meant the devil in question was always present but not always visible, that it showed in the eyes when something triggered it, that most of the other prisoners gave Vessel a wide berth.

She saw Makima speak to Vessel on a Tuesday afternoon near the east wall.

She was close enough to observe, not close enough to hear. What she saw: Makima speaking quietly, with that same precise warmth she deployed in every context. Vessel listening. And then—this was the thing, this was the thing Jolyne kept examining afterward—Vessel's body shifting, slightly, the way a compass needle shifts. The way a body shifts when something in it recognizes a force and realigns around it without any decision being made. Vessel moved to the left and made space and did something with their hands that looked like an offering.

Makima accepted it pleasantly and moved on.

Jolyne waited until evening.

"What did you say to them?"

Makima looked up from the book she was reading—an actual book, which suggested she had access to things Jolyne hadn't negotiated yet—and her expression was one of mild, helpful confusion. "To Vessel? I asked them to help me carry something. I was moving some things between rooms." She paused. "I don't have many resources for that kind of thing. Vessel was kind enough to help."

"They were shaking."

"Vessel sometimes shakes. It's a response to—" Makima tilted her head. "The devil doesn't always sit easily. You may have noticed they have difficulty with prolonged eye contact. With close proximity. It's not personal."

"I've had prolonged eye contact with Vessel. They were fine."

"Were they?" Makima's tone was genuinely curious. "I'm glad. You must put people at ease."

The fence along the east wall rattled. Jolyne noticed, with the part of her brain that catalogued things without being asked, that it had rattled when Makima walked past it. She'd noticed this before. She hadn't registered it as distinct from her own rattling until now—the sound was slightly different, the frequency of it, the duration. Like a the same instrument played by a different hand.

"What are you, exactly," Jolyne said. It wasn't really a question.

"I'm here, the same as you." Makima smiled, and the lag was there, and this time it felt less like a tell and more like the sliver of time between a lock mechanism turning and the door releasing. Something happening in the space before. "Same as everyone. I try to make what I can of the situation."

Jolyne went back to her cell and lay on her bunk and stared at the ceiling and thought about leashes.

---

Makima came to her with a proposal on a Saturday morning, in the common area, in full view of eight other prisoners who couldn't hear what was said but could observe the geometry of it.

"I want to understand your ability." She said it the way you might say *I'd like a glass of water*—simple, reasonable, as if the want were uncomplicated. "Not to use it. To understand it. How the strings interact with different materials, with different types of anomalous being. You've encountered things in this facility that most Stand users never have. I think there's something to learn."

"From studying me."

"With you." She amended it smoothly. "I'd share what I learn. Everything. No asymmetry."

Jolyne looked at the eight other prisoners who were pretending not to watch. She looked at the fence along the near wall, which was still. She looked at Makima's hands, which were folded in her lap with the composure of someone who had not considered the possibility of refusal—or had considered it and found it irrelevant.

"If you come near me with the intention of studying anything," Jolyne said, keeping her voice level and friendly, because level and friendly carried farther and hit harder than loud, "I'll put you through the wall. I'll unravel you down to component parts and use what's left to tie the fence shut. And whatever it is you've been doing to Vessel, and to the others you've got so quiet I can't even hear them breathing anymore—I'll find out what that is, and I'll pull on that thread until it comes apart." She smiled. "Do you understand completely?"

Makima held her gaze for a long moment.

"Completely," she said. And she smiled. "Thank you for being honest with me."

She left.

That night, Jolyne was checking the placement of the tripwire strings she'd begun threading through the facility—through door frames, along baseboards, across the junctions of corridors—when she found one of them disturbed. Not broken. Disturbed. Touched and replaced with the specific care of someone who'd identified it as a sensing mechanism and wanted to demonstrate awareness of it without triggering the response.

The string ran from the east corridor to her cell to the yard. It had been touched at a point she'd never told anyone about, in a section of corridor she'd secured three days ago and not revisited.

Jolyne sat with that for a long time.

She sat with the fact that she'd been watched more carefully than she'd watched back. She sat with the fact that every scene she replayed—the water station, the common area, Vessel at the east wall—she'd been operating under the assumption that she was the observer. That her paranoia was the most present thing in any given space.

She sat with being wrong.

Then she got up, because sitting with being wrong was only useful for as long as it took to correct it.

---

She spent three nights threading the facility.

Stone Free gave her advantages in low-visibility, close-quarters work that no one with a conventional Stand could match—she could unravel just enough to send a single thread along a corridor without the rest of her body following, without making a sound, without requiring the bulk of physical presence. The strings were pressure-sensitive in a way that wasn't quite feeling and wasn't quite hearing, something more like proprioception extended outside the body. She'd built them into tripwires before, but this was different—this was a map. This was reading the facility as a nervous system, learning its twitches.

She learned that the fences rattled differently under different bodies. She learned which doors Makima favored. She learned the routes, the timings, the small repetitions that constituted a pattern.

She learned that on Sunday mornings, Makima visited the cells of four specific prisoners—not together, separately, brief visits, always with that quality of quiet that Jolyne now understood as the silence after someone stops breathing freely.

On the fourth Sunday, Jolyne's strings were already there.

Makima moved through the first corridor and Jolyne felt it—the pressure differential, the specific frequency of someone who displaced air without appearing to displace it, who moved through space the way authority moved through space, not faster or louder but more gravitational, other things curving slightly toward her without deciding to. The first prisoner's cell. The second. The third was a woman named Ida who had a Stand that manifested as a bird, always perched on her shoulder, and who had stopped looking directly at anyone since Makima's first visit.

Jolyne pulled.

Not strings around Makima—not yet—but the architecture of the thing she was about to do, the prisoner Ida reaching for the door at Makima's unspoken request, and Jolyne's string was already threaded along the inside of the door frame and when it pulled back it pulled the door shut and the lock re-engaged from the inside with a sound like finality.

Makima stopped.

Jolyne stepped out from the junction of the corridor.

The fences were audible even here, inside—some trick of the cliff-face acoustics—rattling in the particular frequency that Jolyne now understood as a response to her, as something in this place that recognized unraveling and resonated with it.

"You've been busy," Makima said.

"Sunday mornings." Jolyne began to walk toward her slowly. "You do this every Sunday. Same route, same order. I want to know what they're doing for you when they leave those rooms."

"Remaining well," Makima said. "I check in on people."

"Vessel ended up in the medical ward."

"Vessel's condition is unstable—"

"Vessel's condition got unstable after your check-ins started." Jolyne kept walking. The strings along the baseboards were alive with pressure, tracking Makima's weight shift, her slight adjustment of footing. "I can feel where you're thinking of going before you decide to go there. I want you to know that."
