# Overthinking It

The thing about Marcus Chen is that I had already decided he was significant before Mr. Paulsen said anything, which meant that when Mr. Paulsen said our names in the same sentence—*Nadia and Marcus, group four*—I had approximately 0.4 seconds to rearrange my face into an expression that communicated: *this is fine, this is a school project, I am a person who does school projects.*

I think I did it. I'm not sure I did it.

The desks scraped. People moved. Marcus picked up his backpack and his notebook and walked the six feet between his old seat and the desk next to mine, and I watched him do this with the careful neutrality of someone watching a nature documentary about something that does not affect them personally in any way.

He sat down.

*Hi,* he said, or maybe *hey*, I was not fully tracking the specific phoneme at that point because I was managing several other things simultaneously: my posture (straight but not rigid, relaxed but not slouched, something in the middle that probably looked like I was trying to pass a sobriety test), the placement of my hands (on the desk, I decided, flat and calm like they belonged to someone whose heart rate was in a normal range), and the fact that I had known for six weeks that sitting next to Marcus Chen would matter and now it was happening and I had prepared for this and none of my preparations were accessible to me.

*Hey,* I said.

And then silence, which I needed to fill, and I had things ready for the silence—I'd had things ready for weeks—so I ran them.

Option one: *I heard this project's actually kind of interesting, which, for Paulsen, is either a miracle or a trap.* Pros: positions me as someone with opinions and a sense of humor. Cons: requires him to agree that Paulsen is trap-prone, and what if he likes Paulsen, what if Paulsen tutored him through something, what if I have just insulted the man who delivered his intellectual awakening. Discarded.

Option two: just the question, *have you done anything like this before?* Low-risk, invites him to talk, makes me seem curious rather than someone who has been composing conversation openers since October. Cons: vague to the point of uselessness, he'll say *like what* and I'll have to specify and I'll have already spent the casual-opener capital and now we're in a clarification conversation, which is not a conversation with momentum. Discarded.

Option three: nothing, let him lead. This is strategically sound. This is also cowardice dressed up as strategy, and I know it, and I'm noting it, and I'm noting that I'm noting it, and none of this is helping.

Option four—the real one, the one I'd rehearsed three times this week in the shower, once while brushing my teeth—was *I've been hoping we'd end up in the same group.* Simple. True. Devastating in its openness. In the version I rehearsed, his face did something good when I said it—not dramatic, not movie-dramatic, just a small recalibration, something that said *oh*, something that moved things forward from whatever they were.

I did not say option four.

That one I had already discarded before I walked into class. Obviously.

Instead I was sitting with the silence when the smell hit me—sharp and chemical and distinctly custodial—drifting from somewhere down the hall, or maybe the storage closet by the window, or possibly from the floor directly beneath our chairs, which someone had recently mopped with something that smelled exactly like the inside of a swimming pool locker room. I noticed it. I noticed that I was noticing it. I wondered if Marcus was noticing it. I wondered if he was attributing it to anywhere in particular, specifically whether he was attributing it to me, whether this was something that happened—whether cleaning products could somehow transfer to clothing, whether I had walked through a mopped hallway and absorbed it without realizing, whether I now smelled like institutional cleaning fluid, whether Marcus was sitting next to me right now thinking *she smells like institutional cleaning fluid* while I was sitting here thinking—

I pulled myself back.

The smell was from the floor. Or the closet. It didn't matter. It wasn't me. This was insane. I was insane.

*So I was thinking,* Marcus said, pulling the project rubric across the desk so it sat between us, *we could probably split the research and then—*

*Yeah,* I said.

He looked up.

I had said *yeah* before he finished the sentence. I had said *yeah* at the comma. I had agreed with the first half of a proposal whose second half I had not yet heard.

*—combine it at the end?* he finished.

*Yeah,* I said again, and then because two *yeahs* required some kind of supplementary action I added, *that makes sense,* which was true, it did make sense, splitting research was a reasonable and standard approach to a research project, I had contributed a confirmation of basic logic to this conversation and that was what I had to show for the last forty-five seconds.

He nodded, wrote something on the rubric, and slid it eight inches toward me.

I examined the rubric as though it contained information I had not read three times before class. I examined the rubric with the focused attention of a person for whom this rubric was the most interesting document in the room, which it was not, because the most interesting thing in the room had just slid it across the desk and was now reading something in his notebook, and his handwriting from this angle was almost illegible and I was not trying to read it, I was studying the rubric.

Was my voice too flat? It had sounded flat. Not flat like bored—or maybe flat like bored, I couldn't be certain, I was on the inside of my own voice when I produced it, I didn't have access to what it sounded like arriving—flat like I was performing calm, which was different from calm, and possibly detectable as different. Did I sound like I was trying to sound unbothered. Did *trying to sound unbothered* have a sound.

I became aware of my face.

This is a thing that happens, where you become aware of your face in the way you might become aware of a word you've said too many times—suddenly it seems wrong, constructed, foreign, a collection of parts that you are now operating manually because the automatic mode has been suspended. I was aware of my mouth. I was aware of my eyes. I became aware that I had not blinked in what might have been several seconds and I blinked, but consciously, which is not how blinking is supposed to work, and now I was blinking consciously and wondering if conscious blinking was perceptibly different from unconscious blinking and whether the difference was visible.

I was also smiling—I could feel this—a neutral, vaguely attentive smile, the smile of someone engaged in a collaborative academic endeavor, but I had lost track of whether this was the right amount of smile. Too much and I seemed eager, possibly unhinged. Not enough and I seemed hostile, or bored, or like the mood I was trying not to seem like I was in. I tried recalibrating. I was recalibrating my smile manually. I had reached the part of the class period where I was manually operating my own face.

*The thermal conductivity section is actually kind of interesting,* Marcus said.

I looked up.

He was looking at the rubric, tilting it slightly like the angle would clarify something. *Like, whether the surface absorbs or reflects—it varies so much more than I thought. I went down a fairly long road on this yesterday.*

And there was something about the phrase *a fairly long road*—the precision of the self-deprecation, the acknowledgment that he had been interested enough to fall into it—that made me say, without running it first:

*The applications list goes on forever. Aerospace, building materials, even food packaging—*

*Right, the food packaging one is weird.*

*It's so weird.* I looked at the rubric again, then back at him. *They use aerogel in, like, industrial ovens and also to keep a sandwich at a specific temperature in transit, and those seem like they should be two different conversations.*

He laughed—a real one, short, through the nose—and said, *that's a great observation, actually, I might steal that for the intro.*

I said he could, and he said thanks, and I said it was fine or *of course* or something, I wasn't fully tracking the language anymore because I was having a conversation, an actual one, where I was saying things and they were landing and he was responding to the things I said as though they were things worth responding to, and for a moment I was just—there, in the conversation, two people talking about aerogel, and it was simple, and nothing was difficult.

I noticed this.

The moment I noticed it, it ended.

The naturalness evaporated and I was back inside the observation booth, watching myself talk, watching him respond, watching my hands (still on the desk, which was still the right decision), tracking the register of my voice (slightly more animated now, which, was that good, was that readable, was the animation visible as evidence of something) and the moment that had been easy was now a memory I was already filing and reviewing, and the filing and reviewing had taken its place, and we were in different territory again.

The conversation continued. We assigned sections. He wrote things down. I wrote things down. He said something about meeting in the library before next class and I said *sure, that works,* and he said *cool,* and Mr. Paulsen circulated with some question about our approach and we answered it, together, and Marcus's answer and my answer worked off each other like adjacent pieces of something, and none of this was bad, objectively none of this was any kind of disaster. The disaster I had prepared for—the horrible silence, the visible wrongness, the moment where something I said landed with a bruising wrong-note thud—did not materialize. Marcus was fine. The class was fine. I was, in all visible external metrics, a person who had participated normally in a group project.

The bell went.

Chairs again, bags again, the practiced choreography of ending. I capped my pen. I slid the rubric into my folder.

*See you Thursday,* Marcus said.

*See you Thursday,* I said—and here is the thing about this, here is the small catastrophe inside the harmless exchange: I had a half-second before I said it where I considered whether *see you Thursday* was the correct response, whether *sounds good* was better, whether either of them carried a valence I didn't intend, whether *see you Thursday* as a direct echo of what he'd said was either natural mirroring or weird repetition, and I chose it anyway, and then it was gone, out, done, irreversible, and he was already turning toward the door.

I walked out.

The hallway was loud and I moved through it and I went over the session the way you go over a test after the fact—not to change anything, because nothing could be changed, but because the accounting needed to happen regardless. The *yeah* before the comma. The smile recalibration. The laugh at the aerogel moment, which was real, which meant something or meant nothing. *See you Thursday,* and whether it landed right.

And already—already, with three days between now and Thursday, already before I reached my locker—I was thinking about how to open.

Not too eager. Not too casual. Something that picked up the aerogel thread without seeming like I'd been thinking about the aerogel thread, something that moved us forward, something that cost almost nothing, something in the middle, something I would rehearse until it sounded natural and then say wrong and then spend the walk home examining.

Something. I'd find something.

I had three days.
