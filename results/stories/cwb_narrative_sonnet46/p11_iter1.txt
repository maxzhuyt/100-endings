# Overthinking It

The worksheet says *Ecosystem Dynamics*, which is either a unit on food webs or a metaphor for what is happening inside my body right now, and I am already three exchanges into a conversation I haven't started yet—the one where I say something and he says something and it goes the way it goes in the version I've been running since Tuesday—when I register, again, the squeak of the projector cart's left wheel, which Ms. Okafor has been dragging across the same linoleum since September, and I think: that is exactly the kind of detail a person notices when she is, clinically speaking, paying attention to the wrong things. I know I am doing this. I want you to understand that I know.

He sits down.

Not next to me, exactly—we're assigned as partners, so it's more accurate to say he sits down *here*, in the chair that has been waiting for him, which I have not been watching. The worksheet lands between us. He flips it over to check the back, which means there's a back, which means I haven't been paying attention to anything I should be paying attention to, and I think: say something. Say one of the things.

The options, ranked:

**Option One** (*low-risk, low-reward*): *Do you want to do the vocabulary section first or the diagram?* Pros: practical, task-adjacent, completely deniable as an attempt at human connection. Cons: reveals that I have thought about vocabulary sections.

**Option Two** (*medium-risk, possible-reward*): *I actually read ahead on this one—ecosystems are kind of fascinating when they're not on a test.* Pros: implies intellectual life, creates opening for him to agree or disagree, either of which is conversation. Cons: the word *fascinating* will leave my mouth and then exist in the air between us, and I will have to live with that.

**Option Three** (*high-risk, unclear ceiling*): *I feel like we've never actually talked, which is strange, since we've— * and then something that requires knowing how to finish a sentence that starts with *since we've*, which I do not know right now, so.

He uncaps a pencil. I say nothing. He draws a small circle in the margin, for no reason, the way people do when they're waiting for something to start, and I think about the circle for what is probably too long, and then Ms. Okafor's projector squeaks, and the unit begins, and the options expire.

---

I say: *Oh, I have a highlighter if you need one.*

It's not on the list. It comes out of some reflex I didn't authorize, some pre-verbal department that operates without my approval, and it's fine—grammatically, tonally, it is fine—he says *thanks, I'm good*, and he smiles, which is also fine, and then I spend the next forty-five seconds conducting a full incident report on my own sentence.

*Oh*—unnecessary, slightly breathless, implies I have been waiting for a moment to offer the highlighter, which I have not, except possibly I have.

*I have a highlighter*—accurate.

*If you need one*—appropriate qualifier. Polite. Leaves him an exit. The exit was important.

He said: *Thanks, I'm good*—and then either *though* or *so*, and I would know which one if the projector hadn't squeaked exactly then, in the half-second between *I'm good* and whatever came after it, and now I don't know, and the difference between *thanks, I'm good though* and *thanks, I'm good, so* is—I'm aware that this is—

It matters. It doesn't matter. It matters to me.

---

He's reading the worksheet. There's a diagram of a carbon cycle that he's frowning at slightly, which means I have a moment, which means I go somewhere else.

What I go to: seventh grade, Ms. Patel's English class, the day she asked us what our favorite book was and I said *Holes* because it was the honest answer, and then Connor McAllister said he'd never met anyone who read for fun, and I said, *I don't read for fun, it's just— it's just required reading sometimes*, and Ms. Patel was nice about it, she moved on, and nobody else probably remembers it. I replay it maybe once a month. The specific sound of the word *Holes* in my own voice, flat and deliberate, a word that became an answer became a sentence became, in the precise second after I said it, the funniest thing anyone had ever said. Connor McAllister doesn't know my name anymore. I think about this more than I think about most things that have actually happened to me recently, which I understand is a ratio problem, but understanding it doesn't make the ratio different.

The thing about seventh-grade English is that it's still happening. I know that sounds wrong. What I mean is: it's still a thing that happened, it's just happening in a room I can't leave, and I can visit whenever I want, which means I visit whenever I don't want to.

He looks up from the worksheet.

I look at the worksheet.

---

He says something about the carbon cycle being circular, which: yes, that's what a cycle is, and he knows that, he's being—I think he's being a little funny about it, observational, like he finds it mildly absurd that we're being asked to diagram the obvious, and I think yes, that's the correct response, which means—I could—there is a version of right now where I say—

*I know, it's like being graded on drawing a clock.*

I get as far as *I* in my head. As far as the specific feeling of opening my mouth with something true in it.

The projector squeaks.

He finishes writing something in the first box of the carbon cycle.

I write *CO₂* in my own box and the moment is past, and there is a specific word for what I felt in that second that I am not going to use out loud, and that's fine, and I'm fine.

---

Here is what I understand, sitting here:

I am not bad at talking. I am too good at it, in a place where it doesn't count. I have hosted entire conversations in the interior of my head—eloquent, reciprocal conversations, conducted at the pace of real speech, with pauses and clarifications and the good kind of interruptions—and that proficiency has made me worse at the version that exists outside my body. I have practiced too much in the wrong space. I have spent so long drafting the thing I will say that the moment has no room left in it for me to say anything.

I understand this. I understand it correctly, I think. I understand it with the part of my brain that is good at understanding things.

The carbon cycle waits.

He is drawing an arrow from the atmosphere box to the ocean box, and his handwriting is not particularly good, and I am watching his handwriting and I am understanding the complete mechanism of my own problem, and the understanding sits there, accurate and useless, like a very good map of a place I cannot leave.

I have done this before. Not with him specifically—with everyone, with every version of a moment that required me to say something real at the real speed of things. I know the shape of what comes next: the project will end, I will go home, I will have the conversation in my room at 11pm, I will be tremendous at it, and it will count for nothing at all.

The projector squeaks, and I think: that wheel. That wheel has needed oil since September. And somehow that seems—I don't know what it seems. I just notice it.

---

Ms. Okafor says five minutes, and we agree on things.

He will handle the diagram. I'll handle the vocabulary. We need poster board, and I say I can bring it, and he says he can bring markers, and I say any color is fine, and he says he has blue somewhere, and I say blue is good, and he says *cool, blue it is*, and that is—that's the conversation we had. I write *poster board* in my notebook so I don't forget.

He packs his pencil away. He says *see you Thursday*, and I say *Thursday*, which is not a complete sentence but seems to land okay, and then he is not sitting next to me anymore, so I look at the worksheet, which is mostly done.

In the first box of the carbon cycle, I wrote *CO₂*.

In the margin, I didn't write anything, but the almost-word is there—it's been there since the moment before the projector squeaked and I let it go. It's a small word. It's the plainest possible sentence. I'm carrying it home now in the way I carry things: carefully, in both hands, taking up too much room in my own chest.

Blue, I think. He said blue is fine.

That's what we decided.
