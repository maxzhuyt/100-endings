# The Barley Men

I have survived eleven years in the arena by counting things. This is not something I have told anyone, not the lanista, not the men I sleep beside, not the physician who examines my joints each month the way a horse-trader examines fetlocks. They know me as Castus, the Thracian, which is not my name and not my origin but is what I am called when the crowd calls for me. What I am, privately, is a man who counts.

I count the stones in the ceiling from my pallet before dawn. There are forty-three, and the number has not changed in four years, and this soothes me in a way I cannot explain and will not try to. I count the steps to the training ground. I count the other men's breaths when the barracks go quiet and the city beyond the wall goes on making its city sounds, which are also numerous and also, in the darkest hours, countable: a dog, a cart's wheel on stone, someone's voice lifting and then cut off. I lie on my back and I count until the numbers arrange themselves into something like order, and then sometimes I sleep.

The thing that undoes me is beeswax. The smell of it. I am a practical man—I have stayed alive by being practical, by reading the angle of a shoulder before it commits to a strike, by never spending myself early in a fight, by eating the barley they give us and not complaining about the barley and sleeping when I can sleep—but the smell of beeswax arrives and I am briefly elsewhere, briefly undone, briefly not Castus the Thracian or anyone who has ever held a sica in his hand. It does not last. Thirty seconds, perhaps, perhaps less. Then I return. But I cannot prevent the leaving when it comes.

---

This morning, like most mornings, I am the first awake.

The barracks hold twenty-two men at present count, though this changes. Macer the retiarius has been coughing since the cold came, a wet sound low in his chest, and the physician has been watching him with the expression that means he is calculating something. Across from me, the Nubian whose name none of us can pronounce correctly sleeps on his side with one arm thrown over his face, enormous even in sleep, the muscles of his back like something carved and then softened slightly into flesh. He snores. He has always snored. In the beginning it kept me awake and now it is part of the count, a rhythm I have built other rhythms around.

The morning porridge comes before light. I eat it standing near the cooking fire, the way I always eat it, feeling the warmth of the bowl through both palms, which in winter is a meaningful warmth and in summer is a different kind of warmth and always I count the swallows. Barley and water and sometimes a scraping of fat from whatever the lanista's household ate the night before. I have eaten this porridge for eleven years and I no longer taste it, which is fine. Taste is not what it is for.

Duilius is already arguing with someone near the water basin. Duilius is always already arguing. He is a dimachaerus, fights with two swords, and has the personality that method produces or else was born with the personality and the method is its natural expression. I do not know which. He is arguing about something involving the new man from Gaul, who stands apart from the argument looking at his own hands, which are very large and which he holds in front of him the way a man holds things that confuse him.

The physician crouches beside Macer's pallet, pressing two fingers below his ear. Macer is awake and looking at the ceiling with the particular blankness of a man who is not looking at the ceiling.

I count his breaths. They are not even.

---

I do this between my morning meal and the training ground, and I do it quietly, because it is not something for which there is a Latin word that doesn't sound like prayer, and I am not interested in it sounding like prayer.

First the left hand: thumb, forefinger, middle finger, each one pressed briefly to the back of the other hand in sequence. Three. Then the right. Three more. I have done this since before the arena—since the ship, to be precise, since the third morning on the ship when I discovered that my hands were mine regardless of circumstance and that this fact, if held carefully, was sufficient to keep me from going somewhere I could not come back from. I still don't know what I believe about Nemesis or any of the gods the men invoke. But Macer once told me Nemesis keeps the accounts, balances what comes around against what has gone, and I thought: I understand that. I understand the keeping of accounts.

I touch each finger in sequence.

I am not praying. I am counting.

---

The lanista's name is Tiberius Marcellius Sura and his voice, when he speaks to the men, has a particular sound that I recognized within the first week of being here: the sound of a man who values us at exactly our market price and no further, which is not cruelty but is also not its opposite. When he speaks to a sponsor or a sponsor's man, his voice acquires a second register underneath the first, something almost melodic, something that belongs to a younger man who wanted something, and every year the gap between the two voices narrows slightly, which tells me something about what money does to a person over time.

Today he comes at midmorning with a man none of us knows.

The stranger wears good sandals—intricate, with leather worked soft as cloth, the kind that have never touched unpaved ground. He is not young. He has the smooth forehead of someone whose anxieties are managed by other people and the careful, acquisitive gaze of someone who manages other people in return. He moves through the training ground the way men move through markets: looking for quality, cataloguing, pricing.

The lanista is speaking to him in the lower register.

I am going through the footwork patterns we have drilled since my second month here, forty-three steps in a fixed sequence, and I do not stop to watch them come through the gate. But I watch. The stranger pauses near Duilius, who performs without being asked, two blades describing arcs that catch the light in a way that is designed to catch light, and the stranger nods in the slow deliberate way of a man confirming something he already believed. His hands are clasped behind his back.

The new Gaul is standing near the wall, where the lanista's man told him to stand and where he has stood for an hour without complaint. The stranger's gaze moves to him and lingers. The Gaul's wrists are still marked. Not healed yet, the rope-mark or the chain-mark, whichever it was. He holds his enormous hands in front of him and looks back at the stranger with an expression that is impossible to read, not because it conceals emotion but because it is entirely outside the vocabulary of emotion I know, something older than that, something that came off a boat a different way than I came off a boat.

The lanista's voice lifts slightly. A figure, perhaps. An arrangement.

The Gaul looks at his hands.

I count the steps. Forty-three. Begin again.

---

There was beeswax in my mother's house because she kept bees, four hives at the field's edge, and she harvested them each autumn and rendered the comb and used the wax for everything: the preservation of leather, the smoothing of thread, the making of small lamps that burned with a clean, sweet smell throughout the winter. I was the one who helped her carry the frames. This is a factual thing I know about my life. There was a field. There were four hives. The hives were positioned at the field's eastern edge because she believed, and she had this from her mother, that bees preferred morning light for their returning.

I do not miss the field. I do not permit myself to miss things in the way that has a name, because the things that have that name are also the things that, if held directly, unmake a man. I have seen it happen. There was a fighter here two years ago, a Samnite, who was excellent and who one day received a letter—someone had found a way to send him a letter, I still don't know who or how—and after the letter he was no longer excellent. He made decisions in the sand that were not decisions, that were something underneath decisions. He died in March. It was not a good death, the particular way he died, and the crowd's sound was not the sound they make for a good death.

I carried the frames because my mother's hands ached in the cold and I was twelve and my hands did not yet ache. She wore a cloth over her face. I didn't, because I liked the sound the bees made, which was also something I counted: how many, and from which direction. I was always counting. I did not know then that this was a skill with applications.

There was also a dog. I have not thought about the dog in some years. He was not a remarkable dog. He was brown and he followed me.

The beeswax smell does this: it takes me back before I decide to go, and holds me there for less than a minute in a room that is not a room, with a sound that is not a sound anymore, and then releases me. I do not know why it is beeswax rather than something else. The mind makes its connections without asking permission and then sends you the invoice.

I return. I am always able to return.

This is the thing I hold most carefully: that I am always able to return.

---

In the afternoon they let us into the small courtyard that faces nothing—a wall on all sides, the sky above, a sound of Rome pressed through stone—and this is the hour I prefer above all hours because the shape of it is simple: the sky is the sky, the wall is the wall, and I can stand in the corner where the afternoon light comes last and feel the warmth of it at the angle it arrives, which in this season is low and reddened, and count the shadows moving.

Children somewhere. Running. The slap of their feet on stone, which I can hear over the city's other sounds, which are the sounds of a city that means to last forever and will last, I think, past anything I will see.

I think about the fight coming. It is twelve days away, perhaps fourteen—the lanista has given us a number that rounds itself differently depending on negotiations I'm not part of. I think about the fight the way I always think about a fight coming: practically, technically, with the same attention I give the ceiling stones. What I know about my opponent, which is almost nothing. What I know about the sand's behavior in the afternoon games, which is that it is loose near the walls where the shadow has kept it cool and compacted toward the center. What I know about my own body, which I know very well: the right knee that tightens in cold weather and must be walked out before any serious movement, the shoulder that was reset four years ago and is fine, is completely fine, but which I watch with a private vigilance I don't entirely trust.

I know that I want to see one more morning with the barley porridge and the Nubian's snoring and Duilius already arguing and the ceiling's forty-three stones arranged the way they are arranged. I know that this is not a large want, not a wanting that any of the poets whose phrases circulate secondhand through the literate men in this building would find worthy of the name. It is small and specific and probably the only want I carry that connects directly to the truth.

The light moves across the wall. A long orange band, narrowing.

The children's footsteps recede, replaced by a vendor's voice, by a cart, by the vast undifferentiated sound of the city restating itself.

I watch the light until it's gone from the stone. The stone is pale in shade, a different pale than it is in light, and the difference between the two is the difference between one kind of cold and another kind of cold, and I don't know what to do with that except notice it.

I count the seconds until the evening bell.

---

At night, before sleep, I do it again.

Left hand: thumb, forefinger, middle finger. Three. Right hand: three more.

Macer is coughing across the room, softer now, which might mean better and might mean the other thing. The Nubian settles into his breathing. Duilius has stopped talking. The new Gaul is on the empty pallet near the wall and lies on his back with his eyes open, which I know because I can see enough in this dark to identify what open eyes reflect.

He will close them, eventually.

The city sounds come through the stone and I begin to count them: a dog, a cart, a voice lifted and held and then absorbed by everything around it.

I press my fingers in order, quietly, so no one hears.

Three, and three.

Still here.
