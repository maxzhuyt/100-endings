# The HOA Board Will Now Come to Order

## I.

The violation notice had been affixed to the handrail with blue painter's tape, the kind that promises a clean removal and usually lies. Meredith Chen peeled it off carefully anyway, because the railing was new and she had watched the contractor sand it twice, and she would not allow even the adhesive residue of the Sycamore Glen Homeowners' Association to mar the wood her husband would grip every morning for whatever number of mornings remained available to him.

The notice was two pages, stapled. The header featured the Association's logo—a clipart oak tree, though there were no oaks in Sycamore Glen, and, for that matter, no sycamores—and the text beneath it cited Article IV, Section 12, Subsection (c)(iii) of the Sycamore Glen Architectural Standards and Covenant Compliance Code, pertaining to "Exterior Structural Modifications Requiring Prior Review and Approval by the Architectural Compliance Committee." The ramp, which ran six feet from the front door to the walkway at a grade of precisely one inch per twelve inches, which met the ADA guidelines Meredith had printed and taped to the refrigerator three months ago, which Leonard needed because his left leg no longer did what his brain asked of it reliably enough to manage the two porch steps that had been there when they'd bought the house eleven years prior—this ramp was in violation. The daily fine was fifty dollars. It had been in effect for two days. The sincerity of the Architectural Compliance Committee's regret was noted in a subordinate clause.

From inside the house, Leonard's voice, slightly muffled, accompanied by a sound she had learned not to name but which was the sound of a man's hand sliding on a wall he was using for balance: "Mer? Who's at the door?"

She folded the notice into the back pocket of her jeans.

"Nobody," she said. "Just the wind."

## II.

Meredith Chen had spent forty-one years as an actuary for Midland Casualty & Surety, a mid-tier insurance company whose regional offices occupied two floors of a building in Dayton that smelled, in every season, faintly of microwaved popcorn. She had begun in 1981 as one of three women in the department and had ended in 2022 as the department's senior risk analyst, a title that meant she was the person you consulted when you needed to know, with decimal-place certainty, how likely a thing was to go wrong. She had been, by all accounts, very good at this. Risk, in her hands, became a domesticated animal—still dangerous, technically, but on a leash, quantified and kenneled in a spreadsheet.

She had married Leonard Briggs in 1987 at the Hamilton County courthouse because neither of them had wanted a fuss. He was a high school band director who had once, on their third date, watched her calculate the tip on a dinner bill to the cent and said, with a straight face, that it was the most romantic thing anyone had ever done for him. He was the man who, on a Tuesday evening in 1994, had interrupted her review of a mortality table to lead her to the kitchen window, where the sky was doing something absurd in orange and violet, and when she'd said, "Atmospheric refraction; perfectly normal conditions," he had looked at her as though she were the sunset and said, "Meredith, that is not, in fact, an acceptable risk of atmospheric refraction. That is Tuesday trying to kill us with beauty, and I need you to let it."

She had let it.

The diagnosis came in March of the previous year. She would say the name once—progressive supranuclear palsy—and after that would refer to it only through its effects: the ramp, the schedule of medications arrayed on the kitchen counter like a tiny pharmacological skyline, the way she had trained herself to sleep so lightly that his breathing was, all night, the shallow river she monitored from the opposite bank. Early retirement had been for travel. They had reservations in Lisbon that she had not yet canceled because canceling them would be an act of actuarial clarity she was not ready to perform.

The HOA handbook was a spiral-bound document, sixty-two pages, printed on the kind of paper that resists highlighting. She had never read it. She suspected nobody had—that it existed in that category of American documents, alongside software license agreements and the terms of service for everything, that derived their authority precisely from being unread. But now it was open on the kitchen table, and she had annotated it in three colors of ink: red for provisions that were ambiguous, blue for provisions that contradicted other provisions, and green—a color she had chosen deliberately, the color of money, the color of go—for provisions that could be used.

She was performing due diligence. It was important to her that she understand this as due diligence.

The green annotations outnumbered the others by the third reading.

## III.

Claudette Barnes lived two streets over, on Larkspur Lane, in a house distinguished from its neighbors by the begonias—roses, usually, were the Sycamore Glen standard, but Claudette had been a woman of specific tastes since long before the neighborhood existed—and by the garden gnome. It was ceramic, fourteen inches tall, wearing a blue cap and holding a fishing pole. It stood among the begonias with the fixed smile of a creature that had survived eleven violation notices and owed its continued existence to the stubbornness of the woman who watered around it every morning.

Claudette had been a court stenographer for twenty-three years in the Montgomery County Common Pleas Court, a career she did not discuss in detail except to say it had ended "over a matter of editorial principle." The matter, as the neighborhood understood it, was this: during a particularly acrimonious divorce proceeding, Claudette had, in the official transcript, inserted—within parentheses, as though they were stage directions—the phrases *(husband is lying)*, *(wife knows he is lying)*, *(judge also appears to know he is lying)*, and, most fatally, *(the court stenographer wishes to note for the record that this man has the moral architecture of a wet napkin)*. She had been removed from her position. The judge, privately, had sent flowers.

Meredith sat in Claudette's kitchen and placed the violation notice on the table between them.

Claudette read it once, turned it over to confirm there was nothing on the back, and said, "Unauthorized ramp."

"Unauthorized ramp."

"For Leonard."

"For Leonard."

Claudette looked out the window at the gnome, which stared back with its painted, unblinking gaze. "What do you need?"

"Someone who takes good notes."

Gerald Fiske answered his phone on the first ring, which was not unusual—Gerald answered everything on the first ring, opened all mail on the day it arrived, and had once, according to neighborhood legend, filed his taxes on January 3rd. He had run for city council in 2014 on a platform he'd described, without irony, as "procedural hygiene," a concept that involved the strict enforcement of meeting protocols, the proper formatting of municipal agendas, and what he called "the spiritual discipline of the point of order." He had received 340 votes, which he considered a mandate, since 340 people had independently endorsed the foundational importance of parliamentary procedure.

He had no grievance with the HOA. His lawn was regulation height. His house was a compliant shade of approved beige. His mailbox was positioned at the prescribed distance from the curb. Gerald was not a man who suffered under rules; he was a man who found in them the same consolation that others found in prayer.

"The HOA bylaws," Meredith said, "specify that a special meeting can be called by petition of fifteen percent of homeowners in good standing."

Gerald was quiet for a moment. She could hear, in the background, what she was fairly certain was a pencil being sharpened.

"You've read the bylaws," he said.

"Three times."

"Article III, Section 7."

"Section 7, paragraph 2. Fifteen percent of homeowners in good standing, submitted in writing to the board president no fewer than fourteen days before the proposed meeting date."

Another silence. The pencil sharpener stopped.

"Go on," Gerald said.

"The current board was elected under a provision that requires annual elections with a minimum thirty-day notice period. I can find no evidence that such notice was provided for any of the last four election cycles."

She could hear him breathing. It was the breathing of a man who has just been told that the cathedral he'd admired all his life has a hidden room.

"Mrs. Chen," he said. "I'd like to see those bylaws."

The book club met on Tuesdays at rotating houses, which this week meant Bev Kowalski's living room, which smelled of the zinfandel that was already open on the coffee table despite the meeting's having been called for seven and it being six forty-three. They were theoretically reading *Middlemarch*. They had been theoretically reading *Middlemarch* since October. Linda Tran was on chapter four. Bev claimed to be on chapter thirty but could not describe any event after chapter twelve. Eleanor Flood, the quietest member—a retired something-or-other in information technology whose precise former job title nobody could remember because Eleanor deflected questions about herself with the gentle, practiced opacity of a woman who had spent thirty years behind a screen—Eleanor had finished *Middlemarch* twice already and was rereading it a third time but didn't say so, because Eleanor did not say things unless they required saying.

Meredith brought the violation notice. She also brought, on a single sheet of paper, the running total of the daily fine, which was now at $750 and climbing.

She set both on the coffee table, next to the zinfandel.

She said: "They're fining me fifty dollars a day for the ramp Leonard needs to get out of his own house."

The room did what rooms do when a thing that has been known privately is spoken aloud: it contracted. Six women stared at the paper. Linda Tran picked it up and read the section numbers and the subsection clauses and the clipart oak tree. She set it down and picked up her wine.

"I've been fined twice for my window boxes," she said.

"My patio umbrella," Bev said. "Wrong shade of green."

"They sent me a letter about my doormat," said Janet Okoye, who had been in the club the longest and had never, in all those years, raised her voice above the conversational. "My *doormat*, Meredith. It said 'Welcome' in a font they considered 'inconsistent with community aesthetic standards.'"

Silence again. But a different kind—a room-shaped silence, fitted to its container, pressurized.

Meredith did not pitch a plan. She did not describe the bylaw annotations or mention Gerald's name or use the word *coalition*. She let the violation notice sit on the table, accruing its fifty dollars a day in everyone's imaginations, a piece of bureaucratic origami that folded itself into whatever shape each woman's resentment required.

Eleanor Flood set down her wine glass.

"I'll bring the projector," she said.

## IV.

Doug Whitmore arrived on a Saturday morning with banana bread wrapped in aluminum foil and the unhurried gait of a man who believed himself to be performing a kindness. He was sixty-four years old, broad in the chest and thinning on top, and he wore a fleece vest over a button-down shirt with the sleeves rolled to the forearms, which was what he always wore, in every season, as though he had found the Platonic form of neighborly attire and seen no reason to deviate. He had been president of the Sycamore Glen Homeowners' Association for nine years. Before that, he had served as vice president for three, and before that, as chair of the Architectural Compliance Committee for four, a tenure during which he had reviewed 412 modification requests and approved, by his own accounting, "well over half," a statistic he cited with the evident pride of a man who considered a 51 percent approval rate to be the hallmark of judicious governance.

"Meredith," he said, standing on the ramp he had cited her for, holding the banana bread in both hands like a sacrament. "I just wanted to stop by. I heard about Leonard's—well. I heard it's been a tough stretch."

She let him in.

He sat at the kitchen table, the same table where the annotated bylaws had been spread two days earlier and were now locked in the bedroom filing cabinet. He accepted coffee. He admired the kitchen. He said the backsplash tile was "a really nice choice" with the automatic aesthetic assessment of a man who had spent nearly two decades evaluating the exteriors of other people's homes and had allowed the habit to metastasize inward.

"Listen," he said, and leaned forward with his elbows on the table, which brought his face into the range where sincerity becomes almost tactile. "I know the ramp situation has been stressful. I want you to know—and I mean this—I wish I could just wave a wand and make it go away. But we have a process, and the process is there to protect everyone. You, me, all of us. If I make an exception for one person—even for a reason this sympathetic—then the next person wants an exception, and the person after that, and before you know it..." He opened his hands, a gesture meant to indicate entropy, the collapse of ordered systems, the HOA's equivalent of the heat death of the universe. "It's really in the committee's hands at this point. But I'm pushing for them to expedite. I really am."

He squeezed her arm as he stood to leave. Not her hand—her arm, just above the elbow, a gesture that was neither too familiar nor too formal, a gesture he had clearly performed many times before, calibrated to communicate concern without presumption.

"We'll get this figured out," he said. "That's what our little community is for."

Meredith stood in the doorway holding the banana bread and watched him walk down the ramp—*Leonard's* ramp, down which Leonard had not yet managed to walk because the physical therapist came on Mondays and Wednesdays and the ramp had been finished on a Thursday and then the fine had started on a Saturday, and the sequence of these days, set against the sequence of Leonard's diminishing capabilities, was a calculus she performed every morning before her feet touched the floor.

Doug waved from the bottom of the ramp.

He was not performing.

That was the thing that sat in her chest like a stone dropped from a modest height. He genuinely believed every word. The process, the committee, the little community—it was not a script; it was his actual cosmology. He lived inside a world where fence heights and setback requirements and approved exterior paint colors were the loadbearing walls of civilization, and he maintained them with the sincere conviction that their collapse would bring ruin, and he could not see—it was not that he chose not to see; he was *structurally unable to see*—that the rules he loved were, at this moment, requiring a man with a deteriorating brain to be carried down two porch steps by his sixty-seven-year-old wife.

She set the banana bread on the kitchen counter. She did not eat it. She did not throw it away.

From the bedroom, Leonard's voice: "Was that Doug?"

"That was Doug."

"What'd he want?"

She looked at the banana bread. "To help," she said, and this was, in its way, the second lie, because it was also, in its way, true.

## V.

Gerald Fiske had commandeered Meredith's dining room table and covered it with documents arranged in the taxonomic precision of a man who believed that organization was a moral act. HOA budgets from the past fifteen years, obtained through a combination of legitimate records requests (Gerald's), the Ohio Public Records Act as applied to any entity with a common-area maintenance obligation (Meredith's research), and a method Eleanor Flood described only as "I found them," delivered with the flat affect of a woman who had no intention of elaborating. The budgets were cross-referenced with bank statements. The bank statements were cross-referenced with vendor invoices. The vendor invoices were cross-referenced with Gerald's index cards, which were arranged in chronological order along the table's edge, each one bearing a date, an amount, and a notation in Gerald's microscopic handwriting.
