# The Commissar's Confession

## I.

The ink is the Committee's standard issue, an iron gall mixture that goes down wet-black and dries to the color of old blood, and I have used it for eleven months now without once permitting myself to notice this. The blotter is linen rag. The desk is oak, requisitioned from the Hôtel de Béthune, and there is a gouge along the front edge where someone, in whatever haste attended its confiscation, brought an axe handle against the lip. The paper smells of tallow—this particular tallow, the Committee's candles, rendered from a chandler on the Rue Saint-Honoré who supplies the Public Safety offices at a rate I happen to know because I requisitioned his contract in March—and the smell has settled into everything, the curtains, the plaster, the fibers of every document that passes through this building, so that to read a decree of the Committee is also to smell the fat of cattle processed two streets from where Danton died.

I am reading the directive. I am noting the names.

Seventeen names. The Committee's clerk has a steady hand—Gérard, his name is, a man of fifty who worked for the Parlement before and who now copies death lists with the same stiff wrist he once used for land deeds—and each name is set in a column against which I have been asked to place my initials, confirming identification, confirming address, confirming the substance of the denunciations that brought each name to this page.

I know every name on the list because I put them there.

Not on this page. Not in Gérard's hand. But in reports I have written over eleven months, reports that traveled from my desk to the Committee's files, reports in which I identified, described, and located members of a network I was trusted to infiltrate—a network I was sent among as an agent of the Republic, with tricolor sash and the Committee's own seal on my credentials—and which I have, for eleven months, attempted to preserve by the method of appearing to destroy it.

I initial the first name. Bertrand Léon, age forty-four, printer's assistant. I recruited him in October. I told him the moderate republicans needed his press skills for an underground pamphlet series and I believed, when I told him this, that I was telling the truth, that the pamphlets would reach the sections and the sections would remember what the Republic had meant to be before the Committee made it into an instrument of subtraction. I taught Bertrand the protocol for dead drops. I taught him which streets were watched. I wrote his name in a report to the Committee six weeks later, listing him as a minor associate, a man of no consequence, someone the surveillance could safely deprioritize.

I initial the second name. I initial the third.

My hand moves. The pen is a goose quill, cut badly—Gérard's work, he over-trims the nib—and it scratches. The sound is the sound of this office, of every office in this building, the industrious scratching of quills that are cutting names into the Republic's ledger of the dead. I initial the fourth name, the fifth, the sixth. At the ninth name I pause—not because of feeling, not because of any sentiment that would be legible as hesitation to an observer, but because the pen needs ink. I dip. I continue. I am aware of the window to my left, the light coming in gray and wet—it has rained for three days, the gutters on the Rue Saint-Honoré are running—and I am aware of my own breathing and I am aware that I am performing awareness of my own breathing, that I am narrating to myself the calm of my own body as though calm were something that could be manufactured by description, as though the sentence *I am breathing steadily* could make it true.

I am at the fourteenth name and the fourteenth name is wrong.

Geneviève Morel. Except the directive reads *Geneviève Moret*, and the *t* is not Gérard's error. It is mine. I see it—I see the shape of the mistake—with the particular clarity of a man recognizing his own handwriting in a document he did not write. Eight months ago I submitted a report on the printer's widow, the woman whose courtyard I had first entered in the autumn of 1791, the woman whose ink-stained hands had given me the pamphlet that made me believe law could replace vengeance, and in that report I misspelled her name. Moret for Morel. A single letter. I did it with purpose. It was a small, stupid act of protection—the kind of gesture a man makes when he has no power and mistakes orthography for mercy—because a misspelled name is a name that does not perfectly match the parish records, the Section rolls, the tax ledgers, and a clerk checking denunciations against civic records might find the discrepancy and set the file aside for clarification, and clarification takes time, and time, in the year II of the Republic, is the only currency that purchases life.

The misspelling has been copied faithfully. Gérard, with his steady hand, his land-deed wrist, has transcribed my deliberate error into her death warrant. The Committee's machinery does not correct; it absorbs. My sabotage has been digested. It sits on this page, perfect and useless, a fossil of care embedded in the document that will kill her.

I initial the fourteenth name.

Moret. The *t* sits there like a small door that was supposed to open onto delay, complexity, bureaucratic friction—all the things that slow a killing apparatus—and has instead opened onto nothing. The machine copied it and moved on. The machine does not need correct names. It needs initials on a list and a cart to the Place de la Révolution and a blade that falls and a crowd that cheers, and none of these things require accurate spelling.

I initial the fifteenth, the sixteenth, the seventeenth.

I put the pen down. I square the page against the desk's edge. I note the gouge in the oak, the tallow smell, the scratch of Gérard's quill in the next room where he is copying another document, another list, another set of names moving through the building's digestive system toward the Revolutionary Tribunal and from there to the scaffold. I fold the directive along its original crease and place it in the tray for documents requiring transport to the Conciergerie.

Now I stand. Now I take my coat from the hook. Now I check the buttons—three, brass, one tarnished—and I settle the tricolor sash and I feel its weight on my left shoulder, the specific gravity of republican cloth, and I move toward the door.

## II.

My boots on the corridor stone make a sound I have heard before.

It is not that I choose to remember. The feet move and the sound they make on these particular flagstones—limestone, laid in the last century, worn to a dull polish by the traffic of functionaries who under the old regime administered the business of the Crown and who now, many of them the same men in different cockades, administer the business of the Republic—pulls from somewhere beneath my sternum the other sound, the older sound, boots on cobblestones in a printer's courtyard on an afternoon in the autumn of 1791 when the light came in low from the west and hit the stones at an angle that turned them the color of bread crust and I stood at the back of a crowd of perhaps thirty people and listened to a man I did not yet know speak about law.

I am walking. I am in the corridor. The corridor is dark—they do not waste candles on passageways—and the darkness is cool and damp and it smells of stone and of something sweeter beneath the stone, something organic, the accumulated breath and sweat of the hundreds of bodies that pass through this building daily, prisoners and guards and clerks and commissars and the women who bring food and the boys who carry messages and all of them breathing the same limestone air, all of them feeding the walls with their exhalations. I am in this corridor and I am simultaneously in the courtyard, because the mind does not ask permission. The mind hears boots on stone and answers with boots on cobblestone, and what can be done about this. Nothing can be done about this.

The man speaking in the courtyard was called Dufresne and he is dead now—he was arrested in Ventôse and executed six days later for the crime of *modérantisme*, a word that did not exist in 1791, a word the revolution had to invent in order to make patience into treason—and he stood on an overturned crate and spoke without notes about how the Republic could be built on procedure rather than fury. He said: *The law must be slower than anger, because anger serves only itself, but law, if it is real, serves the ones who come after us*. He said: *Justice requires the most radical patience—the willingness to let the guilty go free today so that the innocent are not killed tomorrow*. He said these things in a voice that was not loud, not oratorical, not any of the things that the voices of the Convention would become—the roaring of Danton, the crystalline precision of Robespierre, the hoarse fury of Hébert—but instead conversational, as though law were something you could discuss the way you discussed the price of bread or the best way to set type.

I am on a staircase now. The stairs descend. Below me are the holding cells.

In the courtyard the printer's widow came toward me—I was standing at the edge, near the gate, uncertain whether I was a participant or an observer, uncertain whether this gathering was dangerous or merely earnest—and she held out a pamphlet. Her hands were stained. The ink had settled into the creases of her knuckles and under her nails and along the edges of her fingers where they would have gripped the lever of the press, so that her hands looked—I see them now, I see them against the damp wall of this staircase as though projected there—as though they had been writing on themselves, as though the ideas contained in the pamphlets had passed from language through ink through metal through paper and finally into her skin, so that she carried the arguments in her body. She said: *The latest from Monsieur Dufresne. Have you read him?* And I took the pamphlet and her ink came off on my fingers and I carried it home and read it under a candle that was not tallow, that was beeswax, because in 1791 there were still beeswax candles for men of modest means, and the pamphlet argued that a republic worthy of the name must enshrine the right of the accused to face the accuser, that anonymous denunciation was the mechanism of tyranny regardless of which tyranny employed it, and I remember thinking: this is what I believe, this is what I have always believed without knowing the words for it, and these people have the words.

My boots reach the bottom of the staircase.

The words. They had the words. Dufresne had them and the printer had them and Geneviève set them in type and pressed them into paper and handed them to strangers in a courtyard and the words traveled outward through the sections of Paris and for a time—a brief time, a time I measure now in the distance between the top of this staircase and the bottom—the words and their meanings traveled together, like a man and his shadow in noonday sun, inseparable, and then the sun moved, and the words kept traveling but their meanings fell away, and *justice* became the name of the Tribunal and *virtue* became the name of the Terror and *patience* became the name of treason and the words arrived at their destinations empty, drained, serviceable for anything, and Dufresne was killed by a vocabulary he helped to build.

I am at the bottom. The corridor branches. To the left, the holding cells. To the right, the stairs to the street. In thirty minutes the cart will depart.

My hand is on the wall. The wall is damp.

## III.

The wall is damp and the dampness has a temperature—not cold exactly but *removed*, a coolness that does not belong to the air or to the season but to the stone itself, to the mineral fact of limestone that has been underground for longer than any republic, any monarchy, any human arrangement of power—and my fingers against this coolness go somewhere else.

There is a trench at the Errancis cemetery and it is March and I am standing at its edge.

I did not plan to go. I told myself I was walking. I told myself the route past the Errancis was incidental—it is near the Monceau barrier, I had business in that Section, it was plausible—but the body carries its own purposes and I was at the trench and looking down before I had decided to look.

They had been placed—no—they had been deposited. The bodies in the trench were not arranged. They had been brought by cart and the cart had been tilted and they had slid and fallen and settled into the postures that physics dictates for objects of unequal weight and dimension when they are released from a height of four or five feet onto other objects of similar composition. Some face down. Some twisted. The lime had been applied but not enough—it was March and lime was scarce, requisitioned for construction, for sanitation, for all the purposes a republic finds for calcium oxide when it is simultaneously building and dissolving—and so the bodies were still legible. Faces could be distinguished. Hands.

One hand.

At the near edge of the trench, as though reaching over the lip—though this was only the accident of how the body had been deposited, the arm extended by gravity or by the angle of the cart's tipping, not by volition, not by any final grasping—a left hand, palm down, fingers slightly curled, and across the knuckles a scar. A white ridge of scar tissue running diagonally from the base of the index finger to the outside of the little finger, a scar I had seen hundreds of times because Clément gestured when he talked and his left hand was the hand he led with, the hand that drew shapes in the air when he was excited about an idea, the hand that had held a glass of wine in his apartment on the Rue de Tournon when he told me that the network needed someone inside the Committee's surveillance apparatus, that I had the credentials and the temperament, that I could save people if I was willing to become the thing that killed them.

The scar was from childhood. He told me once. A wall he climbed at eight years old—his father's orchard in the Auvergne, a stone wall between the orchard and the road, and he slipped and his hand caught the edge and the edge of the stone opened his knuckles and his mother sewed the wound with silk thread because there was no surgeon in the village and she had been a seamstress before she married. He told me this in the apartment on the Rue de Tournon, and the scar was visible as he told it because he was holding his glass in his left hand and the candlelight caught the ridge of tissue and I could see the line his mother's needle had taken, slightly irregular, the work of a woman who knew cloth but not skin, and he laughed about it, he said *she was furious, she said the wall would outlast me, and she was right about the wall but wrong about me*, and he drank, and I drank, and the candle guttered, and we discussed the network's protocols for the forging of *certificats de civisme* and which officials in which Sections could be bribed and which could be persuaded and which had to be avoided, and the scar on his hand moved through the air as he talked, tracing the architecture of a resistance that would, ten days before I stood at this trench, cease to exist when they came for him at four in the morning.

I did not touch the hand.

I stood at the trench's edge and I looked at the scar's angle—approximately thirty-five degrees from horizontal, though who measures such things, though apparently I do, apparently my mind encountered his dead hand and reached for geometry the way another mind might have reached for prayer—and I catalogued its position relative to the trench's lip: seventeen centimeters from the edge, the fingers four centimeters apart at their widest splay, the thumb tucked under the palm, invisible, the wrist disappearing beneath the shoulder of another body whose face was turned away. I recorded these measurements the way I record everything now, the way the Committee has taught me to record: precisely, dispassionately, as data. The hand was data. The scar was data. The angle was data. And if I had permitted myself to see the hand as Clément's hand—as the hand that gestured, that held wine, that once gripped an orchard wall in the Auvergne while a boy's mother ran toward him with her mouth open in the shape of his name—then I would have knelt at the edge and I would have touched it and I would have been seen and I would have been reported and I would have been in the trench within the week and the three people still hidden, the three I am still protecting, would have followed me.
