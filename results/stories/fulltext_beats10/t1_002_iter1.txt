# The Sutton County Transcripts

---

**IN THE DISTRICT COURT OF SUTTON COUNTY, TEXAS**
**87th JUDICIAL DISTRICT**

**THE STATE OF TEXAS**
**v.**
**ELEANOR RUTH VANCE**

**Case No. 87-CR-4421**

**TRANSCRIPT OF PROCEEDINGS — DAY 1**
**September 14, 1987**

**BEFORE THE HONORABLE JUDGE R. WADE CHILDRESS**

---

THE COURT: This court is now in session. We are here in the matter of *State of Texas v. Eleanor Ruth Vance*, Case Number 87-CR-4421. The defendant is charged on three counts of murder in the first degree pursuant to Texas Penal Code Section 19.02. Ms. Vance, you are present with your counsel?

THE DEFENDANT: I am, Your Honor.

THE COURT: And you are represented by Mr. Harlan Gaines of the Public Defender's Office?

MR. GAINES: That is correct, Your Honor.

THE COURT: Ms. Vance, you have been informed of the charges against you. How do you plead?

THE DEFENDANT: Not guilty, Your Honor.

THE COURT: So entered. Mr. Alderman, is the State prepared to proceed with voir dire?

MR. ALDERMAN: The State is prepared, Your Honor.

THE COURT: Then we will begin jury selection this afternoon. I want to remind all parties that I have granted the State's motion in limine regarding pre-trial publicity. There will be no reference to media coverage of this case in the presence of prospective jurors. We are in recess until 1:30 p.m.

*(Court in recess at 11:42 a.m.)*

---

**AUDIO DIARY — DONNA KEELER**
**September 14, 1987, 9:47 p.m.**

This is Donna Keeler, court reporter, Sutton County, Texas. Fourteen years certified. Recording personal notes on case 87-CR-4421, *State v. Vance*. Day one.

Presiding: Judge Childress. Courtroom B. Prosecution is Dale Alderman from the D.A.'s office. Defense is Harlan Gaines, public defender. Jury selection starts tomorrow.

Not much to report. Preliminary motions, scheduling, the plea. Standard.

The defendant sat very still through all of it. Hands in her lap. I don't mean she was calm the way defendants sometimes are when they're medicated — she was still the way the stenograph is still. Like a machine at rest. Ready.

Courtroom smelled like cedar polish. Bobby must have done the woodwork over the weekend. I'll have to thank him.

That's all for today.

---

**TRANSCRIPT OF PROCEEDINGS — DAY 2 (EXCERPT)**
**September 16, 1987**

MR. ALDERMAN: Detective Rourke, in your investigation of the death of Cody Allan Briggs, age four, what did the defendant say when you asked her why the child was found in the bathtub?

DET. ROURKE: She said — and I'm reading from my notes — "He was already chosen. I was trying to keep him from being used. I held him under so whatever was in the water wouldn't pull him down first."

MR. ALDERMAN: And these statements were made voluntarily?

DET. ROURKE: Yes, sir. She was cooperative throughout. She provided similar statements regarding the deaths of Alma Reyes and of Jacob Parr.

MR. ALDERMAN: And were those deaths also consistent with the pattern?

DET. ROURKE: Ma'am — excuse me, sir — all three children were foster children in the defendant's care. All three died within a fourteen-month period. Alma Reyes, age three, asphyxiation. Jacob Parr, age five, ingestion of a sodium hydroxide-based household cleaner. And Cody Briggs, drowning. The defendant reported each death herself. Each time she told us they were "already chosen" and she was "trying to keep them from being used."

---

**TRANSCRIPT OF PROCEEDINGS — DAY 3 (EXCERPT)**
**September 17, 1987**

MR. ALDERMAN: Dr. Wendt, in your professional opinion, does the defendant's repeated use of phrases such as "already chosen" and "being used" correspond to any recognized psychological framework?

DR. WENDT: Yes. These are textbook features of what we're seeing nationally in cases involving Satanic Ritual Abuse claims. The language of selection — children being "chosen" for ritualistic purposes — the belief in an organized, covert network of abusers operating under a religious or *quasi*-religious framework —

THE DEFENDANT: *Quasi*.

DR. WENDT: I'm sorry?

THE DEFENDANT: The word is *quasi*. You said "kwah-zee." The prefix is Latin. It's *quasi*. As in *quasi modo geniti infantes*. If you're going to diagnose me with a fixation on Latin ritual, you might pronounce it correctly.

THE COURT: Ms. Vance, you will have your opportunity to speak through your counsel.

THE DEFENDANT: Of course, Your Honor.

---

**TRANSCRIPT OF PROCEEDINGS — DAY 4 (EXCERPT)**
**September 18, 1987**

MR. ALDERMAN: Mr. Hall, you investigated the McMartin preschool case in Manhattan Beach, California, is that correct?

MR. HALL: For two years, yes, sir.

MR. ALDERMAN: And in your expert assessment, do the defendant's claims resemble the types of allegations you encountered there?

MR. HALL: Structurally, yes. You have an individual who has constructed an elaborate narrative in which children are subjected to ritualized harm by unseen forces. The narrative is internally consistent, which makes it persuasive, but it has no corroborating physical evidence. No cult. No accomplices. No ritual materials found in the home. What you have is three dead children and a woman with a story.

**AUDIO DIARY — DONNA KEELER**
**September 18, 1987, 10:22 p.m.**

Day four. Prosecution rested.

I've been going back through my notes and I want to record something about the defendant's behavior, since the transcript won't capture it and I want an accurate personal record.

Eleanor Vance never looks at the jury. Not once in four days. When she speaks, she faces forward. When she listens, she faces forward. But her eyes — I'm at the stenograph, which is positioned between the witness stand and the bench, slightly below and to the right — her eyes track to the machine. To my machine. Not to me. Every time there's a pause in testimony, she looks at the Stentura the way you'd look at someone you recognized across a room.

I've been reporting for fourteen years. Defendants look at the judge. They look at their hands. They look at the jury. They don't watch the stenography machine.

I don't know what to do with that observation. I'm just putting it on the record.

My record.

**AUDIO DIARY — DONNA KEELER**
**September 23, 1987, 11:58 p.m.**

The official transcript says "unintelligible." That is inaccurate. The vocalization was perfectly intelligible. It just wasn't in English.

I need to re-examine my shorthand notes.

That's all for tonight.

**AUDIO DIARY — DONNA KEELER**
**September 27, 1987 (Saturday), 2:14 p.m.**

I'm going to talk about my work, because I need to think about my work right now.

I use a Stenograph Stentura 200. Twenty-two keys. The paper tape is thermal, and I replace the roll every ninety pages, which means I go through two and a half, sometimes three rolls a day during trial. Daily output runs 200 to 250 pages. I proofread every page manually. Corrections are made with splice tape. I photocopy in triplicate — one for the court file, one for each counsel, one for my personal records.

The archive room is in the courthouse basement. Climate-controlled to 68 degrees, which means it's the only comfortable room in the building in summer. Fluorescent lights — the long tube kind that hum at B-flat, I checked once with a pitch pipe. Files organized by case number going back to 1911. I've touched most of them. Reorganized the ones from before 1940.

My hands are — I have a callus on my right ring finger, third knuckle, from the key arrangement. It's been there so long it has its own callus underneath it, like a geological stratum. I wear a wrist brace at night. I use Eucerin on my hands before bed. My mother used to say I had piano hands, but the stenograph is harder on them than a piano would have been. The keys need more force than people think. You're pressing twenty-two keys in chords, 200 words a minute, for six or seven hours. Your hands learn a kind of intelligence that has nothing to do with your brain. My hands could transcribe testimony in my sleep. Sometimes I think they do.

I've been the sole court reporter for Sutton County for nine years. Before me, there was Glenda Parkus.

*(Pause — approximately four seconds of tape hiss.)*

I think I'll make the green chile chicken tonight. I've got a can of Hatch chiles left from that case I bought in August.

---

**AUDIO DIARY — DONNA KEELER**
**September 29, 1987, 1:03 a.m.**

I've been going back to my audio recordings of the outburst from Day 7. I record the courtroom feed through an auxiliary line into my backup deck — standard practice, I've done it for years, it's how I check my transcripts against the original audio if there's ever a dispute.

I slowed the playback to half speed. The syllabic pattern held. It wasn't glossolalia. Glossolalia doesn't maintain consistent syllable structure across repetitions. This did. Three identical cycles with rising pitch on the final syllable of each.

Then I reversed the tape.

I know how this sounds. I want to state for this record that I am not a superstitious person. I have a two-year degree in court reporting from Del Mar College in Corpus Christi, plus certification in phonetic transcription. I reversed the tape because the stress pattern suggested the vocalization was generated from a source pattern being produced backward, and I wanted to test that hypothesis.

On the reversed audio, the vocalization resolved into Latin. Fragmented, but syntactically coherent. I transcribed it:

*Quae scribit eligitur.*

"She who writes is chosen."

*(Sound of a tape being rewound, then played:)*

*...quae scribit eligitur... quae scribit eligitur... quae scribit eligitur...*

That's my function. I'm the one who writes.

*(Pause — seven seconds.)*

There was a second outburst. Day 9. Shorter. The official transcript marked it *(unintelligible vocalization, approximately 15 seconds; order restored without recess)*. I didn't think much of it at the time. It was quieter than the first one. Almost murmured. But I had the auxiliary recording, so tonight I ran the same procedure. Slowed playback, then reversed.

*(Sound of a tape being rewound, then played:)*

The Latin is cleaner this time. Less fragmentation. As though she'd been practicing.

*Nominatim. Eligitur nominatim.*

"By name. She is chosen by name."

*(Pause.)*

I need to pull the Glenda Parkus files from the archive. Glenda was court reporter here before me. She left in 1978. I replaced her. I was told she moved to Abilene. But I was also told — and this is not something I've ever said out loud — I was told by Bobby, the courthouse custodian, that Glenda didn't move anywhere. That she stopped showing up one Monday and nobody filed a report. Her Stenotype machine was still at her station. Bobby said the tape in the machine had been used, but when he looked at the paper, the impressions weren't shorthand. He said they looked like the same key pressed over and over. He showed me once, years ago. He'd kept a section of the tape in his desk drawer. Twenty pages of the same chord. I didn't know what chord. I didn't look that closely.

I'm going to go back to the courthouse tomorrow and look at the case logs from Glenda's last year. What was on her docket. What she was transcribing the week she disappeared.

I'm also going to look at that tape, if Bobby still has it.

I want to be clear: I am documenting this because it is my professional obligation to ensure the accuracy of the court record. The official transcripts of *State v. Vance* contain at least two demonstrable inaccuracies — two instances where specific, structured, translatable vocalizations were reduced to "unintelligible." If the record is wrong, it's my responsibility to determine why and to what extent.

That's all this is.

*(Pause — three seconds.)*

The wrist brace isn't helping anymore. I can feel the keys even when I'm not touching them. The chords. Like they're still being pressed into my tendons.

Eleanor looked at the machine again today. Looked at it and smiled.

Not at me.

The machine.

End of entry.

*(Tape hiss. Then, faintly, almost at the threshold of audibility, a sound. A rhythmic vocalization. Not Donna's voice. Coming from somewhere in the room, or from inside the recorder, or from the tape itself — the same rising tonal pattern, the same three-cycle structure, as though the machine were playing back something that hadn't been recorded.)*

*(The tape runs for eleven more seconds, then ends.)*
