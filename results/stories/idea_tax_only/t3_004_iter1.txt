# The Clockmaker's Menu

---

## I.

You want the family. Fine. I'll give you the family.

Start with the name. Karadžić on one side, Horváth on the other, and the restaurant in the middle like a child neither parent wants to hold but neither will put down. *Baba Marijana's* — though she'd been dead four years by the time anyone thought to paint her name on the awning, and by then the name was less a tribute than a territorial flag. The restaurant sat on Karlovac Street, ground floor of a Habsburg-era building with walls thick enough to survive two empires and one grandmother, which is roughly the same thing.

The grandmother: Marijana Karadžić, née Tomić, married once, widowed once, opinionated perpetually. She had four children. I will list them the way she listed them, which is to say in order of disappointment.

First: Ivo. The eldest son. The one who was supposed to inherit the restaurant, run the restaurant, continue the restaurant, *be* the restaurant. Instead he — well, depending on which faction you ask, he either (a) gambled it away, (b) signed it over under duress to satisfy debts that weren't his, (c) was swindled by his own brother, or (d) simply walked out one morning in 1987 and didn't come back for six years, by which time half the kitchen equipment had been sold and the other half had been claimed by Uncle Miro, who had never cooked a meal in his life but who had, by then, physical possession of the deed. Or a deed. Or something that looked like a deed if you didn't have a lawyer and even if you did, the lawyer was Miro's wife's brother, so.

Ivo was sixty-one when the siege started. He looked seventy. He moved like a man who had been told, repeatedly and by everyone, that he was the source of the family's ruin, and who had — this was the thing about Ivo — *mostly agreed.* Not out of guilt. Out of a kind of exhaustion with the alternative, which was defending yourself to people who had already written the verdict.

Second: Miro. The younger brother. Built like a municipal building — squat, gray, load-bearing. Miro had run the restaurant since 1989 or 1990, depending on which set of paperwork you believed. He had three sons: Branko, Nikola, and Goran. Branko was a bully with a bookkeeper's instincts. Nikola was a bookkeeper with a bully's instincts. Goran was seventeen and mostly irrelevant except that he was the only grandchild Baba Marijana had actively seemed to like, which made him a living piece of evidence in the inheritance dispute.

Third: Aunt Vesna. The eldest daughter. Married a Horváth — Tibor Horváth, from Osijek, a Hungarian Catholic in a family that counted these things the way other families count silverware. The marriage was 1971. The last time someone in the Karadžić line had spoken to Vesna voluntarily, not counting funerals and one hospital visit conducted under the pretense of collecting a borrowed suitcase, was 1993. Vesna had a daughter, Lena, who had married a man nobody bothered to learn the name of because Lena, by virtue of being Vesna's, was already filed under *their problem.* Vesna also had opinions about the will. She had opinions the way the Adriatic has water.

Fourth: my mother. Daria. The youngest. Married a Slovene, moved to Ljubljana, had me, came back when the marriage dissolved, died of pancreatic cancer in 1991 at the age of thirty-nine. She lands last in every list because she was last in Baba Marijana's affections, last to receive any portion of anything, and first to be dead, which is typically how it works.

I'm Petra. I'm Daria's daughter. I was fourteen when my mother died and I was placed — *placed*, like a piece of furniture between rooms — in the household of whichever relative was least inconvenienced by my existence at any given time. I have slept in Miro's back bedroom (six months, 1991–92, until Branko needed the room). I have slept on Vesna's sofa (four months, until Tibor's snoring drove me to the hallway and Vesna decided this reflected badly on her hospitality rather than on her husband). I have slept in Ivo's apartment, which was a single room above a barber shop and which he gave to me entirely while he slept in a chair by the door, for two years, the longest anyone kept me.

I don't belong to a faction. I am a shared expense, an inherited inconvenience, a girl passed between households like a coffee cup no one will wash. This is not self-pity. This is positional analysis. In a family where every word is monitored for partisan loyalty, where a comment about the weather at the wrong dinner table can be decoded as a referendum on the will, I am the one person no one bothers to perform for. They say things in front of me they would never say to each other. They argue across me as if I were a hallway.

I have been assembling this for years. I have dates. I have contradictions. I have the specific wording Uncle Miro used, at Easter dinner in 1994, when he said Ivo was "constitutionally incapable of sustaining anything — a job, a building, a relationship, a thought," and I have the silence that followed, and I have the fact that Aunt Vesna laughed — a single, barking laugh, the only time those two agreed on anything, and they agreed on this: that Ivo was the family's central failure.

I want you to hold that. Hold the sound of Vesna laughing at Miro's cruelty, because they were natural enemies who found common ground in one shared contempt, and hold it against everything that happens later in the basement when the shelling starts and these people who have rehearsed each other's destruction for thirty years are suddenly required to keep each other alive.

But before the basement: a document. I found this in a kitchen drawer in Miro's apartment, written on the back of a menu in blue ballpoint, dated 1996. It was passed around at a family dinner — Miro's faction only, no Horváths present — and it was considered, by everyone at that table, hilarious.

---

**OBITUARY (DRAFT)**

**VESNA HORVÁTH (née KARADŽIĆ), 1948–**

Vesna Horváth (née Karadžić), who famously believed she was owed the Karlovac Street property despite all evidence and decency, has died. She is survived by her husband Tibor, a man of presumably Hungarian extraction and definitely limited contribution, and by her daughter Lena, who inherited her mother's talent for claiming things that belonged to other people.

Vesna will be remembered for her tireless campaign to rewrite her mother's wishes, her unshakable conviction that being the eldest daughter entitled her to what the eldest *son* had already been promised, and her refusal to attend any family gathering without first delivering a forty-minute speech about her own suffering. She famously described the forged will — which was not forged, which was notarized, which was *right there in the filing cabinet, Vesna, Christ* — as "a document produced by criminals," by which she meant her own brothers.

In lieu of flowers, the family requests that mourners send copies of the actual will, notarized, highlighted, and laminated, to Vesna's home address, where they will finally be allowed to enter the premises.

She will not be missed at dinner.

---

That was 1996. Peacetime. They wrote that about a living woman and laughed so hard that Branko knocked over a bottle of *šljivovica* and nobody cleaned it up because they were too busy adding amendments. Nikola wanted to include a line about Tibor's mustache. Miro suggested they fax it to Vesna. Someone, I think it was Goran — young Goran, thirteen, just old enough to understand what cruelty was and not old enough to be ashamed of enjoying it — suggested they print it in the actual newspaper when the time came.

I was in the next room. I heard everything. I was always in the next room.

I kept the draft because I keep things. Because someone should. Because this family writes each other's obituaries as jokes, and I had a feeling — I had a feeling even then, before anything, before the siege, before the basement, before the Clockmaker — that eventually the joke would need a punchline, and I wanted to be the one holding it.

---

## II.

The shelling started on a Tuesday and did not stop. I don't mean continuously — I mean it didn't stop *mattering*. There is a difference between a bombardment and the knowledge that a bombardment is now a feature of your life, the way weather is a feature, the way gravity is. The first shells hit the east side of the city and we heard them the way you hear a neighbor's argument: with attention, with a small amount of relief that it isn't yours, and with the growing awareness that walls are thinner than you thought.

By Thursday the shells were hitting our district. By Friday the power was gone. By Saturday the water came out of the taps in a brownish sigh, then stopped, then dribbled, then stopped for real.

The restaurant basement presented itself not as a choice but as a geometric fact. Miro's apartment was directly above the restaurant, accessible through an internal staircase. Vesna and Tibor lived across the street, forty meters of exposed pavement that was, for the first week, still crossable if you ran and if you ran at the right time. Ivo's room above the barber shop was around the corner, maybe seventy meters by the back alley, partially shielded by the old tobacco warehouse.

The basement had been built in 1911 by an Austrian engineer who either expected another war or simply believed in structural overkill. Stone walls half a meter thick. A vaulted ceiling. A single high window at street level, which Miro had boarded and sandbagged within the first hours, not out of prudence but because the window faced Vesna's building and he said he'd rather be shelled than looked at by that woman. He actually said that. Saturday. While his sons carried bags of sand.

Miro's faction arrived first, of course. They were already there — Miro, his wife Jelena, Branko, Nikola, Goran. They had arranged themselves in the north end of the basement, near the old wine racks, before anyone else appeared. The wine was gone within a day — a decision framed as medical necessity by Jelena and executed by Branko with the efficiency of a man who has been waiting his whole life for a socially acceptable reason to drink at nine in the morning. They had supplies: jarred peppers from the restaurant pantry, two cases of mineral water that Nikola had brought up from a storeroom, blankets, a camping stove, and the quiet, fortressed demeanor of people who believed that being first conferred ownership. Which, in this family, it did.

I arrived Sunday, from Ivo's. I had been staying with him again — not officially, not in a way anyone would describe as custodial, but in the way a stray returns to the hand that fed it last. I came alone. I carried a bag with three changes of clothes, a toothbrush, a notebook, and two cans of sardines I had taken from a shop whose front window had been blown out. I will not describe the moral reasoning involved. There was no moral reasoning. There were sardines.

Miro said: "You should have come earlier."

Jelena said nothing but moved a box so I could sit in the gap between the wine rack and the wall. The gap was approximately the width of my body. This was, I understood, my allocation.

Vesna arrived Monday. She came with Tibor and with Lena and with Lena's husband, whose name was — I would eventually learn — Damir, though for the first three days no one called him anything. They came through the front door at street level and descended the stairs with the specific energy of people who have just sprinted across an open street and are furious about it. Tibor was bleeding from a cut on his forehead — not shelling, just a fall on the stairs, but he held a kitchen towel to his head with the gravity of a man who wanted it known that he had suffered in the crossing.

Vesna entered the basement and surveyed the arrangement. Miro's faction in the north. Me in my crevice. Eighty percent of the floor space claimed. The supplies visible, possessive, a display.

"Typical," Vesna said.

"You're welcome," said Miro, in the way you say *you're welcome* when what you mean is *get out*.

They did not greet each other. Vesna's faction installed itself in the south end, near the drain, which smelled faintly of old dishwater and which Vesna would, over the coming weeks, scrub with a persistence that was less about hygiene than about refusing to accept anything about her circumstances. Tibor sat against the wall with his towel and his wound and his silence. Lena and Damir unrolled a blanket and established a perimeter that was, in its precision, a property line.

Between the two factions: a no-man's-land of approximately four meters. Restaurant furniture — stacked chairs, a folding table, crates of paper napkins with *Baba Marijana's* printed in the corner. This was the DMZ. I sat against the east wall, perpendicular to both camps, in the gap between the wine rack and the stairs. My position was, as in all things, lateral. Neither north nor south. A margin.

And then the following happened, and I am going to give it to you the way I heard it, without my voice over it, because my voice would soften it, and you should hear what it actually sounded like in that basement on the first night everyone was together. I want you to hear the echo off the stone. I want you to hear how their voices sounded when there was no weather and no traffic and no world — just the basement and the dust that the shelling shook from the ceiling and the absolute impossibility of leaving.

---

**[INTERIOR — RESTAURANT BASEMENT — NIGHT]**

*Half-light from two candles and a battery-powered lantern placed on an overturned crate. The ceiling shudders. Dust falls in a thin, continuous powder onto everything. No one reacts to the shelling anymore. It has been forty-eight hours. They react to the silence between shells.*

*The south end of the basement. VESNA is arranging canned goods on a shelf that once held restaurant napkins. MIRO approaches from the north end. He stops at the border — the stacked chairs. He does not cross.*

**MIRO:** We need to talk about water.

**VESNA:** Then talk.

**MIRO:** There are eleven of us—

**VESNA:** I can count.

**MIRO:** —and I filled three containers before the pressure went. That's enough for four days if we ration. Six if we're smart.

**VESNA:** "We."

**MIRO:** Yes, "we." Unless you brought your own source and you've been hiding it behind Tibor's enormous sense of injury.

*TIBOR shifts. The kitchen towel has been replaced by a bandage. He does not speak. He has not spoken since arriving.*

**VESNA:** We brought what we could carry while running across a street your side of the family has never once offered to help anyone cross.

**MIRO:** My side of the family wasn't aware your side of the family required an *invitation* to survive.

**VESNA:** An invitation would have been novel, yes.

*Pause. A shell hits something close enough that the candle flames flatten. Everyone is still. Five seconds. Ten. The shelling resumes at its previous distance. Breath returns.*

**BRANKO:** *(from the north end, not rising)* We should establish a rotation. For the water. And for the latrine bucket.

**VESNA:** "Establish a rotation." Listen to him. He sounds like his father at a board meeting.
