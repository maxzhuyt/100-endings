# The Cartographer's Confession

---

## I.

The thing about mapping an evacuation route across the Brunt Ice Shelf is that you have to believe, on some level, that the people you're evacuating will still be alive when it matters. You have to believe in the future tense. You have to draw lines that say: *here is where the living will walk when everything goes wrong*, and you have to do this while your intestines are staging a one-organ coup against the rest of your body, and you have to do it knowing that the satellite imagery you're working from is six months old, and six months in Antarctica is a geological epoch now, the ice rewriting itself faster than the satellites can gossip about it, which means what you are drawing is, technically, a map of a place that no longer exists. A memory of terrain dressed up as a plan.

I'm very good at my job.

I want to be clear about that, because everything I'm about to tell you will make more sense if you understand that I am good at the one thing I do, and the one thing I do is draw lines on surfaces so that other people — braver people, more important people, people whose digestive systems do not declare war on them at the first sign of barometric pressure change — know where to put their feet. I am the person who makes the map. I am not the territory.

Maren was the territory.

No. That's not right either. That's the kind of thing I say when I'm trying to make it sound like I've processed this, like I've metabolized it into metaphor, like the thing that happened has been digested (everything is digestion with me, I'm sorry, I'm sorry, the body is the only language I've ever really spoken fluently) and turned into something clean enough to write down. It hasn't. I'm telling you this from afterward, from the other side of what happened, and I still don't have a clean line from here to there. The map of this story is the worst map I've ever drawn.

My name is Peche Idowu. I am — I was — the cartographer and spatial logistics coordinator for the Solvik Antarctic Survey, which is a fancy way of saying I drew the lines so the important people knew where to walk. I am thirty-one years old. I have irritable bowel syndrome, generalized anxiety disorder, and what my mother calls "a gift for making everything harder than it needs to be," which is, I think, the Nigerian-British way of saying I am a catastrophist. I imagine the worst thing that could happen in any given situation, and then I imagine the worst thing that could happen *about* the worst thing, and then I imagine someone finding my body frozen in the posture of imagining and thinking: she died as she lived, in a full-body clench.

The worst thing did happen, by the way. On the ice. But it wasn't the thing I imagined. It was worse than the worst thing, because the worst thing I could imagine always involved failure — *my* failure, systems failure, structural failure, the ice opening up and swallowing us because I drew a line three degrees off true. What actually happened wasn't a failure. It was a success. It was the most successful expedition in the history of human discovery, probably, and I watched it succeed, and I am here to tell you that there are victories that will hollow you out more completely than any defeat, and the woman I loved — the woman I loved — became something I still can't—

Okay.

Okay. My stomach is doing the thing. Even now. Even here, at this desk, ten thousand kilometers from the ice, it's doing the thing, which means we're getting close to the part I can't map, the part where the lines I've drawn run off the edge of the paper and into whatever void cartographers fall into when the territory refuses to hold still.

I'll start where it started. I'll start with Maren.

No. I'll start before Maren. I'll start with the crew. Because the crew is the part you need to understand to understand what Maren did, and what Maren did is the part you need to understand to understand why I'm writing this instead of sleeping, or eating, or doing any of the things my therapist says I should be doing instead of "compulsively documenting as a form of emotional avoidance."

She's right, my therapist. She's completely right. This is avoidance. This whole document is a map I'm drawing so I don't have to stand in the territory.

But I'm a cartographer. It's what I do.

## II.

Maren Solvik recruited the way other people breathe: constantly, invisibly, as if it were simply what her body did to stay alive. She didn't post job listings. She didn't conduct interviews. She *appeared*. In your office, at your door, at the bar where you drank alone on Tuesdays after the disciplinary board met. She appeared, and she already knew. That was the terrifying thing, in retrospect. She already knew the shape of the hole in you, and she had brought the exact piece that fit.

Dr. Fen Ásgeir was the first. Marine archaeologist, formerly of the University of Bergen, formerly credentialed, formerly respected, formerly a lot of things. The story, as I understood it, was this: Fen's team had excavated a medieval Norse trading vessel off the coast of Greenland, beautifully preserved, historically significant, the kind of find that makes careers. The university sold exclusive access rights to a private collector — some hedge fund ghost with a nautical fetish — before the cataloguing was complete. Fen refused to hand over the site notes. The university said she was in breach of contract. She said they were in breach of the basic covenant between the living and the dead. They had lawyers. She had principles. You can guess who won.

When I met Fen, she was fifty-three, silver-haired, radically steady. She had a way of holding objects — a coffee mug, a sediment brush, a piece of equipment — as if each one were the last of its kind and she had been personally entrusted with its continuity. She didn't talk much. When she did, it was in complete, grammatically immaculate sentences that sounded like they'd been drafted and revised before leaving her mouth. She called everyone by their full name. She never abbreviated anything. I think she believed that precision was a form of respect, and I loved her for it immediately, the way you love someone who takes the same things seriously that you do but with more dignity.

Maren found Fen in Tromsø, living above a fish shop, writing a monograph no one would publish. Maren didn't say: *I need an archaeologist.* Maren said: *I know a place where no one owns what's buried.* Fen was on a plane within forty-eight hours.

Tomas Herrera was the sonar engineer. He was blind. He had been blind for six years, since an industrial accident at a deep-sea mining operation in the Atacama Trench — a pressure seal failed, and the sonic backlash destroyed his optic nerves and most of his inner ear's confidence in the concept of "up." He spent two years rebuilding. Not his sight — that was gone — but his *methodology*. He developed an algorithmic sonar modeling system that translated acoustic data into three-dimensional haptic maps: surfaces he could touch, geometries he could feel with his fingertips. His system was, by several independent assessments, more accurate than any visual-spectrum imaging technology currently in use. No one hired him. He was blind, and the industry that had blinded him required its engineers to meet visual acuity standards that existed, as far as I could tell, primarily to protect the industry from liability claims by blind engineers it had created.

Tomas was thirty-seven, Chilean-Finnish, quiet in a way that was different from Fen's quiet. Fen was still because she was precise. Tomas was still because he was *listening*. Always listening. He heard things in rooms that the rest of us had edited out of our perceptual field — the hum of a generator cycling down, the particular frequency of wind against a specific thickness of tent fabric, the sound my stomach made forty-five minutes before a full episode, which he once described, with genuine scientific curiosity, as "a kind of tidal signature." He was not being cruel. He was the least cruel person I have ever known. He simply heard the world as it actually sounded, which is something most of us cannot survive.

Maren found Tomas in Helsinki. She didn't say: *I need someone who can see without eyes.* She said: *I need someone who has already rebuilt the way humans perceive the world.* Tomas told me later that what made him say yes wasn't the offer. It was that Maren hadn't once mentioned his blindness as a limitation. She'd mentioned it as a *method.*

I loved her for that too. At the time.

Sable Osei-Mensah was the linguist. I call her the linguist because that's the charitable version; the accurate version is that Sable was a forger, a con artist, and a polyglot of dead languages who had spent a decade manufacturing fake provenances for looted antiquities. She spoke Sumerian, Akkadian, Coptic, Linear B (insofar as anyone "speaks" Linear B), classical Nahuatl, Old Norse, and two forms of demotic Egyptian. She had learned each one because her work required it: you cannot forge a convincing provenance for a stolen Mesopotamian cylinder seal if you can't read the cuneiform, and you cannot fabricate a plausible excavation history for a Pre-Columbian jade mask if you don't know the naming conventions of sixteenth-century Spanish colonial archaeology.

Sable was forty-one, Ghanaian-American, and operated at a frequency of moral complexity that made my anxiety want to file an insurance claim. She was simultaneously the most dishonest and most transparent person I'd ever met. She *told* you she was a liar. She told you with relish, with specificity, with anecdotes that were themselves probably forty percent fabricated. She had a gold tooth she claimed was Ptolemaic. She wore a ring she said she'd stolen from a museum in Cairo, which she later admitted she'd bought at a market in Camden Town, which she *later* later admitted she actually had stolen from a museum in Cairo but enjoyed the irony of pretending the theft was the lie.

I could never get stable footing with Sable. That was, I think, the point.

Maren found her in Accra, or London, or Alexandria — the story changed. Maren didn't say: *I need a criminal.* Maren said: *I need someone who understands how history gets made up.* Sable later told me, during a long night on the ice when neither of us could sleep, that she'd agreed because Maren was the first person who'd ever framed her career not as "a gifted woman wasting her talents on crime" but as an education in how the entire system of historical truth was a negotiation, and Sable had simply been negotiating more honestly than the institutions that condemned her.

That sounded like Maren. That sounded exactly like Maren: the ability to take the most broken thing about you and hold it up to the light and turn it just a quarter-degree until it looked like a qualification. She did it to all of us. We were all, in our various ways, broken instruments. And Maren picked us up and said: *You're not broken. You're tuned to a frequency no one else can hear.*

It's a beautiful thing, to be seen that way.

It is also, I know now, a diagnostic technique. A way of assessing capacity. Maren saw what each person could *do*, and she called it who they *were*, and we loved her for it because the gap between use and identity is the loneliest place most people live, and she closed it. She closed it, and we felt whole.

But there's a difference — I need to say this, I need to say it now, even though I didn't understand it then — there is a difference between seeing what a person can do for you and seeing who a person is. The first builds a team. The second builds a family. Maren built a team.

And then there was Reva.

Reva Chaudhary. Demolitions and structural engineering. Indian-British, forty-five, built like a post box, with forearms that could have appeared in a medical textbook under "forearm, ideal." She had been a combat engineer in the Royal Engineers for twenty years. She was brilliant. She could read a rock face the way Tomas read sonar data — instinctively, structurally, as if the geological stresses and fracture lines were a language her body understood before her mind caught up.

Reva had a condition. I don't know the clinical name; she called it "the wiring." A neurological crossover — some synaptic handshake between the part of her brain that processed percussive force and the part that processed grief. When she triggered a detonation, she wept. Not from guilt. Not from fear. Not from sorrow, exactly. From an involuntary neurological response that flooded her lacrimal system every time she felt a particular register of concussive vibration. The military had used it as grounds for psychological unfitness. They said a demolitions expert who cried at explosions was a liability. They said it suggested emotional instability. They gave her a medical discharge and a pension and a diagnosis that, as far as Reva was concerned, described a woman who didn't exist.

"I'm not sad when it happens," she told me once, early on, while we were inventorying det cord in the supply tent. "I'm not anything when it happens. The tears are just — information. They're data. My body registering force. It's like sweating. You don't think sweating means you're sad."

She said this calmly, practically, with the same tone she used to discuss shear strength and blast radii. But her hands, I noticed, were very still. The way a person's hands are still when they have disciplined themselves not to shake.

A demolitions expert who weeps at every explosion.

I think about Reva more than anyone. More than Fen, more than Tomas, even more than Sable, whose chaos I found privately comforting because it was so much louder than mine. I think about Reva because she was the emblem of what this expedition demanded of all of us: that we do the thing we were made to do, and that the doing would cost us something visible, something the body could not hide, and that the cost would be held against us by people who had never once been asked to pay it.

Maren found Reva in a pub in Leicester. She didn't say: *I need someone who can blow things up.* She said: *I need someone who understands that destruction and loss are not the same thing.*

Reva didn't tell me what made her say yes. But I watched her face when Maren spoke, many times, over many weeks, and I think I know. Reva said yes because Maren was not afraid of the tears. Everyone else who had ever watched Reva work had seen her weep and flinched, or pitied her, or looked away. Maren watched Reva weep and saw *efficiency.* A body so attuned to force that it registered detonation in its most vulnerable system.

That was Maren's genius. I don't use the word ironically. She was a genius at reading the broken place in people and renaming it as power.

The problem — the problem I am writing around, the problem I have been mapping the edges of for this entire document without once looking directly at it — is that the same genius that let Maren see brokenness as capacity also let her see people as instruments. And instruments are things you *use.* And when you use an instrument for long enough, in conditions harsh enough, toward an end ambitious enough, eventually you stop hearing the music and start hearing only the mechanism.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. I always get ahead of myself. My therapist says it's the anxiety — that I experience time as a threat, as something that is always about to deliver the catastrophe it has been promising, and so I rush toward it, trying to arrive at the disaster before it arrives at me
