# The Plumbing Wants What It Wants

## I.

The mop bucket started humming at 8:47 PM, a low B-flat that traveled through the aluminum handle and settled into the bones of my wrist, and I thought: *Here we go.* I set the mop against the wall and waited. The bucket's water trembled in concentric rings like something vast had shifted beneath the foundation, which it had, and which it had been doing for eleven days, and which I had reported to no one because there was no one left to report to. The university had condemned Whitmore Hall in April. It was now October. I was the only line item the administration had failed to cancel.

The intercom crackled three hours before the first car door. I was on my knees in the basement corridor, re-caulking a baseboard seam that would not hold because the building kept breathing it open, and the speaker above the fire exit — a speaker that had not been connected to any electrical source since the condemnation — exhaled a long, wet sound, like sediment moving through copper, and then resolved into fragments of a group text I was not part of. I heard Yuna's voice, or a version of it filtered through thirty-year-old wiring and the mineral deposits of a hundred thousand showers. I heard *72 hours*. I heard *gravitational*. I heard *come back*. I heard five names I had spent seven years trying to forget and three years failing to forget and the rest of my life, apparently, cataloguing in the dark.

I did not call campus police. I did not lock the doors. I did not do anything at all except finish the caulking, rinse my hands in a utility sink whose water ran two degrees warmer than the pipes could account for, and mop the lobby. I mopped it twice. I mopped it the way I mop when I am afraid, which is to say methodically, in overlapping arcs, leaving no streaks, because a clean floor is the only form of prayer I have ever been able to sustain, and I have been sustaining it in this building for eleven years through two administrations, one asbestos scare, one genuine haunting in the east wing that turned out to be raccoons, and one event that was not raccoons and that I have never spoken of.

The first car door slammed at 11:22. I was at the basement window by then — the one at the bottom of the landing where the stairs turn, set into the foundation at ankle height, level with the parking lot's cracked asphalt. I knelt. Through the glass, which was filthy with a grime I was no longer capable of removing because the building replenished it overnight, I saw a pair of brown leather oxfords cross the pavement. The left shoe had a replaced heel — I could tell by the slight difference in tread depth, the half-millimeter discrepancy that changed the wearer's gait from confident to almost-confident. Gerald. The disgraced meteorologist. He still dressed like a man delivering a forecast to two million viewers, even though the scandal had been four years ago and the only audience he'd had since was the Nebraska Department of Transportation, where he read road conditions into a phone line no human being had ever called voluntarily.

I pressed my forehead against the glass. The hum started — not in the pipes this time but in my sternum, a vibration like a tuning fork struck against the inside of my ribs. I have felt it for years. I felt it the first time all five of them were in the common room at once, back when this was a dormitory and they were students and I was the janitor who refilled the paper towels and emptied the recycling and knew, from the specific archaeology of their trash, more about their inner lives than they would have been comfortable with. The hum is mine and the building's simultaneously. I have never been able to determine where one ends and the other begins, and I have stopped trying, the way you stop trying to separate the ringing in your ears from the silence of the room that contains it.

The second car door: 11:34. Yellow rain boots, the left one with a small tear at the ankle that had been repaired with duct tape and then re-repaired with something better, some kind of marine sealant, the work of someone who understood that certain objects must outlast their reasonable lifespans because the alternative is unbearable. Yuna. The competitive eater. She was wearing the same boots she'd worn the night she ate forty-six pierogies in the common room and wept afterward for reasons none of us understood, and I thought: *The boots have outlasted the friendship. The boots have outlasted the building's structural integrity. The boots may outlast the town.* She walked with the same purposeful heaviness, each step a small commitment to the ground, as though she had decided long ago that the earth deserved to know she was on it.

Third: 11:49. Two pairs of shoes, exiting the same car from different doors, walking at a pace calibrated to maintain exactly eight feet of separation. The twins — Marcus and Martin Okafor. Marcus wore white sneakers, immaculate, replaced quarterly; I knew this because I had found eleven pairs in the dormitory dumpster over the years, each pair surrendered at the first sign of wear. Martin wore boots with steel toes, work boots, though the work he did — freelance translation, mostly Yoruba and Igbo for legal depositions — required nothing heavier than slippers. They did not speak to each other as they crossed the parking lot. They did not acknowledge their synchronized stride, the mirror-image arm swing, the way they both paused at the same crack in the pavement and stepped over it with the same leg. They had not, to my knowledge, publicly acknowledged their relation in over seven years. They told people they had met in college. They told people they were friends. I had cleaned their shared bathroom for three semesters and knew things about their genetic overlap that I had no right to know and no one to tell.

Fourth: 12:01 AM. Sandals. In October. The toll booth operator — Neil — who had moved to the state to be closer to something he never named and farther from something he never discussed and had spent four years in a booth on I-80 handing change to strangers with the gentle, anonymous efficiency of a man who had found his calling in the transactional. His sandals were the kind that could be tightened or loosened with a single pull, the kind designed for quick exits, and he wore them in all weather, which Yuna had once told me — told me, directly, during one of the three conversations we had ever had — was because Neil had spent a period of his life unable to put on shoes fast enough, and now he refused to wear anything that could slow him down.

Five pairs of shoes. Five car doors over forty minutes. The building's hum shifted a quarter tone, and the water in my mop bucket rippled, and I pressed my palm flat against the basement wall and felt what I always feel, which is the pulse of a structure that has been alive longer than anyone has been willing to admit.

I tried to go upstairs.

This is worth describing because it is the thing that no one would believe if I described it, which is a sentence I have thought so many times it has worn a groove in my thinking, a rut that my mind settles into like a wheel into a track. I walked to the stairwell door at the end of the basement corridor. I turned the handle. The door opened onto another corridor — not the stairwell, not the lobby, but a hallway I had mopped ten minutes ago, recognizable by the still-drying arcs on the linoleum. I walked to the end of that hallway and opened the next door and found myself at the top of the basement stairs, facing down, as though the building had picked me up and rotated me 180 degrees without my legs noticing. I descended. I tried again. This time the stairwell door opened onto the supply closet, which is on the opposite side of the basement, and which contained my mop bucket, still humming in B-flat.

I sat down on the supply closet floor, next to the bucket and a shelf of industrial cleaning products and a fondue set with a straightened handle that I have kept for seven years, and I put my head in my hands.

The building was not imprisoning me. I need to be clear about that. It has never hurt me, never threatened me, never done anything that could not be interpreted, by a generous reader, as protective. What it does is *edit*. It restructures space to remove me from scenes I am not meant to be in, reroutes hallways so that I arrive where it wants me, opens doors onto rooms I did not choose. I am a sentence the building keeps moving to a different paragraph. I am a clause it cannot find the right place for. I have been accustomed to this for so long that what I felt, sitting on the supply closet floor, was not panic but the low, familiar ache of a man who has been gently excluded from everything that matters to him for so long that the exclusion has become a kind of home.

Above me, through the ceiling, I heard their voices for the first time in years. Muffled. Overlapping. The specific acoustic signature of five people in a room who are trying to talk without listening. The hum in my sternum thickened.

I picked up my mop.

## II.

She was sitting on an overturned bucket in the boiler room, talking to the main water line.

Not formally. Not as an incantation or a plea or anything that would suggest she had thought this through. She was sitting with her knees apart and her yellow boots planted on the concrete and her hands wrapped around a thermos of something that steamed, and she was speaking to the pipes the way you speak to a dog that has done something unexpected and possibly brilliant — with a mixture of respect and gentle bafflement.

"You don't have to do the gravity thing," she was saying when I came around the corner. "I get that you're making a point. But Marco's phone floated into the toilet and now he's acting like it's a federal emergency, and Gerald is doing the thing where he draws on whiteboards because it makes him feel like a person who matters, and honestly, if you could just — I don't know. Pull back a little. Just on the second floor. As a gesture."

The main water line, a four-inch copper artery that ran the full height of the building, made a sound like a human stomach after a long meal. Yuna nodded as though this were a response she had expected.

I had not chosen to come here. I had been mopping the basement corridor — or the corridor the building had decided was the basement corridor tonight — when the floor developed a slight grade, perhaps two degrees, barely perceptible, just enough to make the mop water pool at the eastern end, and I followed the grade the way I always follow the building's suggestions, because the building's suggestions are the only kind I have ever known how to accept, and the grade led me to the boiler room door, which was open, which it never is, and there she was.

She looked up. She did not startle.

"Dmitri," she said.

My name in her mouth was a thing I had not been prepared for, despite having had, conservatively, eleven years to prepare. It was the same as it had always been — slightly compressed, the first syllable swallowed, the second syllable given more weight than it deserved, as though the important part of me were in the second half. Which perhaps it was.

"Yuna."

"You still work here."

"Yes."

"The building's condemned."

"Yes."

She studied me with the evaluative attention of a woman who had spent her professional life assessing physical quantities — how much was in front of her, how much she could take in, how much remained. I had seen her compete once, on a video Marcus had shown everyone in the common room. She ate with a mechanical precision that was, I thought then and think now, a form of respect for the eaten. She did not waste. She did not perform. She consumed with the seriousness of someone who understood that the world offered itself to you in portions and your job was to not refuse.

"Sit down," she said.

I sat. There was no second bucket, so I sat on the floor, which put me below her, which was correct and familiar. The concrete was warm. The boiler room was the building's sternum, its thermal center, and the warmth that radiated from the floor felt intentional, as though the building were offering the only comfort it knew how to give.

"They're all upstairs arguing about the gravitational anomaly," she said. "Gerald's mapped three floors. He's using colored markers. He looks almost happy."

"That sounds right."

"The twins are standing on opposite sides of every room pretending they arrived separately."

"That also sounds right."

"And Neil has identified six exits and tested four of them and determined that two don't lead where they should." She took a sip from her thermos. "He's handling it well. He likes knowing where the exits are even when the exits are wrong."

We sat in the specific silence of two people who have circled each other for years without ever establishing the terms of their acquaintance. I had cleaned up after her meals. She had once left a note on the common room table that said *Thank you for always making this place feel like a place* — unsigned, but I knew her handwriting, just as I knew everyone's handwriting in that building, because the things people leave behind are a language and I am, if I am anything, a reader.

"It's not angry," she said. She was looking at the pipes again. "The building. Everyone keeps talking about it like it's a threat. Gerald's using words like 'anomalous pressure system' and Neil keeps saying 'structural compromise.' But that's not what this is."

The hum in my sternum shifted. A half step up. I pressed my hand against my chest as though I could hold it steady.

"What is it?" I asked. I knew. I wanted to hear her say it.

"It's *asking*." She looked at me. Her eyes were the color of the building's original brickwork — a warm, oxidized brown — and I catalogued this detail because I catalogue every detail about all of them, because it is the only form of participation I have ever allowed myself. "Can't you feel it? It's been asking for a long time."

I could feel it. I had been feeling it for eleven years. I had felt it the night they moved in and the pipes sang a chord I had never heard and the water pressure in every faucet on the third floor fluctuated in a rhythm that matched, I later determined, the collective resting heart rate of the five of them asleep. I had felt it the night of the fondue set, when the building's hot water went from 120 degrees to 97 — body temperature, precisely — and stayed there for a week, as though the plumbing were trying to hold someone. I had felt it every night since the condemnation, as the empty building's pipes cycled through sounds that were not sounds but *sentences*, articulations of loneliness so specific that I sometimes sat in the basement with my back against the main line and wept, not for myself, though there was that, but for the impossible position of a consciousness that could feel but not move, that could speak but not be heard, that was made of copper and iron and the salvaged organs of three demolished buildings and had somehow, through combination or accident or miracle, learned to *want*.

I could have told her. I came within one sentence of it — I opened my mouth and the first word was forming, something with the shape of *There are logs*, when a valve above us turned, a quarter-rotation, and the sound it made was not mechanical but deliberate, a throat being cleared, and the floor tilted — two degrees, maybe three — and my hands went out to steady myself, and the word dissolved, and the moment passed the way every moment has ever passed for me: with the thing unsaid intact, lodged in my chest beside the hum.

Yuna watched me not speak. Something moved across her face — not surprise, not disappointment, but a kind of recognition, as though she had seen people not-speak before and had developed a taxonomy for the varieties of silence.

"Okay," she said. She did not press. She turned back to the pipes.
