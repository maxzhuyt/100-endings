# The Midwives' Water

## I.

The water took the light wrong.

I'd set the pot on at quarter to eleven, same as always — biggest pot Darla Caudill owned, twenty-quart, dented on one side where her boy had taken it sledding. The stove was gas and it ticked three times before it caught. I'd done this so many nights in so many kitchens that my body didn't consult me anymore. Hands filled the pot. Hands set it on the ring. Hands laid out the kit on the towel I'd ironed that morning and rolled tight as a bandage. My sister Bess was upstairs with Darla, humming something and checking her dilation, and I could hear Darla's breathing change — that low, caught sound, like wind shifting in a flue — and I knew we had maybe two hours, maybe less. Third baby. Thirds come when they want.

I was standing at the counter tearing a length of string when I looked at the pot, and the water was wrong.

Not the water itself. The surface of it, just before the boil, when it was still enough to hold a reflection but hot enough to blur it. There was a sheen. An iridescence. Like a slick of gasoline on a puddle, except thinner — a membrane of color that shifted from green to violet as I moved my head. I'd seen it before. I knew I'd seen it before. Maybe a dozen times over the past year, in a dozen different kitchens, always from the same wells and springs that fed this side of the hollow. I had noticed it each time the way you notice that a window sticks or a step creaks: you file it somewhere behind the thing you're actually doing, and the thing I was actually doing was delivering a baby.

The water boiled. The sheen broke up into nothing. I poured it into the basin and brought it upstairs.

Darla's bedroom was small and overheated and smelled of the lavender oil Bess had the mothers dab on their wrists. The overhead bulb had no shade. Darla was on her side, gripping the headboard with one hand, and Bess was behind her with both palms flat on the small of her back, pressing hard, her lips still moving with the hum. They looked like figures in a painting, the kind where the light picks out hands and faces and lets the rest go dark.

"How far?" I said.

"She's ready," Bess said.

Darla looked at me with that expression I had learned to recognize after all these years — not pain exactly, not fear, but the understanding that she was about to do something enormous and that the doing of it was already underway and could not be taken back. I've seen that expression on a hundred faces. It has never stopped scaring me. I knelt at the bedside and pulled on my gloves and spoke to her in the voice I keep for this, the low one, the one that Bess says sounds like our mother's.

The birth itself went the way births go when they go well. Darla was strong and practiced and her body knew the sequence. I guided the head. Bess held the space. At twelve forty-one in the morning, a girl came into the world in Caleb Hollow, Marrow County, and she cried immediately, which is the sound that empties every midwife's held breath.

I laid her on Darla's chest and waited for the cord to stop pulsing and clamped it and cut it and then I did what I always do, which is look. I look at everything. I count fingers and toes and check the palate and the fontanelles and the hips and the spine and the way the eyes track toward a voice. I have a system. Bess is the one who holds and hums and makes the mother feel sovereign. I am the one who inspects, who catalogs, who keeps the record. We divided the labor years ago without discussing it, the way twins do.

The girl's left hand was wrong.

The third and fourth fingers were fused from the base to the second knuckle. Not webbed — fused. The skin over the join was smooth and seamless, as if the fingers had never been intended to be separate, as if the blueprint had been drawn that way on purpose. I flexed them gently. Two bones where there should have been four, merged clean as a grafted branch. The symmetry of it unsettled me more than the fusion itself. It did not look like an accident. It looked like a revision.

Darla didn't see it yet. She was kissing the crown of the baby's head and weeping with relief, and the girl's fused hand was curled under her chin, hidden. I looked at Bess. Bess looked at the hand. Her expression didn't change but something behind it shifted, the way the water's surface had shifted in the pot.

I finished the exam. I delivered the placenta. I checked Darla for tearing and found none. I swaddled the baby and gave her back and helped Darla latch her for the first feed. Through all of this I was performing the calculations I always perform, running back through every step of my care during Darla's pregnancy: the herbs I'd recommended, the position of the baby at thirty-six weeks, the slight anemia I'd noted and corrected with nettle tea and liver. Had I missed something? Had I been rough during the version at thirty-two weeks when the baby was breech? Had I pressed too hard? Was there a moment — some moment I'd forgotten or misremembered — where my hands had done something wrong?

I knew, in the part of my mind that was neither rational nor irrational but simply the engine of my guilt, that this was mine. Not mine to claim, exactly. Mine to carry. I had attended Darla through all three trimesters. I had been the one to palpate and listen and advise. If the baby had an anomaly, then the anomaly had formed under my care, in the waters I had told her to drink, in the body I had told her was healthy. The fault might not be direct. But the responsibility was. I had been the intermediary between this woman and the world, and the world had marked her child, and I had not seen it coming.

Bess came back from the bathroom, drying her hands on her jeans. She stood in the doorway and looked at Darla nursing and said, quietly, so that only I could hear:

"That's the third this spring."

I didn't answer. I was writing in my notebook — the private one, the one with the marbled cover that I kept in the bottom of my kit bag. I wrote the date, the time, the outcome, the weight, the Apgar, and then, in the margin, in the small cramped hand I used for the things I didn't want anyone else to read: *Left hand, 3rd/4th digits fused. Syndactyly, osseous. My care throughout. Add to ledger.* The word *ledger* was my word for it. Not a medical term. A moral one. The ongoing, private accounting of every birth I had attended where something had gone wrong, however minor, however unrelated to anything I had done or failed to do. The ledger was long. It had started when I was twenty-three, with a stillbirth I could not have prevented but that I absorbed anyway, the way a wick absorbs flame. That was sixteen years ago. The ledger had become, over time, not a record but a practice — a devotional, almost. A way of saying to whatever listened: *I know. I know what I am. I know what my hands are capable of, even when they do everything right.*

Bess knew about the notebook. She had never asked to read it. She had said, once, years ago, that keeping it was like picking at a wound to keep it from closing, and I had said yes, that's correct, and she had looked at me with an expression I still can't name, and we had never discussed it again.

## II.

The map had been on the wall for at least two months before I saw it. That's not quite true. I had seen it. I had looked at it the way you look at any familiar wall decoration — the way I looked at our mother's cross-stitch sampler above the door, or the 1986 calendar from the Farm Bureau that neither of us had ever taken down. Bess had tacked things to the wall of our shared room since we were girls. Pressed flowers. Postcards from people who'd left the county. Clippings from the Marrow County Advocate. I registered the map as another of her projects and I let my eyes slide past it because I was tired, because I was always tired, because the spring had brought more births than any spring I could remember and every one of them had taken something out of me that sleep could not put back.

It was a Saturday in May. I was at the table shelling beans, because I shell beans when I can't sit still and I can never sit still. Bess came in from the garden with dirt on her knees and a look on her face that I recognized from childhood — determined, nervous, already braced for my refection. She poured two glasses of cold coffee from the pot on the counter and set one in front of me and sat down across the table and said, "Come look at the map."

"I've seen it."

"You haven't looked at it."

She was right and we both knew it, so I put down the beans and followed her to the wall.

It was hand-drawn on a sheet of butcher paper three feet wide, done in Bess's careful draftsman's hand, which was better than mine and always had been. She'd traced the ridgelines and the creek beds and the roads from a USGS quad and then she'd added the things the government maps don't show: the houses, marked with small squares; the springs and wells, marked with blue circles; the cemetery, the churches, the school. The lithium mine — Appalachian Lithium Solutions, which had started extraction six years ago in what used to be the Saul Gentry tract — was a large gray rectangle at the top of the watershed, crosshatched to indicate the tailings ponds.

Below the mine, descending along Little Caney Creek and its tributaries, were pins. Dozens of pins. Color-coded.

Red for cleft palate. Yellow for syndactyly. Green for cardiac anomaly. Blue for neural tube defect. White for stillbirth.

Each pin had a tiny paper flag with a date and initials.

The pins clustered. They clustered the way starlings cluster on a wire — not uniformly, but densely, irrefutably, in a pattern that was visible from across the room. They clustered along the watershed below the tailings ponds. They thinned as you moved east toward Dry Fork, where the water came from a different source. They vanished entirely on the ridge where the Pentecostal families caught their water from rain cisterns.

I stood in front of the map for a long time. I could feel Bess behind me, waiting.

"Where'd you get the birth data?" I said.

"Our records. Yours and mine. Going back five years."

"You went through my notebooks?"

"I went through the practice log. The one we share. I didn't touch your private one."

"And the water data?"

"There's no water data. That's the point. Nobody's testing. I asked the health department in February and they said the mine's in compliance with its discharge permit. I asked what the permit allows and they sent me a PDF with half the numbers redacted."

"That doesn't mean —"

"Look at the pins, Cora."

I looked at the pins. I had been looking at the pins. The problem was not that I couldn't see the pattern. The problem was that the pattern was incompatible with the story I had been telling myself for years. If the anomalies were environmental — if they were the water, the creek, the tailings leaching lithium and arsenic and God knows what else into the aquifer — then they were not *mine.* They were not entries in my ledger. They were not evidence of my failure or my insufficient hands or the curse I had decided, at twenty-three, that I carried in me like a gene. If Bess was right, then I had been wrong — not wrong in my technique, which I had always known, in the rational part of my mind, was sound — but wrong in my *interpretation.* Wrong in the way I had read the world. Wrong in the way I had assigned cause.

And if I was wrong about that, then the notebook — the sixteen years of private penance, the ledger of harm I had maintained like a monk maintains a fast — was not devotion. It was vanity. The most selfish kind: the kind that lets you stand at the center of a disaster and believe you are its author, because authorship, even of catastrophe, is a form of control, and control is easier to bear than helplessness.

"Three this spring," I said.

"Six this year. Fourteen in two years. Before the mine, our anomaly rate was below the county average. I checked."

I went back to the table and picked up the beans. My hands were shaking and I didn't want her to see.

"We have to do something," Bess said from the wall.

"We have to be sure."

"We won't be sure until someone tests the water, and no one will test the water until someone makes noise, and no one will make noise because everyone in this hollow either works for the mine or is kin to someone who does. That's why it has to come from us. We're the midwives. We're the ones who see what's happening first."

I shelled a bean. The pod split clean. The beans inside were white and smooth and perfect.

"Let me think," I said.

But I was not thinking. I was recalculating. I was going back through the ledger in my mind, entry by entry, and asking each one the question I had never permitted myself to ask: *What if it wasn't me? What if it was never me?* And the answer that came back was not relief. It was terror. Because if it was never me, then I had spent sixteen years genuflecting to the wrong altar, and the real thing — the thing that was actually hurting these babies — had been in the water the whole time, and I had let the sheen on the pot become background, and I had let my guilt do the work of looking away, and the looking away had a name, and the name was *complicity,* and the complicity was worse than any sin I had ever confessed in my notebook, because at least the things I wrote in the notebook I believed I had done with my own hands.
