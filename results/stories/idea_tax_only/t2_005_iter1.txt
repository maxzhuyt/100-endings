# The Legend of Kenji Sato

## I.

The check engine light has been on since Winnemucca, which was in October, and it is now either late March or early April depending on whether you trust the little calendar I keep rubber-banded to the sun visor or the actual progression of the natural world, which here in the parking lot of the Reno Oriental Wellness and Martial Arts Center—"oriental" being Kenji's word, not mine, selected because he says it repels the type of client who would complain about it and attracts the type of client who has neither the vocabulary nor the inclination to interrogate anything, which is, operationally speaking, the point—where I am sitting with the engine running and my seat reclined to the second notch, the one that says *I'm waiting for someone* rather than the third notch, which says *I live here*, anyway out here in the parking lot the progression of the natural world mostly means the progression of the wind, and the wind says it could be any month at all. It could be the Pleistocene. It could be last Tuesday. The wind in Reno has no opinion about your calendar.

Kenji has been inside for two hours and eleven minutes.

I know this because I have a Casio F-91W, the terrorist watch, the one the CIA used to flag detainees with at Guantánamo, which Kenji bought me at a truck stop as a joke that neither of us has ever fully resolved into comedy or sincerity. I wear it every job. I check it with a frequency that probably does qualify as some form of radicalization, though in my case the ideology is just waiting. I have become a radical waiter. I wait with the fervor of the newly converted. Two hours and eleven minutes, and across the parking lot the Subway is closing—the girl in the visor wiping down the sneeze guard with the glazed, beautiful efficiency of the last worker on a late shift, her movements backlit and hypnotic, and I have to stop watching because Kenji once told me that desire observed from a parked car is the saddest frequency on the electromagnetic spectrum, and I told him that wasn't a real thing, and he said, "Daryl, everything you feel in that car is a real thing. That's the problem," which is exactly the kind of observation that makes him both the best friend I have ever had and a person I would like, on occasion, to hit with a shoe.

He is inside with a woman named—or at least called—Marina, who is the girlfriend and de facto bookkeeper of Vincent Delacroix, who runs or co-runs or at least violently oversees most of the unlicensed gambling infrastructure in three counties, and when I say Kenji is "inside with her" I mean he is in the process of making love to her, and when I say "making love" I am not being euphemistic or arch, I am using the term Kenji uses, the term he insists on, the term he once spent forty-five minutes distinguishing from "fucking," "having sex," "hooking up," "hitting it," and "laying pipe" in a Denny's booth at 3 AM while I ate a Grand Slam and contemplated the Grand Slam of my own emotional life, which—let's be honest, let's just get this out there because the night is long and the engine is running and the Subway girl has turned off the open sign and is now just a silhouette performing the sacred closing rites of American sandwich commerce—which has not involved any form of the act in question, in any of its terminological variants, for going on nineteen months now, since Carla left, since Carla left and took the exact half of the furniture I would have chosen and also the dog, who was mine, who was *mine*, a fact I could have proven with vet records if the situation had escalated to a level requiring vet records, but it did not, because the situation did not escalate at all, it simply ended, the way a song on the radio ends when you pull into a parking lot and turn off the engine except in my case I am always in a parking lot and I never turn off the engine.

So. Kenji is making love to Marina so that Marina will, in the aftermath, in the specific post-coital state Kenji calls *ma*—which is a Japanese aesthetic concept meaning roughly "negative space" or "the pause that gives form to what surrounds it" and which Kenji applies to the interval after mutual orgasm when the self is briefly ungoverned and the truth moves through a person the way wind moves through an open room—so that Marina will, in that state, share what she knows about the routing numbers and the offshore accounts and the way Vincent Delacroix launders money through a chain of self-storage facilities in Sparks and south Reno.

This is what Kenji does. This is what Kenji is.

I should say that more clearly. Kenji Sato is a ninja. He is also, formerly, a PhD candidate in the philosophy department at UC Davis, where he completed everything except his dissertation, which was on Watsuji Tetsurō's ethics of *aidagara*, or "betweenness," the idea that the self does not exist prior to relationship but is constituted by it, that you are not a person who *enters* relationships but a relationship that occasionally *produces* a person—which, when you think about it, when you really sit with it at 1:47 AM in a strip-mall parking lot while your best friend is inside generating mutual climax as an intelligence-gathering methodology, is either the most profound thing a human being has ever argued or the kind of thought that gets you kicked out of a doctoral program, which is also what happened.

What happened between the program and the strip mall is a story I have assembled from fragments over four years of these nights, and the fragments do not connect sequentially so much as they accrete, the way a reef accretes, each piece of coral dead and calcium-hard and yet somehow, in aggregate, a living structure. I know he trained in two martial arts I can't pronounce in a place in Shizuoka he won't name. I know he spent a year in Reno doing private security for someone who turned out to be adjacent to someone who turned out to be Vincent Delacroix. I know the dojo started as a genuine school—he teaches children's judo on Saturdays, and those kids love him with a ferocity that makes me want to weep in the dairy aisle—and became a front, or not a front exactly, a *dual-use facility*, the way a cathedral is dual-use if you are also running a soup kitchen out of the nave.

And I know about the code. What I call the code. What Kenji calls—when he calls it anything, which is rarely, because the code is to Kenji what water is to a fish, an environment so total it does not require a name—what Kenji calls *the way things are done*.

The code is this: To extract information from a target through sexual intimacy, the intimacy must be real. The orgasm must be mutual. Not performed. Not approximated. Not the civilized theater of completion that, as Kenji once observed without any apparent cruelty, constitutes the majority of heterosexual intercourse in the industrialized world. Mutual. Verified—though I still don't fully understand the verification methodology and my one attempt to ask produced a silence so deep and so architecturally complete that I felt like I had walked into a room in a Japanese temple that wasn't empty but was *made of* emptiness.

If the orgasm is mutual, then the information shared afterward—in the *ma*, in the open room—is freely given. It is testimony, not confession. It is a gift between two people who have, however briefly, however transactionally, however insanely, genuinely met each other. And information freely given is honorable to use.

If the orgasm is not mutual, the contract is void. The information is coerced. The intelligence is dirty. You leave. You eat the cost. You start over.

I am his alibi. I am the guy in the car. When someone—a husband, a bodyguard, a neighbor with a Ring doorbell and an anxiety disorder—asks where Kenji was on such and such a night, I have a story. We were at the movies. We went fishing at Pyramid Lake. We drove to Fallon to look at a truck on Craigslist and the truck was gone and we ate at a Carl's Jr. and drove back. I have become an architect of plausible evenings, a novelist of the mundane, and the thing about being very good at constructing a fake version of a night is that it makes you exquisitely aware of how empty the real version is. The real version is: I sat in a parking lot. I checked my watch. I thought about Carla. I thought about the Subway girl. I thought about Kenji, inside, doing the thing I cannot imagine and cannot stop imagining—not the sex, or not only the sex, but the *other part*, the part after, the part where another human being turns to you with their defenses dissolved and tells you something true.

Two hours and fourteen minutes.

The check engine light pulses. The wind pushes a Wendy's bag across the asphalt in a trajectory that, if you were the kind of person who found meaning in the movements of parking-lot detritus, and I am, I absolutely am that person, I am the president of that person's fan club, could be read as either a spiral or an escape.

## II.

The first time I fully understood what Kenji did—not the abstract version, not the "I do information work" version he'd used for the first several months of our friendship, back when I was still technically employed as an adjuster for Far West Property & Casualty and our entire relationship was me coming to the dojo twice a week for a beginner's self-defense class I had signed up for after Carla left because I had a theory, since discredited, that physical confidence would translate into emotional repair—the first time I understood the full scope of it was the Molina job, and I know this because it was the night I ate the worst patty melt of my life and also the night I realized my best friend was either a genius or a holy fool or the most elaborately self-deceived sexual operative in the history of the Great Basin, and I have since come to believe he is all three, in exactly the same way a prism is all three sides simultaneously.

Hector Molina was a mid-level enforcer for a man named Turk, which was not his name but which was the only name anyone used, the way you only call a hurricane by its assigned name and not by its meteorological designation because the point is to be afraid and then to move on. Turk operated out of a suite of rooms above a bar on Fourth Street that Kenji said smelled like a humidifier filled with bourbon, and Turk had, through a series of intermediaries, expressed interest in private martial-arts instruction for his nephew, who was either sixteen or twenty-two depending on who you asked and who, in any case, never materialized, because the nephew was a pretext and the real reason Turk sent Hector Molina to the dojo was to assess Kenji as a potential recruit for—I am still not sure what. Muscle, maybe. Or an assassin. Kenji has the build. Kenji has the face. Kenji has the quality of stillness that makes certain men think *this person could kill for me* and makes certain other men—me, for instance—think *this person will never need me the way I need him, and I have to sit with that*.

But what Turk didn't account for—what no one ever accounts for, because who would, because the scenario only exists inside the very particular moral architecture Kenji has built out of Watsuji and bushidō and whatever else he absorbed in Shizuoka and whatever else he was *born* absorbing, the way certain minerals absorb certain wavelengths of light and emit others and that's why they're the color they are—what no one accounted for was that Hector Molina was not precisely straight.

I say not precisely because Kenji's taxonomy, which he once outlined for me on a napkin at Anna's Diner using a system of overlapping circles that looked like a Venn diagram designed by someone who had read every human being's sexual autobiography simultaneously, does not operate on the straight-gay axis. It operates on what he calls the axis of *receptivity*, which is not about who you want but about the degree to which you are capable, at a given moment in your life, of being *met*. And Hector Molina—twenty-nine, built like a fire hydrant wrapped in denim and sadness, with a tattoo of the Virgin of Guadalupe on his left forearm and a habit of cracking his knuckles in sequences of three—Hector Molina was, by Kenji's assessment, the most receptive person he had ever encountered. A tuning fork in the shape of a man. Vibrating at a frequency only Kenji could hear.

"He walked in," Kenji told me—this was at Anna's, 2:40 AM, Kenji eating nothing, Kenji drinking black tea, Kenji sitting with the posture of a man who had been manufactured in a posture factory and then briefly loaned to the world of diner booths—"and I knew."

"Knew what?"

"That he was ready."

"Ready for what?"

And Kenji looked at me, and the look was patient the way geology is patient, the way a river is patient with the rock it's cutting, and he said: "To be known."

Now. This is the point in any retelling where I have to be honest about the narratorial situation, which is that I was not inside the dojo when any of what followed occurred. I was in the car. I was in the parking lot. I was listening to a podcast about the Donner Party because I had developed, during the long hours of automotive waiting that constitute my vocational existence, an obsessive interest in stories about people trapped in small spaces with dwindling resources, and if the psychological implications of that are obvious then the psychological implications of that are obvious and I'm not going to insult either of us by pretending I don't see them. So everything I'm about to describe is reconstructed from what Kenji told me afterward, filtered through my own imagination, sharpened by my own longing, distorted by my own desperate, embarrassing, unrequested fascination with the mechanics of how two people go from being strangers to being—even briefly, even transactionally—not strangers. If that makes me an unreliable narrator, fine. Show me a reliable one and I'll show you someone who's never sat in a parking lot at 3 AM wondering what it sounds like when another person decides to trust you.

Here is what happened, or what I believe happened, or what I have built from Kenji's silences and Kenji's fragments the way an archaeologist builds a civilization from pot shards:

Kenji taught Hector. He actually taught him. This is important. This is the part that everyone—the FBI, later, and the various law-enforcement-adjacent people who would eventually enter and complicate and ruin everything—this is the part they never understood. Kenji didn't fake the instruction to get to the seduction. The instruction *was* the seduction. Or rather—and I'm going to get this wrong, I always get this wrong, because the distinction Kenji draws is so fine it's basically a hair on a hair on a hair—the instruction was the process by which Kenji and the target entered a shared physical grammar. A vocabulary of the body. You spend two hours teaching a man to fall—Kenji started Hector with *ukemi*, the art of receiving a throw, the art of meeting the ground without resistance—and by the end of those two hours, you and that man have a language. Your hands have been on his body in contexts of care. You have caught him. You have corrected the angle of his hip with two fingers and a word. You have, if you are Kenji, done this with a totality of attention that most people have never received from anyone, including their parents, including their lovers, especially their lovers, because most lovers—Kenji said this once and I felt it land in my sternum like a dart—"most lovers are performing a memory of desire, not experiencing desire itself."

The sessions continued. Three a week, then four. Hector told Turk the training was going well. Turk, who conceptualized all activity as either useful or not useful the way a machine conceptualizes voltage, was satisfied. Kenji, during this period, was doing what he always does, which is gathering the peripheral data—the things a person reveals when they don't know they're revealing anything. The way Hector parked (always backing in, always nearest the exit—a man ready to leave). The fact that Hector's phone rang constantly and he never answered it during training, which meant he honored the space, which meant he was capable of honoring a space, which meant there was something in him that wanted a space worth honoring.
