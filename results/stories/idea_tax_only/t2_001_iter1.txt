# The Notebook

---

## I.

The man across from me is losing an argument with his phone.

I know this the way I know most things about strangers — from the outside in. His thumb has gone white where it grips the case. The tendon in his neck, the one that runs from the clavicle to just behind the ear, has thickened into a cable that twitches each time his mouth stretches wide around what I assume are the important words, the ones people always think will land differently if they're pushed out harder. His free hand chops air — down, down, sideways, down — the rhythm of a man building the walls of an argument he already knows won't hold. There's a shine on his lower lip. Spit, probably. People who argue into phones do not realize what their mouths do, how the lips go loose and wet, how the jaw unhinges a little wider than speech requires, as if the body is reaching for something that language can't quite deliver.

Behind the counter, the receptionist has developed a flinch. It comes at intervals I can map: the man's hand chops down, holds, chops down again — and on the third chop, when his chin lifts and the tendons go taut and his chest wall expands with what must be a full breath poured into a single syllable, her shoulders contract upward by maybe three millimeters and her pen hesitates over whatever form she is pretending to fill out. She waits. His chest deflates. The pen moves again. She has calibrated herself to his cycle without knowing it, the way a person walking beside a busy road adjusts their stride to the gaps between trucks.

I've been watching for eleven minutes. I know this because the clock on the wall behind her — institutional, white-faced, the kind with a red second hand that moves in small shudders rather than a sweep — reads 9:47 and I sat down at 9:36. There are four other people in the plastic chairs. A woman in a denim jacket has been filling out the same line on a form for the entire eleven minutes, her pen tracing and retracing the same three or four words until the ink has gone shiny and thick and will probably tear through the paper if she doesn't stop. A boy — eighteen, maybe nineteen, a tattoo on his neck still raw and scabbed at the edges — sits with his legs wide and his hands clasped between his knees and his eyes fixed on the middle distance in the way people fix their eyes when they are constructing a version of themselves that does not include being in this room. A man in a canvas jacket that smells — I can smell it from two chairs away — like fryer grease and cigarette smoke and the particular human sourness that collects in fabric that has not been washed but has been rained on and dried on a body many times, sits with his head tipped back against the wall, mouth open. Sleeping, or performing sleep. His eyelids flutter at irregular intervals, which means performing.

The phone man's mouth is still moving. His lower lip catches on his upper teeth — an *f*, probably, or the start of a *v* — and then his jaw drops and the back of his tongue arches and there is a brief flash of pink before his lips stretch wide and flatten, which could be a lot of things, most of them useless to me at this distance and angle. Lip-reading is not what people think it is. It is not subtitles. It is a probabilistic exercise performed against a shifting field of ambiguity, and most of the time the probability is low and the ambiguity is total and what you get is not language but a kind of meat-puppetry that suggests language the way a cloud suggests a horse. You see the horse. You know it's a cloud.

His hand chops down one final time. He pulls the phone away from his head, stares at its face the way people stare at objects that have betrayed them, and puts it in his jacket pocket. His jaw works sideways. He looks at the clock. He looks at the receptionist. He looks at me, briefly, and his gaze slides off the way gazes do — no hostility, no recognition, just the frictionless passage of attention across a surface that offers nothing to grip.

I have been looked at this way for thirty-one years in a world built entirely for people who can hear. There is a particular quality to the gaze of a person who clocks you as someone they will never need to exchange information with. It lasts less than a second. It involves a fractional relaxation of the muscles around the eyes — the opposite of what happens when a person recognizes someone they'll have to talk to, which is a subtle tightening, a gathering, a readiness. When the muscles relax, it means the social calculator has run its numbers and returned zero. You are furniture. You are part of the room's thermal mass.

The receptionist's mouth forms shapes in my direction. I watch her lips part and come together, part and come together, the jaw descending twice on what are probably two-syllable words. She might be calling my name. She might be calling someone else's. I stand because three of the four other people do not, and the boy with the raw tattoo is already looking at me with the peripheral awareness of someone who has noticed I haven't responded to whatever cue they all responded to, and his brow comes down a quarter-inch, not confusion yet, just the pre-confusion, the body's first draft of a question it hasn't bothered to fully form.

I walk to the counter. I point to my ear and shake my head — the gesture I have made ten thousand times, the shorthand, the semaphore. The receptionist's face does the thing. It is always the same thing. A tiny flinch followed by an overcompensation: the smile widens, the head tilts, the eyebrows lift into the configuration that means *oh, oh, I see, let me help you, I'm a good person*. She reaches for a pen and a sticky note — yellow, three inches square, the whole of our interaction about to be compressed into a space designed for reminding someone to buy milk — and writes with the pen held at an angle I can read upside down.

*Name?*

I take the pen. I write my name. She nods with too much enthusiasm and holds up a finger — *one moment* — and turns and walks through a door behind the counter, and I stand there with the pen and the sticky note and the fluorescent light pressing down on my shoulders like a hand that is not hostile, exactly, but is very certain that I should stay where I am.

## II.

The workforce reintegration center is on the eighth floor of a building that seems to exist in a permanent state of apology for itself. The elevator shudders between floors. The hallway carpet is the color of a headache — not any specific color, really, but the brownish-greenish-grayish nothing-color that institutions choose because it will not show stains and will not provoke feelings and will quietly absorb whatever is spilled on it, bodily or otherwise, and keep going. The overhead lights are the long fluorescent tube kind that flicker at a frequency I can see, a faint pulsing in the periphery that most people probably filter out because they're listening to the buzz the tubes make and the buzz somehow tells their brains *this is normal, this is fine*, but I have no buzz to smooth it over, and so it pulses, and the hallway pulses, and I walk through it.

The door has a laminated sign: **COMMUNITY REINTEGRATION & EMPLOYMENT PATHWAYS — SUITE 804**. Below it, a smaller sign, handwritten on cardstock in marker that is already fading: *All participants must sign in at front desk. NO EXCEPTIONS.* The *NO EXCEPTIONS* is underlined twice. Someone has added, in pencil, a small drawing of a frowning face. Someone else has attempted to erase it and failed.

Inside: a large room divided by cubicle partitions into the suggestion of smaller rooms, none of which achieve actual privacy, all of which achieve the particular claustrophobia of a space that wants to be open and helpful but has been furnished by someone whose budget ran out at the folding chairs. There is a circle of folding chairs in the center. There are desks along the walls with ancient computers whose monitors are the thick, deep kind, the ones that take up more desk than they illuminate. There is a whiteboard on the far wall with writing I will read later. There is a fridge — beige, humming if it hums, scarred with magnets and sticky notes and one photograph of a cat wearing a birthday hat that has been there so long its edges have curled and yellowed, so that the cat looks like it is celebrating its birthday inside a sepia-toned memory of better days.

The sticky notes on the fridge. I read them all, because I read everything — it is not a choice, it is a condition; when your primary sensory channel is visual, text is not background, text is foreground, text is the loudest voice in any room:

*THIS SHELF IS FOR TASHA'S LUNCH ONLY. DO NOT TOUCH.*

*Whoever took my creamer — I know who you are.*

*Please do not microwave fish. This is a shared space. Thank you.*

*SOMEONE has been eating my yogurt. There are cameras. — Mgmt*

There are no cameras. I can see every corner of the ceiling from where I stand, and there is nothing but stained acoustic tile and the occasional vent.

On the far wall, above the whiteboard, a poster. It shows an eagle in flight against a sunset of the kind that exists only in motivational posters and low-budget church bulletins — the sky a gradient from tangerine to magenta, the clouds backlit to a golden trim, the eagle frozen in a posture of soaring determination that actual eagles achieve only briefly between periods of sitting in trees looking annoyed. Below the eagle, in a font that suggests both authority and compassion:

**INTEGRITY IS DOING THE RIGHT THING WHEN NO ONE IS WATCHING.**

I look at the eagle. The eagle looks at the distance. Neither of us is doing the right thing. I am here because a judge said I must be. The eagle is here because a graphic designer in 2004 dragged it from a stock-photo library and dropped it onto a gradient and thought: *yes, this will inspire the downtrodden.*

Beside it, another poster: a kitten dangling from a branch. **HANG IN THERE!** The kitten's eyes are wide with what the poster intends as plucky determination but which, if you look at actual cat body language — the dilated pupils, the flattened whiskers, the rigid claw-extension — is abject terror. The kitten is not hanging in there. The kitten is in the grip of a mortal fear that its small body will fail it and it will plummet into whatever lies below the frame, which in this context is a room full of people with ankle monitors and mandatory attendance and a shared fridge that has become a theater of war.

A man in a short-sleeve dress shirt — white, translucent enough at the shoulders to show the ghost of an undershirt beneath — approaches me. His lanyard says KEVIN and below that, in smaller type, PROGRAM COORDINATOR. Kevin has the build of someone who was athletic fifteen years and forty pounds ago and who still moves with the echo of that athleticism, the shoulders back, the stride a little too long, so that his body is always arriving somewhere slightly before his gut catches up. He smiles. It is a real smile, as far as it goes — the cheek muscles engage, the crow's feet crinkle — but it is a professional-grade real smile, the kind that activates on contact and deactivates the moment the person it was aimed at looks away. I have watched thousands of these smiles. They are the standard-issue expression of people whose job is to process other people, and they mean: *I will be warm to you for exactly as long as this interaction requires, and not one second longer.*

Kevin's mouth moves. I watch his lips. There's a *w* sound — the lips push forward, round — and then something wide and open. *Welcome*, maybe. His arm extends, palm up, gesturing toward the circle of folding chairs where five people are already sitting in the various postures of mandatory attendance: slumped, rigid, performed-casual, checked-out, and — in the case of a woman in the far chair, the one closest to the wall — vibrating with a low-grade fury that I can read in the locked jaw and the white-knuckled grip she has on her own forearms, folded across her chest like a barricade.

I sit. The chair's metal is cold through my jeans. The circle has the geometry of a confessional — all eyes facing inward, no cover, no corners — and I can feel the room's attention land on me and slide off in the same way the phone man's gaze did earlier. I am new. I am brief. I am absorbed into the room's inventory of bodies and then filed under *not relevant*.

A man across the circle picks at the cuticle of his left thumb. He has been at it for a while. The skin around the nail is raw, a crescent of irritated pink, and as I watch, he works a small flap of skin between his thumbnail and forefinger and pulls — slowly, steadily, the way you pull a thread from a hem — until it separates with a tiny elastic snap I can see in the recoil of the skin, a red dot blooming where the flap was. He does not look at his hand. He looks at Kevin, who has taken the chair at the head of the circle — if a circle can have a head, which this one does, because Kevin has positioned his chair six inches forward of the curve, breaking the geometry just enough to assert hierarchy without admitting it — and whose mouth is moving in the steady, rhythmic way of someone who is delivering a speech they have delivered many times. The lips rise and fall. The jaw maps a topography of familiar syllables. *Pathways*, I think I see. *Opportunity*. *Accountability*. The big words, the poster words, the ones that mean nothing when they come from a man in a translucent shirt in a room with a fridge full of stolen yogurt.

The cuticle man pulls another flap. The angry woman grips her forearms tighter. A man in a hoodie whose drawstring has been removed — a detail I note, a detail with a history I can guess — rests his chin on his fist and closes his eyes. Kevin's mouth keeps moving. The eagle soars. The kitten hangs. The fluorescent lights pulse at their frequency that no one else can see, or that everyone can see but only I can't not see, and the carpet holds its silence the way it has always held its silence, which is the only way anything in this room holds anything: permanently, and without meaning to.

## III.

On the third day, I notice Kevin's hand.

I have been watching Kevin for three days, the way I watch everyone, which is to say: constantly, involuntarily, with the unblinking patience of an animal that has learned to survive by reading the movements of larger animals. Kevin is easy to watch because he is always performing — his body is a broadcast, all signal, the gestures big, the smiles deployed on schedule, the posture calibrated for the room. He is a man who lives in his front, in the face and hands and forward lean, and who assumes — as most people do — that this is where the information is, because this is where he puts it.

But on the third day, at 10:17 by the institutional clock, a man enters through the main door, and Kevin's hand goes to the back of his neck.

The man is wearing a suit. This is the first unusual thing, because no one wears a suit to this office — not the parolees, not the staff, not the facilitators who come on Tuesdays to lead sessions on "résumé building" and "interview confidence" and who dress in the business-casual uniform of people who work for nonprofits and buy their khakis at a place that also sells camping equipment. This man is wearing a suit — dark, charcoal, cut well enough that even I, who do not care about suits, can see that it fits the way suits fit when they are chosen by someone who understands the language of fit, the way the shoulder seam sits exactly at the shoulder's edge, the way the lapel lies flat without gapping. The suit is fine.

The shoes are wrong.

Not wrong for the suit — wrong for the *wearer of the suit, in this building, in this context.* They are black, leather, and from where I sit I can see the sole's edge where his foot rests on the carpet, and the sole is red. Not the scuffed, faded red of old shoes but the specific, arterial red that I have seen in magazine pages and shop windows and on the feet of men who walk through airports with the frictionless confidence of people for whom airports are living rooms with better lighting.
