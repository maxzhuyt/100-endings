# The Reykjavik Frequency Heist

---

## I.

She was right.

I want to say that first, before any of the rest of it, because I spent the better part of 1971 constructing an elaborate interior argument for why she was wrong, and I have spent the forty-eight years since then living inside the knowledge that she was not, and if I begin this story the way I lived it — with my own confident skepticism as the scaffolding — then I will be lying to you in the specific way I spent decades lying to myself. Which is the comfortable way. Which is the way where Magnús Þorsteinsson is a sensible man surrounded by eccentrics, and the story is a funny one, and nobody has to sit with it afterward.

Elísabet Jónsdóttir was right. About the signal, about its content, about what it meant, and about what had to be done. I am telling you this now because I will spend much of what follows making her sound impossible, and I need you to understand that when I say *impossible* I am describing the limitations of my own imagination and calling them the walls of the world.

She had a word for that. For me. For what I was.

But I'll get there.

---

## II.

The Krafla volcanic system sits in the northeast of Iceland, in that stretch of country where the earth is either frozen or molten and cannot seem to decide between the two. In April of 1971, the Icelandic National Broadcasting Service logged a series of complaints from residents in the Mývatn region about interference on their radio receivers. The interference was not unusual in itself — volcanic activity generates electromagnetic noise, and anyone living near Krafla understood that their reception of *Útvarpið Ríkisins* would occasionally be interrupted by the subsurface opinions of the planet. What was unusual was the pattern. The interference was not random. It occurred at predictable intervals, on specific frequencies, and when the INBS dispatched a technician to investigate, his report noted — in language that I would later recognize as the careful prose of a man trying not to sound insane — that the interference appeared to be *structured.*

I read that report in June. I was at the time employed by the Post and Telephone Administration, working out of the Skúlagata facility in Reykjavik, maintaining the longwave transmission equipment that kept the country's radio service operational. My job was waveforms. I understood them the way a carpenter understands grain — not theoretically but through the hands, through years of contact with the material. I could look at an oscilloscope reading and tell you whether the irregularity was atmospheric, mechanical, or human. I had a reputation for this. I was not modest about it.

Elísabet found me because of that reputation, and because, I suspect, no one else would work with her.

---

She arrived at Skúlagata on a Tuesday, which I remember because Tuesdays were when I performed the weekly calibration of our Rohde & Schwarz EK 07 receiver, and I did not appreciate interruptions to that process. She was perhaps forty, thin-faced, with the particular Icelandic pallor that suggests a person who has been indoors thinking for longer than is medically advisable. She wore a man's coat that did not fit her. She carried a leather briefcase that was older than either of us.

She introduced herself as a physicist attached — loosely, I would learn, very loosely — to the University of Iceland's geophysics program, and she said she needed a radio engineer who could isolate a signal in the 147 to 152 megahertz range emanating from the Krafla caldera, and that the signal was a temporally displaced broadcast from the future.

She said this the way one might say the signal was bouncing off the ionosphere. As a technical detail.

I told her I was in the middle of a calibration.

She sat down on the bench beside the EK 07 and opened her briefcase and removed a sheaf of spectrographic printouts that I recognized as output from a Brüel & Kjær frequency analyzer, and she laid them on my workbench, one after another, in chronological sequence, and she did not say another word for eleven minutes while I looked at them.

I know it was eleven minutes because the EK 07's calibration timer was running and I could hear it ticking.

The printouts showed a signal. It was real — measurably, demonstrably, physically present in the electromagnetic spectrum. It appeared in the VHF band, which was wrong for volcanic noise. It recurred at intervals of approximately 137 minutes, which corresponded to no known geomagnetic cycle. Its waveform was not random. It was, as the INBS technician had carefully noted, *structured.* It bore the unmistakable signature of an amplitude-modulated audio broadcast.

There was a signal coming out of the Krafla volcanic system, on a VHF frequency, and it was shaped like a radio program.

I looked at the printouts for eleven minutes and then I said, "This is interesting."

She said, "It is a broadcast from the future and I can prove it."

I said, "This is an interesting *signal,*" with emphasis.

She looked at me with an expression I would come to know well — the flat, calibrating patience of a woman who has identified the precise boundary of a man's willingness to think and has decided to work around it rather than through it.

"I need your hands," she said. "Not your beliefs."

---

## III.

I want to be precise about why I agreed to help her, because the version where Magnús the sober engineer joins the madwoman's expedition out of secret romantic interest, or hidden adventurousness, or some latent appetite for the impossible, is the version that makes a better story and is not what happened.

What happened was the spectrographs.

The signal was *there.* It was in the data. And it did not belong to any category of electromagnetic phenomenon I had encountered in fourteen years of professional work. Not volcanic noise — wrong frequency band. Not atmospheric skip — wrong propagation pattern. Not military transmission — wrong modulation scheme (I had spent two years monitoring NATO signals during a contract period and knew the signatures). Not a hoax, because the Brüel & Kjær analyzer that had produced the spectrographs was a serious instrument that could not be fooled by a serious person, and Elísabet, whatever I thought of her conclusions, was clearly a serious person.

The signal was a puzzle. I am an engineer. I solve puzzles. That is the whole of it.

I did not believe the signal was a broadcast from the future. I believed it was a genuinely anomalous electromagnetic event with a prosaic explanation that had not yet been identified. My working hypothesis was that a previously undocumented interaction between Krafla's subsurface magmatic chemistry and the region's basalt geology was producing a natural resonance that happened to mimic the structure of an AM broadcast — a geological coincidence, remarkable but not supernatural.

I explained this hypothesis to Elísabet at length, with diagrams. She listened. When I finished, she said — and I am quoting her exactly, because some sentences remain in the memory like splinters — "You have drawn me a very detailed map of a country that does not exist, and the draftsmanship is excellent."

I did not enjoy working with Elísabet Jónsdóttir.

---

## IV.

*I did not enjoy working with her, and I need you to understand that what I am about to describe was not an adventure. I know how it will sound — the misfit team, the improvised equipment, the secret operation. It sounds like the kind of story that is fun to have lived through. But it was not fun while I lived through it. It was tedious and cold and often frightening in the specific way that doing something technically illegal in a small country where everyone knows your name is frightening — not dramatically, but with the steady low-grade nausea of a man who can see his pension evaporating.*

*Sólveig keeps telling me I should explain who was on the team. Sólveig is my granddaughter and she is the one who convinced me to do this. She says I should "set the scene." She grew up watching films where the expert assembles a team and each member has a specialty and a colorful nickname. I will disappoint her.*

---

There were five of us.

Elísabet, who was the reason for everything and who I have described sufficiently for now.

Myself, who was the reason any of the equipment worked.

Guðmundur Halldórsson, a fisherman from Húsavík, whose trawler — the *Njála* — had been partially decommissioned the previous winter after an engine fire, and who was willing to let us strip the vessel's radio equipment in exchange for a sum of money I considered irresponsible and Elísabet considered trivial. She came from money. Old Reykjavik family money, the kind that does not announce itself but is always somehow present when needed. Guðmundur was sixty-one and smelled permanently of cod and diesel, and he had the finest pair of hands I have ever seen on a non-engineer. He could strip and rebuild a marine radio transceiver in the dark, by touch, in a rolling sea. These are not skills you learn. They are skills you are born with and then spend forty years refining, and when I tell you that Guðmundur's hands were as good as mine, I am paying him the highest compliment I know how to offer.

Þórdís Magnúsdóttir, a graduate student in Elísabet's department, who served as our driver, our logistician, and our interface with the world of people who went to sleep at reasonable hours. She was twenty-three, fiercely competent, and treated Elísabet with the particular mixture of worship and exasperation that I associate with bright young women in the orbit of brilliant older women who do not notice they are being worshipped. Þórdís kept a logbook. Her logbook was better organized than mine. This bothered me.

And Haraldur Benediktsson, a bureaucrat in the Icelandic Telecommunications Administration who was, very quietly and very carefully, losing his mind.

Haraldur is the one who requires explanation, because Haraldur was our access point to the frequency allocation records that told us exactly which bands were monitored by whom, and more importantly, which bands were *restricted* — allocated by international agreement to military or governmental use and therefore patrolled. The 147–152 MHz range in which Elísabet's signal appeared fell within a band designated for NATO and Warsaw Pact maritime coordination in the North Atlantic. This meant two things: first, that any sustained monitoring operation on those frequencies by Icelandic civilians would be detected and shut down within days; and second, that other people — professional, state-sponsored, well-equipped people — were already listening to those frequencies and had presumably already noticed the anomalous signal.

Haraldur confirmed this. The Soviets had noticed. They had a monitoring station at Þingvellir — this was not widely known but was one of those open secrets that the Icelandic government preferred to treat as a closed one — and they had logged the Krafla signal as early as February 1971. They had classified it. They had not, as far as Haraldur could determine, identified it.

This was the problem. The signal was real, it was on restricted frequencies, the Soviets knew it existed and were actively monitoring it, and any attempt by our team to capture it with sufficient fidelity for analysis would require sustained access to those same frequencies, which would light up every monitoring station in the North Atlantic like a Christmas display.

Elísabet's solution was to bribe the Soviets.

---

I said this was insane.

She said, "You keep using that word."

I said it was specifically, technically, and legally insane — that bribing Soviet intelligence personnel was a crime against the Icelandic state, a potential violation of NATO security agreements, and the kind of thing that got people put in rooms with no windows.

She said she was not proposing to bribe Soviet intelligence personnel. She was proposing to bribe a technician. There was a difference. Soviet intelligence ran on ideology. Soviet technicians ran on boredom and Western cigarettes and the particular slow-burning desperation of men stationed in Iceland who had expected to be stationed in Cuba.

She had already made contact with the technician. His name was Grigory. He worked the night shift at the Þingvellir facility, and for a total price of four hundred American dollars and twelve cartons of Pall Mall cigarettes, he would designate a six-hour window — 2200 to 0400 on a night of our choosing — during which the Krafla frequencies would be logged as "atmospherically compromised" and excluded from active monitoring.

Six hours. That was what we were buying. Six hours of silence from the people who were listening.

"And if someone in Moscow decides to check the logs?" I said.

"Then Grigory will have a bad week," Elísabet said. "But we will already have what we need."

I asked her if she cared about Grigory.

She said, "I care about what we are going to hear."

---

## V.

*The question Sólveig keeps asking — she is here, behind the camera, she thinks I can't see her mouthing the questions she wants me to answer — is whether I was afraid. She is asking the wrong question. I was not afraid. Fear requires that you believe something dangerous is happening. I did not believe anything dangerous was happening. I believed I was helping a deluded physicist conduct an elaborate exercise in anomaly chasing that would end with a deflating natural explanation and a good story for dinner parties.*

*I was annoyed. That was my condition. Annoyed at the logistics, annoyed at the cold, annoyed at Elísabet's refusal to entertain my geological-resonance hypothesis, annoyed at the discovery that Guðmundur's hands were almost as good as mine. I narrated my own experience to myself as a comedy of inconvenience: the serious engineer tolerating the eccentrics because the puzzle was interesting enough to justify the discomfort.*

*The thing about being annoyed — and I have had forty-eight years to understand this — is that it is the most effective form of not paying attention. Annoyance is a wall you build facing inward. You are so busy cataloging the ways you are being inconvenienced that you do not notice the ways you are being changed.*

---

## VI.

We spent three weeks preparing. I will describe the technical work because it is what I know and because it matters, and because the nature of the equipment we built is part of the story in a way that only becomes clear later.

The *Njála* yielded a Sailor R105 marine receiver, a Skanti TRP-6000 transceiver that Guðmundur had personally modified with an extended VHF capability, and — the real prize — a magnetic tape recorder, a Tandberg Model 64, that some previous owner had installed for reasons Guðmundur could not or would not explain. The Tandberg was beautiful. Four-track stereo, 7.5 inches per second, frequency response flat to 16 kilohertz. It had been exposed to salt air for years and the transport mechanism was corroded, but the heads were intact, and when I rebuilt the tape path with components scavenged from a consumer-grade Philips unit that Þórdís acquired from a student in her department, it ran as smoothly as the day it left the Norwegian factory.

I also built, from a combination of purchased and stolen parts, a directional antenna array specifically tuned to the 147–152 MHz band. The design was a stacked Yagi-Uda, four elements, with a calculated gain of approximately 11 dBi, mounted on a wooden frame that Guðmundur constructed from scrap lumber because we could not afford aluminum. Wooden antenna frames are not standard practice. They work less well than metal. This is the kind of compromise you make when your budget is finite and your physicist refuses to spend money on "scaffolding" when she could spend it on Pall Mall cigarettes for a Soviet technician named Grigory.

Elísabet and I argued about the antenna frame for two days. This is not interesting in itself but it is representative. We argued about everything. We argued about cable impedance matching. We argued about recording levels. We argued about whether to use 3M Scotch 111 tape or BASF LGS 52, a dispute that occupied an entire evening and that she won by producing a technical paper on BASF's superior oxide coating uniformity that I had not read and could not rebut. I argued from experience. She argued from first principles.
