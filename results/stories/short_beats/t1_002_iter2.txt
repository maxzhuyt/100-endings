HUTCHINS: — and the State expects to show, through forensic and testimonial evidence, that the defendant maintained what investigators have described as a working altar in the southeast bedroom of her residence. MR. ODOM: — and I'll ask you to keep an open mind. I'll ask you to listen carefully to what the State can actually prove and what it cannot. Ms. Vance is a deeply troubled woman. Nobody in this courtroom will dispute that. She suffers from documented psychiatric conditions — schizoaffective disorder, diagnosed in 1989 — and much of what the State is going to present to you as evidence of ritualistic intent is, in fact, the documented symptomatology of a woman in crisis. She kept notebooks, yes. She wrote in them compulsively. She copied text she did not understand from books she found in the Sutton County Library. She was not organized or systematic. She was ill. And being ill, members of the jury, even being severely ill, is not the same as being guilty.

MR. HUTCHINS: Sergeant Brower, I want to direct your attention to State's Exhibit 4. Can you identify what you're looking at?

SGT. BROWER: That's one of the notebooks we recovered from the southeast bedroom.

MR. HUTCHINS: And can you describe the contents for the jury?

SGT. BROWER: It's — it's handwritten, front to back, both sides of every page. Some of it's in English but most of it's not. Some of it looks like Latin, maybe, or — I'm not an expert. There are diagrams. And in the back portion there are three sections with children's names written at the top, and underneath each name there are physical details. Hair color. Eye color. One section mentions a birthmark.

MR. HUTCHINS: A birthmark.

SGT. BROWER: Yes, sir. On the Salazar boy. Left shoulder blade. Described as — I'm reading — "in the form of a mouth."

MR. HUTCHINS: Did the Salazar boy have a birthmark?

SGT. BROWER: The medical examiner confirmed it, yes, sir. Left shoulder blade.

MR. HUTCHINS: And how would you characterize it?

SGT. BROWER: I couldn't characterize it.

MR. HUTCHINS: But the defendant characterized it.

MR. ODOM: Objection, leading.

THE COURT: Sustained.

MR. HUTCHINS: Let me rephrase. Sergeant, was the defendant known to have had any personal contact with the Salazar family?

SGT. BROWER: Not that we could establish.

MR. HUTCHINS: No babysitting, no school events, no church?

SGT. BROWER: No, sir. The Salazars are Catholic. The defendant was not affiliated with any church we could identify.

**AUDIO DIARY — MARGARET A. CASSIDY**

**TAPE 1, SIDE A**

**[Undated. Contextual evidence suggests April 7 or 8, 1997. Recorded on standard microcassette, Olympus Pearlcorder model. Moderate tape hiss. Background: kitchen sounds — dishes, faucet. Female speaker throughout.]**

Long day. Long, long day.

I should probably — let me start this differently. I'm starting a tape diary for this case because it's going to be a long trial and I may want notes beyond the official transcript. I've done this for — the Montalvo trial, I think, was the last time. It's just helpful for my own memory. Nothing formal.

So. *State v. Vance.* Eleanor Ruth Vance, capital murder times three, 34th District, Judge Pruitt. Pruitt's fine. Pruitt's — he runs a tight room. He drinks that terrible coffee from the machine in the hallway, the one with the brown water that nobody else touches, and he does this thing where he stirs it with a pen cap. Not even his pen cap. Whatever pen is nearest. You can hear the plastic clicking against the mug when the jury's out. Drives me up a wall but that's — that's Pruitt.

[Pause. Water running.]

The chair is a problem. The reporter's station in 34th District has a folding chair. Not even a good folding chair — it's one of the cushioned ones where the cushion has completely died and now it's just a flat pad that tells you where comfort used to live. I brought my own lumbar support. Four weeks minimum on this trial, probably six. I'm not destroying my L4 for Sutton County.

Parking is across the street. Lot B. The meter situation is — it's fine.

[Long pause.]

The defendant. I should say something about the defendant.

She speaks in a way that's hard to render. That's the professional version. I've been doing this for thirteen years. I've transcribed people who mumble, people who shout, people who speak in thick accents, people with jaw injuries, people who are actively sobbing. You learn to get it. You train your ear and your hands and you get it. When she — when Eleanor Vance speaks normally she's actually quite articulate. Clear diction. Educated, if I had to guess, or at least well-read. But when she has the outbursts — and she had two today — what comes out is —

I noted it as unintelligible vocalization, which is standard. That's the correct notation when a speaker produces sounds that cannot be reliably transcribed as words. The protocol is clear. I followed it.

[Pause.]

I don't know. It's fine.

[Pause.]

Oh — so, the one thing I should note. I requested this assignment. Case like this doesn't come through 34th District very often, and I knew the schedule fit mine. But also the previous reporter, a woman named — I have it somewhere — Dolores Marsh, she left the position kind of suddenly. It wasn't a firing, from what I can tell. She just didn't come back one day. That was in October, I think, and they left the position unfilled until the Vance trial came up and I put in the request. Nobody said anything unusual about it. I think somebody in the clerk's office said she moved. People move. That's not a story. I'm mentioning it because it's in my head for some reason. Probably because of the chair. If Dolores Marsh had stayed, the chair would be her problem.

So. Day one. Four weeks to go. Hutchins for the prosecution — he's a true believer, I think. Not in the occult. In the narrative. He's building a satanic-panic case and he's building it like they all do: mysterious notebooks, Latin fragments, altars and candles, the works. I've seen this before. I transcribed a daycare case in Tom Green County in '92 that was almost beat for beat the same architecture, minus the actual dead children. That case fell apart. No convictions.

This one — this one has dead children. That's different. But Hutchins is prosecuting it the same way, and I wonder if he knows how that framework sounds to anyone who's been through one of these before.

Not my job. My job is the transcript. What they say, I write. What they say is what the record is. And the record is what happened.

That's the whole thing.

[Clicks off.]
