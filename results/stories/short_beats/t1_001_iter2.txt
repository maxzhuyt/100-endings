# The Thirty-Seven Letters of Monk Hideo, As Compiled by His Opponent

---

## Editor's Preface

I have no talent for prefaces. In sixty-two years of competitive judo I have written exactly four: two forewords for coaching manuals, one dedication in a collection of tournament photographs, and a brief introduction to the 1974 reissue of the All-Japan Judo Federation's competition rulebook in which I described, in three paragraphs that took me nine weeks to compose, the proper etiquette for bowing before a match. I mention this not to excuse what follows but to explain why I have so long delayed producing it. The writing of prefaces requires a kind of authority I have never felt I possessed, though the world has, for forty years, insisted on granting it to me.

I am dying. The doctors have given me language for it, but the language is medical and I have always distrusted any vocabulary I did not earn through practice. What I can say is that I will not see another winter, and that the knowledge has clarified certain questions of priority. For four decades I have maintained a private archive. It fills two cedar boxes of the kind used to store calligraphy scrolls, and it contains thirty-seven letters from the man the sporting world knows as Hideo Watanabe — national champion at twenty-three, Olympic bronze medalist at twenty-four, winner of every significant title in Japanese judo between 1958 and 1963, and the man who, on the fourteenth of September 1963, forfeited the final of the All-Japan Championship to me without throwing a single technique.

These facts are known. They have been written about extensively. The forfeit has been analyzed, debated, mythologized, and — in one American film I could not bring myself to finish — dramatized with actors who moved nothing like us. I have been asked about it in every interview I have given since that day. My answer has never varied: *I don't know why he forfeited. I have no special insight. I was as surprised as everyone else.*

Two of these three statements are lies.

I was not surprised. In the half-second before Hideo bowed — before his body began the motion that would carry him off the mat and out of competitive judo forever — I saw something in his face. I have spent forty years failing to name it. It was not fear. It was not resignation. It was not the blankness that precedes a seizure or the vacancy of a man who has simply decided to quit. It was closer to — and I will be forgiven, I hope, for reaching beyond my competence — the expression of a surgeon who looks down at an open body and sees something he cannot repair but also cannot, in conscience, close back up and pretend he did not find.

He saw something. He saw it in me, or on me, or through me. And whatever he saw made the match — our match, the culmination of a rivalry that had organized both our lives for five years — impossible for him to continue.

Then he bowed. And then he left.

The arena held eleven thousand people. I stood alone on the mat while the referee raised my hand, and I understood with a certainty I have never since recovered that I had won nothing. That something had been done *to* me, something immense and irrevocable, disguised as a gift.

Three months later, I received the first letter.

I publish the correspondence now — all thirty-seven, unaltered, in their original sequence — not as an act of exposure. Hideo, if he lives, will recognize that I have honored the letters exactly as they were written. I have changed nothing. I have redacted nothing. I have added only my own annotations, which supplement certain references, correct minor errors of date or attribution, and — in places — offer the parallel account that the letters themselves never request and that Hideo, in four decades of writing, never once invited.

The reader may take these annotations as commentary or confession, as they prefer.

This is the only move I have left.

— *The Opponent*
*Tokyo, August 2003*

---

## A Note on Editorial Method

The letters are presented in chronological order. Hideo's original texts are given in full. My annotations appear as numbered notes following each letter or, where a specific passage requires comment, as bracketed insertions within the text. I have not attempted to standardize Hideo's punctuation, which is occasionally erratic, nor his habit of leaving sentences unfinished, which grows more pronounced in the later correspondence. Where his calligraphy permits multiple readings of a character, I have chosen the reading that seemed most consistent with context and noted alternatives where they significantly alter meaning.

I have not contacted Hideo regarding this publication. Our communication has been, for forty years, asymmetric. He writes. I receive. This arrangement was not negotiated. It was imposed — by him, silently, beginning with the first letter, which bore no return address, and continuing through every subsequent letter, none of which has ever included a means of reply.

I say this so the reader understands: I have never answered him. Not once. Not because I did not wish to, but because he made answering impossible, and I have come to believe that this impossibility was the point.

## Letter 1

*Postmarked Eiheiji, Fukui Prefecture*
*December 19, 1963*

The mountain receives snow without ceremony. I mention this because you will perhaps remember that I was always bothered by ceremony — the weighing-in, the formal announcements, the photographs before and after. You handled all of it better than I did. You had a talent for standing in the right place.

I am well. The temple is cold but not unpleasantly so. The senior monks are strict. I am learning to sit, which is harder than anything I learned to do while standing.

I hope this finds you in good health.

— H.

---

**Annotation to Letter 1:** The postmark is Eiheiji, the principal training temple of the Sōtō school of Zen Buddhism. I confirmed later, through indirect channels, that Hideo entered the monastery in October 1963, approximately three weeks after the championship. The reference to ceremony is, I believe, genuine; Hideo visibly disliked the pre-match rituals and was twice reprimanded by officials for insufficient formality during the parade of athletes. His remark that I had "a talent for standing in the right place" is, on first reading, a minor compliment. I have read it many times since. It is possible that it is not a compliment at all.

## Letter 2

*Postmarked Eiheiji, Fukui Prefecture*
*March 3, 1964*

Spring comes later here than in Tokyo. The plum blossoms along the river path opened this morning and the abbot sent us out to look at them, which I found embarrassing, all of us standing in a row looking at trees as though trees were a new development. But the blossoms were very fine.

I read in a newspaper left by a visiting layman that you have been appointed head coach of the university judo program. This is deserved. You were always a better teacher than I was. I could demonstrate a technique but never explain why it worked. You understood the architecture of things. I only ever understood the timing.

— H.

---

**Annotation to Letter 2:** I was not appointed head coach. I was appointed assistant coach. The head coach was Master Saitō, who did not retire until 1971. I note this not to correct a trivial error but because it is the first instance of a pattern that recurs throughout the correspondence: Hideo consistently elevates my accomplishments beyond their actual dimensions. Whether this is generosity, guilt, or a subtler form of something I lack the vocabulary to name, I leave to the reader.

## Letter 4

*Postmarked Eiheiji, Fukui Prefecture*
*January 8, 1965*

I have been studying the *Shōbōgenzō* and finding it impenetrable, which the senior monks assure me is the correct response. Dōgen writes that to study the self is to forget the self, and to forget the self is to be enlightened by all things. I do not know what this means. But I notice that when I sit in meditation and attempt to forget myself, you are the last thing that remains before the forgetting. Not you specifically — I do not mean to flatter or to alarm — but the *problem* of you, which I carry like a stone in my left hand that I cannot put down because I cannot find a surface I trust to hold it.

The monks would tell me this is attachment. Perhaps. But I think it is something else. I think it is a debt that cannot be paid because the creditor does not know he is owed.

The snow has been heavy this year. I sweep the walkways each morning before dawn. It is good work because it empties completely and refills completely each night. Not like other kinds of work, which accumulate.

— H.

---

**Annotation to Letter 4:** I have omitted Letter 3, which concerns only temple food and the difficulties of sleeping on a thin mat. I include Letter 4 in full because it marks the first moment where the correspondence ceases to be ordinary.

The phrase "a debt that cannot be paid because the creditor does not know he is owed" arrested me when I first read it. I was twenty-nine years old. I sat at my kitchen table in the apartment I shared with my wife — my first wife, Mariko, who would leave me in 1970 for reasons not unrelated to the contents of these letters — and I read the sentence three times and felt something move in my chest that I can only describe as the feeling of a lock engaging. Not opening. Engaging. Something that had been unlatched fastened itself shut.

I did not know what I was owed. He did not tell me. He spent the next thirty-eight years not telling me.

## Letter 7

*Postmarked Eiheiji, Fukui Prefecture*
*September 14, 1966*

Three years today.

I have been reading the *Hagakure* again — not for guidance but as a diagnostic instrument, the way one might X-ray a bone that has healed but still aches in certain weather. Yamamoto Tsunetomo writes that the way of the warrior is death, by which he means, or by which I have come to believe he means, that the samurai must rehearse his own death each morning so that when the moment of action arrives he is already on the far side of the fear that prevents decisive movement.

But what Tsunetomo does not address — and I think this is the crack in the foundation, the fault line running through all of bushido — is the question of what a warrior owes when the decisive moment reveals not his *own* death but his opponent's. Not his opponent's literal death, of course. We are not those kinds of warriors. But the analogue. The moment when one sees clearly that the contest is not what both parties believed it to be. When one discovers, mid-engagement, that the ground beneath the other man is not solid.

I will put it as a hypothetical, because hypotheticals cost nothing.

Say two swordsmen face each other. They are evenly matched — the record between them is close, the respect mutual, the rivalry the organizing principle of both careers. On the morning of their final duel, one swordsman — call him A — wakes early, performs his forms, and in the course of preparation discovers something about swordsman B. Not through espionage or treachery, but simply through the accumulated knowledge that years of combat produce. He discovers a vulnerability. Not a minor one. A vulnerability so fundamental that, once seen, it renders the contest — which both men and all observers believe to be a meeting of equals — into something else entirely. Something closer to an execution.

What does swordsman A owe?

To fight is to exploit knowledge the other man does not possess about himself. To withdraw is to deny swordsman B the contest he has earned and trained for, which is its own form of violence. To *reveal* the vulnerability is perhaps the most destructive option of all — it hands the other man a piece of self-knowledge that cannot be unfound, that will restructure every memory of every previous contest, that will make him wonder, for the rest of his life, whether every blow he believed he earned was in fact *permitted*.

The *Hagakure* does not cover this. Tsunetomo's warrior dies at his post. He does not have to decide what to do when he discovers that his opponent's post is rotten.

I apologize for the length. The autumn here sharpens the mind in ways that are not always comfortable.

— H.

---

**Annotation to Letter 7:** I note that this letter is dated September 14 — the anniversary of the championship. I do not believe this is a coincidence, and I suspect Hideo did not intend me to believe it was. He marks the date the way one marks a death anniversary, with a visit to the site of the loss.

The hypothetical about swordsmen A and B is transparent. It was transparent to me in 1966 and it is transparent now. But I must also record that when I first read it, I did not feel the shock of recognition that the reader might expect. I felt instead a sensation closer to relief — the relief of a man who has suspected he is being followed and who, turning a corner, glimpses the shadow and confirms it. Yes. He saw something. He has always been telling me, in his way, that he saw something.

What he saw — the "vulnerability," the "flaw," the "rotten post" — he does not name. He has never named it. I have read this letter, by conservative estimate, four hundred times. In certain years I read it every week. The flaw remains unnamed, and I have been left to locate it on my own body, which is a particular kind of torment — to know that somewhere in the architecture of your movement there lives a thing that made a great man walk away from the greatest victory of his life, and to be unable to find it, and to be unable to stop looking.

My left side. That is all I will say for now. My left side has ached since 1961. I did not ask why. I trained around it the way one trains around all persistent pain — by compensating, by favoring, by building an elaborate secondary structure that conceals the weakness without correcting it. It is possible that this compensation was itself the flaw. It is possible that what Hideo saw was not a single deficit but the entire constellation of adjustments I had built to hide it, visible to his eye the way a fault line is visible to a geologist even when the ground looks solid to everyone else.

But I do not know. He did not tell me. He asked what swordsman A owed and then did not answer his own question.

## Letter 11

*Postmarked Eiheiji, Fukui Prefecture*
*June 2, 1969*

The monastery has a cat now. It arrived uninvited and the abbot, who dislikes disorder, has been unable to persuade it to leave. The cat sits in the meditation hall during zazen and cleans itself with a concentration I find both admirable and reproachful. It has no koan to solve. It has no opponent. It is simply a cat, exhausting the possibilities of being a cat, and I envy it with a fervor that I recognize as spiritually immature but cannot extinguish.

Do you ever wish that we had never met?

I do not mean this the way it will likely be read. I do not wish it. I am asking whether the wish is structurally available to you, as it is not available to me. You won. You went on. You have a career, students, a trajectory. The line of your life passes *through* the championship and continues forward. The line of mine terminates there, by my own choosing, and everything since has been a kind of *aftermath* — which is, I am told, a word the English use to describe both the consequences of a battle and the second mowing of a field. I find both meanings appropriate. I have been mowed twice. Once by the event and once by the leaving.

— H.

---
