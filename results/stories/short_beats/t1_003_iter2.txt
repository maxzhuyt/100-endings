# The Collectors

## I.

Dana's hands found the desk before the rest of her found the courage to look up.

The check-in station was still there, bolted to the wall of the main lodge where it had always been, though the wood had gone gray and the varnish had bubbled into tiny amber blisters. Someone had left a stack of brochures on the counter — old ones, from the last year the camp had operated, their covers showing a lake so blue it looked irradiated and children so happy they appeared to be in tremendous gastrointestinal distress. Her fingers began before her brain had even registered the paper. She squared the edges of the stack. Tapped it against the counter. Turned it ninety degrees. Tapped again. Began dividing: large brochures in one column, small brochures in another, and the medium-size ones — there were only two — she folded in half so they would belong somewhere. Her cuticles were raw and splitting. She'd organized the entire glove compartment of her rental car during the drive up, then re-organized it at a rest stop outside Gilman when the arrangement had started to feel like a mouth with a crooked tooth.

She heard gravel crunch outside and did not look up.

---

Maren crossed the parking lot at the clip of someone who had somewhere better to be, which she achieved by choosing a spot on the far tree line and staring at it with purpose. The sunglasses helped. They were enormous, black, gas-station polarized, and they still had the price tag dangling from the left temple — $14.99, swinging against her jaw like a metronome — and beneath that, the smaller anti-theft sticker she hadn't bothered to peel. She had not needed sunglasses. It was dusk. She had not needed to enter the Mobil station on Route 12 at all, but the car had needed gas, and once she was inside, the rack of sunglasses had been right there beside the register, six rows of identical black frames, and her hand had closed around a pair the same way a hand closes around a stair railing — without decision, without even the dignity of desire. Just the mechanical completion of a circuit that started every time she stood near a thing she could take.

She had four more pairs in her jacket. Different stations, different towns. A trail of $14.99 absences stretching from her apartment in Bridgeport to this parking lot in the mountains in western Vermont.

She pushed through the lodge door and saw Dana's back and Dana's hands doing their quick, architectural work on the brochure stack, and she felt something drop through her chest like a coin through water.

"Hey," Maren said.

"Hey," Dana said, not looking up, her fingers spidering a brochure into alignment.

"Love what you've done with the place."

Dana did look up then, and whatever she saw behind the ridiculous sunglasses — or because of them, because of the tag — made her mouth do something complicated.

"You look exactly the same," Dana said.

"Liar."

"Okay. You look like yourself if yourself had been left in the rain."

"That's closer."

They did not hug. Maren leaned against the doorframe and watched Dana's hands, and Dana watched Maren watching, and for a moment both of them were twelve again, in the dim competent recognition of each other's wrongness, and then the gravel crunched again and they both turned toward the sound with the kind of gratitude people feel when an interruption saves them from sincerity.

---

Jesse came in eating.

She was working through a bag of peanut butter pretzels — the Costco size, the bag you buy when you are feeding a soccer team or when you are not feeding anything at all, when the hand-to-mouth motion has become its own closed economy, independent of hunger or taste or the physical reality of a stomach that has been screaming for twenty minutes. Her jaw moved in a metronomic rotation. Her eyes were somewhere else. She held the bag in the crook of her left arm the way other people held infants, and her right hand traveled the circuit — bag to mouth, bag to mouth — with the precision of a piston. She was tall, thinner than she should have been given the rate of intake, and there was a faint reddish chapping around her knuckles that Dana clocked and looked away from fast.

"Jess," Maren said.

Jesse's hand paused midway to her mouth. A pretzel hung in the air between them.

"Oh, Christ," Jesse said, looking from one to the other, and then she glanced down at the pretzel in her fingers as though discovering a small animal she didn't know she was holding. She put it in her mouth anyway. "Are we doing this? Are we actually doing this?"

"We're doing this," Dana said.

"I thought it was a joke. I thought it was — I don't know. A scam email. Like a Nigerian prince but instead of money it's your worst summer."

"And yet here you are," Maren said.

Jesse hoisted the pretzel bag. "I brought snacks."

"Is that what we're calling it?"

"It's what I've always called it."

From outside: the sound of a car engine. Then the engine cutting. Then starting again. Then cutting. Then — impossibly — starting again.

---

Theo's Saturn had been circling the lot for nine minutes.

Dana had counted. She'd moved to the window when the engine sounds started and she'd watched the faded silver sedan make its first lap around the perimeter of the cracked asphalt, slow and deliberate, as though he were casing the place. Then a second lap. On the third, he pulled into a spot near the flagpole, sat for fifteen seconds, reversed out, and started again.

It was the watching of it that hurt. Not the act itself — she'd seen stranger things this week in her own bathroom mirror, hands deep in the cabinet under the sink, re-sorting cotton balls by size and then by density and then by some third quality she couldn't name but could _feel_ — but the way it was so plainly visible from where they stood, the three of them at the window now, watching a grown man drive in an ovoid loop around an empty parking lot at dusk because something in the wiring of his brain would not let the circuit close.

"How long do we give him?" Jesse asked, chewing.

"He'll get there," Maren said.

"Will he, though? Because I'm watching a man audition parking spots like they're lead roles."

"He used to do this with his bunk. Remember?" Dana said. "He'd make his bed and then unmake it and make it again. Three times minimum."

"Four," Jesse said. "It was always four."

They watched the Saturn complete its sixth lap. Seventh. On the eighth, Theo pulled into a spot directly in front of the lodge — the most obvious spot, the one he'd passed seven times — and cut the engine. He sat in the car for a long time. They could see his hands on the steering wheel, adjusting, adjusting, finding a grip and releasing it and finding it again.

"Should we—"

"No," Jesse said. "Let him finish."

He sat for another thirty seconds, hands working. Then he opened the door, stepped out, and stood very still, looking at the building with an expression of someone who had just been asked to swallow something large and dry.

He was narrow in the shoulders and wide in the forehead, and twenty years had given him glasses and taken his hairline and replaced the anxious stillness she remembered with a different kind of stillness, harder and more practiced. His jacket had been buttoned and unbuttoned recently — the top button showed thread-stress — and Dana could see, even from the window, the small unconscious movement of his right hand already beginning: touching his left cuff, then his right cuff, then his left cuff, then his right, a tiny bilateral ritual that he performed so automatically it looked like a tic rather than what it was, which was architecture. A structure he built and rebuilt and rebuilt through the day, brick by invisible brick, to keep the day from collapsing.

He walked toward the entrance. Stopped. Turned and looked at his car. Turned back. Walked four more steps. Stopped. Touched both cuffs.

"Theo," Maren called from the doorway, a gentle firmness in it, the way you'd call a dog who'd gotten to the edge of a road.

He looked up at her and his whole face did something that was trying to be a smile but was, more honestly, the expression of a man who has just been identified.

"I couldn't figure out the parking," he said.

"I saw."

"The lines are faded."

"There are no lines, Theo. There were never lines. It's a dirt lot."

He stood there with his hands on his cuffs and she stood there with her $14.99 tags swinging, and the stupidity of it — the absolute slapstick machinery of their respective dysfunctions — hung between them in the graying air. Behind them, Dana was re-squaring the brochures and Jesse was eating pretzels directly into an esophagus that no longer reported to her conscious mind, and for one stacked, overlapping second all four of them were performing their private rituals simultaneously, like a clockwork display in a town square, four mechanical figures emerging on the hour to enact the same programmed gestures they'd been enacting since their gears were set.

Theo came inside.

---

They stood in the lodge's main room, the four of them, in a rough semicircle that was nobody's idea of a formation. The light through the windows had turned the color of weak tea. Everything smelled like mildew and pine sap and the specific chemical sweetness of rodent poison.
