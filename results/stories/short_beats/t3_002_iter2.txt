# The Last Watch of the *Mara Celeste*

---

I made her tea at six that morning, same as every morning. Oolong, loose leaf, the tin she kept locked in the cabinet above the chart table because she said the crew would burn through it in a week if she let them. I knew where she kept the key. She knew I knew. That was the arrangement.

The cup was the white one with the blue stripe, chipped on the handle where I'd knocked it against the sink edge in rough water off Hatteras two seasons back. She never said anything about the chip. She just kept drinking from it. I'd offered to replace it in Nassau and she'd looked at me like I'd suggested we replace the keel.

I brought it to the wheelhouse at six-fifteen. She was already there, which was unusual. Normally I'd find her in the cabin, dressed but sitting on the bunk with her boots half-laced, staring at something I couldn't see. Mornings were hard for her lately. But that day she was at the wheel, both hands on it, watching the sky to the southeast like she was reading a letter she'd been expecting.

I set the cup on the ledge beside the compass. She said thank you. She didn't pick it up.

I went back to the galley and started breakfast. Rice porridge with salted fish, which was what we had because the provisions were running thin and I'd learned to stretch rice into anything. There were four of us left on the *Mara Celeste* by then — Wen, myself, old Fong who handled the sails, and the boy Marco who was somebody's nephew and had come aboard in San Juan thinking this was going to be a career. Fong ate whatever I put in front of him without comment. Marco complained, but he was nineteen and far from home, so I let him. He'd push the porridge around his bowl and ask when we'd make port, and I'd say soon, because that was what you said.

Wen didn't come down for breakfast. I didn't call her. I knew by then that calling only made it worse — she'd come down with that stiff face, sit across from me, move the spoon around the bowl exactly four times, and go back up. It was theater by that point, the pretending to eat, and it cost her something each time she performed it. So I stopped asking. I wrapped a bowl and left it on the counter where she could find it if she wanted.

She didn't want.

By eight, the barometer had dropped far enough that even Marco noticed. The glass sat on the shelf above the galley stove, and I watched it the way I always did, the way my mother taught me to watch it on her boat when I was a girl — not staring, just glancing, the way you check on a sleeping child. You're not worried, exactly. You're just aware.

At eight-thirty I went up to collect the teacup and it was full and cold.

I poured it out over the rail. The sea was the color of an old bruise. I rinsed the cup, dried it, put it back. Then I went to find the cards.

---

We played Zheng Shangyou, the two of us, which doesn't work well with two people, but we'd developed our own version over the months. Stripped-down, no teams, a bastardized thing that probably would have offended any serious player. We played it every morning after breakfast, at the little table bolted to the galley floor. It had started as a way to pass time during a long crossing from Guangzhou, and then it had become a habit, and then it had become something else — the thing we did instead of talking about the things we couldn't talk about.

I dealt the cards at nine. She came down at nine-fifteen, which was late for her but she'd been watching the sky and I understood.

"Four-card or six-card?" I asked.

"Six," she said, and sat down across from me.

Her hands were cold. I could see it in the way she held the cards, the stiffness in her fingers. I wanted to say something about it. I didn't. The space between *noticing* and *saying* had become where I lived, those last weeks.

She played a pair of sevens. I played a pair of jacks. She looked at her hand, looked at me, and set down three queens, which was a waste of queens but she'd always played like that — recklessly, burning her high cards early, daring the game to punish her for it. It used to infuriate me. I'd scold her about strategy and she'd shrug and say she'd rather lose fast than win careful.

The hull groaned. It had been groaning since dawn, a low sound like a door someone was leaning on, and I'd been hearing it the way you hear the engine — as part of the boat's living noise, its constant conversation with the water. But this groan was longer, and it came from deeper, from the planking below the waterline, and when it stopped there was a small silence where the sea seemed to be considering something.

Wen played a two, which is the highest card. I had nothing to beat it. She collected the trick.

"Wind's coming around," she said.

"I know."

"Southeast."

"I know."

She looked at her cards. She didn't look at the porthole, where the sky had gone the color of wet concrete, or at the barometer, which I could see from where I sat and which had dropped another three millibars since breakfast. She looked at her cards. She played a five of diamonds, which was the weakest possible opening, and I understood that she was stretching the game, making it last, dealing out the small cards first so there would be more tricks to play.

I played a seven on her five. She played an eight. I played a ten.

The kettle slid off the burner. It didn't fall — it moved, deliberately, the way objects move when the angle of the floor has changed so gradually that your body adjusts before your mind does. I got up and wedged it back into the burner frame and tightened the gimbal, and when I sat down Wen had rearranged her cards and was waiting for me with an expression that was almost patience.

I played a pair of fours. She played a pair of nines. The cards slid a quarter-inch to port across the table and I pressed my hand flat on them, and we both pretended that was a normal thing to do.

"Your trick," I said.

She collected it. She held the cards in a neat stack, aligned the edges with her thumb. Her hands weren't shaking. I want to say that clearly. Her hands were not shaking. They were cold, and they were steady, and they held the cards the way they always held the cards — like small things mattered, like the alignment of edges was worth attending to even now, even with the hull singing and the kettle walking and the sky pressing down on us like a hand on a chest.

She played her last card. I don't remember what it was. I remember that she set it down gently, and that the game was over, and that she looked at me across the table with an expression I have been trying to describe for as long as I have been trying to tell this story, and I cannot. It was not sadness. It was the other side of sadness, the place you get to when you've walked all the way through.

I said, "I'll make more tea."

She said, "All right."

I made the tea. I boiled the water on the gimbal stove, and the flame flickered sideways in a wind that shouldn't have been inside the galley but was, and I measured the leaves from the locked tin — one scoop, pressed flat, the way she liked it — and I poured the water and I set the lid on the cup and I carried it up to the wheelhouse with both hands because the deck was tilting and I would not spill it.

She took the cup this time. She held it between her palms. She did not drink.

---

The pumps gave out at half past noon, though by then it was hard to tell the time because the sky had closed over us completely, a low, revolving ceiling the color of iron, and the clock on the wheelhouse wall had stopped when a wave broke the porthole glass and the spray got into everything. Fong had been working the manual pump since eleven, and when it seized he came up the companionway with bilge water to his shins and a face I'd only seen once before, on a man who'd been told something by a doctor.

"How much time?" Wen asked.

Fong held up his hand, fingers spread. Five hours, maybe. Maybe less. It depended on the sea, and the sea was not in a negotiating mood.

Marco was in the aft cabin, sick and terrified, curled on a bunk with his coat over his head. I'd checked on him twice. The second time he grabbed my wrist and said something in Spanish I couldn't follow, and I held his hand until the grip loosened and he went back to that place the very young go when they cannot believe what is happening.

Wen sent the distress signal at one o'clock, or what I think was one o'clock. She sent it calmly, reading our position off the chart, repeating it twice. Her voice on the radio was the voice she used for port authorities and customs agents — clipped, exact, the voice of a woman who had been doing this since she was twenty and her grandfather's hands first closed over hers on the wheel of this same vessel and he said, *This is how you call for help, and this is how you don't need to.*

No one answered. She tried again at what was probably two. The static had a quality to it that I can only describe as *thick* — it wasn't silence, it was the sound of a great deal of air and water moving between us and anyone who might be listening.

She turned the radio off. She did it carefully, the way she did everything — she turned the dial back to its resting position, and she set the handset in its cradle, and she looked at the radio for a moment as though she were putting it to bed.

Then she went to the mast.

I followed her. I followed her out the wheelhouse door and into the full force of it, and I want to be precise about what *it* was because people hear *typhoon* and they think of wind, they think of rain, but what I remember is the sound — a sound so total it replaced thought, a sound that was not loud so much as *everywhere*, the way water is everywhere when you're underwater. The wind was the least of it. The least of it was the rain, which hit sideways and tasted of salt because by that point the sea was in the air and the air was in the sea and the distinction between them had become academic.

Wen walked to the mainmast. She had rope. I don't know when she'd taken the rope — I think she'd had it for hours, coiled under the chart table, waiting. She began to lash herself to the mast, wrapping the line around her waist, around her chest, knotting it with the quick competence of a woman who had been tying knots since she could hold rope.

From the outside — if there had been an outside, if anyone had been watching from some impossible steady vantage — it would have looked like what it's supposed to look like. The captain lashing herself to the mast. The old image. The noble image. You've seen it in paintings. You've seen it in films. The captain who goes down with the ship because that is what captains do, because duty is a thing that binds you to the wheel even when the wheel is connected to nothing, even when the rudder is gone and the ship is no longer a ship but a collection of materials in the process of becoming wreckage.

But I knew why. I'd known for weeks. I'd known since before the storm, before the bilge, before the barometer started its long confession. I'd known since the letters.

---

The first one came in Bridgetown. I know because I was the one who went to the post office — Wen hated going ashore for errands, said it made her feel like a tourist on her own boat, so I handled the mail and the provisioning and the port fees. There were three envelopes from the same bank, the name printed in that particular shade of blue that financial institutions use, the blue that is meant to suggest trust and suggests instead the color of a vein being opened. I brought them back. I set them on the chart table next to her coffee. She opened them while I was still in the room, which, looking back, was either a sign of trust or a sign that she had stopped caring who saw.

Her face didn't change. That was how I knew it was bad. If it had been a normal problem — a fee, a delay, an administrative tangle — she would have cursed, or sighed, or made the small disgusted sound she made when bureaucracy intruded on the work of actually sailing. She didn't do any of that. Her face went flat. Not angry. Not sad. Flat, the way the sea goes flat before something terrible, that glassy stillness that fishermen learn to read as a held breath.

She folded the letters back into their envelopes. She put them in the chart table drawer. She drank her coffee.

I said, "Everything all right?"

She said, "Fine."

Over the next weeks, more letters came. I stopped opening the mail in front of her — I'd sort it, separate the blue envelopes from the rest, leave them where she could find them privately. I don't know why I did that. I think I thought privacy was a kindness I could offer. I think I was wrong. I think what she needed was for someone to sit across from her and say, *I can see what is happening to you, and you don't have to fold it back into the envelope.*

But I made her tea instead. I dealt the cards. I cooked rice and salted fish and put the bowl where she could find it. These were the things I knew how to do, and I did them with the diligence of someone who believes that if the small things are maintained, the large things might hold too.

They didn't hold.

I overheard the radio calls. I wasn't trying to listen — the galley was directly below the wheelhouse, and the ventilation grate carried sound. I heard Wen's voice, and I heard the voice on the other end, and I heard words like *default* and *seizure* and *forfeiture*, and I heard a date, and I heard the name of a port. Bermuda. The repo men were in Bermuda. They had filed the papers and the papers said the *Mara Celeste* — her grandfather's boat, the boat she'd sailed since she was twelve, the boat whose every plank and fitting she could identify by touch in total darkness — the papers said it was no longer hers. The papers said it belonged to the bank now, and the bank had sent men to Bermuda to collect it, and all Wen had to do was bring it to them and step off and watch them take it away.

After that call, she stopped answering the radio. She'd turn it on for weather reports and turn it off before anyone could reach her. The bank's frequency became a kind of static she could feel, I think, even when the radio was silent — a pressure on the other side of the dial, patient and clerical and certain of its right.

I learned the repo men's names from the documents she left on the chart table. I won't say them here. They are ordinary names. That is the worst part. They are the names of men who go to work in the morning and do their jobs and go home and believe they have done nothing wrong, because the paperwork supports them, because the system says this is how it works: you borrow, you cannot repay, we take. The mechanism is so clean. There is no blood on it. There is no cruelty in it, if you define cruelty narrowly enough. There is only process, which moves like weather — impersonally, comprehensively, and without appeal.

Wen's grandfather built the *Mara Celeste* in a shipyard in Fuzhou in 1962. He named it for a song his wife sang, which Wen once hummed for me during a night watch and then stopped halfway through and said she'd forgotten the rest. The boat passed to Wen's mother, who didn't sail, who kept it maintained and moored and waiting, and then to Wen, who did sail, who had always sailed, who I believe could not have lived on land the way some people cannot live at altitude — the stillness of the earth would have been the wrong pressure, the wrong rhythm, a wrongness in the bones.

The bank did not care about any of this. The bank cared about a number, and the number was not being met, and so the bank would take the boat, because the boat was collateral, because someone at some point had signed something that turned a grandfather's love into a financial instrument, and now the instrument was being called.
