# Perdition Gulch

---

## I.

The surveyors came on a Tuesday, which Perdition Gulch would remember only because Tuesdays were when the general store received its flour shipment, and the mule wagon had to be rerouted around the three men hammering pine stakes into the cracked alkali south of the creek bed. The stakes were painted red at their tips. They looked, in the flat morning light, like fingers dipped in something.

Harlan Goss, chief surveyor for the Southern Arizona Land and Rail Company, unrolled his chain-of-title document on the hood of the supply wagon and held its corners against the wind with both hands. The paper was immaculate—notarized in Tucson, counter-stamped in the territorial office, bearing the looping signature of a judge whose name nobody in Perdition Gulch had heard spoken aloud. Goss was a careful man. He had arrived with documents sufficient, he believed, to answer any challenge that a town of this size could produce.

He had not accounted for Edith Clary.

She came down the center of the road with the unhurried gait of a woman walking to her own kitchen—shoulders level, eyes already reading the document from a distance at which the print should have been illegible. She was fifty-three years old and had a face that recorded everything and volunteered nothing. In her left hand, she carried a leather journal, though she did not open it. She had never needed to open it.

"Section twelve, paragraph four, clause six of the Territorial Mineral Rights Reservation Act of 1867," she said, arriving at the wagon. She did not greet Harlan Goss. "Any survey conducted for purposes of commercial rail construction across land previously designated as open range under the Act of 1862 requires co-signature from the sitting territorial governor *and* written consent from established residents of record. Your document carries neither." She paused. "Your judge's name is Ackerman. He was disbarred in California in 1859 for forging deeds in Mariposa County. He was reinstated on appeal, but the reinstatement was conditional, and the conditions exclude exactly this type of territorial endorsement." She looked at the document as though it were a stain on the wagon's hood. "You are holding expensive wastepaper, Mr. Goss."

Goss opened his mouth.

He did not speak, because at that moment he became aware of two figures who had not been there when he last checked the perimeter of his work site. To his left, on the low ridge above the creek bed, a woman stood with her arms crossed, a rifle slung diagonally across her back, her hat brim throwing her face into shadow. To his right, on the opposite bank, a man stood in the identical posture—arms crossed, rifle slung at the identical angle, hat brim cutting the identical shadow. They were perhaps sixty yards apart and they looked at Goss with a single expression distributed across two faces. The woman shifted her weight to her left foot. The man shifted his weight to his right. It was not mimicry. It was something worse than mimicry. It was unity.

These were Lena and August Salt.

Goss had been warned, in Tucson, that this town had protectors. He had been told the word *strange*. He had assumed it meant merely competent. Standing between the Salt twins, he revised this assumption.

"There's also the matter of your outriders," said Edith Clary, without looking up. "The four men you have stationed in the arroyo east of the livery stable, who arrived last night and believe no one has noticed them."

Goss's face performed a complicated journey.

"I mention them," Edith continued, "because Cora has been speaking with them."

This was not entirely accurate. Cora had been standing near the arroyo's mouth, seventeen years old and small-boned and silent, her head tilted at the angle she adopted when she was listening to frequencies the air did not carry. The outriders—hard men, hired men, men who had been selected because they were comfortable with the application of force to reluctant landscapes—had experienced, each of them, a sudden and unshakeable feeling that every private thought in their skulls was being read aloud to someone they couldn't see. One of them had already left. He'd walked his horse out of the arroyo without a word, and his colleagues had watched him go and understood completely why, and hated that they understood.

Cora walked back toward the center of town now and passed close to Edith and murmured, without breaking stride, "The foreman doesn't believe any of this will hold. He's planning to come back Thursday with a revised filing and a marshal he's hired from Bisbee. The marshal's name is Coogan and he has a warrant Goss doesn't know about yet."

Edith filed this. Edith filed everything.

It was Silas who walked out last. He was the oldest of them, sixty-one, with hands so steady they seemed carved. He was the town's doctor, though the word *doctor* sat oddly against what Silas actually did, which was closer to a kind of communion—his hands knew what flesh needed before flesh knew it itself. He had set bones that set themselves under his touch. He had drawn fever from a child's forehead by pressing his palm to it and breathing once, slowly, as though exhaling the illness out of the room. Perdition Gulch did not have a cemetery, which for a town of its size, in this territory, in this decade, was a statistical impossibility that no one discussed.

He walked to Harlan Goss and extended his hand. Goss, disarmed by the sheer normalcy of the gesture, shook it.

"You should go home, Mr. Goss," Silas said. His voice was the voice of a man who had never been injured in a way that mattered. "This is not a town that needs what you are bringing."

Goss went. His surveyors pulled their stakes and loaded them into the wagon and drove south without looking back. The outriders, what remained of them, dissolved into the landscape. By noon, the road was empty. Perdition Gulch exhaled. Its ninety-six residents returned to the small businesses of living—the laundry, the livestock, the bread, the gossip, the ordinary texture of a town that did not know it was a miracle and would not have used that word if it did.

The five did not celebrate. They did not convene. They had learned, years ago, that the most effective performance of normalcy was to *be* normal in all the spaces between crisis. Edith went home and transcribed, from memory, the entirety of Goss's chain-of-title document, including the watermark irregularities and the pressure pattern of the notary's stamp, into a journal that joined three hundred and eleven identical journals on shelves that lined every wall of her small house. The Salt twins climbed the ridge together and sat watching the southern road until the dust from the departing wagon was indistinguishable from the desert's ordinary exhalations, and then they parted without speaking, because they did not need to speak, because they had never needed to speak, because the space between their two skulls was a single lit room in which both of them had always been standing. Cora went to the arroyo and sat where the outriders had been and pressed her palms over her ears, which never helped, and breathed, and waited for the volume of the world's thinking to subside to its usual dull roar.

Silas went home and began to prepare supper.

He was reaching for a tin of beans on the high shelf when the nail caught him. A roofing nail, driven inadequately into the support beam above the pantry, protruding just far enough that the meat of his palm dragged across it as he reached. A small wound. A nail scratch. The kind of injury that, for Silas, had not been an injury for thirty-seven years—the kind his body had always answered instantly, closing the breach before the blood had time to understand it was free.

He watched the blood come.

It came slowly, as blood does from a shallow cut—a bright line filling the scratch, a bead forming, swelling, falling. It struck the countertop. Another bead formed. Silas stared at it. He pressed his other palm against the cut, not to heal it but to hold it, the way an ordinary person holds an ordinary wound. He waited. His body did not answer. The flesh remained open. The blood continued its modest, unhurried work.

He stood there for a long time.

*

*They are younger here. Younger by decades. Five figures standing at varying distances from each other on the rimrock above Perdition Canyon, which does not yet have that name, which is just a canyon, which is just a crease in the earth filled now with smoke that rises in a column so black it looks architectural—a pillar, a monument. Below and within the smoke there are sounds, but the sounds are ending. The five do not look at each other. They do not look at each other because to look at each other would be to confirm what each of them knows, separately, in the sealed privacy of their separate skulls: that they are close enough to see, that they were closer when it started, and that the smoke is not a thunderstorm or a wildfire or an act of God but instead the conclusion of an act of men, committed against other people, which the five of them watched from beginning to end and which is now over. The canyon holds its dead the way a bowl holds water. The smoke thins. Nothing moves below. One of them—it is not clear who—turns away first. The others follow, at intervals, as though by turning away separately they can maintain the fiction that they do not yet constitute a group.*

*

Silas wrapped his hand in a clean cloth. The blood soaked through in a pattern like a small rose, dark at the center, lighter at the edges. He changed the cloth. He changed it again. On the third cloth, the bleeding stopped—not because his body had healed itself but because the wound was shallow and that is what shallow wounds do, eventually, in the bodies of ordinary men.

He sat at his kitchen table in the town he had helped make safe and he looked at the cloth and he understood, in some stratum below language, that whatever had underwritten his strangeness had begun to withdraw. He did not yet know the word for what was happening, so he used the word that doctors use when they mean *I don't know*: he called it a symptom.

Outside, the sun went down on Perdition Gulch, and the dark came on like something that had been waiting.

## II.

In the weeks that followed, Edith Clary's memory began to shed its perfection the way a roof sheds shingles in a wind—not all at once, not catastrophically, but in discrete losses that left gaps she could feel but not repair. A date would go missing. A face would blur at its edges. She reached for the text of the Mineral Rights Act and found the clauses rearranged, their sequence jumbled, whole sentences replaced by a silence that felt like a room she'd entered only to find the furniture removed.

She told no one.

She had told no one about anything that mattered for thirty years. This was not a departure from her character but its deepest expression. Edith Clary had been cursed—she would have used the word *gifted*; the distinction had collapsed long ago—with a memory so total and so unyielding that her mind was less a mind than an archive, each moment of her fifty-three years preserved in the amber of absolute recall, retrievable in full sensory detail, permanently lit, permanently loud, permanently *there*. She remembered the smell of her mother's kitchen in 1841. She remembered the number of boards in the floor of the schoolhouse she'd attended for two years in Missouri. She remembered the color of the sky on every one of the approximately nineteen thousand days she had been alive. These memories did not sleep. They did not fade. They stacked, each day's addition pressing down on the mass below, and Edith carried them the way the earth carries its own weight: invisibly, at great cost, without complaint.

The journals were not aides-mémoire. Edith Clary did not need aides-mémoire. The journals were *pressure valves*—the act of writing a thing down created the faintest, most temporary illusion that the thing had been externalized, placed outside the skull, entrusted to paper and therefore no longer solely her burden. It never worked. The memory remained, as bright and as heavy as before. But the ritual of transcription gave shape to her days. Three hundred and twelve journals, each one filled margin to margin in a hand so small it required a magnifying glass to read, each one containing the complete unedited record of everything Edith Clary had perceived in a given period.

Including—in volume forty-seven, on pages she had never reread, in a section she had written in a single unbroken session that lasted from midnight to dawn on a night in 1852—the massacre in the canyon.

She began burning the journals on a Thursday.

She did it in the firepit behind her house, three or four volumes at a time, watching the pages curl and blacken and give up their words to the heat. She was not systematic about the order. She pulled journals from the shelves at random, or near-random—though later, when her daughter examined what was left, it would become clear that the randomness had a shape, that the volumes Edith burned first were the ones closest to volume forty-seven, as though she were trying to burn toward the center of something but could not bring herself to begin there.

Her daughter was Ruth.

Ruth was thirty years old and had no gift. This was unremarkable—the abilities of the five had never been hereditary, had never been explicable, had simply *been*, like weather, like geography—but Ruth had spent her childhood in a house where every surface was covered in the written evidence of impossible memory, and she had understood from an early age that her mother's mind was a country she could not enter. She lived in Perdition Gulch because this was where her life was. She worked at the general store. She was practical and kind and ordinary in ways that Edith regarded with a feeling that was not quite envy and not quite grief but lived somewhere in the country between.

Ruth found the half-burned page on a Saturday morning.

She had come to check on her mother—Edith had been erratic, absent from town, her face carrying a new expression Ruth didn't recognize, something between relief and panic—and had found the firepit still warm, ash drifting, the remnants of a journal smoldering at the edges. Most of the pages were gone. But the fire had been imperfectly tended, as Edith's fires increasingly were, and a single page had fallen outside the pit's ring of stones, had smoldered and gone out, and now lay in the dirt like a leaf, three-quarters blackened, one quarter legible.

Ruth picked it up.

The handwriting was her mother's—tiny, precise, inhumanly regular, the script of a woman who did not need to pause or reconsider because the source material was always playing. The surviving fragment began mid-sentence:

*—counted thirty-one, including the children, though one of the children may have been deceased prior to the attack, as I observed from my position on the ridge a small form wrapped in blankets and laid near the fire that did not move during the initial—*

Ruth stopped reading.

She read the rest.

*

The canyon had no name then. It was a scallop in the earth where the rock shelved back from the plain, deep enough to block wind, wide enough for wagons, fed by a seasonal creek that in late spring still carried enough water to sustain stock animals and fill cooking pots. It was the kind of place that travelers found and stayed in, briefly, before moving on. The migrants had found it in early April, a group of thirty-one people—twenty-two adults, nine children—traveling from points east toward the promises that the territory extended to anyone willing to believe them. They were not a single family. They were a loose confederation of households that had merged on the trail, pooling resources, sharing labor, combining their vulnerabilities into something that felt, from the inside, like strength.

They had been in the canyon for three days when the riders came.

The riders were seven. They came from the south. They were not soldiers and not lawmen and not affiliated with any enterprise that operated under a name. They were men who had discovered that the territory's great empty spaces contained, in their emptiness, a permission that the settled world did not extend—that where there were no witnesses, there were no laws, and where there were no laws, there was nothing between an impulse and its satisfaction.
