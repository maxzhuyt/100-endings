## I. STATEMENT OF PRIOR FILING ERRORS

Coral-Marie Davis had not been important for seventeen years, and she was fine with it.

She wanted this on the record. She wanted it known, catalogued, filed in whatever system was listening, that she had not spent the interregnum composing a secret theory or nursing a heroic suspicion. She had spent it the way she had spent the eleven years before it — her years at the Palmdale branch of the California Department of Motor Vehicles — which is to say: she had done the work in front of her and not complained about the lighting. On the colony ship *Archival*, the work in front of her was inventory management, a position she had been assigned because someone on the emergency governance council had reasoned that a woman who once processed four hundred license renewals in a single shift could probably count boxes of powdered protein.

She could. She was, in fact, quite good at it. Nobody remarked on this.

The ship carried 3,012 human beings away from the coordinates where Earth had been. *Had been* was the only accurate phrasing, though different factions aboard the *Archival* preferred different constructions. The captain, Rénard Achebe, favored *was destroyed*, emphasizing agency, demanding a perpetrator. The ship's counselor preferred *was lost*, the gentleness of which made Coral want to bite through her own tongue. The Religious Continuation Committee said *was taken*. The children — there were forty-seven children, all under the age of three at the time of departure, none of whom remembered anything — said nothing about it, mostly, because to them the ship was simply the world and the world had always been the ship and the blue marble in the archival photographs was just a picture, the way a drawing is just a drawing.

What had happened to Earth was this: the Auditors had processed it.

That was the term. *Processed.* Coral, who had spent her adult life inside the specific American subspecies of bureaucracy, recognized the word immediately for what it was — not a euphemism but something worse: a *category designation.* A euphemism knows it is hiding something. A category designation does not know anything. It is simply the label on the folder. Earth had been foldered. The folder had been filed. Eight billion people and every blue whale and every unreturned library book had been re-categorized as *Processed — Standard Cosmological Audit, Sol Sector, Cycle 1,448,302,117.* And the 3,012 people who had made it onto the ship — a ship that had been, until seventy-two hours before the Processing, an experimental deep-space research vessel never intended to carry more than a crew of forty — had been flying toward a planet called, in Auditor nomenclature, Permitted Habitation Zone 7-G/4422, and in the stunned, grief-thick shorthand of the survivors, *Sanctuary.*

Seventeen years. Seventeen years of rationed air and recycled water and a captain who refused to stop broadcasting into the void, demanding answers, accountability, the name and badge number of whoever had signed the order. Seventeen years of children growing up with Earth as bedtime story. Seventeen years of Coral counting boxes, and sleeping, and not being important.

She discovered the error on a Tuesday.

Not ship's Tuesday — there were no real days aboard the *Archival*, only the agreed-upon fiction of a twenty-four-hour cycle maintained because human circadian rhythms, like human beings themselves, refused to accept that the context for their existence had been eliminated. But the ship's systems called it Tuesday, and Coral, who respected systems, called it Tuesday too.

She had been reviewing the archive of humanity's filed communications with the Auditors. This was not her job. Her job was counting boxes. But seven months earlier she had counted all the boxes — every box, a complete and accurate inventory, the first one the ship had ever had — and nobody had given her a new task, and Coral did not do well without a task, so she had started reading the communications archive the way another person might have started knitting.

Seventeen years of communications. Seventeen years of formal petitions, emergency appeals, requests for reconsideration, demands for tribunal. The captain had authored many of them, in the ringing rhetorical style of a man who had been told his entire life that his voice was commanding. The ship's lawyer — a former patent attorney from Düsseldorf who was the closest thing they had — had drafted the legal ones, full of citations to interstellar conventions the Auditors had never heard of, because the Auditors had written the conventions after they had written the conventions, and all the way down, and the conventions humanity knew about were, in a sense that the patent attorney could not yet fathom, *fan fiction.*

The forms were wrong.

Not the arguments. Not the rhetoric, not the legal reasoning, not the desperate eloquent fury of three thousand people screaming into the administrative void. The *forms.* The formatting. The classification headers. The jurisdictional codes. The species identification number that every communication opened with — the number that said, in effect, *we are the ones talking to you, and we exist, and here is our receipt.*

The number was wrong.

Not incorrect, exactly. Not *someone else's* number. It was a number that had once been assigned to a species in the Sol system. But the species it had been assigned to was, by Auditor reckoning, *Homo erectus.* The form had expired 1.8 million years ago.

Coral stared at the screen for a long time. The hum of the ship was the hum it always was, the sound of a world that knew it was a hallway pretending to be a house.

She pulled up the next communication. Wrong number. She pulled up the next. Wrong number. She went back to the first message, the one the captain had fired off fourteen hours after departure, the one that was essentially a scream in the shape of a formal letter: *To whom it may concern, you have destroyed our planet—*

Wrong number. Filed under a species that had been extinct for nearly two million years. Which meant that every communication, every appeal, every filing, had gone — where? Into the archive of a closed account. Into the equivalent of a P.O. box whose owner had been dead since before *Homo sapiens* had learned to bury their dead. For seventeen years, humanity had been sending its most anguished mail to an address that didn't exist, and the Auditors, who processed everything, had been processing it into a folder marked, in the flattest possible sense of the word, *inactive.*

Earth had not been condemned. Earth had been — Coral reached for the word the way she had once reached for the laminated cheat sheet under her counter at the Palmdale DMV, the one that listed every error code and its remedy — Earth had been *misrouted.*

Coral closed the archive. She opened it again. She checked the species classification index that the ship's computer had downloaded from the Auditor beacon during the first weeks of the journey, the one that no one had ever read because it was 1.4 billion pages long and written in a notation system that made the U.S. tax code look like a haiku.

She found *Homo sapiens.* The entry was three lines long.

> **Species 447-2290-18-HS.** *Homo sapiens.* Sol-3. Carbon-based, oxygen-dependent, moderately encephalized. Status: PENDING INITIAL CLASSIFICATION. No audit action authorized until classification is complete. See: Precursor Notation 1127-a (visual-semiotic filing requirement outstanding).

*Pending initial classification.* Humanity had never been classified. The Auditors had never finished the intake paperwork. The audit of Earth — the Processing, the annihilation, All Of It — had been performed on a *pending* account, which meant, in any bureaucratic system Coral had ever known, from the State of California to whatever this was, that it had been performed *without authorization.*

It was not a verdict. It was not a judgment. It was not even a crime, because crime required intent, and the Auditors, as far as anyone could tell, did not have intent the way that aeli had teeth — they had *procedures*, and procedures do not intend, they merely *execute*, and if the procedure executes on the wrong file, the procedure does not apologize, because apology is not a procedure.

Earth had been destroyed by a clerical error.

Eight billion people, dead. Every whale, every library. The Pacific Ocean. The Grand Canyon. Coral's mother. Coral's apartment in Palmdale, with the succulent on the windowsill she had been keeping alive for eleven years, the longest relationship she'd ever had. The particular quality of afternoon light through the windows of the DMV at 4:15 p.m. on a Thursday in October. All of it. Processed. Filed. Closed. Because someone — *something* — had pulled the wrong file.

The grief was not new. The grief was seventeen years old, as weathered and load-bearing as the ship's hull. What was new was the shape of the grief — or rather, the re-shaping, the way you can live in a house your entire life and then be told that the foundation is not concrete but cardboard, and the house is the same house, and it still stands, but your legs go strange.

Coral sat in the inventory office, alone with the boxes she had counted, and placed her hands flat on the desk the way she had always done at the DMV when a customer was screaming at her — palms down, fingers spread, a gesture that said *I am here, I am not leaving, and I understand that you are upset, and the form is still the form.*

Then she went to find the captain.

---

She found him on the bridge, which he never left during waking hours and sometimes did not leave during sleeping hours. Captain Rénard Achebe was a man who had been sculpted by fury the way other people are sculpted by religion: it was the structuring principle of his days, the thing that got him upright, the lens through which the world — such as it was — cohered. He had been the commander of a deep-space research mission. He was now, by default and desperate election, the leader of the human species. The promotion had not come with a raise.

"Captain," Coral said.

"Davis." He did not look up from the navigation console, which was showing the same star field it had been showing for seventeen years, the slow crawl toward Sanctuary. "If this is about the protein powder count—"

"The forms are wrong."

"What forms?"

"All of them. Every form we've filed with the Auditors since departure. They're all filed under the wrong species code."

He looked up then. Achebe had a face that could make silence feel like weather — a pressure drop, a change in the air. "Explain."

She explained. She had to explain it three times, in three different ways, because the first time he didn't understand and the second time he understood but couldn't believe it, and the third time he believed it but could not hold the shape of it in his mind because the shape was this: *we were not sentenced, we were misfiled.*

"You're telling me," he said, and his voice was the voice of a man whose rage — the rage that had kept him alive, that had kept him *captaining*, that had given his every broadcast into the dark the character of a man who would not stop until he got an answer — was being informed that it had no valid target, "that Earth was destroyed by *accident.*"

"I'm telling you that Earth was destroyed under a species classification that has been inactive for 1.8 million years," Coral said. "And that our species was never fully processed into the Auditor system. We were — are — pending. The audit action was not authorized."

"So it was illegal."

"I don't think *illegal* is a category they have. I think *procedurally irregular* is as close as it gets."

"Eight billion people. *Procedurally irregular.*"

"Yes."

The captain's hands were gripping the edge of the navigation console. His knuckles had gone the color of old bone. Coral knew that color. She had seen it on the knuckles of a man in the Palmdale DMV who had been told, after waiting four hours, that he was in the wrong line, and that the correct line was in a different building, and that the different building was closed on Tuesdays. The man had gripped the counter and made a sound that was not quite a word, and Coral had said, *I understand, sir. If you'll take a number, I can help you with the reassignment form.* The man had taken a number. People usually did, in the end. There was nothing else to do.

"What does this mean for us?" Achebe said. "Practically. What does this mean."

"It means we have grounds for an appeal. A real one. Not a petition, not a protest — an appeal based on procedural error within their own system. But the appeal has to be filed correctly. Under the right species code, in the right format, through the right channel."

"And you know how to do that?"

"I know how to learn how to do that."

The captain looked at her. It was the first time in seventeen years that anyone had looked at Coral-Marie Davis with a particular expression — the expression that says *you might be the most important person in the room.* She did not enjoy it. She had never been the most important person in any room, and she understood, with the deep structural intuition of someone who had spent her life in waiting rooms, that *the most important person in the room* is a designation that comes with responsibilities and no additional resources.

"Do it," said the captain.

Coral went back to her inventory office and began to read.

The news moved through the ship the way all news moved through the ship, which is to say: incorrectly at first, then too completely, then in mutant variations that bore the same relationship to the truth that a photocopy of a photocopy bears to the original — recognizable but degraded, slightly off-center, smudged in the places that mattered most.

*Earth was a mistake.* That was the first version. Then: *They didn't mean to kill us.* Then: *There was a typo.* Then: *We've been writing to the wrong department.* Then, the version that spread fastest because it was the simplest and the most painful: *It was an accident. All of it. Just an accident.*

There was a vigil in the common hall. There was a fight in the water reclamation corridor. There were three hundred people who said they had always known, because there are always three hundred people who say they have always known, and saying it costs nothing and buys the comfort of a world where someone, somewhere, was paying attention. There was a woman who laughed for forty-five minutes and could not stop and had to be sedated. There was a man who demanded that the captain turn the ship around, go back to the coordinates where Earth had been, and *make them look at it.* There was a child — a seventeen-year-old girl named Zhen, who had been two months old at departure and had no memory of anything except the ship — who did not react at all, at least not visibly.

Zhen drew.

She had been drawing since she could hold a crayon, which on the *Archival* meant since she could hold a nutrient stylus repurposed for the task by a botanist who'd noticed the girl reaching for pigmented things. Zhen drew constantly, instinctively, the way some people talk to themselves: not as expression but as *processing*, as a way of running the world through her hands. She drew the ship. She drew the corridors, the people, the particular way light fell through the observation deck's scuffed viewport. She drew Earth from photographs, over and over, not with grief — she had no experiential grief for a planet she had never touched — but with the focused attention of a student copying a masterwork. She drew oceans. She drew what she'd been told were trees.

The adults treated her drawings as a coping mechanism, or as a symptom, or, most often, as something to put on the wall to make the corridors feel less like corridors.
