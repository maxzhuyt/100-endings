# Perdition Gulch

## I.

The boy said, "The man with the bay horse is lying about the deed."

He said it the way he said everything—without emphasis, without accusation, the way a person might observe that rain was coming or that the bread had gone stale. He was ten years old and had never known a world in which other people's thoughts were private. He sat on the edge of the porch with his bare feet hanging over the dust and a piece of whittled pine in his lap that was becoming, slowly and without particular ambition, a bird.

Ruth Calvary leaned back in her chair and closed her eyes.

"September fourteenth, eighteen fifty-one," she said. "Horace Lamb filed the original claim for that parcel. Transfer to James Delacroix, witnessed by Arthur Pell, March ninth, eighteen fifty-three. Delacroix died intestate. No surviving issue. The property reverted to the territory."

She opened her eyes. The evening light caught the lines at the corners of her mouth, which were deep, because Ruth was fifty-seven and had spent each of those years remembering everything—every conversation, every meal, every cruelty, every face of every dead person she had ever known, arranged in perfect sequence like a library whose shelves extended in all directions without end.

On the far side of the porch, the Harper twins sat together on a bench made of railroad ties. Emmeline was mending a shirt. Josephine was watching the road. Neither had spoken aloud in several minutes, but between them a conversation had been running without interruption since before they could walk—a continuous tandem of thought and sensation, impression and response, so thorough that the question of where one sister ended and the other began had never admitted a clean answer. Now Emmeline's hands paused over the needle, and Josephine tilted her chin toward the road's eastern bend.

"Two more," Josephine said aloud, for Ruth's benefit and the boy's. "On foot. Maybe an hour out."

"Railroad?" Ruth asked.

"Emmeline thinks so."

"Emmeline is right," the boy said, not looking up from his carving. "They've got the company's name in their heads like a song they can't stop singing."

The fifth member of the council was not on the porch. Abel Crane was behind the smithy, shirtless, holding his left hand in the forge. He had placed it there six minutes ago. The skin had blackened and split and the fat beneath had rendered and the bones had begun to calcify in the heat, and now he withdrew it and watched, with the same flat, clinical attention he always brought to these experiments, as the flesh began to rebuild itself—the bones cooling, the tendons respooling, the skin crawling shut over the muscle like water filling a depression in sand. It took ninety seconds. He flexed the hand. Good as it had been that morning. Good as it had been forty years ago, when he had first discovered that his body refused, with what seemed like impersonal stubbornness, to remain damaged. He put his shirt back on and walked toward the porch.

The town of Perdition Gulch spread below them in the last of the light: forty buildings, maybe two hundred souls, arranged along a single rutted street that ran east to west and terminated at each end in empty country so vast it made the settlement look like a typographical error on the face of the earth. It was not a prosperous town. It was not a beautiful town. But for nineteen years it had been, by the standards of the territory, an impossible town—a place where disputes were settled before they became violence, where predatory interests were turned away before they could take root, where a drunk with a gun was disarmed by someone who had known he would draw before the thought had fully formed in his own mind. The people of Perdition Gulch did not fully understand why their town worked. They had theories—good water, good leadership, an absence of the mineral wealth that drew the worst of men. They were wrong about all of it, but they were not wrong that something held. Something did hold. It held every evening on this porch, in the configuration of five people whose abilities interlocked like the works of a clock: the boy to hear intention, Ruth to verify fact, the twins to coordinate response, and Abel to stand as the inarguable enforcement—a man who could not be killed and who, by his mere unkillable presence, made violence against the council a proposition without profit.

They did not call themselves a council. They did not call themselves anything. Ruth called it "the porch." Abel called it "the evening." The boy did not call it anything because the boy did not think in terms of institutions; he thought in terms of the noise that other people made inside his skull, which was quieter here, among these four, than anywhere else, because these four knew what he was and did not flood him with the fear and suspicion that others could not help but produce.

On this particular evening, the work was routine. The man with the bay horse and the fraudulent deed was told, politely, by Ruth, that the territory's records did not support his claim, and the boy confirmed to Emmeline with a glance that the man would accept this and leave without trouble, and Emmeline relayed as much to Josephine, who relaxed the hand she had placed, casually, on the stock of the rifle leaning against the bench. The two railroad men, when they arrived an hour later, were met by Abel, who told them that Perdition Gulch was not interested in a spur line, and who stood in the road with his arms folded and his expression empty of menace but also empty of doubt, and who did not move until they turned and began the long walk back the way they had come.

The town settled into its night. Lamps were lit. A dog barked twice and stopped. Someone played a fiddle, badly, in one of the houses near the creek, and someone else shouted at them to stop, and they did.

It was an ordinary evening, which is to say it was a miracle of the kind that had become so routine that no one recognized it as such.

Including the evening nineteen years earlier, which had also been routine, which had also been a miracle, but of a different kind—the kind that requires, for its mechanism, a death that someone could have prevented and did not.

---

The Coyle outfit rode into Perdition Gulch on the evening of November eleventh, eighteen forty-nine, and the boy—who was not yet born, but whose predecessor in the council's configuration was a woman named Patience Dark, who heard thoughts as he would later hear them, as involuntary noise—knew what they intended before they had dismounted.

Seven men. The Coyle brothers and five hired guns out of Abilene. Their thoughts were not complicated. They had come for the Alderman family's silver. They would kill anyone who interfered. They expected no interference because Perdition Gulch was small and unarmed and had no sheriff and no marshal and nothing resembling organized defense except, impossibly, the five people sitting on this same porch, who were all of those things at once.

Patience Dark said, "Seven men. They mean to kill the Aldermans."

Ruth Calvary—thirty-eight then, her memory already a perfect and unbearable instrument—said, "Thomas Coyle. Wanted in two territories for murder. His brother Daniel, same. The other five I don't have."

Emmeline Harper closed her eyes and thought to Josephine: *Abel could stop them.*

Josephine thought back: *Abel could stop anything.*

Abel Crane stood at the porch railing and watched the seven men ride slowly up the street. He was calculating. He was always calculating. His body's refusal to die had not made him brave; it had made him precise. He could walk into those seven men and absorb every bullet they could fire and remain standing, and the spectacle would be so incomprehensible that they would break and run or, failing that, he could physically beat them until they stopped. He had done comparable things before. But the calculation was not about whether he could stop the Coyle outfit. The calculation was about what would happen after.

"If I go down there," he said quietly, "those men will see what I am."

"Seven men," Ruth said. "Seven men who are wanted and running. Who will tell?"

"Someone always tells."

Patience Dark's hands were trembling. The Alderman household was four buildings away. She could hear—she could not stop hearing—the family inside. Thomas Alderman was reading to his daughter. His wife was washing dishes. The silver they had pulled from a creek bed in the mountains was in a strongbox under the floorboards of their bedroom, and they believed it was secret, and it was not.

"Seven men," Ruth said again, but now the repetition carried a different weight. She was not counting the threat. She was counting the witnesses.

"If they see Abel—" Emmeline said.

"——they tell someone," Josephine finished, aloud.

"And if they tell someone," Abel said, "then what we are becomes a fact in other people's mouths. And then someone comes for us. Not seven men. An army. A government. Someone who wants to know what we are and how we work and whether we can be used."

This was the calculation. It was not made coldly. It was made in the way that a doctor calculates whether a surgery will kill the patient—with full knowledge of the stakes, with nausea held carefully below the waterline of the voice. They were five people whose existence was impossible and whose safety depended entirely on that impossibility remaining unknown beyond the circle of a town that had accepted them only because the town had been given no choice, and they had earned, over years, something that was not quite trust but was adjacent to it.

Patience Dark said, "The girl is nine."

No one answered.

"The girl is nine and he is reading to her and she—" Patience stopped. She pressed her palms against her temples. "She is thinking about the book. She's thinking about whether the dog in the story will come home. She doesn't know. She can't know. She has no idea those men are—"

"Patience," Ruth said.

"I can *hear* her."

"I know."

"She's thinking about a dog."

"I know."

In the street, the Coyle outfit dismounted in front of the Alderman house. Thomas Coyle checked his revolver. Daniel Coyle spat in the dust. The five hired men spread out with the practiced geometry of people who had done this before—two to the back, one to each side, one at the front with the brothers. It was efficient. It was professional. It would take less than four minutes.

Emmeline thought to Josephine: *We could warn them. Just a shout. No powers. Just a human voice.*

Josephine thought back: *And then the Coyles turn on us. And Abel has to step in. And they see.*

*And they see.*

Ruth Calvary sat in her chair and did not close her eyes, because closing her eyes would not help—she would remember this with her eyes open or shut, in perfect detail, for every remaining day of her life, and she knew this, she knew it with the same certainty with which she knew every other fact she had ever encountered, and this knowledge—that she was in the process of manufacturing a memory she would never be able to set down—was its own particular species of horror, distinct from the horror of what was about to happen, though seated beside it, a companion horror, a horror about the machinery of her own mind.

Abel Crane gripped the porch railing. The wood cracked under his hands. His body was indestructible but the railing was pine and it splintered, and he held the broken pieces and stared at them as if they were a message in a language he had not yet learned.

The first gunshot came at seven minutes past eight. The second came three seconds later. The rest followed in a cluster, and then there was a gap, and then two more, and then silence.

Patience Dark screamed. Not with her voice—she could not scream with her voice, because any sound would draw the Coyles—but internally, with every capacity her mind possessed, a scream directed at no one and heard by no one except herself, because the nine-year-old girl's thoughts had stopped, the way a song stops when the singer is struck, mid-note, and the absence was louder than anything Patience had ever heard.

The Coyle outfit left Perdition Gulch seventeen minutes later, carrying the strongbox. They rode east. They did not look at the porch as they passed. They had no reason to. They had encountered no resistance. Perdition Gulch was, as expected, small and unarmed and helpless.

In the Alderman house, Thomas Alderman's body lay across the threshold of his daughter's room. He had gotten that far. His wife was in the kitchen. The book was on the floor, open to a page with an illustration of a dog on a road, and the girl was beside it, and none of them were anything anymore.

Patience Dark never spoke again. She died eight months later, of causes Ruth could enumerate with medical specificity but which Abel, privately, called a broken mind. The boy was born two years after that in a town sixty miles north, already hearing thoughts, already drowning in the noise of others, and he was brought to Perdition Gulch at the age of four by a mother who could not understand why her son knew things he should not know and who had heard, through the kind of rumor that travels in the territory like weather, that there was a town where strange people were welcome.

He sat on the porch now, nineteen years later, carving his bird.

"The railroad men are gone," he said.

"Good," Ruth said.

"The man with the bay horse is halfway to Colton."

"Good."

"Emmeline is worried about the creek level."

"Emmeline is always worried about the creek level," Josephine said, and smiled at her sister, and Emmeline smiled back, and between them passed a thought so rapid and so interwoven with a lifetime of shared consciousness that it could not be translated into language—it was something like *I know, I know, but the creek*, and also something like *I love you*, and also something like the memory of rain on a tin roof when they were young, all of it compressed into a single pulse of mutual recognition that lasted less than a second and contained more intimacy than most people experience in a year.

The town slept. The porch held. Five people sat in the dark, listening to the night and to each other, and the night was quiet, and the dead were still dead, and the dog in the story never came home.

## II.

The first sign was a cut on Abel Crane's left hand.

He got it splitting wood—a splinter, barely worth noticing, the kind of minor damage his body had been erasing without effort for over fifty years. He brushed it absently and went on working. Ten minutes later he looked at his hand and the cut was still there.

He stared at it.

It was a small cut. A quarter-inch, maybe less. The blood had clotted normally, the way blood clots in an ordinary body, which was the problem—his blood had not clotted normally in half a century. His blood did not clot at all. His wounds did not progress through stages of healing. They *reversed*, as though time ran backward in the locality of the damage, the skin simply returning to the state it had occupied before the injury as though the injury were a clerical error being corrected.

He went behind the smithy. He picked up a knife. He drew it across his forearm—a deliberate cut, two inches long, deep enough to part the skin and the fat beneath. He watched.

The blood ran. The wound gaped. The air touched the raw tissue and it stung, and he realized that he had forgotten what *stung* meant—not the concept but the sensation, the actual experience of a body objecting to its own damage, which he had not felt since he was eleven years old.

He made another cut. Deeper.

Nothing.

He drove the knife into his thigh. He left it there for ten seconds. Twenty. He pulled it out. Blood poured down his leg, soaked his trousers, pooled in the dust at his feet. The wound did not close. It did not begin to close. It behaved exactly as a wound behaves in a body that is subject to the ordinary jurisdiction of physics—it bled, and it hurt, and it was going to keep bleeding until he did something about it with his hands, with rags, with the crude and pathetic technologies available to a mortal person.
