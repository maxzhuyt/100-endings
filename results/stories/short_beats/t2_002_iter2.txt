# The HOA Board Will Now Come to Order

---

## I.

The letter was dated the fourteenth, a Tuesday, which Meredith Chen would later note was the same day she had driven Frank to his last oncology appointment, the one where Dr. Petrossian stopped talking about treatment timelines and began talking about comfort. She found the envelope in the mailbox between a circular from the grocery store and a sympathy card from someone at the old firm. The return address read Cedarview Estates Homeowners Association, and the typeface was the same officious serif the Association used for everything — meeting agendas, holiday reminders, notices about trash can placement on non-collection days.

**RE: Violation Notice — Unauthorized Structural Modification**
**Property: 4412 Briarwood Court**
**Nature of Violation: Installation of non-approved accessory structure (ramp, front entrance) without prior Architectural Review Committee submission, in contravention of Article IX, Section 3(b) of the Cedarview Estates CC&Rs.**
**Fine: $50.00 per day, compounding at 1.5% monthly on unpaid balance, effective retroactively to date of installation (October 2).**

She read it standing at the mailbox with her purse still on her shoulder. She read it again at the kitchen table. The ramp had been installed on October second because on October first Frank had come home from the hospital in a wheelchair, and the two concrete steps leading to the front door of the house they had lived in for twenty-three years had become, overnight, a wall.

The contractor had come the next morning. He was a young man named Tomás who had built ramps for the VA hospital and who measured the slope with a level and said he could have it done by afternoon. He did. It was pressure-treated pine with a non-slip surface and a handrail on both sides and it met every specification of the Americans with Disabilities Act, a fact that would prove irrelevant, because the ADA did not apply to private homeowner associations, as the letter's second page explained in a paragraph that had clearly been drafted by someone who enjoyed explaining this.

Meredith appealed. The process required a written submission to the Architectural Review Committee, which was Doug Whitmore and two people Doug Whitmore had appointed, and a fourteen-day waiting period, during which the fine continued to accrue. She submitted the appeal with a letter from Dr. Petrossian, a photograph of the ramp, and a copy of the ADA specifications, which she included not because it was legally relevant but because she wanted them to see it.

Doug came to the house on a Saturday. He brought banana bread. It was wrapped in plastic wrap with a small bow, and he carried it cupped in both hands like an offering, and he stood on the ramp he was fining her for and rang the doorbell and smiled when she opened the door.

"Meredith," he said. "May I come in?"

He sat at the kitchen table. He asked about Frank. He listened with his head tilted and his hands folded and he said, "I am so sorry you're going through this." He said it the way people say it when they mean it and also when they're about to say *but*.

"The committee reviewed your appeal," he said. He broke off a corner of banana bread for himself, a gesture of informality, of neighborliness, of *we're-all-in-this-together*. "And I wish I could tell you differently. I do. But the bylaws are the bylaws, Meredith. If we make an exception for one modification, we open the door for everyone, and then what do we have? You understand. You're a numbers person."

She was. She had been an actuary for Midwestern Mutual for thirty-eight years. She had spent her professional life calculating the statistical probability of catastrophe — the likelihood of flood, of fire, of a healthy thirty-four-year-old developing a terminal illness, of a house built on stable ground shifting on its foundation. She understood risk. She understood compounding.

"The fine is compounding," she said.

"I know. And I want you to know that's not my choice. That's in the documents. Section twelve, paragraph eight. The compounding schedule is automatic. It's in there to incentivize timely compliance, not to punish anyone."

"Frank can't get into the house without the ramp."

"I understand that. And I want to be clear — nobody is asking you to remove the ramp right now. We're just asking you to submit a proper ARC application so we can work toward a resolution that's consistent with community standards. The fine pauses as soon as the application is approved."

"You just denied the application."

"We denied the *appeal.* The application process is separate. There's a form. I can email it to you."

He emailed the form. It was eleven pages. It required a site plan, a materials specification, a neighbor impact assessment, and a $200 processing fee. It required the signature of the property owner, which was Frank, who could no longer hold a pen without his hand shaking so badly the signature would be illegible, and who died on November ninth, a Thursday, at 4:15 in the afternoon, in the hospital bed that Meredith had rented and set up in the living room so he could see the dogwood tree through the front window.

The funeral was on a Monday. Forty people came. Doug was one of them. He wore a dark suit and he shook Meredith's hand and he said, "Frank was a good man and a good neighbor." He brought banana bread.

On Tuesday the mail came. In it was a notice from the Cedarview Estates Homeowners Association informing her that her outstanding balance for the ongoing violation at 4412 Briarwood Court was $2,137.50, reflecting forty-one days of non-compliance at $50 per day plus accrued compounding interest, and that failure to resolve the violation within thirty days would result in the placement of a lien on the property.

Meredith sat at the kitchen table. The banana bread from the funeral was still on the counter, untouched, the plastic wrap still on. Outside, through the window Frank had looked through in his last weeks, the ramp descended from the front door to the walkway. It was well built. The handrail was smooth and the slope was gentle and it led to a door that no wheelchair would ever pass through again, and the fine on it was growing by $50 a day plus 1.5% monthly and it would keep growing, she understood, until she removed it or paid it or the numbers became large enough to take her house. The math was not complicated. She was an actuary. She knew how compounding worked. It was the most patient force in the universe.

She took the CC&Rs from the drawer where she kept household documents — the mortgage, the deed, the insurance policy, the HOA file. She had read them once, twenty-three years ago, the way you read terms and conditions: scanning, trusting, assuming the document existed to protect you. Now she opened to page one and began to read the way she had once read actuarial tables — not for the general sense but for every specific, every exception, every clause and sub-clause and defined term, every place where the language was precise and every place where it was not.

She read for three hours. She made notes in the margins in pencil, small and neat, the handwriting of a woman who had spent a career writing in margins. When she finished, she went back to the beginning and read again. By the end of the second reading she had filled four pages of a yellow legal pad with references, cross-references, and questions. By the end of the third reading the questions had become observations and the observations had begun to harden into something structural — a pattern, a series of gaps between what the bylaws required and what Doug Whitmore actually did, a distance between the document and its implementation that was not a flaw in the system but was, in fact, the system itself.

She picked up the phone.

Bonnie Okafor lived on the next block over, in a house with a fence that was four inches too tall. The HOA had fined her $600 for the fence. The fence had been built to that height because Bonnie's ex-husband had spent the six months after the divorce sitting in his car across the street at varying hours, and four inches was the difference between being able to see him and not being able to see him, and the Architectural Review Committee had denied her variance request because the CC&Rs specified forty-two inches and her fence was forty-six and, as Doug had explained, the bylaws were the bylaws.

Before the fence, before the divorce, before any of it, Bonnie had been a court stenographer in the County Superior Court for nineteen years. She had been removed — officially, she had resigned; functionally, she had been removed — after an incident during the sentencing phase of a DUI case in which the defendant, a twenty-year-old kid, was sentenced to six years and Bonnie had stopped typing, looked up at the judge, and said, clearly enough for it to be entered into the record by the substitute stenographer who replaced her: "He's somebody's son and you know this is wrong and you're doing it anyway." She had not been wrong. The conviction was later overturned on appeal. This did not get her job back.

What Bonnie had, besides an illegally tall fence and a stenographer's memory for the exact words people used, was a Steno machine in her living room that she kept oiled and functional the way a retired surgeon might keep a scalpel sharp — not to use, but to remain a person who could use it.

"I need transcripts," Meredith said. "Of every HOA meeting. And I need them verbatim."

Bonnie was quiet for a moment. Then she said: "I have three years' worth of recordings on my phone. I started recording when he denied my fence appeal. I just sit in the back and let it run."

"How are the recordings?"

"The audio's clean. He projects."

"Of course he does."

"Meredith, what are you doing?"

Meredith told her. Bonnie was quiet for a longer moment. Then she said, "What time do you want me there?"

Gerald Fitzroy lived alone in a townhouse at the end of the development, Unit 14-C, and had lived there for eleven years, during which time he had attended every single HOA meeting, including four emergency sessions, two budget ratification hearings, and the special election of 2019, which was conducted by show of hands despite the fact that Article IV, Section 7(a) clearly required a paper ballot for any election in which more than one candidate was nominated, a procedural violation Gerald had raised at the time and that had been dismissed by Doug as "not meaningful to the outcome," a phrase Gerald could still quote from memory because it was, in his view, the single most dangerous sentence in the English language.

Gerald had discovered Robert's Rules of Order in 1987, during a period of his life he did not discuss in detail but which involved a great deal of solitude and a library card. He had read the book the way some people read scripture — for structure, for meaning, for the reassurance that somewhere, someone had sat down and determined the correct way for human beings to make decisions together, and that the correct way was knowable, and consistent, and fair, provided everybody followed it. He had the current edition and two prior editions and a spiral-bound notebook in which he had, over many years, catalogued every parliamentary situation he had ever encountered and cross-referenced it to the relevant section of Robert's Rules, sometimes with annotations on where the Rules were, in his opinion, insufficient — which was rarely, but occasionally, in matters of subsidiary motions.

Nobody had ever asked Gerald to use this knowledge for anything that mattered.

Meredith asked.

She called him on a Wednesday evening, introduced herself — they had spoken perhaps twice, both times at meetings — and told him what she needed: someone who knew Robert's Rules well enough to deploy them under pressure, in real time, against an adversary who used them selectively and badly.

Gerald was quiet for so long that Meredith checked if the call had dropped.

"Mrs. Chen," he said. "I have been waiting for a very long time for someone to take this seriously."

"I'm taking it seriously."

"He chaired the 2021 budget meeting without establishing a quorum. I counted the room. There were fourteen people present. The quorum threshold is twenty. I raised the issue. He said presence was implied by proxy. That is not a thing. That is not a thing in Robert's Rules, it is not a thing in the CC&Rs, it is not a thing in any parliamentary authority recognized on this planet. I have a letter. I wrote a letter. I sent the letter. The letter was not acknowledged."

"Gerald."

"I have the letter. I have a copy."

"Bring the letter. Bring everything. Can you come Thursday at seven?"

"I can come Thursday at six."

The book club met every other Tuesday in rotating houses and had, for the past three years, been reading with a thematic focus that one of its members described as "systems of power" and another described as "the bastards" and a third described as "honestly, it started with *The Handmaid's Tale* and just kept going." They had read Atwood, Solzhenitsyn, Arendt, a biography of Robert Moses, two histories of redlining, and, most recently, a slim book about the topology of fascism that one of them had found in a university bookshop and that they had discussed for four meetings because it argued, convincingly, that authoritarianism did not require charisma or ideology — only procedural capture of small institutions by patient individuals who understood that most people do not read the rules, and that this is enough.

The members were: Helen Park, sixty-seven, retired school administrator, whose garden shed had been fined because it was painted a shade of green not on the approved palette, the approved palette containing eleven shades of green, none of which matched the shade Helen had used, which was, according to Doug, "clearly teal." Jean Mackey, seventy-one, whose outdoor holiday lights had incurred a violation for remaining up until January 22, three days past the January 19 deadline, because Jean's son was supposed to take them down and Jean's son had the flu and Jean could not reach the gutters and Doug had expressed sympathy and also issued the fine. Patricia Huang, sixty-six, whose formal complaint about the dead ash tree on the HOA common ground behind her property — a tree that was shedding limbs onto her patio, one of which had cracked the glass on her outdoor table — had been "received" seventeen months ago and neither resolved nor acknowledged since. Denise Carter, sixty-nine, whose request to install a doorbell camera had been denied on the grounds that exterior-mounted electronics required ARC approval, which Denise had then applied for, and which had been denied on the grounds that the camera's sightline included a portion of the common walkway, which constituted a "surveillance encroachment" — a term that appeared nowhere in the CC&Rs. And Ruth Abernathy, seventy-three, who had no personal violation but who had been friends with Meredith for forty years and who, when Meredith called and said, "I need the club," said only: "It's time, isn't it?"

Meredith did not pitch revenge. She sat in her living room on a Thursday evening with Bonnie and Gerald and the five women of the book club and she laid the CC&Rs on the coffee table and she said:

"The annual meeting is in six weeks. Article IV, Section 2, requires that the meeting be held on the date specified in the governing documents. He cannot move it without a two-thirds vote of the membership, which he has not obtained. Article IV, Section 5, specifies the quorum threshold: twenty percent of eligible voting members, in person or by valid proxy. There are two hundred and twelve homes in Cedarview Estates. Quorum is forty-three. He has not had a legitimate quorum in at least three years. Every motion he has passed in that time is voidable."

She paused. Gerald was nodding so vigorously that his glasses were migrating down his nose.

"I am not proposing that we argue with him," Meredith said. "I am not proposing that we yell, or complain, or make speeches. I am proposing that we attend the annual meeting with a valid quorum of our own, that we follow every rule in this document more precisely than he has ever followed any of them, and that we use the procedures he has been ignoring for fifteen years to remove him from the board."

Helen Park picked up the CC&Rs and opened to a page she had evidently already been reading. "The motion for a vote of no confidence," she said. "Article IV, Section 9(c). Requires a simple majority of members present and voting."

"Yes."

"He's never had a room bigger than twenty people. If we pack the room—"
