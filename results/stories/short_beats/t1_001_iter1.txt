# The Thirty-Seven Letters of Monk Hideo, As Compiled by His Opponent

---

## Preface by the Compiler

I have spent the better part of six months arranging these letters in their proper sequence, verifying dates where possible, and appending such notes as I believe may be of use to students of our tradition. The task has not been simple. Monk Hideo — I will use the name he chose for himself, though it was not the name I knew him by — was not a systematic correspondent. His letters arrived at uneven intervals, sometimes three in a season, sometimes none for two years. They were not numbered. Several bore no date at all and had to be placed by the condition of the paper, the color of the ink, and certain internal references to datable events. I have done my best. Where I have guessed, I have said so.

The facts of the matter are known to anyone with even passing familiarity with the Kōsetsu Tournament. In the autumn of the twenty-third year of the Meiji era, the swordsman who would later become Monk Hideo stood in the final round of the tournament that had been his sovereign domain for the better part of a decade. He was undefeated. His opponent — and here I will exercise the only privilege a compiler may claim, which is to manage his own presence in the text with economy — was myself. The match was expected to last some time. It did not. Before the first exchange was completed, Hideo lowered his shinai, stepped backward, and announced his forfeit. He left the hall without explanation. Within the month, he had entered the monastery at Eiheiji. Within the year, his name had been struck from the tournament records at his own request.

His forfeit was never adequately explained. I have read the accounts. Nervous collapse, spiritual crisis, a sudden apprehension of violence's futility — the theories multiply in proportion to the theorist's distance from the event. I stood across from him. I will add nothing to the speculation. I compile these letters, which began arriving six weeks after his departure and continued for four decades, because I believe they belong to the tradition that produced us both, and because I will not be able to compile anything much longer.

I have added annotations where I judged them useful. I have not altered the text of any letter. I have not withheld any letter. Whether this constitutes an adequate discharge of my obligations to the author, to the tradition, and to whatever else may be owed, I leave for others to determine.

I thank my daughter Mariko for her assistance in preparing the manuscript.

—K.
Tōkyō, autumn, the thirty-first year of Shōwa

## The Letters

---

### Letter 1

*[Undated. Received approximately six weeks after the compiler's final match. Written on monastery stationery, the calligraphy notably unsteady.]*

K.,

I have arrived. The room is adequate. I am told I will not be permitted to write again for some time, so I am writing now before silence is imposed and while I still understand what I wish to say, which is only that I owe you an account, and that I intend to provide it, and that I ask for the patience appropriate between men who have faced one another honestly.

I will write again when I am able.

— H.

**Compiler's note:** *He arrived at Eiheiji on the fourteenth of the eleventh month. The monastery's records confirm a probationary silence of ninety days for new postulants. His next letter arrived on the hundred and third day.*

---

### Letter 2

*[Dated: second month, twenty-fourth year of Meiji.]*

K.,

The silence has ended, though in truth it has only changed form. I am permitted to speak to the other monks about practical matters — the location of firewood, the schedule of meditation, the name of a bird I cannot identify. But I find that the language trained into me over forty years of the sword is not the language spoken here, and the attempt to learn a new one has made me, at fifty-one, a kind of infant. I sit with the others and the words for what I need to say do not exist in the vocabulary available to me. Perhaps they do not exist at all. I am beginning to suspect that what I owe you is not expressible in the manner I first imagined, and that the account I promised will take longer than I thought.

The bird was a bush warbler. The oldest monk told me its name and then said: "Now you know the name. Can you still hear the song?" I mention this only because it struck me as relevant, though I cannot yet say to what.

— H.

**Compiler's note:** *The bush warbler (uguisu) is traditionally associated with the arrival of spring and, in certain poetic registers, with messages that are heard before they are understood. Whether Hideo intended the allusion is unclear. He was never a literary man.*

---

### Letter 3

*[Dated: ninth month, twenty-fourth year of Meiji.]*

K.,

I have been assigned to the kitchen garden. The soil here isite clay,iteiteiteite — I apologize, I crossed that out poorly. The soil resists the hoe in a way that reminds me of certain parries. I had not expected that the body trained in one discipline would insist on interpreting another through its vocabulary. When I cut a radish from the earth, my hands perform the cut. Not a specific cut. *A* cut. The motion that the body knows before the mind selects its application.

I have been thinking about the tournament. Not the last match. The ones before. The years of them. I have been thinking about how one comes to know another person's movement so well that it ceases to be another person's movement and becomes a landscape one inhabits, the way I now inhabit this garden, knowing where the stones are, where the soil gives, where it holds. I knew the movements of every man I faced. But there are different orders of knowing, and I am only now beginning to understand that what I knew in the final match was of a different order entirely.

I am circling the thing. I am aware that I am circling. Give me time.

— H.

**Compiler's note:** *He knew. He is already, in this third letter, constructing the circumlocution that will occupy him for the next four decades. The reader should note the distinction he draws between general knowledge of an opponent and the specific knowledge he acquired in the final match. He will return to this distinction. He will return to it many times.*

---

### Letter 4

*[Dated: fifth month, twenty-fifth year of Meiji.]*

K.,

A question that arose during meditation, which I put to you because I have no one else to whom I may put it, and because it concerns your art as much as mine:

If a warrior perceives a weakness in his opponent — not a temporary lapse, not a failure of attention that might be corrected between one breath and the next, but a structural flaw, a thing built into the opponent's body and training as permanently as the grain is built into wood — is the warrior obligated to exploit it?

The question seems simple. The tradition answers: yes. The match exists for the revelation of such things. The superior eye perceives, the superior hand acts. This is the mechanism by which the art refines itself. The flaw is found, the flawed man falls, and the art advances.

But consider: what if the flaw is such that its exploitation would not merely defeat the opponent but destroy him? I do not mean his body — I mean the thing in him that makes him what he is. His understanding of himself as a practitioner. His knowledge of his own movement. What if the perception of the flaw is so total that to act on it would be to say to the opponent: *I have seen you more completely than you have seen yourself, and what I have seen is the place where you end*?

Is the obligation to the art? Or to the man?

I am told I am improving at the garden. The abbot says I have a feeling for which plants want more water and which want less. I do not tell him that I am only reading their stances.

— H.

**Compiler's note:** *The question is not hypothetical. It was never hypothetical.*

### Letter 5

*[Dated: first month, twenty-sixth year of Meiji.]*

K.,

Do you remember the Kōsetsu of the eighteenth year? Matsuda. You and I were both in the secondary bracket, and we watched him from opposite sides of the hall. He fought a man named — I cannot recall, it does not matter — a man from the Jikishinkage school, a tall man with an unusual two-handed grip. Matsuda dismantled him in four exchanges. What I remember is not the match but the tall man's face afterward. He was sitting on the bench holding his shinai across his knees as though it were a strange object, and there was a quality to his stillness that I recognized even then, though I lacked the language for it. He had been *read*. Completely. To the root. And the reading had destroyed something.

He never competed again. I followed his name for several years and then the name disappeared. One hears, occasionally, of men who leave the art after a public defeat and enter business or the civil service, and the explanation given is always shame, but I wonder now whether shame is the correct word. I wonder whether the word is something more like *exposure* — the unbearable experience of having been seen in a place one did not know one was visible.

I have not answered my own question from the previous letter. I am aware that I have not.

The snow here comes earlier than in Tōkyō. I do not miss Tōkyō.

— H.

**Compiler's note:** *The competitor he cannot recall was Inoue Jirō. He retired from competition in the nineteenth year and became a schoolteacher in Nagano. He died in the thirty-sixth year of Meiji, of no remarkable cause. Hideo's supposition about the reason for his retirement cannot be verified. Matsuda himself never spoke of the match. The anecdote, like many of Hideo's anecdotes, is offered as parable, and like many of his parables, it is a door he opens, walks toward, and then closes before passing through.*

### Letter 7

*[Dated: eighth month, twenty-seventh year of Meiji. Letter 6 is omitted here; it concerns exclusively the monastery's renovation of its western dormitory and contains no material relevant to the collection's central themes.]*

K.,

You have not written back. I have not asked you to. I am aware of the asymmetry of our arrangement and I do not wish to compel a correspondence that is not sought. These letters are — I will admit it — selfish. They are the practice hall I have built in the absence of a practice hall. You are the wall against which I throw the ball, and I do not require the wall to throw it back, only to be there, to be the surface that gives my throwing its shape.

And yet.

Some mornings I wake and I think: he must wonder. He must turn it over. Forty men I defeated in that tournament across eight years and the forty-first I face and I step back. He must look at that moment from every angle the way I look at it. He must have his own account, his own theories. I forfeited and left, and the world supplied its reasons, and perhaps he accepted them, but I think — I believe — that you did not. I think you know that the reasons the world supplies are the wrong shape. Convenient shapes. Shapes that allow the story to close. And you have always been a man who noticed shape.

I am saying too much and not enough. That is the condition of these letters. I apologize for it without being able to correct it.

The bush warbler has returned. The same one, I think, though the monks say they are seasonal and cannot be distinguished. I distinguish it. Its song has a flaw in it — a place where the trill falters — and I have learned the flaw well enough to recognize it as an identity.

— H.

**Compiler's note:** *He is correct. I did not accept the world's reasons. He is correct that I noticed the shape. I have nothing further to add to this note.*

### Letter 9

*[Dated: sixth month, twenty-ninth year of Meiji. Letter 8, a brief seasonal greeting, is omitted.]*

K.,

I have been given a duty I did not expect: I am teaching the youngest monks a set of physical exercises derived, in their distant origin, from martial forms. The exercises have been so thoroughly domesticated by monastic tradition that they no longer resemble combat. They are stretches, balances, breathing patterns. But my body recognizes the ancestry. This is iaijutsu with all the steel removed. This is footwork with no opponent. The monks perform these movements for health and flexibility, not knowing they are rehearsing the grammar of killing, and I stand before them and correct their form, and the thing I feel is not irony but a kind of horror at how thin the membrane is between a healing thing and a destroying thing.

This is relevant. I am trying to tell you why it is relevant. The membrane. The thinness. The way a single perception can sit on one side or the other and the body makes no distinction, the body only *knows*, and the question of what to do with the knowing — that is the question that is not built into the body, that the body has no mechanism for, that must be answered somewhere else, in some organ or faculty for which I have found no name despite eight years of the most rigorous spiritual discipline available to me in this country.

I corrected a boy's elbow today. He was overextending. His joint was open. I placed my hand on his arm and moved it inward three degrees and the flaw was corrected and I felt — I cannot describe what I felt. A memory that was not a memory. A possible future that I had prevented from becoming the past.

I am sorry. I will write more clearly next time.

— H.

**Compiler's note:** *He will not write more clearly next time.*

### Letter 11

*[Dated: second month, thirty-first year of Meiji.]*

K.,

A question. Another question. I have become a man made of questions. The abbot says this is progress. I doubt him but continue.

Is silence about a flaw — a flaw one has perceived in another, a flaw whose disclosure would cause suffering — is such silence a form of contempt? Or is it a form of love?

I have asked this in five different ways over the course of these letters, and I detect you noting the repetition from wherever you sit reading this, if you still read these, and I will ask it again because I have not yet found the angle at which the question yields its answer.

Consider: to remain silent about a truth one has perceived is to say, implicitly, *you cannot bear this knowledge*. It is to make a judgment about the other's capacity. And is that not contempt? To decide on another's behalf that he is too fragile, too proud, too thoroughly *constructed* to withstand the information? And yet. To speak — to lay the truth in the other's hands — is to prioritize one's own need for honesty over the other's need for the ground beneath his feet, and is that not a species of aggression disguised as virtue?

The monks here practice a thing they call *noble silence* — silence held not from inability to speak but from the recognition that speech would cause more harm than withholding. I have tried to take refuge in this category. I have told myself for a decade now that my silence is noble. Some days I believe it. Other days I understand that I am using the monastery's vocabulary to ennoble what is, in truth, a paralysis — that I am not nobly silent but merely trapped between two injuries and unable to choose which one to inflict.

The plum blossoms have opened early. The monks say this augurs well. I have never understood augury. I understand only consequence.

— H.
