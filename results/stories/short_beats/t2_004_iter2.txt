# The Hateful Immortals

## A Documentary by Dr. Amelia Thornton
## Transcript — Final Edit

---

## I.

The marriage certificate is unremarkable. You can view it yourself — the Massachusetts Historical Society digitized the document in 2019 as part of a broader initiative to catalog vital records from the 1840s, and it appears on their website between a ship captain's probate filing and a tax dispute over three acres of orchard land in Concord. The clerk who filed it had mediocre handwriting. The paper has foxed unevenly, producing a stain in the upper-left corner that looks, depending on your disposition, like either a moth or a small heart. The date is September 14, 1847. The groom is listed as James Chen, age twenty-two, occupation laborer. The bride is Margaret Greenbriar, age twenty, occupation clerk.

I have spent the better part of six years researching the lives attached to this document. What follows is, to the best of my ability, the truth — which is to say, an argument disguised as a narrative, built from journals, letters, legal filings, medical records, three hundred and twelve hours of recorded interviews conducted by various parties over the last eight decades, and the fourteen handwritten volumes that Margaret Greenbriar has kept in continuous production since 1852 and which she has, with some reluctance, permitted me to examine under supervised conditions. I will do my best to be precise, because Margaret will certainly read this, and she will certainly remember every error.

They met in 1845, in the offices of Hartwell & Crane, Attorneys-at-Law, on State Street in Boston. Margaret had been employed there for two years. She had manifested — if that's the correct word, and the literature on the topic remains fractured — at thirteen, when her schoolteacher, a Miss Abigail Prewitt, noticed that the girl could recite verbatim not only the day's lesson but every lesson from the preceding four years, including Miss Prewitt's digressions, coughs, and the one occasion on which she had used an indelicate word after dropping a paperweight on her foot. Margaret's father, a practical man, pulled her from school and apprenticed her to a solicitor. By the time James walked into the office, Margaret could reproduce from memory every contract, deed, and letter the firm had processed during her tenure. She did not merely remember the contents. She remembered the color of the ink, the pressure of the pen strokes, the places where the paper had been damp. She was, by any reasonable measure, the most valuable legal clerk in the city of Boston, and she was paid eleven dollars a month.

James was there because a beam had fallen on him at the shipyard.

This requires some context. James Chen — born Jia-Ming Chen in Guangzhou, arrived in Boston in 1840 at the age of fifteen aboard a merchant vessel whose name he would later refuse to share with any interviewer — had discovered his own peculiar quality the way most people in his situation report discovering it: by accident, and with witnesses. The beam was oak, twelve inches in diameter, and fell from a height of approximately thirty feet. Two coworkers saw it strike him across the shoulders. James stood up, brushed sawdust from his shirt, and went back to work. He did not understand the stares he received. He told me, in our first interview, that his initial assumption was that the beam was simply lighter than it looked. "I didn't know I was different," he said. "I just thought it was a small beam."

It was not a small beam. The foreman filed a report. The report reached the firm of Hartwell & Crane because the shipyard's owner wanted documentation of the incident before the insurance assessors arrived. Margaret read the report, remembered the report — she had no choice in this — and was, in her own account, curious. She visited the shipyard. She asked for the man who had been struck by the beam.

"He was filthy," she wrote in her journal on October 3, 1845. "His shirt was torn and his hands were stained with pitch, and he looked at me as though I had arrived from a planet he had not been informed of. I asked him how he had survived. He said he didn't know. I told him I could remember every word anyone had ever said to me. He said, 'That sounds terrible.' He was the first person who had ever said that."

I want to sit with that exchange for a moment, because it contains, in miniature, the entire structure of what followed. Margaret presents her ability as remarkable. James's immediate response is not awe or curiosity but *sympathy*. He does not say *how wonderful* or *what a gift* — phrases Margaret had heard, by her own count, from every other person she had ever told. He says: *that sounds terrible*. And Margaret, who had been keeping precise track all along, recognized it instantly as the first time. She could not help but recognize it. Her memory would not allow her to be wrong about this.

The courtship lasted two years. By the standards of the period, it was both conventional and not. James attended church with Margaret's family, escorted her on walks along the harbor, presented himself to her father for the requisite interview about his prospects and intentions. What was unconventional was the private world they were building between the public rituals. Margaret's journal from this period — the first three volumes of which are held at the Boston Athenaeum under a restricted access agreement that took me fourteen months to negotiate — reveals a young woman experiencing the novel pleasure of being *fully known*. James, who could not be hurt, was also the first person who did not flinch from the full force of Margaret's recall. She could tell him, word for word, what he had said on their first walk, and he did not accuse her of keeping score. She could correct his memory of his own stories, and he did not bristle. She told him that she remembered the sound of every conversation happening simultaneously in the next room at every gathering they attended, and instead of looking at her with the particular expression she had learned to identify as *unease wearing the mask of admiration*, he asked which conversation had been the most interesting.

"He listens to me the way a building withstands wind," she wrote on April 12, 1847, five months before the wedding. "It does not occur to him to fall down."

They married in September. The ceremony was small. Margaret's family attended; James's did not, having been separated from him by eight thousand miles of ocean and a visa regime that the historical record treats as unremarkable and that was, of course, monstrous. The officiant was a Reverend Collis, about whom I have been able to learn only that he had a slight stammer and that Margaret recorded, in her journal, the exact rhythm of his hesitations during the vows. She remembered the temperature. She remembered that a horse on the street outside whinnied twice during the prayer. She remembered the pressure of James's hand on hers — she describes it as "firm but not presumptuous, as though he were holding an egg he respected."

I like that line. I imagine Margaret would want me to note that she did not write it to be liked — she has said as much, in multiple interviews — but rather because it was accurate. James held her hand as though it were an egg he respected. That is what happened. She reported the data.

There is one more entry from that day that I must share, because it is the reason I made this documentary, and because it is — I will try to say this with the scholarly restraint that my position requires — the most devastating sentence I have encountered in a career spent reading other people's private anguish.

Margaret wrote it on the evening of September 14, 1847. The handwriting is steady. She had just become Mrs. Chen, though she would continue to use Greenbriar in certain legal contexts for reasons that were both practical and, I suspect, temperamental. She wrote:

*"I will remember every moment of this happiness, and that is the greatest gift God could grant a wife."*

I have read this sentence perhaps two hundred times. I need you to understand that Margaret Greenbriar was correct. She was precisely, literally, and completely correct. She would remember every moment. Every moment of the happiness _and_ every moment of everything that came after the happiness, because her memory did not distinguish between what she wished to keep and what she wished to discard. It was not a gift. It was not a curse. It was a *capacity*, constant as a pulse, and it would treat the wedding night and the worst night with identical, immaculate fidelity.

"Till death do us part" is, of course, the phrase everyone fixates on, and I understand why — it is dramatic, it is paradoxical, it lays bare the structural absurdity of their situation with a neatness that feels almost literary. James cannot die. The exit clause is void. They are bound by a contract whose termination condition cannot be met. I have read the essays. I have seen the online discussions. I find them, on the whole, insufficient, because they focus on the wrong impossibility.

The more important impossibility is this: ordinary marriages survive because people forget. I don't mean that they develop amnesia. I mean that the specific texture of a wound — the exact words, the precise tone, the way the light fell on a face twisted into something unkind — *fades*. It softens. It becomes a summary rather than a recording. A couple has an argument in 1987. By 1990, they remember that it happened and roughly what it was about. By 2000, they remember that the mid-eighties were a difficult period. By 2020, they remember that they went through some hard times early on. The wound is metabolized. It is composted by time into something the marriage can use as soil.

Margaret cannot compost. Margaret has, in her head, at all times, the complete and unabridged recording of every unkind word James has spoken in 177 years — the words themselves, the timbre of his voice, the angle of his jaw, the smell of the room, the quality of the light. She has the same recording of every kind word, every tender gesture, every private joke, every ordinary Tuesday evening when nothing happened except that they existed in the same room and the existing was good. She has all of it, simultaneously, without hierarchy, without the mercy of blur.

And James — James, who cannot be cut, burned, broken, pierced, or killed by any force yet documented — has been, for 177 years, in a marriage where his wife can reproduce his cruelest throwaway remark from an argument in a century he barely remembers existing.

That is the subject of this documentary.

Not immortality. Not the supernatural. Not the question of what it means that such people walk among us, which has been covered more than adequately by other scholars and which frankly bores me.

The subject is marriage. The subject is what happens when two people who love each other are denied the single mechanism — the slow, merciful erosion of perfect recall — by which love typically outlasts the people practicing it.

## II.

I want to describe the pattern before I describe the deviations, because the pattern is the point.

Between 1847 and 2024, Margaret and James have lived in, by my count, twenty-three cities across eleven countries. The average duration of a stay is seven years and four months. The shortest was Kyoto, 1923 — five months, terminated by a house fire that James walked out of without burns, in front of nine witnesses. The longest was Buenos Aires, 1938 to 1954, sixteen years and two months, to which I will return. In every case, the arc of the stay follows an identical structure: arrival, establishment, contentment, exposure, flight. I call this the *Greenbriar-Chen Cycle*, not because it is a particularly elegant name, but because Margaret, who has read my earlier work and offered corrections, approved of it — and because James, who has also read my earlier work, said he didn't care what I called it as long as I spelled his name right, then added: "She'll remember if you don't."

Paris first.

They arrived in 1887, which is to say they arrived in a Paris that was finalizing designs for Monsieur Eiffel's peculiar tower and debating whether it constituted art or vandalism. James found work as a laborer on the construction — the irony of an invulnerable man building the most conspicuous structure in France is not lost on me, though it was, evidently, lost on his foreman. Margaret, whose French was by this point flawless — she had learned it from a single conversation with a Québécois merchant in Boston in 1859 and subsequently refined it through every French text, conversation, and overheard remark she encountered, all of which she retained in full — established a small translation office on the Rue du Bac.

The office was successful. Margaret could translate between eleven languages, and she could do it from memory without consulting the source text, because she had read the source text once and once was all she required. Clients found this unsettling, then convenient, then indispensable. By 1891, the office employed three other translators who functioned primarily as cover — Margaret did the work, and the employees provided the comforting illusion that the work required more than one person.

They were happy. I use this word carefully. Margaret's journals from this period — Volumes 3 and 4, now held in a private collection in Zurich whose owner has requested anonymity — describe a life that is, by any measure, rich. They hosted a Thursday salon in their apartment on the Rue de Sèvres. James cooked. He had become, by Margaret's account, a genuinely gifted cook, in part because Margaret could recall the precise measurements and timings of every recipe they had ever tasted and relay them to him with the fidelity of a studio recording. "He cooks," she wrote, "and I am the cookbook that talks back." She marked this with an asterisk and a marginal note: *James laughed when I read this to him.* She frequently annotated her annotations. The margins of her journals contain entire subsidiary conversations.

They had friends. They went to the theater. They walked along the Seine on Sundays, and Margaret's journal entries from these walks are — I will permit myself one subjective judgment — beautiful. She describes the light on the water with the specificity of an Impressionist painter who is also a spectrograph. She notes the color, the angle of refraction, the movement of shadows on the embankment, the precise shade of James's eyes in afternoon light versus morning light versus gaslight. She had no choice in this level of detail, but she appears, in this period, to have found joy in it. She was a woman who was in love in Paris, and her recording was, for the moment, in harmony with what there was to record.

Then: a Tuesday in March of 1893. A neighbor's daughter, Claire Beaumont, age nineteen, visited the apartment to deliver an invitation to a christening. Margaret answered the door. Claire studied her face and asked, with the frank tactlessness of the young, whether Madame Chen had a much older sister, because she looked exactly — *exactement, Madame* — like a woman in a photograph that Claire's mother kept in a drawer. The photograph, Claire explained, had been taken in 1874, nearly twenty years earlier. Claire's mother had purchased the apartment's previous contents at auction.

Margaret told Claire that she was thinking of someone else.

They were packed within the week. The translation office was closed; clients were referred elsewhere. Margaret wrote a single journal entry, dated March 22, 1893: *"The girl was right, of course. It was me. It is always me. It will always be me. James says we should try the Southern Hemisphere."*

I will note that this is the entry in which Margaret's prose style shifts for the first time from the expansive, almost luxuriant descriptions of the Paris period to the clipped, efficient mode that will characterize much of what follows. She is, at this point, forty-six years old and looks twenty. She has understood, for some time, the practical implications of this. But understanding a thing and having a nineteen-year-old girl confirm it in your doorway are different experiences, and Margaret's memory ensured that the difference would never fade.

Buenos Aires, then.

James selected it, as he selected most of their destinations — Margaret's perfect recall of every atlas, travelogue, and geography text she had ever read made her, paradoxically, indecisive about location. She knew too much about everywhere. James simply liked the sound of the name.

They arrived in 1938. James purchased a vineyard in Mendoza province with money that the couple had accumulated across several lifetimes' worth of employment and careful investment — Margaret's memory for financial data being, as one might imagine, a significant advantage in portfolio management. She could recall the exact price of every stock on every exchange she had ever glanced at, which made her, in James's words, "a very tedious person to play cards with, but a wonderful person to plan a retirement with." I should note that neither of them has ever retired in any meaningful sense. They simply change occupations as each identity expires.

The vineyard was called *Los Dos Ríos*.
