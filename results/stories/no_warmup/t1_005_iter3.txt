# Seventy-Two Hours

## I

The ball hit the rim with the particular deadness that meant the shooter's elbow had drifted again, and before the rebound even came off the glass, Devon Okafor was already rolling a strip of athletic tape between his fingers, waiting.

The gym at Westfield Academy of Math and Science smelled the way all gyms smell—rubber, sweat, the faintly chemical ghost of yesterday's floor polish—but beneath that was a scent that belonged only to this program, to this team: the mineral tang of wheelchair metal, the oil Devon used on the axle bearings every Tuesday and Friday, the particular warmth of bodies working harder than anyone expected them to. The overhead lights were the old fluorescent kind, two of them perpetually flickering in the southeast corner, and Devon had submitted the maintenance request three times. He had a folder for things like that. He had a folder for everything.

"Elbow," Devon said, tossing the tape to Toni Reeves at the free-throw line. Not commanding, not coaching—just naming a fact, the way a barometer names pressure.

"I know my elbow," Toni said, but she was already adjusting.

There were seven players on the Westfield Rolling Wolves varsity roster and four who mattered to this story, though none of them knew yet that they were in a story, which is one of the mercies available to the living. They believed they were in a season. A very good season—seventeen wins, two losses, a first-ever berth in the state wheelchair basketball championship—and the particular electricity of that belief ran through the gym like a low current, making every drill sharper, every trash-talk exchange a little louder, every moment feel rehearsed for some future telling.

James Barrera played center. He was six-foot-one in the chair, which measured nothing because everyone was seated, but the number mattered to James because it was the only physical statistic that hadn't been taken from him. He'd been deaf since age seven—meningitis, the kind that arrives quietly and leaves loudly—and he played with a ferocity that coaches described as intense and opponents described as unhinged and James himself would have described, if anyone had thought to ask, as *necessary*. He communicated in a braid of sign language, lip-reading, and the occasional spoken word that emerged with the slightly too-loud precision of someone monitoring a sound he couldn't fully hear. On the court he was violence and geometry, sealing off the paint with his chair at angles that shouldn't have worked but did, every time, because James had learned long ago that the world was a series of rooms that hadn't been designed for him, and he had decided to fit anyway, even if fitting meant rearranging the furniture by force.

Toni Reeves, the shooting guard, had the purest release on the team—a one-motion stroke she'd built by watching YouTube videos of Steph Curry and then translating everything downward, recalculating for a seated center of gravity with the obsessive patience of someone debugging code. She was a T4 complete spinal cord injury, car accident at age twelve, and she did not speak about the accident except once, to Devon, in a conversation neither of them ever referenced again but that Devon had written about in his journal, which no one had yet read. Toni's loyalty was the uncomplicated kind, the kind that doesn't negotiate. When she loved you, she loved you stupid and permanent, and she expected the same in return, which is a beautiful and dangerous way to move through the world.

Rico Padilla played four minutes a game on a good night. He was the twelfth man on a seven-man roster, a mathematical impossibility that nonetheless captured his precise position: present, counted, not quite necessary. He had spina bifida, mild enough that in another context he might have played standing sports—poorly, but played them—and severe enough that in this context he was one step below adequate. His stat lines were tombstones: 0 points, 0 rebounds, 1 assist, 2 minutes. But Rico had qualities that did not appear in box scores. He moved through the school the way water moves through limestone—finding every crack, every opening, present in places that forgot to exclude him. He heard things. He logged them without quite deciding to. Devon had noticed this quality in Rico very early, which is why Devon had given him a key to the equipment cage, a gesture of trust that Rico had never spoken about and never would.

And there was Macy Claiborne, who is where this story lives.

Macy ran the point. She ran it the way an air traffic controller runs a radar screen: always two plays ahead, always talking, always certain, occasionally wrong but never uncertain, which is a distinction that matters. She was a T10 incomplete injury—she had some sensation in her legs, some flickers of motor function that were medically interesting and practically useless—and she had been in the chair since she was six. She was now seventeen. Eleven years is enough time to build a personality around a circumstance, and Macy had built hers like a cathedral: layer over layer, buttressed and deliberate. At ten she had attended a junior detective camp and discovered that the human impulse to see a wheelchair before seeing a person could be weaponized, because people talked freely in front of those they'd already categorized as less. She had built an identity around this discovery. She read rooms the way James read lips—obsessively, from edges, cataloging everything.

She was very smart. Everyone agreed on this. What fewer people noticed was that her intelligence had a specific shape: it was *prosecutorial.* She gathered evidence of the world's logic not to understand it but to argue with it, to build cases, to prove. This made her an extraordinary point guard—every possession was a closing argument—and it would make her, very soon, an extraordinary and compromised detective.

But that was later. In this moment, the moment before everything, Devon tossed tape to Toni and Macy ran a pick-and-roll with James and Rico sat at the end of the bench retying a shoelace that didn't need retying, and the gym was the whole world. It was their world. They had built it by hand from nothing—the program hadn't existed four years ago, the school hadn't even had accessible gym equipment—and it functioned. It was good. The reader must understand what is about to be lost, which means the reader must first feel it breathing.

Devon moved through practice the way he always did: quiet, systemized, essential. He adjusted James's footrest between drills. He kept the spare tires inflated in the cage. He knew that Macy's left wheel had a pull and compensated the air pressure a half-PSI to correct it. He knew that Toni's chair frame was developing a stress fracture near the diagonal brace and that she wouldn't report it until it snapped because she didn't trust replacement equipment. He knew Rico's push rims were wrapped too tight, which was costing him acceleration. He knew all of these things because he paid attention through love, which is the quiet kind, the kind that fixes things before they break.

The omniscient eye, which sees what the characters cannot, might pause here to note a single detail: Macy, who noticed everything, had stopped noticing Devon. Or more precisely, she had started un-noticing him—her gaze skipping past him when he entered a room, her conversation pivoting when he approached, her body in its chair angling two degrees away when he was near. It was the kind of avoidance that looks, to anyone watching closely, exactly like guilt. No one on the team was watching that closely except Devon himself, and Devon had stopped expecting anything from Macy three weeks ago when she'd said the words *just wait, just until the championship, then you can do whatever you want* and he'd understood that she was negotiating with something she should have been surrendering to.

But this was practice, and practice was sacred, and the last drill was a full-court scrimmage that Macy's squad won by six.

---

After they left—towels gathered, gym bags loaded onto the backs of chairs, the sounds of departure echoing and then fading into the specific silence of an empty athletic space—Devon stayed behind.

He checked the equipment cage. He latched it. He rolled the ball rack to its position against the east wall and clicked the rubber stoppers into their floor grooves. He counted the spare push rims (four) and the spare cushions (three) and the rolls of tape (eleven, which would last the championship plus two practices if no one was wasteful, and someone would be wasteful, because they always were, and he had already ordered more). He did these things with the precision of ritual. They were the last ordinary things he would ever do and he did not know this, because no one ever does, which is the central cruelty of dying.

Then he walked to the gym's side entrance, the one that opened onto the concrete steps overlooking the faculty parking lot, and he took out his phone.

He dialed a number. The phone rang three times before being answered.

"I'm done waiting," Devon said. His voice was steady in the way that voices are steady when the speaker has practiced the words. "I'm telling them Thursday whether you handle it or not."

He listened. Whatever was said on the other end made him close his eyes.

"I don't care," he said. "I don't care about that."

He hung up. He locked the gym. He walked to the bus stop on Archer Avenue because his car had been in the shop since last week and he hadn't told anyone because Devon did not like to need things from people. The bus came. He rode it home. He ate leftover stew. He opened his laptop and typed three lines of an email he did not send.

Devon Okafor was nineteen years old. He was meticulous and decent and scared, and the next time anyone from Westfield would see him, he would be on the gym floor beside the overturned chair rack, and the seeing would be the kind that can't be unseen.

## II

The freshman who found him was named Aliyah Torrance, and she had come early to practice her free throws because she was shooting thirty-one percent and believed, with the dogged faith of fourteen-year-olds, that one extra morning session would fix it. She entered through the main gym doors at 6:42 AM, which were unlocked because the custodial staff began at 6:30. The lights were already on. This bothered her in a way she couldn't name—the lights should have been off, the gym should have been dark and waiting for her to bring it alive—and as she pushed through the doors in her chair, she registered the wrongness before she saw the cause of it.

Devon was on the floor near the equipment cage.

He looked, from a distance of forty feet, like he had fallen asleep there. He was on his side. One arm was under his body. The other was extended, fingers open, reaching toward nothing or toward everything or toward the door, and it is possible to read any meaning into the posture of the dead, which is why we should be careful about reading anything at all. But Aliyah could see, even from forty feet, the bruising on the side of his neck—dark, mottled, fading to a sick yellow-green at its edges—and the chair rack behind him, heavy steel, tipped onto its side in a way that looked like violence, the kind that doesn't happen by accident, no matter what you tell yourself at 6:42 AM when you're fourteen and the world has just become a different place than it was a minute ago.

She screamed. This is not a detail the story will dwell on because Aliyah Torrance was not the kind of person who screamed often, and she would spend the rest of the school year embarrassed by it, which was unfair, because screaming was the right thing to do.

Within thirty minutes the gym was police tape and radio static and adults speaking in the low, firm voices that adults use when they are trying to contain something that has already escaped containment. Within an hour the full roster knew. They arrived in ones and twos, summoned by the group chat that had lit up with fragments—*something happened* and *don't come to the gym* and *Devon* and *please call me*—and each of them met the news in the specific language their body had been trained to speak.

Macy went cold. When James texted her the word *dead* she was in her mother's kitchen eating toast and the toast stayed in her hand for a full minute, uneaten, while her mind ran the word through every filter she had. Dead. She put the toast down. She opened her laptop. She Googled *Westfield Academy incident* and found nothing yet because it was too early for the digital world to have processed what the physical world had barely begun to absorb. She closed the laptop. She brushed her teeth. She drove to school in the adaptive van her mother's insurance had paid for, and by the time she arrived she had built the first draft of a theory, which was wrong, but the building of it had kept her from falling apart, and that was the point.

James went silent. This was, for a person who had built an elaborate lattice of communication methods to exist in a hearing world, a profound choice. He stopped signing. He stopped speaking. He sat in the hallway outside the gym with his hands in his lap and his eyes on the floor and he breathed, and anyone who looked at him might have mistaken his stillness for calm, but it was not calm. It was the opposite of calm. It was the silence of a person who had seen something he had not yet told anyone about, and the guilt of that secret was compressing his chest in a way that felt medical.

Toni called her mother. She called her mother first, before texting back, before doing anything else, because Toni's mother was the fixed point in Toni's universe, the voice that had talked her through the first year in the chair when nothing worked and everything hurt, and Toni needed to hear that voice say *I'm here, baby* before she could process that Devon—who had once stayed after practice for two hours to help her recalibrate her push rims, who had listened to her talk about her father without interrupting, who had been kind in the way that doesn't perform itself—was gone. Her mother said *I'm here, baby.* Toni said *Okay.* Then she cried in her car for four minutes, washed her face with a water bottle, and went inside.

Rico stood in the hallway. He stood in the part of the hallway where the lockers created a slight recess, a nowhere-space between the water fountain and the janitor's closet, and he stood there for a long time while people walked past him without registering his presence: teachers, administrators, two police officers, the grief counselor who arrived at 8:15 with a lanyard and a clipboard. Rico watched them all. He noted which adults looked shaken and which looked merely inconvenienced. He noted which doorways were being guarded and which were not. He noted that the side entrance to the gym equipment hallway—the one with the keycard lock, the one Devon had showed him how to use—was not part of the crime scene perimeter. He noted this the way he noted everything: quietly, completely, and with the understanding that no one would ask him what he'd seen.

These were teenagers. The narration must say this plainly because the rest of the story will ask them to be more than that, and it is important to remember what is being asked and of whom. Some of them had already lost more than teenagers should. Rico's older brother had been killed in a car accident when Rico was nine. Toni had watched her father leave and return and leave again until *leaving* became his only reliable behavior. James had lost his hearing and, with it, a version of his childhood that he remembered with the painful clarity of something viewed through very clean glass. Macy had lost the use of her legs so early that she didn't remember the losing, which meant she grieved not an event but an absence, which is harder in ways that people who've never experienced it will never fully understand.

They knew grief. It was not new to them. What was new was this particular grief, the one that came with the specific weight of Devon's absence—Devon, who had been the connective tissue of the team, the person who held things together not by force but by care—and the particular poison of what some of them were already feeling beneath the sadness: guilt. Because grief and guilt are alloys. They fuse at low temperatures. And several of the people now gathering in the hallway outside the yellow tape had reasons to feel guilty that they had not yet shared with anyone, and the pressure of those reasons was already beginning to deform the shape of their mourning.
