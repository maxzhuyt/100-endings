# THE COLLECTORS

## Part One: The Letters

### I.

The thing about Margaret Hale's apartment was that from the hallway it looked fine. Better than fine. She kept the door freshly painted—a calm sage green she'd chosen from a fan of swatches she'd then kept in a kitchen drawer with forty other swatch fans. The welcome mat was clean. The peephole was unobstructed. A neighbor passing would think: that Margaret, she has it together. Graphic designer, freelance, good clients. Quiet woman. Pays her rent early.

The door opened inward, which was essential, because nothing could have opened outward against the wall of boxes on the other side.

She moved through the apartment the way water moves through limestone—along channels carved by years of patient, repetitive flow. There were paths. She maintained the paths. The bedroom path was thirty inches wide at its narrowest point, between a column of shoeboxes (containing not shoes but hotel soaps, eight hundred and forty-three, each wrapped in paper she would never throw away because some of them had French writing and some of them smelled like places she had briefly been less afraid) and a wardrobe she could no longer open because of the filing cabinets she'd placed in front of it (containing not files but owner's manuals for appliances she no longer possessed, and appliances she'd never owned at all, and appliances that did not exist anymore, discontinued models whose manuals she'd bought in lots on eBay at three in the morning while her heart raced with something she'd learned not to call joy because the word was too small for it and too large).

It was a Thursday in October. She was working at her desk—the one clear surface in the apartment, kept sacred by sheer professional terror—when her phone buzzed. A client needed a revision. She opened the file, stared at it, felt the familiar chemical flicker when the screen showed her the client's logo at 400% zoom, all those crisp vector lines, and thought about how the Pantone book she'd bought last week was still in its shipping box and how she should unbox it and how she should also unbox the other Pantone book, the older edition, the one she'd bought because it had colors they'd since discontinued and you couldn't get those swatches anymore, you *couldn't*, and then she thought about the box the second Pantone book had arrived in, which was a good box, a properly structured double-walled corrugated box, and she could not throw that away because—

She caught herself. She actually caught herself, which was something Dr. Petrov said she should celebrate. She put her hands flat on the desk. She breathed. She looked at the client's file.

But the flicker was still there, beating like a second pulse behind her sternum, and she knew from twelve years of experience that it would not quiet down until she fed it. So she opened a new browser tab and searched for vintage medical instruments, cast iron, pre-1940, and the results bloomed across her screen like a garden, and the thing behind her sternum opened its mouth and sighed.

She didn't buy anything. She added eleven items to a cart she would not check out. This was what she told Dr. Petrov she'd been doing: not buying, just carting. Dr. Petrov had looked at her steadily and said, "How many carts do you currently have open across how many sites?" and Margaret had said, "That's not really the point," and Dr. Petrov had said, "Margaret," and Margaret had said, "Sixty," and Dr. Petrov had written something down.

Margaret worked on the revision. She sent it to the client. She made dinner by navigating the kitchen path—eighteen inches wide at the choke point between the bread machines (four, only one functional) and the tower of Mason jars (empty, clean, waiting for a purpose she could feel pressing against the back of her mind like a word on the tip of her tongue). She ate standing up because both chairs were occupied. She washed her single plate, her single fork, and put them in the drying rack she'd cleared by moving the vintage bottle collection to the top of the refrigerator, where it now lived in three rows, gleaming.

She went to bed. The bed was clear. The bed was always clear. That was the rule, the one unbreakable rule, the seawall against the tide. She had a bed. She could lie in it. She could close her eyes.

When she closed her eyes, sometimes she saw the lake. She saw summer light on water that was not quite blue and not quite green, the color of old glass, and she heard—something. A voice that might have been a child or might have been a bird. And the smell of pine sap and sunscreen and turned earth, dirt under her fingernails, and the way the shovel had felt too heavy for her ten-year-old arms—

Her eyes opened. Her heartbeat was wrong. The flicker behind her sternum had become a roar. She reached for her phone and bought everything in the cart without looking at the total, and the roar quieted to a hum, and the hum quieted to a whisper, and the whisper said, *more*, and she opened a new tab.

### II.

Dean Morales had a rule: never from the same store twice in a month, never anything worth more than forty dollars, and never, ever anything he actually wanted.

This last rule was the one civilians couldn't understand, so he'd stopped trying to explain it. His ex-wife had looked at him across their kitchen table, their daughter's drawings held to the refrigerator with alphabet magnets, and said, "But you don't even *keep* them. I found a bag in the garage with the tags still on—Dean, there were nine identical tubes of hand cream. You don't use hand cream. You don't even have dry skin." And he'd wanted to say: *That's the point. That is exactly, precisely, the point. I don't want them. I've never wanted them. The wanting isn't about the things.*

But how do you say that? How do you explain that the moment of taking—the identification of the target, the calibration of the environment, the pulse-Loss of sound, the movement of the hand, the liquid second when the object transitions from theirs to yours and nothing in the universe has changed except everything in your nervous system—is the only time you feel like a complete human being? How do you explain that the objects are debris, that they're the ash left by a fire you need to keep building because without it you're standing in the dark?

You don't. You let your wife leave. You see your daughter every other weekend, and you are so careful, so goddam careful, to never take her anywhere that might trigger it, which means nowhere, which means your weekends together are spent in your rented condo watching movies, and she's ten and she asks why you never go to the mall, and you say, "Malls are boring," and she looks at you with her mother's skeptical eyes.

Today was a Tuesday. Dean was at work—he managed a mid-tier restaurant in Portland, which was a good job, a legitimate job, and he was good at it because inventory management came naturally to a man who'd spent twenty years cataloguing every object in every room he entered, tracking sightlines and cameras and staff rotations, building maps in his head that he didn't ask to build but couldn't stop building. He was checking a produce delivery when his hands started.

It always started in the hands. A tingling, almost electric, like the nerves were warming up for a performance. He looked at the delivery driver's clipboard, lying on the counter. He looked at the pen clipped to the driver's shirt pocket. He looked at the rubber band around the driver's wrist.

He didn't take anything. He finished the count. He signed the invoice. He went to the bathroom and held his hands under cold water until they stopped.

At six o'clock, he drove to a Walgreens eleven miles from his apartment—not the closest one, not even the second closest—and he walked the aisles for twenty minutes, letting the feeling build, the gorgeous terrible feeling, the way the world
sharpened and slowed, and then he palmed a bottle of nail polish (color: Midnight Orchid, $7.99) and walked out, and in the parking lot he sat in his car and held the nail polish in his hand and felt the wave crest and break, and for ninety seconds he was clean, he was clear, he was a person standing in daylight, and then the wave pulled back and everything was gray again, and he put the nail polish in the glove compartment with the others.

There were forty-one bottles of nail polish in his glove compartment. He didn't paint his nails. He never would.

That night, lying in his condo with the television on, he thought about the lake. He thought about summer, the specific heat of a Midwestern July, the way the air above the water shimmered. He thought about a girl's face—round, sunburned, laughing. Then not laughing. Then—

He got up and drove to a 24-hour Target and took a pack of batteries and a keychain flashlight and a single men's dress sock, size 10-12, black, and the wave came and went and came and went and he drove home and slept like something thrown from a window.

### III.

Sasha Clement planned her Tuesdays and Thursdays the way other people planned weddings. Every detail sequenced. Every contingency addressed. She was an attorney—trusts and estates, a field she found soothing because the clients were either very old or very dead and neither type demanded she perform vitality—and she structured her court schedule and client meetings to leave Tuesdays and Thursdays open after 2 p.m. Not free. Open. They were the most carefully occupied hours of her week.

At 2:15, she left the office. She told her assistant she had off-site meetings. Her assistant, a perceptive young man named Jonah, had never questioned this, which Sasha sometimes interpreted as kindness and sometimes interpreted as evidence that her secret was invisible and sometimes interpreted as evidence that Jonah was gathering ammunition for a future lawsuit.

She drove to Whole Foods first. Then to the bakery on Clement Street. Then to the deli. She filled bags with precision—the right textures, the right densities, the right mouth-feel progression from soft to crunchy to smooth. She drove home. She parked in the garage, where no one could see her carry the bags in.

What followed was ninety minutes that she would not describe to anyone, ever, under any circumstances, including the therapist she'd seen for three sessions before quitting because the therapist had used the word *binge* and Sasha had felt something slam shut in her chest like a vault door.

It was not about hunger. She ate regular meals—controlled, photographable, impeccable. Salads with grilled protein. Quinoa bowls. The food she ate at her desk, in restaurants, in front of other people, was a performance of a woman who had a healthy relationship with eating, and the performance was flawless. She had been rehearsing it for almost twenty years.

The other eating—the real eating, the eating that lived in the Tuesday/Thursday window—was a different animal entirely. It was mechanical and dissociative and enormous. She did it standing up in her kitchen with the blinds closed. She did not taste most of it. Her jaw ached. Her stomach distended. She kept going. The feeling she chased was not pleasure but *fullness*, a totality of sensation that crowded out everything else—every thought, every memory, every flash of summer light on green-blue water—

And then the second phase, the phase she will not describe, the phase that left her on her bathroom floor with burst capillaries around her eyes and a throat that felt scoured and a stillness in her mind that was the closest thing to peace she could manufacture.

She showered. She cleaned the kitchen. She bagged the evidence and drove it to a dumpster behind a strip mall four blocks away. She came home. She brushed her teeth. She did thirty minutes on the Peloton to counteract whatever her body had managed to absorb. She reviewed case files.

Her body was keeping score. Her dentist had noted the enamel erosion. Her doctor had flagged her potassium levels. Her knees ached from the exercise she stacked on top of the exercise she did to appear like someone who exercised a normal amount. She was 38, and she felt, in her joints and her esophagus and the chambers of her heart, like something much older.

She lay in bed that night with her hands on her flat stomach—still concave, always concave, that was the third rail, the number on the scale that must not move—and she did not think about the lake. She had trained herself not to think about the lake. She thought instead about her caseload, her billable hours, the Feldstein estate, the problematic codicil.

And then her hands remembered the shovel. The blisters. The dirt that got under her fingernails and stayed for days, and how she'd told her mother it was from gardening, from the camp garden, and her mother had said, "They have you gardening? In the dark?" and Sasha had said, "We planted night-blooming flowers," which was such an absurd lie that her mother should have caught it, should have pressed, should have—

She rolled over. She calculated the caloric content of what she'd consumed today and felt the number settle into her body like a stone into water, and around the stone the water churned, and in the churning she heard something that might have been a child's voice, calling from across a lake that existed now only in the bodies of four people who never spoke of it.

### IV.

Thomas Keane was up fourteen thousand dollars, which meant he should stop, which meant he would not stop.

He played online now. Had for years. The riverboat casinos and the back-room poker games and the weekend trips to Vegas—those were behind him, or at least behind the version of himself that his colleagues and his brother knew. The physical gambling had been too visible, too classically reckless. People noticed. People staged conversations that weren't quite interventions but had the same script: *We're worried. You seem stressed. Is there anything you want to talk about?*

Tom did not want to talk about it. Tom wanted to play.

He was a civil engineer. He built bridges, or rather, he designed them—load-bearing calculations, stress tolerances, the mathematics of how much weight a structure could hold before it failed. He was aware of the irony. He could calculate to four decimal places the breaking point of a steel truss, but he could not calculate the breaking point of Thomas Keane because Thomas Keane did not appear to have one. He had lost thirty thousand dollars in a single weekend and gone to work Monday morning and designed a pedestrian overpass. He had won sixty thousand dollars over the course of two months and felt nothing—*nothing*—because the winning wasn't the point, the same way Dean's nail polish wasn't the point and Margaret's boxes weren't the point. The point was the bet. The point was the moment before the card turned, the moment when the outcome was still superimposed, still alive in both states, and his entire nervous system lit up like a city grid, and he was, for that levitating instant, a person with no past.

He was the one people described as "doing well." Of the four of them—and he thought of them as the four, always, even now, even after twenty years of silence—he was the success story. Good career. Nice apartment. He dated. He went to his nephew's baseball games. He called his mother on Sundays. His surface was impeccable, his surface was a bridge of stunning structural integrity, and underneath it the river moved fast and dark and took everything he dropped into it.

Tonight he was playing three simultaneous blackjack tables and a poker tournament. His focus was narcotics-grade. He did not see his apartment—the clean lines, the sparse furniture, the almost aggressive absence of clutter (he'd visited Margaret once, eleven years ago, the last time any of them had been in the same room, and the sight of her apartment had sent him home to throw away everything he didn't need, which turned out to be almost everything, which told him something he did not want to examine). He saw only the numbers, the probabilities, the sweet green felt of the digital table.

Fourteen thousand became sixteen. Sixteen became eleven. Eleven became twenty.

He played until 3 a.m. He closed the laptop.
