# The Thirty-Seven Letters of Monk Hideo, As Compiled by His Opponent

---

## Editor's Preface

I have been, for forty years, known principally as one thing: Hideo's final opponent. There was a time when this troubled me. I had my own name, my own record, my own students and methods and small dignities accumulated over a career. But the gravity of that evening in the Nippon Budokan, November 1983, has bent the shape of all public memory concerning me, and I have long since stopped resisting. You will find my name on the title page and in the copyright. It is not important here.

I am dying. The doctors at Keio University Hospital have explained it to me with commendable clarity, and I will extend you the same courtesy: it is pancreatic cancer, stage four, identified too late, and I have declined further treatment. I am seventy-six years old. I have been given two to four months to arrange my affairs. Among those affairs is this book, which I have carried with me, in one form or another, for most of my adult life.

I have chosen to publish now because the silence belongs to both of us, and soon I will no longer be here to keep my half.

You know the story, or you believe you do. You have seen the footage — it circulates still, grainy and overexposed, on those internet sites devoted to the history of the art. The 1983 All-Japan Open Karate Tournament. The final. Two men widely considered the greatest competitive karatekas of their generation: Hideo Yamashiro, the three-time defending champion, and his challenger. The arena was full. Fourteen thousand seats. I remember the heat of the lights and the surprising cold of the floor through my feet, which I had never noticed before in any previous match and which I have never been able to adequately explain.

You have seen the bout. Seven minutes and forty-two seconds of what several commentators have called the finest karate ever performed in competition. You have seen the fourteenth exchange — the combination that was described the following day in the *Asahi Shimbun* as "a conversation between two bodies that have run out of words." You have seen what happened next: Hideo stepping back, raising his hand, and addressing the referee. The forfeit. The silence in the arena, which I can tell you was not silence at all but a roar of fourteen thousand people each drawing a single sharp breath. You have seen me standing there, my fists still raised, waiting for a continuation that did not come.

I have told this story so many times that it no longer belongs to me. It is a public myth. I recite it the way one recites the order of emperors, or the stations of a familiar commute: mechanically, with the accuracy that comes from having worn the words smooth.

What you do not know — what no one has known — is that three weeks after the forfeit, a letter arrived at my home in Yokohama. It was written on heavy washi paper in a hand I did not recognize. There was no return address, but the postmark was from a town in Iwate Prefecture, in the mountains. The letter was from Hideo.

I did not reply. I have never replied. Over the next four decades, thirty-six more letters arrived, for a total of thirty-seven. The intervals between them ranged from five weeks to three years. Some were brief — a single page, a handful of sentences. Others were lengthy, dense, closely reasoned. One arrived on the back of a woodblock print of Fudō Myō-ō. One arrived folded inside a dried maple leaf, which crumbled partially when I opened the envelope, which I have never forgiven myself for.

I kept every one.

What follows is their complete text, arranged chronologically, with my annotations. I present them as a scholarly exercise, an act of editorial preservation. Hideo left no other literary legacy, and his philosophical writings — for that is largely what these are — deserve a wider audience. It is appropriate that they be compiled and introduced by someone who understood their context.

This is what I tell myself. I have been telling myself this, in one form or another, for some years now as I prepared this manuscript.

But I will be dead soon, and perhaps honesty is the last form of discipline available to me. So I will say what I suspect is closer to the truth: that this book is my reply. That every annotation is the letter I never sent. That I have spent forty years composing my response to a man who never asked for one, and this is the only form in which I can bear to deliver it.

Draw your own conclusions. Hideo always trusted his opponents to see clearly. I am trying to extend you the same respect.

— November 2023, Yokohama

---

## A Note on Translation and Presentation

Hideo wrote in Japanese, as one would expect, but his Japanese was not uniform. The early letters employ a formal, literary register — the language of a man who has read the classical texts carefully and is conscious of being observed, even by an audience of one. As the correspondence progresses, his language loosens. Regional inflections from the Tōhoku dialect of his childhood appear, particularly in passages of strong emotion, as though feeling returned him to an earlier version of himself. In the final letters, the two registers blend into something that is neither formal nor colloquial but entirely his own. I have done my best to honor these shifts in translation, though I acknowledge that English has no precise equivalent for the movement between *desu/masu* formality and the rough, contracted vowels of northern rural speech. Where a significant nuance is lost, I have noted it.

A word on the physical letters themselves. Hideo was, among his many qualities, a trained calligrapher, and the early letters in particular are objects of considerable beauty. He wrote with a *fude* brush on handmade washi for the first eleven letters. Beginning with Letter Twelve, the materials become more varied. Letter Sixteen was written in ballpoint pen on lined notebook paper. Letter Twenty-Three was brushed on what appears to be the inside of a tea package — *Yame gyokuro*, a fine choice — and I confess I held it to my nose when it first arrived and could still detect the faintest ghost of the leaves. Letter Thirty was written on the back of a temple donation receipt. Letter Thirty-Four was composed on a single long scroll of calligraphy paper, the brush strokes growing fainter as the ink ran out, the final lines barely legible, as though the letter were disappearing even as it was being written.

I have preserved all thirty-seven letters in a cedar box built for this purpose by a woodworker in Kamakura. They are arranged chronologically, each in its own sleeve of acid-free rice paper. The box smells of cedar and, faintly, of ink. I have opened it perhaps a thousand times. The hinges still move without sound.

I mention this because I want the reader to understand that these are not merely texts. They are objects made by a man's hand — *that* man's hand, a hand I once watched break three boards of hinoki cypress in a single descending strike, a hand that later learned to grind ink on a stone with the patience of falling water. The body is always present in these letters. The pressure of the brush, the angle of the stroke, the places where the ink pools because the hand has paused — these are a form of communication that print cannot reproduce. I ask you to trust me when I note such details. I have spent longer studying these marks than I care to quantify.

One example will suffice. Letter Fourteen arrived in June of 1994, during the rainy season, in an envelope that had evidently been inadequately sealed. Water had reached the second page. Most of the damage was superficial — the ink blurred but remained legible. However, a single character in the third line of the second page was rendered completely illegible. I spent longer than I should admit attempting to reconstruct it. I built a light table. I consulted two specialists in Edo-period calligraphy, presenting the damaged character without context und claiming it was from an antique scroll. I compared the smudged ink pattern against every plausible kanji in Hideo's known hand. I performed this work over the course of three months, primarily between the hours of one and four in the morning, which is a detail I include only because this is, as I have said, an exercise in honesty, and sleep has never come easily to me since 1983.

I was unable to determine the character with certainty. The implications of this will become clear when the reader reaches Letter Fourteen.

## The Letters

---

### Letter One

*Received December 14, 1983. Postmarked Tōno, Iwate Prefecture. Brushed on medium-weight washi, folded twice. No return address.*

---

I have entered a monastery in the mountains. The name does not matter — it is small and the monks here have no interest in what I was before. This is one of its recommendations.

I will describe the routine, since I have no other subject yet.

We rise at four. There is a bell — an iron bell, very old, with a tone that does not so much end as become inaudible, which is not the same thing. We sit in meditation for two hours. My legs ache. I had believed myself accustomed to physical discomfort, but this is a different kind. In combat, pain announces itself and then the body answers it. Here, the pain makes no announcement. It simply accumulates, like sediment.

After meditation, there is a meal. Rice, pickled vegetables, miso soup. Then labor. I have been assigned to chop wood. The axe is old and the handle has been replaced many times. The head is still the original. I find this interesting — the tool persists through continuous replacement of its parts, which is a problem I understand the Greeks considered at some length, though I have not read their account.

In the afternoon, more meditation. In the evening, chanting. Then sleep.

I am writing to you. I do not yet understand why. When I understand it, perhaps I will say.

Hideo

---

**Editor's note:** *I received this letter on a Tuesday. I read it four times. I was angry, then confused, then angry at my confusion. I placed it in a drawer in my desk and closed the drawer and sat with my hands flat on the surface of the desk for what I later estimated was twenty minutes. I opened the drawer and read it a fifth time.*

*There was no return address. There was no invitation to respond. I would not have responded in any case. I want to make this clear, because it will be relevant throughout: I never replied. Not once in forty years. Not a single word. This was not an exchange of letters. It was a man speaking into a room where he knew someone was listening, and the listener chose — I chose — to remain silent. Whether that silence was its own form of speech is a question I have been unable to resolve.*

*What struck me most on first reading was the ordinariness of the subject matter. He had abandoned his career, his title, his reputation, everything — and he wrote to me about an axe. About pickled vegetables. I found this enraging. I wanted him to explain himself. I believed I was owed an explanation.*

*I was thirty-six years old. I understood very little.*

### Letter Two

*Received February 3, 1984. Postmarked Tōno. Brushed on washi of similar weight to Letter One.*

---

Winter here is a serious thing. In Tokyo, winter is an inconvenience. Here it is a fact, in the way that gravity is a fact, or stone. Snow has covered the monastery to the depth of my hip. We dig paths each morning. By afternoon the paths have been softened, their edges rounded. By the next morning they must be dug again. The older monks do not seem to regard this as futile, which I have decided is either wisdom or a failure of imagination, and I am not yet sure which.

My hands are changing. I noticed this yesterday while chopping wood. The calluses are shifting. For twenty years they were thickest along the first two knuckles and the blade of the palm — the striking surfaces, the conditioned points. Now new calluses are forming: across the inner fingers where the axe handle sits, at the base of the thumb where the broom presses during sweeping, along the pads of the fingertips from handling cold stone.

The old calluses have not yet gone. So there is a period, which I am living through, when my hands carry the memory of two lives. I find myself studying them in the evening, turning them over in the lamplight. They are like a palimpsest — you can see both texts at once, the older one fading beneath the newer.

I do not know why I am telling you this. I do not know why I am writing to you at all. But the not-knowing has begun to feel less like a problem and more like a condition, the way altitude is a condition. One adjusts. One continues.

---

**Editor's note:** *The observation about the calluses arrested me. I looked at my own hands after reading this. Mine had not changed. I was still training daily, still competing, still striking the makiwara each morning until the skin split and hardened and split again. My calluses were exactly where they had always been. Hideo's hands were becoming something new. Mine were not.*

*I do not know what to make of this, even now. I note it.*

---

### Letter Three

*Received April 19, 1984. Postmarked Tōno. Brushed on thinner paper — possibly a cheaper stock, or the monastery's own supply.*

---

Spring has begun, though in the mountains it begins slowly, like a conversation between two cautious people, each waiting for the other to commit.

I have begun to sleep better. For the first weeks, my body resisted the schedule. It wanted to train in the evenings. It wanted to condition, to stretch, to rehearse. A body that has been trained for twenty years does not surrender its habits peacefully. It negotiates. It insists.

But something is loosening now.

I must tell you a thing that happened last week, though I am uncertain how to frame it. I woke in the night — not from a nightmare, nothing so dramatic. I woke to find my body halfway through a movement. I was lying on my futon and my right arm had extended and my left hand had drawn back to the hip and my hips had turned, and my body was performing, in horizontal miniature, the opening sequence of *Bassai Dai* — or perhaps not the kata, perhaps something less structured, more responsive. My body was fighting. I was asleep and my body was answering a stance, a configuration, something it had memorized and could not yet release.

The stance it was answering — I say this plainly, because there is no useful way to say it otherwise — was yours. The weight forward and slightly left. The rear foot angled precisely so. My nervous system has preserved you more faithfully than my conscious mind, and when my conscious mind is absent, it returns to you the way the tongue returns to a broken tooth.

I lay there afterward with my arm still extended and I felt the absence of contact — the strike that did not land and the body that was not there to receive it — and the absence had a particular quality that I will not attempt to name.

Spring is progressing. The plum trees have begun.

---

**Editor's note:** *He dreamed of fighting me. Or — more precisely — his body dreamed of fighting me, which is different, and worse, and more intimate than I know how to address in a scholarly annotation.*

*I had the same dream. Not that night — I do not believe in such symmetries, and the dates would not align in any case. But in the months after the forfeit, frequently. I would wake in my apartment in Yokohama with my fists clenched and my weight shifted and the phantom architecture of an exchange still reverberating through my frame. Always the same exchange. Always the fourteenth. Always the moment just before he stopped.*
