# COURT OF SECRETS

## ACT ONE — THE WHISTLE BLOWS

### I.

The squeak of rubber against hardwood was the first language they shared, and tonight it sang in the cadence of a team that had stopped thinking and started knowing. Three days before regionals, the gym at Ridgemont belonged to the Pioneers—five chairs cutting the floor in patterns that would look, from above, like the synchronized calligraphy of some alphabet only they could read.

Maya Cheng drove from the top of the key with her left hand on the push rim and her right cradling the ball against her thigh, angled forward in her chair at precisely the degree that meant she was about to make someone look foolish. She stuttered her push—half-stroke, quarter-stroke, a deceleration so slight it would take a trained eye to catch it—and watched the defense commit. Leah, covering her, bit on the fake right and leaned a centimeter too far. That was all Maya ever needed.

She fired the pass cross-court without looking—a chest-height rope that cut the air and kissed Ro Nakamura's palms at the exact spot where Ro's hands always waited, because Maya had thrown that pass four thousand times in practice and Ro had caught it four thousand and one. Ro pivoted the chair forty-five degrees, set the ball in her lap, and drove for the block. She was six-foot-one and had arms that could make a folding chair look like a throne. When she posted up and released, the arc was so clean it made geometry feel lazy.

The ball snapped through the net. Nothing but cord.

"That's seventeen!" Jesse Vargas shouted from the wing, slapping the side of her chair so hard the sound cracked off the cinder-block walls. "Seventeen to nine. We're going to *murder* Eastfield."

"Scoreboard doesn't care about scrimmage," Coach Warren called from the sideline, though even he was failing to suppress the thing his mouth did when this team ran a set to perfection—a compression of the lips that wasn't quite a smile but lived next door. He sat in his titanium-frame RGK, angled so he could watch the court and the clipboard on his lap simultaneously. He was sixty-one and had coached wheelchair basketball for twenty-three years, and there were those who said he'd forgotten how to exist outside the geometry of a court. He had steel-colored hair and the kind of face that looked like it had been designed to deliver hard truths. Right now, though, his right hand was gripping the armrest of his chair with the intensity of a man holding himself to the earth.

No one noticed.

Or almost no one.

Down at the far baseline, beneath the scoreboard that hadn't worked properly since 2019, Denny Weis was on his knees beside Toni Okafor's backup chair, adjusting the camber of the rear wheels with a torque wrench and the focus of a jeweler setting a diamond. He was forty-four, compact, thick through the shoulders, with the kind of hands that could fix anything mechanical and a face that broadcast, at all times, a species of concern that most people found either comforting or exhausting, depending on what they were hiding.

Denny was the team's equipment manager, and describing him that way was like describing the spine as a bone. He maintained twenty-two sport wheelchairs across the varsity and JV rosters, seven backup frames, four sets of anti-tip casters, an inventory of push-rim covers and axle sleeves and cushion pads that he tracked in binders so meticulous they bordered on a diagnosable condition. He kept the stat books by hand—every shot chart, every turnover, every assist, logged in his slanted left-handed script in black Pilot G2 ink, .38 millimeter, because .5 was for amateurs—and he served as the team's unofficial counselor, physical therapist, travel coordinator, advocate, and, when necessary, bouncer.

He finished with Toni's backup and moved to the rolling cart where the push-rim covers lived, organized by player and labeled with a system only he fully understood. His lips were moving slightly—he was counting, cross-referencing what he had with what they'd need for the championship trip. Tape, gloves, spare axles, the bolt kit, the anti-tip spares for Jesse's chair because Jesse drove like she was fleeing a crime scene and blew through anti-tips at twice the rate of anyone else on the roster.

He looked up.

Coach Warren was ten feet away, near the bottom of the bleachers, drinking from a steel water bottle and looking at nothing. Denny crossed to him and said something low, close to Coach's ear. It was brief—five or six words, spoken with the controlled intensity of a man issuing a final notice.

Coach Warren flinched. Not a dramatic recoil—a micro-event, a contraction of the muscles around his eyes, a stiffening of the neck. The kind of thing you'd have to be looking for. The kind of thing you'd have to know the body's alphabet to read.

He said nothing in return. Denny held his gaze for one more second, then turned and walked back to the equipment cart, where the world made sense because it was organized.

Practice continued. Maya ran a pick-and-roll with Ro that ended in a reverse layup so smooth it made Leah swear in Portuguese. Jesse launched a three from the corner and it banged the rim and Jesse swore in English. Coach blew the whistle, called them in, ran through the championship prep checklist—scouting report on the Eastfield Raptors (yes, *those* Raptors, and no, they weren't to engage the media about the rivalry or the history), travel logistics, evening curfew, the usual.

He did not look at Denny again.

The players dispersed. Ro grabbed her gym bag and paused at the door, watching something across the court—but whatever it was, she processed it and filed it and left without a word. Jesse left loudly, as Jesse left everything, hollering about a postgame meal plan that was ninety percent protein and ten percent delusion. Leah, the backup point guard, rolled out with her earbuds in, already gone to whatever world lived inside her music.

Toni Okafor was the last player through the door, as she was always the last through any door. She was quiet the way walls are quiet—not because they had nothing to say but because they had learned that no one asked. She was five-foot-four, with close-cropped hair and skin that caught the gymnasium's fluorescent light and did something careful with it, and she had the singular talent of occupying a room without registering in anyone's mental census. Teachers gave her keys to supply closets and never asked for them back. Janitors let her into buildings after hours and forgot they'd seen her. She moved through Ridgemont's institutional spaces the way water moves through limestone—silently, finding every crack.

She paused at the gym door. She looked back at Denny, who was now deep in his equipment inventory, surrounded by binders and spare parts, and she thought about saying something. She'd noticed, over the past two weeks, that Denny had started locking his office door when he stepped away, which he'd never done before. She'd noticed that his stat binders—normally arrayed on the shelf behind his desk in chronological order—had been rearranged, as if someone had gone through them and he had put them back in a hurry.

But Toni did not say things she had noticed, because to announce what she saw would be to announce that she saw, and seeing was the one advantage she possessed that no one knew to take from her.

She left.

Denny stayed. The gym settled into its after-hours hum—the HVAC system cycling, the fluorescent lights buzzing their institutional mantra, the particular solitude of a large empty room designed for noise. Denny moved between equipment racks and filing cabinets with the muscle memory of a man who had loved this work for nine years. He opened his stat binder for the current season and ran his finger down a column of numbers and stopped at a name and closed his eyes and breathed.

Then he opened the binder to the back, where he kept pages that were not about statistics.

And if you knew what Maya Cheng knew—which at this moment no one in the world fully did, though she believed Denny came closest—you would have understood the thing she had felt all through practice, the low-frequency vibration in her chest that she had mistaken for competitive focus until she admitted, pulling out of the school parking lot and gripping the steering wheel of her adapted van as if it might otherwise fly from her hands, that it was dread.

Denny had asked to speak with her after the season. She knew what it was about. She had known for weeks, the way you know a sound is a siren before you're consciously aware of it.

She drove home and did not eat dinner and did not sleep and did not open the text thread with Denny, because looking at it would make the conversation real, and if the conversation was real then so was everything it would unmake.

---

Ro Nakamura sat in her car in the Ridgemont parking lot for seven minutes after practice, her hands on the steering wheel, her eyes on nothing. She was Deaf, had been since birth, and she experienced the world as a visual catalog of extraordinary precision. People said things with their mouths, but they said more with their postures, their micro-expressions, the angle of their shoulders when they thought no one was watching.

She had been watching when Denny leaned toward Coach.

She was a lip reader of near-professional caliber—not because she'd trained for it, but because she'd spent nineteen years living in a hearing world that didn't caption itself. She could read lips at thirty feet in good light, and the gymnasium's fluorescent grid provided exceptional light.

Denny had said: *You have until Friday or I tell them everything.*

The "everything" had landed on his lips with the particular emphasis that meant the word was load-bearing—not casual, not filler. *Everything*.

And Coach had flinched.

Ro had told no one. She sat with it the way she sat with hundreds of conversations she had inadvertently read over her years as the only Deaf player on a hearing team—conversations about her, conversations about each other, conversations that were none of her business and yet lived in her memory because her eyes had done what her eyes always did.

But this one was different. This one had a temperature.

Friday was two days away.

She started her car and drove home and did not sleep either, though for different reasons than Maya, and in a different kind of silence.

### II.

The ambulance was in no hurry. That was the detail that Jesse Vargas would remember later—the thing that made her body understand what her mind had not yet assembled. She'd pulled into the Ridgemont lot at 6:12 AM for the early shootaround, half-asleep and running on instant coffee and a protein bar that tasted like regret, and seen the cruisers first, and then the yellow tape, and then the ambulance sitting there with its lights off, patient as a hearse.

An ambulance in a hurry meant someone was alive.

She texted the group chat: *gym is taped off, cops everywhere, nobody go inside until we know whats going on*

She did not have to wait long. Coach Warren arrived at 6:20, and a detective met him at the perimeter, and whatever the detective said made Coach put both hands over his face and hold them there for a long time. Then Coach rolled to the edge of the parking lot and sat facing the empty practice field and did not move.

Maya arrived at 6:25. Ro at 6:28. Toni at 6:31—Toni was always on time but never early, because early attracted attention.

It was Maya who got the information, because Maya always got the information. She rolled to the nearest officer—a young woman with a face still arranging itself around the morning's events—and said, "I'm the team captain. Please tell me what happened."

Denny Weis was dead. Found by the janitorial staff at 5 AM. He appeared to have been killed the previous evening.

Maya did not cry. She processed.

Jesse cried. She cried loudly, with her whole body, because Jesse Vargas did not have a setting between zero and everything. She cried and then she punched the side of her chair and then she cried again, and Ro reached over and put a hand on her arm, and Jesse grabbed it and held on.

Ro did not cry. Her face went very still, the way a lake goes still before a temperature change, and she watched the gym doors with the focus of someone memorizing a crime scene she hadn't been allowed to enter.

Toni did not cry. Toni went to the far edge of the parking lot, near the dumpsters, where no one would think to look for her, and she pressed her forehead against the chain-link fence and breathed until the world stopped tilting.

None of them cried the same way, because Denny had not been the same thing to each of them.

To Jesse, Denny was the only adult at Ridgemont who matched her energy without mocking it. When she ranted, he listened. When she launched her chair too hard into a wall during a frustrating practice, he fixed the dent without a lecture. He kept salted cashews in his desk because they were her pregame ritual, and the last time she'd had a screaming fight with her mother in the parking lot, Denny had sat with her afterward and said nothing for twenty minutes and it had been the most useful twenty minutes of her year.

To Ro, Denny was the person who had learned fifty signs—not fluently, not gracefully, but with the sincerity of a man who understood that meeting someone in their language was a form of respect that mattered more than proficiency. He was the only hearing adult on the team's staff who consistently faced Ro when he spoke, who never said "never mind" when she asked him to repeat something, who had once, in a quiet moment after a road game, said to her: *"You see more than anyone in this gym, Ro. Don't let anyone make you feel like that's not enough."*

To Toni, Denny was the only person who adjusted her chair without being asked. This sounds small. It was not small. Toni's sport wheelchair had a recurring issue with the right rear axle—a slight wobble that didn't affect basic mobility but degraded her lateral movement by a fraction, and that fraction was the difference between being a competent bench player and being the defensive specialist she could have been. No one else noticed because no one watched Toni long enough to see the compensation. Denny noticed. Every practice, before anyone arrived, he checked her chair. He never mentioned it. He never made it a thing. He simply saw her, and isn't that the entire business of love—to see someone without requiring them to perform the act of being seen?

To Maya, Denny was the keeper of a secret that could end her career.

But we are not there yet.

---

By noon, the narrative had been written.

The police arrested Frank Novak at his apartment at 11:40 AM. Novak, fifty-seven, former head coach of the Eastfield Raptors, had been fired two years prior following a tournament incident in which he'd publicly accused Denny of manipulating eligibility paperwork to benefit Ridgemont players. The confrontation had been captured on cell phone video—Novak screaming, Denny standing his ground with the rigid calm of a man who knows he's being recorded. The video had circulated. Novak's contract had not been renewed. He'd spent the intervening years as a bitter ex-coach with a social media presence that vibrated at the frequency of a man convinced the world owed him an apology.

He had been seen near the Ridgemont gym the previous evening. A security camera at the gas station across the street had captured his vehicle in the parking lot at approximately 8:15 PM.

When police arrived at his door, Novak had looked at them with the hollow recognition of a man who had been rehearsing this moment without knowing it. He said: *"Yeah, I did it. He had it coming."*

Open and shut.

The school superintendent released a statement expressing confidence in the police department and grief for the Ridgemont community. The championship game would proceed as scheduled—a tribute to Denny's legacy. The words *closure* and *healing* appeared in the statement with the regularity of punctuation.
