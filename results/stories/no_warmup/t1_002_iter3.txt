## A Personal Compilation Prepared by Margaret A. Hallam

### Certified Stenographic Reporter, State of Texas, License No. 4471

**COMPILER'S NOTE**

This document represents a personal compilation of selected materials related to *The State of Texas v. Eleanor Ruth Vance*, Case No. 87-CR-4419, tried in the 198th Judicial District Court, Sutton County, Texas, before the Honorable Judge Roland T. Keyes, November 2–December 18, 1987.

The materials assembled herein include excerpted passages from the official trial transcript, personal annotations made by the compiler in the margins of her working copies, and transcriptions of private audio recordings made during the trial period. These audio recordings were made on the compiler's own equipment, on her own time, and do not constitute any portion of the court record.

This compilation was prepared in January 1989 for purposes of clarity and personal reference, and in the event of my absence. It is stored at a location known to my sister, CAROL HALLAM-KREIGER of Austin, Texas, who has been instructed to hold it unopened unless circumstances dictate otherwise. I have not described those circumstances to Carol. I trust she will recognize them.

A note on formatting conventions: text appearing in standard typeface represents excerpted official transcript. **Text in bold represents my personal annotations and marginalia, reproduced here as originally written.** *Text in italics represents transcription of my private audio diary recordings.* Where I have omitted material, I have indicated the omission with bracketed ellipses.

I have been a certified court stenographer for eleven years. In that time, I have transcribed proceedings in over four hundred cases, including nine capital murder trials. I have never previously felt the need to maintain personal copies of trial materials. My professional philosophy has always been simple and, I believed, sufficient: I am the record. The record does not interpret.

I am no longer certain that is true.

I am no longer certain of the scope of what a record can be asked to hold.

— M.A. Hallam
January 19, 1989

**EXCERPTED TRANSCRIPT — JURY SELECTION**
*The State of Texas v. Eleanor Ruth Vance*
Case No. 87-CR-4419
Day One — November 2, 1987, 9:07 A.M.

THE COURT: Good morning. We are on the record in the matter of the State of Texas versus Eleanor Ruth Vance, Case Number 87-CR-4419. We are here this morning for the purpose of jury selection. Counsel, will you identify yourselves for the record.

MR. MORSE: Franklin Morse, Assistant District Attorney, on behalf of the State of Texas.

MS. BRYCE: Colleen Bryce, appointed counsel for the defendant, who is present in court.

THE COURT: Thank you. Before we bring in the venire panel, let me address some housekeeping. As counsel are aware, this case has attracted significant public attention. I want to be clear from the outset. This is a courtroom and not a theater. I have restricted recording devices to the designated media pool. I will not tolerate outbursts from the gallery. Anyone who cannot abide by these expectations will be removed and held in contempt. That includes members of the press.

Now. Mr. Morse, you indicated in our pre-trial conference that the State anticipates voir dire will require two days. Is that still your estimate?

MR. MORSE: It is, Your Honor.

THE COURT: Ms. Bryce?

MS. BRYCE: Your Honor, I would like to revisit my motion for change of venue before we proceed with jury selection. Given the media coverage in Sutton County—

THE COURT: That motion was denied, Ms. Bryce. I'm not going to re-litigate it. We discussed this.

MS. BRYCE: For the record, Your Honor, the defense renews its objection. My client cannot receive a fair trial in this county.

THE COURT: Noted. Your objection is preserved. Let's bring in the panel.

[...]

**I should note, for anyone reading this, what the courtroom looked like that first day. The transcript doesn't communicate it. The gallery was full forty minutes before the doors opened. There were three sketch artists and a pool camera crew. I counted fourteen reporters in the designated section. Outside, two groups of protesters had established themselves on opposite sides of the courthouse steps. One group carried signs reading FREE ELEANOR and HYSTERIA IS NOT EVIDENCE. The other carried crosses and at least one sign that read, simply, BURN THE WITCH. A deputy told me during the lunch break that someone had thrown a bottle. I did not hear it from inside the courtroom. I heard almost nothing from outside. The courtroom walls are very thick.**

[...]

THE COURT: Juror Number Fourteen, you indicated on your questionnaire that you attend Grace Tabernacle Church here in Sutton. Is that correct?

PROSPECTIVE JUROR NO. 14: Yes, sir.

THE COURT: Have you heard this case discussed at your church?

PROSPECTIVE JUROR NO. 14: Well, yes. Pastor Caldwell has mentioned it.

THE COURT: Has Pastor Caldwell expressed an opinion about the defendant's guilt or innocence?

PROSPECTIVE JUROR NO. 14: He's said we should pray for the children. That's all.

MR. MORSE: The State finds Juror Fourteen acceptable, Your Honor.

MS. BRYCE: Your Honor, I'd like to follow up. Ma'am, when Pastor Caldwell said to "pray for the children," did he mention the defendant by name?

PROSPECTIVE JUROR NO. 14: I don't remember exactly.

MS. BRYCE: Did he mention the word "evil" in connection with this case?

MR. MORSE: Objection. Counsel is trying to put words in the juror's mouth about what her pastor may or may not have—

THE COURT: Sustained. Move on, Ms. Bryce.

[...]

**Defendant sat motionless during entire proceedings. Hands flat on table. Did not appear to blink for extended periods but this may be my imagination. Courtroom warm.**

[...]

[At approximately 2:40 P.M., a sidebar conference was requested by Mr. Morse and the following exchange occurred at the bench. My equipment captured the following, though sidebar conversations are typically excluded from the official record when conducted at low volume and at the Court's discretion. I retained this in my working notes.]

MR. MORSE: [Low voice] You know what she is.

MS. BRYCE: [Inaudible]

THE COURT: That's enough. Both of you back to your tables.

**Could not capture defense counsel's reply. Unusual. My equipment was functioning. The Stenograph was set to capture at the same sensitivity level I use for all sidebar monitoring, which reliably picks up speech at that distance when both speakers face even partially toward my station. Mr. Morse was in half-profile to me. Ms. Bryce was facing me directly. I should have captured her words. I did not. I am noting this only because accuracy requires it.**

*Audio Diary — Margaret Hallam*
*November 2, 1987, 9:47 P.M.*

*[Sound of a tape recorder clicking on. A chair creaking. What might be a glass being set down on a hard surface.]*

*Okay. I've never done this before, so I'm not sure what the protocol is. I suppose there isn't one. That's the problem. That's sort of the whole problem, which I'll get to.*

*I want to keep a record of this case. I don't mean the official record — that's what I do every day, that's the transcript. I mean a personal record. Because something about this case feels different, and I want to remember why. I want to remember what I was thinking while it was happening, not just what was said. The transcript captures everything that's spoken in a courtroom and nothing else. I have always believed that was enough. What else could matter? The words are the words.*

*But today there were things that weren't words. Or they were words, but not the kind I'm trained to take down.*

*Let me describe the courtroom. It's the main courtroom in Sutton County, the big one on the second floor. Built in 1924 — high ceilings, those old plaster moldings that always look a little yellow no matter how recently they've been painted. The bench is dark wood. The gallery seats maybe two hundred. Every seat was full. You could feel the heat of all those bodies. The ventilation in that building has never been adequate.*

*[Pause]*

*The media was everywhere. I've worked cases with media before, but this was different. It has that energy you get when people have already decided what the story is and they're just waiting for the actors to read their lines. The Satanic panic thing. I know what that is. I've read the coverage — McMartin, the Kellers down in Austin, all of it. You can't be a person in Texas in 1987 and not know what that is.*

*Eleanor Vance is forty-one years old. Licensed social worker. She had a foster care license for six years. Three children in her care died over fourteen months. Those are facts. Those children are dead and that is real and I am not going to lose sight of it, whatever else I think about this case.*

*But then there's the rest of it. The satanic ritual angle. The anonymous tip. The journal. And the way Mr. Morse said "You know what she is" at the sidebar today, as if what Eleanor Vance is has already been established by some authority higher than the court.*

*[Pause]*

*The defendant. Eleanor.*

*She sat through the entire day without moving. I don't mean she was still — people are often still in court, defendants especially, their lawyers tell them to stay calm, look composed. This was different. She was motionless the way a photograph is motionless. Her hands were flat on the defense table, fingers spread slightly, and they stayed that way for hours. I watched her during the breaks when the jury panel was out. She didn't shift. She didn't lean over to whisper to Ms. Bryce. She just sat there.*

*And she looked at me.*

*That's the thing I want to remember. That's why I'm making this tape. They don't usually look at me. Nobody looks at the stenographer. I am part of the furniture. I am a function, not a person, and I have always preferred that — it makes the work cleaner. You transcribe more accurately when you are invisible. I have cultivated invisibility for eleven years.*

*Eleanor Vance looked directly at me twice during jury selection. Not at the judge. Not at the jury panel. Not at Mr. Morse. At me. At my hands on the Stenograph. And her expression was — I don't have the right word. It was not hostile. It was not pleading. It was something like recognition. As if she knew me. As if she had been expecting me specifically.*

*I want to say this is my imagination. I want that noted, even on a private tape that no one will hear. I am aware that I am a woman alone in a new county, assigned to a sensational case at the last minute, and that these conditions are conducive to projecting significance onto ordinary events.*

*Speaking of which — I should mention that I was not originally assigned to this case. The county's regular stenographer, Diane Ogilvie, had been handling pre-trial proceedings. She transferred to another judicial district in September, roughly six weeks before the trial date. I don't know the details. I was brought in through the regional staffing pool. When I asked the court clerk about Diane — just casual conversation, trying to get oriented — he said she'd left "suddenly" and that he didn't have a forwarding address. This is not especially unusual. Stenographers move between districts all the time. It's contract work. People go where the assignments are.*

*[Pause]*

*I'm going to bed. I have a long day tomorrow. I want to note, before I stop, that I am fine. I am professional. I am simply keeping a record, because that is what I do.*

*[Tape recorder clicks off]*

**EXCERPTED TRANSCRIPT — TRIAL PROCEEDINGS**
*The State of Texas v. Eleanor Ruth Vance*
Day Three — November 5, 1987, 9:22 A.M.

[Jury empaneled. Opening statements.]

MR. MORSE: Ladies and gentlemen of the jury, thank you for your service. I know this is not an easy case to hear. I wish I could tell you it will get easier as we go. It won't.

Over the next several weeks, you are going to hear about three children. Michael Torres, age seven. Anna Chen, age five. Lily Kreider, age nine. Three children who were placed in the foster care of the defendant, Eleanor Vance, by the State of Texas, because the State believed—because *we* believed—that she would keep them safe. We failed those children. And now we are here to do the only thing we can still do for them, which is tell the truth about how they died.

Michael Torres died on March 3, 1986, of what was initially ruled aspiration pneumonia. Anna Chen died on July 19, 1986, from what was initially attributed to a severe allergic reaction. Lily Kreider died on December 1, 1986, from injuries consistent with a fall down the staircase of Eleanor Vance's home.

Three children. Three different causes of death. Three rulings, at the time, of accident or natural cause. And if not for an anonymous citizen who came forward in February of this year, that is where this story might have ended. Three small funerals. Three closed files.

But that citizen came forward. And that citizen led investigators to a journal — a journal kept in Eleanor Vance's own hand, hidden in her home — a journal that describes, in meticulous detail, ritual practices, invocations of demonic entities, and the preparation of what the author calls "vessels." The evidence will show that this term, "vessels," refers to children.

The evidence will show that these three deaths were not accidents. They were not coincidences. They were ritual acts, carried out by the defendant in service of beliefs so dark that I will ask you, at times during this trial, to steel yourselves against what you are hearing. The evidence will show—

MS. BRYCE: Objection, Your Honor. Mr. Morse is testifying, not opening.

THE COURT: I'll give him some latitude. Mr. Morse, let's stay closer to what the evidence will show and further from the editorial commentary.

MR. MORSE: Of course, Your Honor. The evidence will show that Eleanor Vance systematically selected, isolated, and killed three children placed in her care, and that she did so in furtherance of an occult belief system documented in her own handwriting.

[...]

MS. BRYCE: Members of the jury, my name is Colleen Bryce, and I represent Eleanor Vance. And I want to begin by doing something Mr. Morse did not do, which is to acknowledge what we don't know.

We don't know why those three children died. Not really. The medical evidence, as you will hear, is ambiguous in each case. Aspiration pneumonia, allergic reaction, a fall. These are things that happen to children — tragically, unfairly, heartbreakingly, but they happen. And for almost a year, that is exactly what every medical professional and investigator involved in these cases believed. That these were tragedies.

What changed? What changed is that someone made an anonymous phone call. We don't know who. The State has not identified this person and apparently cannot. And on the basis of that call, investigators searched Eleanor's home and found a journal that they say is hers, a journal she says she has never seen before in her life.

The State's case rests on two pillars: that journal, and the theory of satanic ritual abuse. I want you to keep something in mind over the coming weeks. Across this country right now, people are being accused of ritual satanic abuse of children. In California, in New Jersey, in Massachusetts, right here in Texas. Hundreds of people. Day care workers. Parents. Teachers. And in case after case after case, the evidence has not held up. What has held up is the fear. The accusation. The willingness to believe the worst about someone because the word "satanic" has been attached to their name.

Eleanor Vance is not a monster. She is a social worker who dedicated her life to children no one else wanted, and she is innocent.

**Ms. Bryce's opening was competent but brief. She had perhaps a third of the resources the prosecution had. No co-counsel. One investigator, part-time. I have seen this disparity in other cases but it felt particularly stark here. Mr. Morse had an easel with photographs of the three children — enlarged, framed in black, positioned so the jury would see them for the entire trial. Ms. Bryce had a legal pad.**

[
