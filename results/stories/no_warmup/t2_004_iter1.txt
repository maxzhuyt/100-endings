# THE HATEFUL IMMORTALS

---

## ACT ONE: "TILL DEATH DO US PART"

---

### I.

Dr. Amelia Thornton sits in a wingback chair in what appears to be a university office, though the camera is too tight on her face for the viewer to be certain. She is a Black woman in her mid-fifties with close-cropped silver hair and reading glasses she has pushed onto her forehead, where they will remain, forgotten, for the duration of the interview. Behind her, slightly out of focus, a corkboard bristles with pinned photographs, timelines drawn in red marker, and a single Post-it note on which someone has written, in aggressive capitals, *CHECK THE PORTLAND TAPE AGAIN*.

"The question I keep coming back to," Thornton says, leaning forward slightly, "the one that made me spend seven years on this project, give up two relationships and one very promising vegetable garden—"

She pauses. Smiles at herself.

"The question is deceptively simple. We all understand love as a thing that exists within the boundaries of a human life. Right? You fall in love, you build something, you hold on as long as you can, and then one of you dies. That's the contract. That's what 'till death do us part' *means*—not just as a promise, but as a mercy. An escape clause. Death is the event horizon of every marriage. It's the thing that gives love its *shape*."

She takes off her glasses from her forehead, notices them as if for the first time, sets them on the table beside her.

"So what happens when the escape clause is revoked? What happens to love when 'forever' isn't a metaphor? When you say 'till death do us part' and you *mean* it, you mean it with your whole heart, except that death is never, ever coming?"

Cut to: an exterior shot, drone footage, golden autumn light. A white clapboard farmhouse in rural Vermont, set back from a dirt road. The house is large and oddly shaped—it appears to have been expanded haphazardly over the years, rooms and wings added without particular regard for architectural coherence. Two chimneys. Two satellite dishes. Two mailboxes at the end of the drive, one labeled *M. Chen* and the other *J. Chen*, each in distinctly different handwriting.

"This," Thornton says in voiceover, "is the current home of Margaret and James Chen, née Margaret Ellsworth and James Chen Wei-Lin. They were married on the fourteenth of June, 1849, in Boston, Massachusetts. They are still married. They have been married for one hundred and seventy-five years. And this is the first time—the *first* time, in nearly two centuries—that both of them have agreed to be interviewed."

A beat.

"Separately, obviously."

Another beat.

"They are currently not speaking over an incident in 2019 involving a dishwasher. I will get to the dishwasher."

---

To understand the Chens, Thornton explains—and here the documentary shifts briefly into an educational register, crisp graphics and clear narration, the visual language of a high-end explainer—one must first understand the world they inhabit.

In this world, as in the one the viewer knows, there are roughly eight billion human beings. The vast majority of them are ordinary. But approximately one in every fifty thousand manifests, at or around the onset of puberty, a superhuman ability. The phenomenon has been documented for as long as there have been documents. It appears in every culture, every era, every population. There is no genetic predictor, no environmental trigger, no discernible logic. One child in a family of millions might manifest. A line of manifested individuals might produce nothing but ordinary children for ten generations. The abilities themselves are wildly, almost absurdly unequal. One person might develop telekinesis powerful enough to reshape a landscape. Another might develop the ability to always know the exact time, to the millisecond, without a clock.

"There is no pattern," Thornton says over an animated graphic showing the statistical distribution of abilities on a bell curve that is barely a curve at all—more of a scatter plot. "There is no 'power level.' There is no chosen one. There's just the genetic lottery, and some people draw a card, and the card might be an ace of spades or it might be a three of clubs, and that's all there is. It's as arbitrary as eye color. More so. Society has spent centuries trying to make it mean something—divine favor, genetic superiority, demonic possession, evolutionary advantage—and it never does. It's just a thing that happens to some people."

The social consequences, however, have been anything but arbitrary. Laws governing "specials"—the common term that emerged in the English-speaking world in the eighteenth century, replacing the earlier and more loaded "prodigies," "marvels," and "afflicted"—have swung wildly from era to era. Registration requirements. Employment restrictions. Marriage statutes. Institutionalization campaigns. Integration movements. A fractious, incomplete civil rights struggle that paralleled and sometimes intersected with every other civil rights struggle of the past two centuries. By 2024, in the United States at least, specials have achieved legal parity—technically. Socially, culturally, interpersonally, the picture is more complicated. It always is.

"They're not gods," Thornton says. "They're not heroes. They're just people with one weird thing about them. That's what makes the Chens' story so universal, even though nothing about their situation is remotely normal. Their marriage isn't a superhero story. It's a *marriage* story. It just happens to be the longest one we have."

---

Thornton catalogs her sources with the mix of scholarly precision and barely suppressed obsessive energy that will characterize her throughout the film.

Margaret's personal journals: 347 volumes, spanning 1846 to the present, written in a hand that has remained eerily consistent across nearly two centuries. Because of Margaret's perfect memory, these journals are not impressionistic or selective in the way that ordinary diaries are—they are near-total transcriptions of lived experience, recorded not because Margaret needed to remember (she could never forget) but because, as she has explained, "writing it down is the only way I can convince my mind that a thing has been *dealt with*. It's filing. If I don't file it, it just stays on the desk." The journals are, Thornton notes, the single most comprehensive first-person account of daily life in the nineteenth, twentieth, and twenty-first centuries ever produced. They are also, in Thornton's professional opinion, "one of the great unintentional works of American literature, and the most devastating document about marriage I have ever read."

James's correspondence: far sparser. James is not a diarist. He has left behind letters—to his mother, to friends, to Margaret herself during their occasional separations—and a series of postcards from the 1960s and '70s that are, Thornton says with obvious affection, "mostly drawings of animals with captions that only make sense if you've been alive for over a hundred years."

The documentary also draws on: photographs, daguerreotypes, and one very early tintype of the couple from approximately 1855; legal documents including six marriage certificates issued under different names in different states and countries as the Chens periodically reinvented themselves to avoid detection; immigration records from the United States, Canada, England, France, Japan, and Brazil; census records in which their listed ages grow increasingly implausible; academic analyses by historians and psychologists; interviews with three very elderly individuals who knew Margaret and James under assumed names in various decades; and one recording, made on a microcassette in 1997 in Portland, Oregon, of a couples' therapy session that Margaret authorized and James agreed to under protest and that Thornton describes, carefully, as "the most illuminating thirty-eight minutes of audio I have ever heard, and I say that as someone who has listened to the Watergate tapes."

And she has the Chens themselves.

"They live in the same house," Thornton says. "Barely. The house has two wings. Margaret lives in the east wing. James lives in the west wing. They are connected by a single hallway."

She pauses.

"Margaret refers to it as 'the demilitarized zone.'"

---

### II.

She appears on screen without introduction or title card, though both will be added momentarily. The woman in the frame looks approximately thirty years old. She has brown hair pulled back in a style that is neither fashionable nor unfashionable—the style of someone who decided how she wore her hair a very long time ago and does not see any reason to revisit the question. Her posture is immaculate. Her eyes are the kind of eyes that make you feel as though you are being recorded, which, in her case, you are. She wears a dark green sweater of obvious quality and no jewelry except a plain gold band on her left hand, which she has not removed in 175 years, though if asked about this she will change the subject with a speed that suggests significant practice.

The title card reads: *Margaret Chen (née Ellsworth). Born 1833, Boston, Massachusetts. Ability: Total Eidetic Memory. Age of manifestation: approx. 13.*

She does not smile for the camera. She does not appear hostile, exactly—she appears *calibrated*. She has the energy of someone who has been interviewed before, who understands precisely what an interviewer wants, and who has decided in advance exactly how much she is willing to give.

"I want to be clear," she says, before Thornton has asked her first question, "that I agreed to this because I believe there may be some historical and sociological value in the documentary record of a sustained long-term relationship between two specials. I did not agree to this to *entertain* anyone. I am not here to be colorful. I don't do colorful."

She pauses.

"James does colorful. That's always been his department."

When Thornton, off-camera, asks Margaret to describe James in one word, Margaret does not answer immediately. The camera holds on her face. It is a remarkable face—not because it is particularly beautiful, though it is handsome in a severe, sharp-boned way, but because it contains within it the visible weight of extraordinary duration. Most thirty-year-old faces are still unfinished, still being shaped by experience. Margaret's face finished being shaped decades ago, perhaps centuries ago, and what remains is something more like a landscape than a portrait—weathered not in the skin but somewhere behind the eyes, in the way they focus, in the faint and particular tension at the corners of her mouth.

She is quiet for nine seconds. The documentary will keep every one of them.

"Present," she says.

---

### III.

The man in the frame also looks approximately thirty. He is tall—this is visible even in a seated interview—with black hair, dark eyes, and the kind of face that appears to have been designed, either by genetics or by long practice, to put people at ease. His features reflect his mixed heritage: the son of a Chinese father and an Irish-American mother, he possesses a face that has, over the decades, been read as racially ambiguous by strangers in a dozen countries, which has been both a survival mechanism and a source of pain and a thing he has learned, with great effort, to carry lightly. He wears a flannel shirt. He sits with one ankle crossed over the opposite knee. He looks like a man who, if you met him at a bar, would have you laughing within two minutes and would remember your name and your dog's name the next time he saw you.

He looks, in other words, nothing like his wife.

The title card reads: *James Chen (Chen Wei-Lin). Born 1829, Boston, Massachusetts. Ability: Complete Physical Invulnerability / Regenerative Immortality. Age of manifestation: approx. 13.*

"Oh, this is exciting," he says, looking around the interview setup—the lights, the camera, the sound equipment. "Very professional. Are you going to do that thing where you make the colors all moody? I love that. Make me look brooding. I've never been brooding in my life, but I think I'd like to see what it looks like."

When Thornton asks him to describe Margaret in one word, he grins immediately.

"Unforgettable," he says.

He waits.

No one laughs.

He laughs anyway—a full, genuine, slightly too-loud laugh, the laugh of a man who has been making this joke for decades and has never once found it less funny than the first time. "Come on," he says. "That's *good*. That's objectively good. I've been waiting to use that one."

His laughter trails off. Something happens to his face—not a collapse, exactly, but a settling. The social performer steps offstage for a moment, and the person underneath becomes briefly, startlingly visible.

"She's the most extraordinary person I've ever met," he says. His voice has dropped half a register. He is not performing this. He may not be entirely aware he is saying it. "And I've met a lot of people. A *lot* of people. I've been alive for a very long time, and I have never met anyone like her. Not once. Not even close."

He looks directly into the camera.

"Don't tell her I said that. She'll use it against me."

---

### IV.

Margaret Ellsworth was born in October 1833 in Boston, Massachusetts, the second of four children of Thomas Ellsworth, a modestly prosperous merchant in the dry-goods trade, and Prudence Ellsworth, née Hartwell, a woman about whom Margaret's journals are fastidiously respectful in a way that communicates, to a careful reader, volumes of complicated feeling.

Thornton narrates over period-appropriate illustrations: lithographs of Beacon Hill, maps of 1830s Boston, an artist's rendering of the Ellsworth home on Pinckney Street. "By all accounts," she says, "Margaret was a conspicuous child. Not in the way that precocious children are usually conspicuous—she wasn't loud, wasn't attention-seeking, wasn't performatively clever. She was conspicuous because she *noticed* things. She noticed and she *retained*, even before the ability fully manifested. Teachers commented. Relatives were unsettled. She was the kind of girl who would sit silently through an adult conversation and then, three weeks later, calmly repeat the entire exchange back to one of its participants, including the parts they had hoped no one was paying attention to."

Margaret, in her 2024 interview, is characteristically precise about this: "I was a difficult child by the standards of the time. I did not mean to be difficult. I was simply paying attention. It would have been more convenient for everyone if I had paid less."

She was a ferocious reader from the moment literacy became available to her—her father, to his credit, encouraged this, though her mother expressed periodic concern that too much reading would "strain" her. Margaret was drawn to history, natural philosophy, and anything that organized the world into comprehensible systems. She wanted structures. Frameworks. She wanted to understand *why things were the way they were*, which was a dangerous appetite for a girl in 1840s Boston and would only grow more dangerous with time.

"The important thing to understand about Margaret before the ability," Thornton says, "is that the ability didn't *make* her who she is. It amplified her. It took the girl who already noticed too much and remembered too clearly and could not stop her mind from cataloging every detail of every interaction—and it turned that tendency up to a volume that could never be turned down."

---

The manifestation began in the autumn of 1846, when Margaret was thirteen.

She does not, in her 2024 interview, describe it as a sudden event. "It wasn't a switch," she says. "I know that's how it's often described in the literature—*one day the power is there*. For me it was more like a lens coming into focus. I had always had a strong memory. But in the autumn of my thirteenth year, the… resolution changed. Things I would have naturally forgotten—the precise color of the sky on a Tuesday, the exact number of steps between our front door and the church, every word of a conversation I overheard between two strangers on the street—these things began to stay. And to stay perfectly. Without blur. Without erosion. I would close my eyes and the day I had just lived would be *there*, complete, as though it were still happening."

She pauses. Her expression does not change, but something shifts behind it.

"And then I would close my eyes and the day before that was still there. And the week before that. And everything I had ever experienced was *still there*, but now rendered in this—this unbearable fidelity. Every moment, every sensation, every feeling I had ever felt, all of it still *live*. Still *present tense*."

Another pause. Longer.

"I did not sleep well that year."
