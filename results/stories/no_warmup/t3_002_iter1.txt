# The Last Watch of the Mara Celeste

---

The tea was cold when I found it on the chart table. That was the morning of the second day, I think. Or the third. I have them out of order now. What I remember is the mug—the green one with the chip on the lip that Wen always used—and the tea I'd brought her two hours before, sitting full and dark and cool to the touch when I pressed my fingers against the ceramic. She hadn't drunk it. She'd been standing at the helm that whole time, and she hadn't drunk it.

I poured it out in the galley sink and the ship rolled hard enough to send me into the counter edge, hip-first. I made another cup.

That's what I can tell you. That's what I know how to tell.

---

People have asked me how I'm alive. I was in the water for some time and then I was not. There was a container ship, a Philippine crew who pulled me out and wrapped me in a blanket that smelled like diesel and instant noodles. I don't remember most of that part. What I remember is before.

---

The Mara Celeste was forty-four feet, wooden-hulled, gaff-rigged. She was not a beautiful boat in the way people mean when they say a boat is beautiful. The brightwork was clouded. The teak on the foredeck had gone silver where it should have been honey. There were patches on the hull where the planking had been replaced—you could see it, the newer wood paler against the old, like skin over a surgery. Some of those patches were forty years old. Some were four months. Wen did those herself, in the boatyard in Savannah, on her knees in the red dirt with a caulking iron that had belonged to her grandfather.

I knew it had belonged to her grandfather because she kept it wrapped in a piece of chamois in the nav station drawer, and once, when I was looking for a pencil, I found it and picked it up and she said, without looking at me, "That was his." Just that. She didn't say put it down. She didn't need to.

---

I came aboard in May, in Charleston. Wen needed a cook because the last one had quit in Norfolk. The ad on the dock board said: *Cook needed for Atlantic crossing. No drunks. Must not be afraid of weather.* No mention of pay. I asked about it when I met her on the dock. She looked at me like I'd asked a strange question—not insulting, just strange—and said, "There's a bunk, and you eat what you cook, and we'll figure the rest when we're across."

We never figured the rest. That was fine. I had nowhere to be.

---

The thing you learn about someone on a small boat is not what they believe or what they've suffered or who they love. You learn when they use the head. You learn how they sound when they're sleeping—whether they breathe through their mouth, whether they talk, whether they go still as stone from midnight to four and then surface all at once like something breaching. Wen slept like stone. I knew this because my bunk was eight feet from hers, separated by a plywood bulkhead so thin I could hear her turn over.

I knew she took her coffee with nothing in it. I knew she checked the bilge pump every morning before she checked the weather. I knew she had a scar on her left forearm that went white when she gripped the tiller in rough seas, and I knew she never mentioned it, so I never asked.

That's not friendship. I don't know what to call it. But after two months of open ocean, I knew the sound of her breathing better than I've known most people's names.

---

Her grandfather built the Mara Celeste in 1961, in a shed in Beaufort. I know this because the date was carved into the keelson—I saw it once, on my belly in the bilge, trying to find the source of a leak. *H.L.W. 1961.* The letters were neat and deep. He'd meant them to last.

Wen told me almost nothing about him directly. But there was an evening in the Sargasso Sea—flat calm, the water so still it looked like poured resin—when she sat on the cabin roof and ran her hand along the coaming the way you'd run your hand along a dog's back, and she said, "He sailed her alone from Beaufort to Havana when he was twenty-three. Before the embargo. He said the stars were so thick over the Gulf Stream you could hear them." She paused. "That doesn't make any sense, I know."

I said that it did.

She looked at me like she was deciding something. Then she went below, and I heard her open the nav station drawer—the one with the chamois-wrapped caulking iron—and close it again without taking anything out.

---

The bank letters I knew about the way you know about weather coming. A change in pressure. You don't see it, but everything responds to it.

The first one I noticed was in June, somewhere east of the Gulf Stream. A long envelope, the kind with the cellophane window, sitting on the chart table next to the dividers. Wen had left the VHF on and gone on deck, which was unusual; she was careful with the battery bank. I didn't read it. I didn't need to. The return address had the word *Fiduciary* in it, and Wen had set her coffee mug on top of it the way you set something heavy on a thing you want to stop from blowing away, or from existing.

After that, I noticed the radio calls. She'd take them in the cockpit, late, when she thought I was asleep. Her voice was different on those calls—flattened, administrative, a voice I never heard her use with the sea or the sails or the crew. I caught words through the companionway hatch. *Lien. Assessed value. Bermuda.*

There was a morning—this was in July, I think, weeks before the storm—when I brought her coffee and she was sitting at the chart table with three envelopes open in front of her, and she'd been crying. I know she'd been crying because the chart beneath her hands was spotted with damp, and the pencil line she'd drawn—our course to Bermuda—had a tremor in it. She looked up at me and her face did something I've only seen once before, on a woman in a hospital waiting room in Memphis. That expression that isn't grief and isn't anger. It's the face of someone who has just been told the specific date on which something they love will be taken from them.

"Coffee," I said, because that's what I had.

She took it. She drank it that time. She drank it all.

---

The others left on the morning of day two. I need to go back to say that properly.

There were four of us on the crossing. Wen. Me. Luis, who was first mate and had sailed with Wen for three years. And a boy named Peter, nineteen, from Connecticut, who was working his way to the Azores and had soft hands when he came aboard and hard ones by the time the storm found us.

The storm. Let me go back further.

---

Day one.

I was making rice and beans for supper. It was my best dish on the boat because the propane stove had two burners and one of them didn't light anymore, so everything I cooked was a one-pot situation and rice and beans is a one-pot meal that people don't get tired of if you change the spices. I had cumin going in the dry pan and the whole galley smelled like it—that warm, dusty, almost-sweet smell—when the barometer dropped.

I know the barometer dropped because Luis said, from the companionway, "Cocinera, look at the glass."

I looked. It had fallen nine millibars in three hours.

That means nothing to most people. On a boat, it means something is coming that is bigger than you. I turned off the cumin. It was starting to burn anyway.

The sky didn't look like anything yet. That's the part people don't understand about typhoons. They send their business card ahead—a long, low swell from the southeast, a milky quality to the upper atmosphere—but the sky itself looks ordinary. Almost gentle. The first rain was light and warm. Peter stood in it and tipped his face up, and Wen told him to go below and secure everything that could move.

By midnight, the swells were fifteen feet and the rain was horizontal.

---

Wen had known the storm was coming. I understand that now. She'd known for at least two days before it arrived—you could see it on the weather fax, the isobars tightening like a fist. Luis knew it too. They'd talked about altering course, running south to avoid the worst of it. I overheard that conversation through the galley vent. Luis wanted to run south.

"If we run south, we add a week," Wen said. "Bermuda is ten days out. The payment's due in twelve."

"If we run into it, we might not make Bermuda at all," Luis said.

Wen said nothing again. That was her way of ending a discussion. Not with a word but with a space where a word should have been.

We did not run south.

---

Day one, continued. The card game.

Wen and I played gin rummy every morning at 0700, in the saloon, on the gimbaled table that swung with the motion of the boat so your coffee didn't slide. This was not a ritual we'd discussed or agreed to. It had started in the second week of the crossing, when I dealt a hand out of boredom and she picked hers up without comment, and after that it was simply what we did. 0700. Gin rummy. I kept score on the back of an old chart of the Chesapeake that we didn't need anymore.

On the morning of day one—the storm already building, the boat already starting to move in a way that felt less like sailing and more like being handled—I dealt the cards. Wen picked up her hand. The ship heeled hard to port and her mug slid six inches toward the fiddle rail, and she caught it without looking down and laid off three jacks.

"Gin," she said.

"You can't gin on the first draw," I said.

"I just did."

I marked it on the chart. She was ahead by four hundred points, but I'd stopped counting somewhere in the mid-Atlantic and was making up the numbers. I think she knew. She never said.

---

Day two. The departure.

The storm had been on us for eighteen hours. The seas were twenty-five to thirty feet—not the worst, not yet, but bad enough that the motion below was violent and continuous, a corkscrew pitch-and-roll that made every action a negotiation with gravity. The galley was unusable. I'd tried to boil water for tea and the pot had leapt off the stove and flung scalding water across the sole and I'd burned my left hand catching it. Not badly. Enough that I could feel my pulse in my palm for the rest of the day.

The radio crackled around 0900. A freighter—the *Konpeki Maru*, Japanese registry, bound for Norfolk—had picked up the Mara Celeste's position and was offering assistance. They could come alongside, they said, in the lee of their hull, and take on crew. They could not take the boat. Just the people.

Luis took the call. Then he went to Wen, who was at the helm, and I watched through the companionway as the wind tore his words apart and she stood there, one hand on the tiller, her oilskin dark with spray. I couldn't hear what she said. I could see her mouth move. I could see Luis's face afterward.

He came below. He looked at me and said, "She's not going."

"I know," I said.

"The freighter's holding for thirty minutes."

"I know."

He packed his bag. It took him four minutes. Peter packed his in less. They stood in the saloon and the ship groaned—that sound, I'll come back to that sound—and Luis looked at me and said, "Maria. Come."

I was holding the kettle. The one I'd burned myself on. I was filling it again, from the hand pump, because the electric pump had already shorted out.

"She shouldn't be alone," I said.

Luis said something in Spanish that I didn't catch, and then he said, "She chose to be alone. She chose this."

I set the kettle on the stove. I lit the one burner that still worked. I didn't look at him.

"Maria."

"I heard you."

He waited. Peter waited. The ship rolled and the kettle slid and I caught it and held it and the blue flame hissed under the rain that was finding its way through the companionway hatch.

Luis left. Peter left. They went over the side into a Zodiac the freighter had sent and the Zodiac disappeared between the swells and I watched from the cockpit until there was nothing to watch. Wen was at the helm. She didn't watch them go.

---

Why did I stay.

I've been asked this so many times that the question has a groove in me, like a line of rope against a cleat. I've given different answers. None of them are right. None of them are wrong, either.

She shouldn't be alone. That's the closest I can get. Not *she shouldn't die alone*, because I don't think I'd accepted yet that dying was what we were doing. Just: she shouldn't be alone. In this storm, on this boat that was her grandfather's boat, with the bank letters in the nav station and the repo men in Bermuda—she shouldn't be alone. Someone should be there to bring the tea she won't drink and deal the cards and say *you can't gin on the first draw* and hear her say *I just did*. Someone should keep showing up in the morning.

That's not a reason. I know it's not a reason.

I made the tea. I brought it to her. She was standing at the helm in thirty-knot gusts with rain running off the brim of her sou'wester and she took the mug and she held it and she didn't drink it and later I found it wedged in the cockpit scupper, full of salt water, and I brought it back below and washed it out and dried it with a towel that was already damp because everything was damp by then, everything, even the air in your lungs.

---

I need to tell you about the boat.

People say ships *creak*. That's the word they use. Creak. As if a forty-four-foot wooden vessel in a typhoon sounds like a screen door. The Mara Celeste didn't creak. She spoke. She had a vocabulary of sounds and I learned them the way I'd learned Wen's breathing through the bulkhead—by proximity, by repetition, by having no choice.

In calm water, she murmured. The frames whispered against the planking, a low, continuous conversation, the wood remembering what it was before it was a boat—a tree, a living thing that bent in the wind.

In moderate seas, she hummed. A resonance you could feel in the soles of your feet, coming up through the cabin sole, through the bilge, through the keel where H.L.W. had carved his initials fifty-some years ago.

On day one of the storm, she groaned. Deep, structural sounds, like a body straining. The frames flexed. The caulking wept. I could feel her working, every joint and seam absorbing and distributing the force of each wave, the way a fighter rolls with a punch. She was built for this. Her grandfather had built her for this. Old-growth white oak frames, copper-riveted, the kind of construction that doesn't exist anymore because no one has the patience or the wood.

On day two, she screamed.
