# Perdition Gulch

## I.

There are places in this country that exist the way a held breath exists—present, pressurized, and doomed by their own continuation. Perdition Gulch was one such place. I have known others.

The town sat in the lap of a valley that had been dry so long even the memory of water had gone alkaline. Dust was the medium of all commerce. It settled in the creases of deeds, in the threads of shirtsleeves, in the corners of mouths that opened to argue and found themselves, strangely, with nothing to argue about. The wells had dropped to a trickle two years prior and to a damp suggestion the year after that. Cattle died standing up, locked at the knees like furniture. The creek bed that cut through the center of town was a white scar of mineral deposit that children used as a road.

By every principle I have observed in the long anatomy of settlements, Perdition Gulch should have been a graveyard with a post office. The drought alone would have done it. But the drought was not alone. To the east, the Southern Pacific had staked its survey flags in a line so straight and so deliberate it resembled a suture—the land being stitched closed behind a needle that moved three miles a month, give or take, and never paused for breath. The railroad's men camped just beyond the town limits in canvas tents that popped and shuddered in the hot wind. They drank better water than the townspeople. They had it shipped in on a supply wagon that came Tuesdays, regular as a heartbeat, a fact that was not lost on anyone with eyes.

The town had a sheriff. His name was Oren Keef, and he wore the star the way a scarecrow wears a hat—because someone had put it on him and no one had bothered to take it off. He was decorative, and he knew it. On a Tuesday afternoon I watched him sit on the jailhouse porch for six consecutive hours, whittling a stick into a smaller stick, and when a pair of cattlemen rode in from the basin road with faces like unsheathed knives, he did not stand up. He did not need to. The cattlemen stayed an hour, bought tobacco, spoke to no one of consequence, and left again with a faint bewilderment in their bearing, as though they'd walked into a room to retrieve something and found, upon arrival, that they could not recall what it was.

This happened with some regularity. Dangerous men arrived. Dangerous men left, defused, confused, occasionally—if you watched very closely—afraid. The town moved through its days with the improbable frictionlessness of a clock whose gears have been oiled by an unseen hand. Disputes over water rights dissolved before witnesses could be called. A rancher caught stealing from his neighbor's cistern was confronted not by a mob but by a small delegation that produced facts so precise and irrefutable that the rancher simply sat down in the dirt and wept. A confidence man passing through on the stage changed his mind about the game he'd planned to run on the hotel owner, though later he could not have told you why, only that the town had made him feel *legible* in a way he did not care for.

Something held Perdition Gulch together. Not its laws, which were ordinary. Not its people, who were ordinary. Not its geography, which was hostile. Something else—patient, distributed, invisible—applied constant pressure along every seam that might have split, and the town held, the way water holds in a cupped hand, and for the same reason: because the hand does not relax.

I could have told you what the hand was. I will tell you. But first I want you to see what the hand *did*, because that is the only way to understand what its absence would later unmake.

## II.

On a Wednesday in September of 1893, the sixth year of the arrangement in its mature form, the hand performed its work across the span of a single unremarkable day, and I attended to each finger in turn.

The morning began with a land dispute.

Oscar Pruitt and Solomon Delacroix had nursed their mutual hatred for the better part of a decade over a property line that either did or did not bisect a seasonal wash—seasonal being a term of sad optimism in those years, as nothing seasonal had flowed there since the previous administration. They stood in the narrow office of the land assessor, a room that smelled of paper and rodent, and they had reached the point in their ritual where voices thicken and hands drift beltward, and the assessor was quietly calculating the distance to his back door.

Then the woman came in.

She was tall and angular, with a face that had been handsome once and was now something more useful: precise. She wore her hair pulled back so tightly it seemed to exert a corrective force on the rest of her features. Her name was Abilene Roe, and she was, in the town's cautious parlance, the *rememberer*—a term spoken as one speaks of weather, as a thing that simply is and need not be examined.

"The line was staked on the fourteenth of April, eighteen eighty-eight," she said, without greeting, without preamble, standing in the doorway with her hands folded. "Oscar, you stood on the south side. Solomon, you stood on the north. The surveyor was Peter Cahill, who was drunk, which you both knew, because you'd bought him the bottle between you the night before at Hannigan's, splitting the cost evenly—a dollar fifteen each—on the understanding that a pliable surveyor would favor each of your claims. Peter set the stake eleven feet north of the wash's true center. You, Oscar, objected. You said—and I am quoting—'That ain't right and you goddamn know it.' Solomon, you replied, 'It's right enough.' Peter Cahill then urinated against the stake, announced he was done for the day, and walked east. Neither of you moved the stake. You shook hands. Oscar, you used your left hand because your right had a blister from digging post holes that morning. The shake lasted approximately two seconds. You were both satisfied. The line was agreed."

She stopped. The silence that followed had a quality I can only describe as *surgical*. Pruitt's mouth was open. Delacroix's was closed, which was worse, because it meant he was thinking.

"I don't recall—" Pruitt began.

"No," Abilene said. "You don't." There was nothing cruel in it. She stated it the way one states the time. "But it happened as I've described, because everything happens as I describe it, and if you require I continue—if you'd like to hear what you said to your wife that evening about the arrangement, Oscar, or what you wrote in the letter to your brother in St. Louis, Solomon, which included a phrase about the surveyor I would rather not have retained but cannot discard—then I will continue. Otherwise I suggest you return to the agreed line and stop wasting Mr. Halloran's afternoon."

They left. They did not argue again—not about this. Halloran, the assessor, wiped his forehead with a handkerchief that had already been deployed past the point of utility and said, to no one, "Well."

Abilene did not acknowledge his gratitude. She had already turned and walked out into the white light of the street, and I watched her go, and I saw what I always saw in her bearing: the posture of someone carrying a weight so precisely balanced that any acknowledgment of it would cause a collapse.

She remembered everything. Not the way people claim to remember everything—with emphasis and distortion and the mercy of fading. She remembered *everything*. Every word spoken in her presence. Every creak of every board. Every shift of light. Every face, at every angle, in every gradation of emotion. Her mind was not a record. It was a *reproduction*, and it ran without pause, and it could not be edited, and it was killing her by inches, though the inches were so small and so uniformly distributed that no one saw the dying.

But I am getting ahead of the hand. Let me show you another finger.

Josiah Breen lived in a room behind the farrier's shop that he rented for six dollars a month and a standing agreement to handle any work the farrier found distasteful, which, given the farrier's robust indifference to most forms of unpleasantness, meant tasks of an extreme and specific nature. Josiah did not mind. He had the constitution for it, though *constitution* is a word that requires some expansion in his case.

On the same Wednesday, sometime after noon, the crossbeam above the farrier's main workbench—a ten-inch timber that had been drying and cracking for three years under the combined assault of heat, aridity, and the vibration of daily hammering—gave way. It fell across Josiah's back and shoulders as he bent over a hoof pick, striking him with a force that, by my calculation, should have shattered his scapula and driven bone fragments into his lung.

He stood up.

He rolled his shoulders, one and then the other, the way a man rolls his shoulders after sleeping on hard ground. The beam lay behind him in two pieces. A bruise was forming across the back of his neck, a dark, spreading thing the color of plum skin, and as I watched, it faded. Not slowly. Not the way bruises fade over days and weeks, cycling through their palette of humiliation. It faded the way ink fades in water—I could see the edges dissolving inward, the color draining, the skin repossessing itself. In forty seconds his neck was unmarked.

Josiah looked around the shop. He was alone. He picked up the hoof pick, examined its condition, found it bent, and swore—not at the beam, not at his back, not at the impossible thing his body had just done, but at the pick, because it was the last good one and now he'd have to straighten it.

He was fifty-three years old. He had been fifty-three years old, by physical appearance, for quite some time. His hair was gray at the temples in a way that had not changed since I first observed him. His hands were scarred with the fine white lines of old injuries—cuts, burns, abrasions—but they were *old* scars, all of them, souvenirs of a period before the gift had fully manifested. Nothing new stayed on Josiah Breen. His body rejected damage the way a healthy organism rejects infection: automatically, without deliberation, and with an efficiency that had made him, at various points in his life, a miracle, a monster, and a deeply tired man.

He straightened the hoof pick. He returned to his work. He did not rub his neck or pause to marvel, because marvel requires novelty, and there was nothing novel in being unkillable when you'd been unkillable for thirty years.

The boy was in the general store.

His name was Emmett Cord, and he was ten or eleven—no one was certain, least of all Emmett, who had been left at enough thresholds in his short life that the documentation of his origins had the consistency of rumor. He was small for whatever age he was, with a face that had the scrubbed, oversimplified quality of a face that has decided, early, to give away nothing. He stood in the dry goods aisle beside a woman named Clara Hutchins, who was examining a bolt of calico with the intensity of a woman who cannot afford the calico and is looking for reasons to justify the expense.

Outside, on the porch, a man in a waistcoat was speaking to Clara's husband, Frank, about a deed.

Emmett tugged Clara's sleeve.

She looked down. She did not startle. She had learned.

"He's lying about the deed," Emmett said.

He said it flatly—not with a child's delight in revelation, not with urgency, but with the same tone one might use to say *it's raining* or *the dog is loose*, a statement of atmospheric condition. His eyes were focused not on Clara but on a middle distance that had nothing to do with the dry goods aisle, as though he were listening to a second conversation beneath or within the first.

Clara folded the calico back onto the shelf. "Which part?" she said.

"The water rights aren't included. He knows. He's hoping Frank won't read past the second page. There's also—" Emmett paused. His eyes narrowed. "He thinks Frank is stupid. He's thought it three times since they started talking. He thinks—" Another pause. The boy's mouth worked around something. "Unkind things. About you, too. I won't repeat them."

Clara Hutchins smoothed her apron, squared her shoulders, and walked out to the porch. I did not hear the subsequent conversation, but I did not need to. Frank Hutchins did not sign the deed that day, and the man in the waistcoat left town on the evening stage with the compressed expression of someone who has been outmaneuvered by a party he cannot identify.

No one in the store remarked on what Emmett had done. The shopkeeper continued measuring flour. A ranch hand examining a saddle blanket did not look up. The boy's ability was known the way gravity is known—as a thing you relied upon and did not discuss, because discussion would require a vocabulary the town had silently agreed not to develop.

Emmett stood alone in the aisle for a moment after Clara left. His face did something complicated and brief—a flicker, a small contortion that might have been satisfaction or might have been something else, something closer to the expression of a dog that performs a trick and understands, obscurely, that the trick is the only reason it has not been abandoned again.

Then he put his hands in his pockets and walked out the back.

The twins were already working.

Miriam and Lorna Pace were twenty-nine years old and identical in a way that exceeded the physical. They had the same hands, the same walk, the same habit of tilting the head leftward when assessing a problem, and the same voice—not similar, *the same*, a thing that unsettled anyone who listened closely enough to notice. But their gift was not symmetry. Symmetry was merely the visible residue of something deeper: they were, in a manner I have never encountered before or since, *one mind expressed through two bodies*.

At four in the afternoon, Miriam stood at the town's communal well—such as it was, a hole in the ground that produced a grudging trickle of silted water that had to be hauled up by bucket and prayer—and spoke to Agnes Farley about the week's water schedule. Agnes was distraught. Her allotment had been cut. Her children were drinking dust. Miriam listened with a face of practiced sympathy and said, "I understand. I understand. Let me see what can be done."

At the same moment, a quarter mile east, Lorna stood at the edge of the railroad surveyors' camp and spoke to a man named Cooperton, who had the title of deputy surveyor and the demeanor of a man who had additional, untitled functions. Cooperton was explaining, with the affable menace of the professionally euphemistic, that the company's latest survey had identified the town's well as sitting on land whose title was, shall we say, *ambiguous*, and that perhaps a conversation about easements—

"Mr. Cooperton," Lorna said, and her voice carried the particular calm of someone who has already heard the end of the sentence, because she had—not through any telepathic gift, but because Miriam's conversation with Agnes had surfaced the well dispute, and the surfacing had passed between the twins as thought passes between hemispheres of a single brain: instantly, without language, without lag.

"Mr. Cooperton," she continued, "the title to the well parcel was recorded in the Yuma County office on March third, eighteen eighty-nine, under the name of the Perdition Gulch Cooperative Water Trust, which was established by communal charter with eleven founding signatories. The charter predates the railroad's filing by six weeks, which I mention only because I've noticed your company's surveys have a habit of discovering ambiguity in titles that are, in a legal sense, unambiguous. I have also noticed"—and here her head tilted left—"that your most recent shipment of water came in four barrels short, which suggests your supply chain is not as reliable as your confidence implies. I wonder if a cooperative arrangement might benefit both parties more than a dispute."

Cooperton blinked. He had the look of a man who had opened a cupboard expecting mice and found a loaded rifle.

Across town, Miriam said to Agnes, simultaneously and without any visible communication: "I've just spoken to the railroad. I believe we can negotiate an emergency supplemental allotment from their supply in exchange for temporary survey access to the north parcel—which costs us nothing, as there's nothing to survey there but scrub. Your children will have water by Friday."
