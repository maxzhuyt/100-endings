# Form 27-B: Application for Continued Existence

---

## Prologue

Here is a thing you should understand about the universe: it loves paperwork.

Not in the way you love things—not with that desperate, mammalian clutching, that warm-blooded insistence on holding something close until your hands cramp and your heart gives out. No. The universe loves paperwork the way a river loves its banks. The way gravity loves mass. Procedurally. Constitutionally. Without choice or complaint.

Every star that ignites does so pursuant to Subsection 9 of the Thermodynamic Accord. Every black hole files an environmental impact statement before consuming its first photon. The expansion of spacetime itself has been, since the very first moment, subject to an approved rate schedule that is reviewed every eleven billion years by a committee that has never, in its entire existence, voted against renewal.

You didn't know this. Hardly anyone does, at first. Most species spend their early millennia under the charming delusion that the universe runs on *physics*—on elegant equations, on forces and fields, on the cold democracy of mathematics. And physics is real enough, in its way. But physics is to the actual operating structure of the universe what the employee handbook is to the actual culture of an office: technically accurate, woefully incomplete, and missing all the important things that happen in the break room.

The actual operating structure of the universe is administrative.

There are forms. There are procedures. There are overlapping jurisdictional authorities with ambiguous mandates and insufficient staffing. There are review periods and grace periods and waiting periods that would make a glacier weep with impatience. There are, at the center of it all, the Auditors—though that is a human word for them, and like most human words for vast things, it is both entirely wrong and surprisingly close.

I am telling you this because I am about to tell you a story about one of your species' finer moments, and I need you to understand the context. The context is: everything you thought you knew about how things work is a local approximation. Reality has a back office. The back office has opening hours.

And against all probability, against all reason, against the explicit recommendations of every risk assessment ever conducted on the species classified under File #4,417,228-C—

A man from the DMV found the door.

I know how this story ends. I am telling it to you anyway, because some stories deserve to be told the way certain people deserve to be remembered: not because the remembering changes anything, but because the thing itself was so unlikely, so stubbornly beautiful, that letting it pass unrecorded would be its own small crime against the archive of what has mattered.

This is a story about forms. About waiting rooms. About a species that should have been dead and buried and forgotten seventeen years before it begins—a species that, by every metric in every database maintained by every authority that has ever catalogued the living and the gone, had been *processed.*

They kept insisting on speaking to a manager.

I liked them for that. I want you to know that. Whatever else I am—and I am quite a lot of things, some of which would give you a headache shaped like a migraine and colored in a frequency you can't perceive—I liked them. Not the way you study something interesting under glass. The way you like a neighbor who brings you terrible soup when you are sick, the kind of soup that is mostly water and salt and intention, and you eat it because the intention is the thing that nourishes.

I liked them. I am not supposed to say it, but there it is, noted for the record.

Let the record show.

---

Seventeen years before the story properly begins, the Auditors came to Earth.

They did not arrive. *Arriving* implies travel, implies a body moving through space, implies the kind of physical presence that could be photographed or shot at or reasoned with. The Auditors did none of these things. What they did was: they processed a form.

Somewhere—and "somewhere" is doing a great deal of heavy lifting in this sentence, because the place in question does not occupy space in any way that would satisfy a physicist—somewhere, a clerk-shaped absence picked up a file. Species File #4,417,228-C. *Homo sapiens.* Third planet from a G-type main-sequence star in an unremarkable arm of an unremarkable galaxy. The file had been flagged for review under Section 412(b) of the Galactic Resource Allocation Protocol: "Species Occupying Disproportionate Ecological Bandwidth Relative to Assessed Developmental Contribution."

There was a hearing. Humanity was not invited, because humanity had never registered for notification services, because humanity did not know notification services existed, because humanity was still at the stage of its development where it thought it was alone in the universe and that the biggest bureaucracy it would ever have to deal with was the Internal Revenue Service.

The hearing lasted—and again, "lasted" is a word straining under a burden it was not built to carry—the hearing lasted the equivalent of eleven minutes. The committee reviewed the file. They noted the species' developmental metrics: moderate tool use, early-stage spaceflight capability, a promising but inconsistent aptitude for mathematics, a troubling fondness for self-destruction. They noted the ecological footprint: extensive, expanding, largely unmanaged. They noted that no petition for preservation had been filed by any galactic neighbor, which typically indicated either that the species had no galactic neighbors who cared or that the species had made itself difficult to care about.

The chair of the committee stamped the form. APPROVED. In a color that human eyes could not have processed even if human eyes had been present. A color that existed specifically for the purpose of stamping approvals on forms that ended things.

And then—

Well.

I could describe it the way your storytellers might. The flash of light. The screaming. The towers falling. But that would be inaccurate. There were no flashes. No screaming. The cities of Earth did not burn or crumble or explode. Nothing so dramatic. Nothing so *physical.*

They were filed.

One moment: eight billion people, waking and sleeping, fighting and loving, standing in their own lines and filling out their own forms and being bored at their own jobs and holding their own children against their own chests and feeling the warmth of their own sun on their own unremarkable, irreplaceable skin.

The next moment: archived.

It is difficult to convey what "archived" means to a species that thinks of archives as places where old papers go. The Auditors' archive is not a place. It is a *status.* The difference between "existing" and "archived" is the difference between a song being sung and a song being *remembered*—technically the information persists, but the music has stopped.

Eight billion voices. Filed. Cross-referenced. Indexed. Stored.

There was a street in Lisbon—in the old part of the city, near the river, where the buildings listed toward each other like old friends sharing a secret. After rain, that street smelled of wet stone and coffee and the jasmine that grew wild in the cracked planter outside apartment 4-B, where a woman named Maria Beatriz Carvalho das Neves had lived for sixty-seven years. She had a way of writing the letter *g* that looked like a small, graceful bird taking flight. She did this unconsciously. She had learned it from her grandmother, who learned it from hers, four generations of women writing small birds into the margins of grocery lists and love letters.

Archived.

There was a stadium in São Paulo. Sixty thousand people, a Tuesday night, a match that didn't matter in the standings but mattered absolutely in the way that things matter when you are alive and in a crowd and the air is warm and the ball is in the air and everyone around you is inhaling at the same time, holding the same breath, and for one moment you are not sixty thousand people but one animal with sixty thousand hearts—

Archived.

A child in Reykjavik, pressing his face against a window, watching snow.

A woman in Nairobi, laughing so hard at something her sister said that she couldn't breathe, couldn't speak, could only wave her hands and gasp and feel the joy of it like a physical thing in her chest.

A man in Kyoto, standing in his garden at dawn, watching a spider repair its web, thinking about nothing except the spider and the web and the light.

All of it. Every street, every laugh, every window, every spider, every grandmother's *g.*

Filed. Stamped. Processed. Done.

The committee moved on to the next item on the agenda. There were seven hundred more species scheduled for review that cycle.

And that would have been the end of it. That would have been the quiet, administrative end of everything that had ever been human. Except.

One ship escaped.

It shouldn't have. The processing was meant to be comprehensive—the Auditors are thorough, if nothing else. But the colony vessel *Provisional* had launched from a lunar construction dock eleven hours before the processing form was stamped, on a trajectory that placed it, at the moment of archival, in a jurisdictional gap between Earth's processing radius and the outer boundary of the solar system's administrative zone. A gap of about forty kilometers. Forty kilometers of unprocessed space, a bureaucratic seam, a margin.

Three thousand people.

The Auditors noted the discrepancy. A remainder. Three thousand out of eight billion. A rounding error. The file was annotated accordingly, a standard processing notification was auto-generated and transmitted to the species' last known communication frequency, and the matter was forwarded to the reconciliation queue.

Three thousand people in a ship built for eight hundred, fleeing through the dark toward coordinates that a dying radio astronomer had transmitted in her last broadcast from the Arecibo dish, her voice breaking over the word she chose, which was *sanctuary.*

The Auditors didn't follow them.

Why would they? There was a process for remainders. There were procedures. The notification had been sent. The species would file a response, or it wouldn't, and in either case the matter would be resolved in due course.

The Auditors sat at their desks, and they waited.

They were very good at waiting.

## Act One: The Wrong Forms

Seventeen years is a long time to run from something that isn't chasing you.

Not that anyone aboard the *Provisional* knew they weren't being chased. That was, perhaps, the cruelest part—though "cruel" implies intention, and the Auditors' failure to pursue was not cruel but merely procedural. They had sent the notification. The grace period was running. There was no protocol for pursuit, because pursuit implied a relationship between the processor and the processed, and the Auditors did not have relationships. They had *caseloads.*

But the three thousand souls aboard the *Provisional* did not know this. They knew only that the Earth was gone, that they were alive, and that the dark behind them might, at any moment, swallow them the way it had swallowed everything else. So they ran. They ran for seventeen years.

And in the running, they built something.

It wasn't much. If you'd seen it from the outside—if you could have stood in the void and watched the *Provisional* tumble past, its hull scarred with patched breaches and improvised antenna arrays and one section of the outer plating that had been replaced, improbably, with material salvaged from a dining table—you might have laughed or cried or both. It looked like what it was: a ship designed to carry eight hundred researchers on a two-year mission to Mars, now carrying four times that number on a journey with no guaranteed destination and no end date.

Inside was a different story. Inside, human beings had done what human beings do when you put them in an impossible situation and leave them there long enough: they had *organized.*

Deck 7 was residential. Families of four shared compartments built for one. Privacy was negotiated in shifts—you got the curtain pulled for ninety minutes in the evening, and you did not ask what anyone did with their ninety minutes, and they did not ask you. Deck 5 was the school, run by a rotating staff of anyone with any expertise in anything, which meant that children might learn calculus from a former hedge fund manager in the morning and pottery (theoretical, as there was no clay) from a former ceramicist in the afternoon. Deck 3 was medical. Deck 9 was engineering, and the people on Deck 9 had not slept properly in seventeen years.

There was a government, of sorts. A council of seven, elected annually, though the elections had become increasingly pro forma as the pool of people willing to serve shrank. There was a set of laws, recorded in a binder that the council secretary updated by hand, because paper was sacred and the act of writing things down had become, aboard the *Provisional*, a kind of prayer.

There was even a bar. It was on Deck 6, in a repurposed storage bay, and it served a drink that was technically a fermented nutrient paste and spiritually a declaration of defiance. The bartender, a former sommelier from Bordeaux named Hélène, described it as having "notes of battery acid and hope." People waited in line for it on Friday evenings. The line itself was the point—the standing together, the complaining about the line, the being part of a Friday that someone had decided to mark as different from the other days. Because days needed to be different from each other. Because without difference there was no time, and without time there was no story, and without a story there was nothing to be.

This is what I mean when I say I liked them.

Three thousand people, hurtling through the dark in a tin can that smelled of recycled air and too many bodies and the specific, ineradicable scent of nutrient paste that permeated everything—clothing, hair, dreams. Three thousand people who had every reason to despair and instead had organized a Friday.

Captain Leda Vasquez stood at the center of this fragile world like a woman holding a roof up with her hands.

She was fifty-three. She had been thirty-six when the *Provisional* launched, a military officer assigned to the Mars mission as security detail, a role she had considered a demotion at the time and would have traded anything to go back to considering a demotion. She had assumed command on Day Three, after the original mission commander locked himself in the navigation room and had to be sedated. She had held command ever since.

Nobody voted for Vasquez. Nobody needed to. She held the ship the way a spine holds a body—not through consensus but through structure. She was the first to ration, the first to enforce, the first to make a decision nobody wanted to make and then stand in front of everyone and own it, jaw set, eyes level, daring you to offer a better option.

She was in the command center now, reviewing the distance logs on her wall chart. It was a physical chart—she insisted on analog backups for everything mission-critical—pinned to the bulkhead behind her station, a long scroll of graph paper on which she personally plotted their progress toward sanctuary. The line moved from left to right. It had been moving from left to right for seventeen years. Somewhere ahead, theoretically, was the destination. She had drawn a small star at that point in red ink. She looked at it every morning. She did not look at it the way a navigator looks at a destination. She looked at it the way a priest looks at a cross.

"Survivors in transit," she said, to no one in particular.

Lieutenant Commander Reyes, her second, looked up. "Ma'am?"

"The newsfeed on Deck 7. Someone called us 'refugees' again. We are not refugees. Refugees are fleeing *toward* something established. We are survivors in transit. There's a difference."

"I'll have it corrected," Reyes said, in the tone of someone who knew the difference did not matter and also knew that it mattered more than anything.

The chart said they were sixty-one percent of the way. Sixty-one percent of the way to coordinates that a dead woman had called sanctuary, based on signal analysis that had never been peer-reviewed, from a dish that no longer existed, on a planet that had been filed.

Vasquez did not acknowledge the possibility that sanctuary did not exist. This was not denial. It was architecture. If sanctuary did not exist, then the seventeen years were a death prolonged, and the three thousand people in her care were already ghosts who hadn't stopped moving yet, and the wall chart was a monument to nothing. This was a load-bearing belief, and she would not test it.

Six decks below the command center, in a closet that had once been an equipment locker and was now formally designated "Records and Personnel Office, HCV *Provisional*," a man named Milo Pratt was filing.

Milo Pratt was forty-one years old, slight, thinning at the temples, with the kind of face that was easy to forget even while you were looking at it. He wore glasses he didn't technically need anymore—his prescription had been corrected by the ship's optometrist years ago—but the frames were from Earth, from a LensCrafters in suburban Maryland, and wearing them was the closest he could come to going home.
