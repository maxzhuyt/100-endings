# PERDITION GULCH

## Part One: The Order and Its Cracks

**I.**

There are places in the world that refuse the logic of their own geography, and Perdition Gulch was one of them.

It sat in a crease of high desert where the Sonoran yielded to nothing—no river, no aquifer anyone could name, no reason for human habitation beyond the stubbornness that drove certain kinds of people into certain kinds of emptiness. The Apaches had bypassed this particular fold of land for centuries, not out of fear but pragmatism. The Spanish, who feared less and pragmatized worse, had established a mission here in 1762 and abandoned it within three years. Its adobe walls still stood at the eastern edge of town, soft as old bread, dissolving by fractions into the ground that had never invited them.

And yet.

By 1887, Perdition Gulch held four hundred and eleven souls. It had a post office. It had a dry goods store that extended modest credit. It had a schoolhouse that doubled as a Baptist church on Sundays and a courtroom on the rare occasions when courts were needed, which was itself remarkable—how seldom courts were needed. It had cattlemen whose cattle should not have thrived and a copper mine that produced just enough to justify its operation and a single well that had never, in living memory, gone dry. It had the Territorial Road running through it like a hem stitch, connecting Tucson to points too distant and too speculative to name. It had dust. It had heat that began in April and did not relent until November, and sometimes not then. It had a cemetery where the stones were spaced far apart and the names on them belonged mostly to the old, which in a town like this, in a territory like this, in a time like this, bordered on the miraculous.

People came to Perdition Gulch running from something. This was true of every western settlement, but here it was truer. The town attracted a particular species of fugitive—not criminals, necessarily, though it had those too, but people who had exhausted every other margin. People for whom Perdition Gulch was not a destination but an end to the act of fleeing. They arrived with little, expected less, and found, to their bewilderment, that the town held. That it worked. That whatever frail arithmetic governed life in the desert had, here, been solved by someone they couldn't quite identify.

They were not wrong about this.

There were five of them. They did not call themselves a council—that word would come later, and from the outside—but they functioned as one. They held no formal authority. They occupied no elected positions. They had simply been in Perdition Gulch long enough, and had involved themselves with sufficient consistency in the resolution of disputes, the management of crises, and the quiet maintenance of civic order, that the town deferred to them in the way that a body defers to its own circulation: automatically, without understanding, and with an unease that surfaced only when something went wrong.

Clara Dunne kept the ledgers at the dry goods store, and people consulted her on questions of history, of boundary, of precedent. She had a reputation for remembering everything—contracts agreed to at a handshake fifteen years prior, the precise terms of a grazing arrangement, the date and hour of every birth and death in the territory. People said she had a mind like a steel trap, and they meant it as praise, not understanding that a steel trap is an instrument of cruelty designed to hold a living thing in place until it dies or chews through its own leg.

Elias Cord was the closest thing Perdition Gulch had to a doctor, though he had no degree and claimed no formal training. He set bones. He stitched wounds. He had, on more than one occasion, placed his hands on a man everyone expected would die by morning and produced, by dawn, a man sitting up and asking for water. People attributed this to skill, to an intuition for the body's workings that exceeded whatever the medical colleges in the East were teaching. If his patients recovered faster than seemed natural, well, desert people were hardy. If the wounds he treated seemed to close with unusual speed, well, the dry air was good for healing. People believe what allows them to continue living without astonishment, and Elias Cord was, by unspoken agreement, merely remarkably good at what he did.

The child was called Wren. No surname that anyone could verify. Wren had appeared in Perdition Gulch eight years ago, alone, aged somewhere between four and six—the town had collectively decided on five—and had been taken in by the widow Harker, who had since died, leaving Wren in the informal custody of the community. Wren was quiet, watchful, and strange in the way that orphaned children were sometimes strange, and people forgave this. What they noticed, when they noticed anything, was that Wren seemed always to know what was needed before it was asked for. Seemed to appear at the site of a conflict moments before it ignited. Seemed to say the precise thing, in a thin and uninflected voice, that defused a situation no adult could have navigated. People called this precocious. People called this an old soul. People said, shaking their heads with a fondness that had an edge of disquiet, *that child knows things.*

They did not know the half of it.

Miriam and Josephine Lark were twins. Identical, though Miriam had broken her nose at eleven and it had set slightly off-center, granting them the single visible distinction they permitted. They ran the boardinghouse on Territorial Road, dividing the labor with a seamlessness that unsettled guests who stayed long enough to observe it. One twin could begin a sentence in the kitchen and the other could finish it on the second-floor landing. They communicated in glances that seemed to contain entire conversations, and when they disagreed—which was rare enough to be notable—the disagreement resolved itself within minutes, as if they had argued at some speed invisible to others and reached consensus before anyone else realized there had been a dispute. People found them eerie but competent. People found them, if pressed, somewhat difficult to think about for very long, in the way that it is difficult to look directly at an optical illusion without your eyes sliding to its edges.

These five. In this town. For twenty years.

It was an arrangement that should not have worked, but did—like the well that did not go dry, like the cattle that did not die, like the town itself, improbable and persisting, held together by forces its inhabitants could sense but not name.

This is the story of how it ended. It is not the first such story. It will not be the last.

**II.**

The railroad surveyor arrived on a Tuesday in September, in the company of two men who carried no surveying equipment and whose coats, despite the heat, remained buttoned high enough to conceal what everyone understood they were concealing. His name was Partridge, and he represented the Southern Pacific's interest in a spur line that would connect Perdition Gulch to the main trunk south of Tucson. He came with paperwork. He came with a language of inevitability—the railroad as a force of nature, the town's capitulation a matter of when, not whether. He was not the first man the Southern Pacific had sent. He was the fifth in three years, and he was the most confident, which meant he was the most dangerous, or thought he was.

He set up in the saloon—Perdition Gulch's sole establishment of the type, operated by a man named Flood who was himself a kind of barometer for the town's disposition—and began buying drinks for anyone who'd listen. His pitch was polished. Prosperity. Connection. The modern age arriving on steel rails. He did not mention the land acquisitions, the rights-of-way that would require the displacement of twelve families, the copper mine's output being rerouted through Southern Pacific freight at Southern Pacific rates. He did not need to mention these things. Everyone already knew. Partridge's confidence lay in his belief that knowing and resisting were entirely different acts, and that the latter required a kind of organization this sun-blasted scattering of buildings could not possibly possess.

He was wrong, but it would take him four days to understand this, and the understanding, when it came, would be incomplete and infuriating.

It began with Clara Dunne.

Partridge had presented his paperwork to Flood—contracts of intent, easement proposals, a territorial land survey that claimed to supersede any existing claims predating 1879. Clara happened to be at the bar, which was not coincidence, though it would have looked like one. She examined the documents with the placid attention of a woman reading a recipe for bread she already knew how to bake, then asked Partridge whether the Southern Pacific was aware that their territorial survey contained an error regarding the boundary of the San Jacinto land grant, which had been adjudicated in 1854 and reconfirmed by the Territorial Legislature in 1871, and which extended, she could state with certainty, a full quarter-mile further east than their map indicated, placing their proposed right-of-way on land to which the railroad had no conceivable claim.

Partridge looked at her with the expression men of his type reserved for women who spoke with specificity about legal matters. He asked her where she'd come by this information. She told him she remembered it. He asked from what document. She said she didn't require a document. She remembered the adjudication itself—the language, the surveyor's notes, the precise coordinates as established by the original Spanish land grant, which she then recited, in faultless Spanish, including the notary's clerk's marginal annotations. She said all of this in the same tone in which she might have observed that Tuesday's flour delivery had been short by two pounds.

Partridge laughed. Then he stopped laughing, because one of the older cattlemen at the bar—a man named Osborn who had been in the territory since before it was a territory—said, quietly, that he'd been present at that adjudication, and that Clara hadn't missed a word.

This was not the end of Partridge's trouble. It was the beginning.

He spent the next morning attempting to verify Clara's claims, which required a ride to the county seat that his two companions made for him while he lingered in town, feeling, for reasons he could not articulate, that it would be imprudent to leave. He was right about this, though not in the way he imagined. While he waited, one of his men—the one with the scarred knuckles and the Nevada vowels—got into a dispute with a miner at Flood's establishment. The dispute was over cards but was really over the particular kind of contempt that armed men from elsewhere feel entitled to express toward local populations, and it escalated in the way such things escalated: quickly, with the scraping of chairs and the reaching toward belts.

Wren was there.

The child had been sitting in the corner, which was not unusual—Wren frequented the saloon not for drink but for the density of minds gathered in a small space, which was, for the child, something between nourishment and compulsion, though no one in Perdition Gulch understood this. Wren stood and crossed the room and stood between the Nevada man and the miner and said, in that thin voice that seemed to arrive from a greater distance than the child's small body could account for: "You should put that away. You're thinking about your brother in Virginia City and how this will go the same way, and it won't, because the man behind you is also armed and you haven't accounted for him."

The Nevada man went very still. Not because a child had told him to—he was not a man who obeyed children—but because no one in Perdition Gulch knew about his brother, or Virginia City, or what had happened there, and no one could have known, because he had not spoken of it, not once, not ever, not even drunk.

He looked at the child. The child looked back with eyes that held no particular expression—not threat, not plea, just an attention so total it was almost surgical.

The Nevada man put the gun away.

Across town, at the same moment—though no one present could have coordinated this, and no one would later understand how the timing worked—Josephine Lark was on the second-floor landing of the boardinghouse, watching the road from the east, where Partridge's second man was returning at a gallop with what she already knew would be bad news for the surveyor. She felt the shape of her sister's thought from four blocks away, where Miriam was entering the saloon through the back door—not to intervene but to be present, to be the second point from which the same situation could be triangulated. The sisters did not think in words when they communicated this way. It was more like weather—a pressure system, a shared atmospheric knowledge that this, here, now, required their joint attention. Miriam entered the saloon and took in the aftermath of the Nevada man's deflection and the child's strange authority and understood, because Josephine understood, that the surveyor's legal avenue was already collapsing and that what remained was the question of whether his companions would escalate from legal to physical, and if so, where and when.

Josephine descended the stairs and intercepted the returning rider as he entered town. She was cheerful, proprietary, a boardinghouse owner inquiring after her guest's comfort and plans. She asked the right questions. She obtained, through the simple mechanism of being someone who seemed to expect answers, the crucial piece of information—that the county records confirmed Clara Dunne's claim, that the railroad's survey was indeed in error, and that Partridge would be unpleasant when he heard.

This information traveled from Josephine to Miriam without being spoken, without being written, without requiring any mechanism the physical world could explain. It simply became available to both of them, the way a thought becomes available to both hemispheres of a brain.

By the time Partridge received the news, Elias Cord was standing outside the saloon, not doing anything in particular—leaning against the post, rolling a cigarette with the unhurried ease of a man who knew that his physical presence, in this context, communicated something. Elias was tall, broad, weathered in a way that looked like competence rather than deterioration. He had, over the years, accumulated a reputation that was factually unearned but functionally useful: people believed he could not be hurt. This was not precisely true. What was true was that he healed from anything—cuts, breaks, fevers, a bullet he'd taken in the shoulder in '79 that had pushed out of his own flesh like a splinter by morning—and that this had been observed just often enough, by just enough people, that his presence outside the saloon, on this particular afternoon, constituted a kind of argument.

Partridge came out. He looked at Elias. Elias looked back and said nothing, because nothing needed to be said.

The railroad surveyor left Perdition Gulch on Thursday. His two companions left with him. The spur line would be proposed again, by different men, with different paperwork, but that was a problem for another season, and for now, the town's borders held.

This is how it worked. Five people operating in concert, their abilities interleaved so tightly that the result resembled not magic but civic competence of an extraordinary order. The townspeople saw a woman with an exceptional memory, a man with uncommon medical skill, a peculiar child, and a pair of well-organized twins. They saw what they could afford to see. What they could not afford—what would have rewritten every assumption they held about the world they lived in, and their place in it, and the nature of the protection they had received for twenty years without asking for it or being asked to pay—that, they did not see.

It was better that way. It had been decided, long ago, that it was better.
