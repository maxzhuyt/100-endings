# FORM 27-B: APPLICATION FOR CONTINUED EXISTENCE

## PART ONE: FILING ERRORS

### Act I — "Please Take a Number"

---

**I.**

I should tell you, before we begin, that I am not God.

This is worth establishing early because the story I am about to relate involves the destruction of a planet, the survival of a species (pending), and the sort of desperate miracle-seeking that tends, in most narratives, to imply divine oversight. There is no divine oversight. There is regular oversight. There are quarterly reviews. There is a help desk, although its hold music has been playing uninterrupted for longer than your species has had ears to be offended by it.

I am—well. The closest analogy your language provides is *clerk.* Not a very good one. Not a very bad one. One who happened to be processing a file in an adjacent queue when everything that happened to you happened, and who, against all professional good judgment, kept watching. You might think of me as the person in the next cubicle who overheard your phone call and has been quietly invested ever since. I have no authority over your case. I have no ability to intervene. I have opinions, which I will share, because no one is auditing *me.*

What I want you to understand—and I will keep returning to this, because it is the thing your species finds most difficult to grasp—is that the universe is not cruel.

It is not indifferent.

It is not meaningless.

It is *administered.*

There's a difference. Cruelty implies intention. Indifference implies a consciousness that has chosen not to engage. The universe has not chosen anything. The universe has a regulatory framework. The regulatory framework has a processing queue. The processing queue has a wait time. The wait time is, depending on your position in the queue, somewhere between three and eleven billion years, though a recent initiative has reduced average processing times by 0.003%, which someone in the Bureau of Temporal Allocations was very pleased about.

You're looking for a point. Here it is.

Every civilization that achieves interstellar capability triggers an automatic review process. This is not a test. It is not a trial. It is not a judgment rendered by vast and terrible intelligences peering down from the cosmic heights to weigh the worthiness of trembling mortals. It is an audit. The distinction matters to no one except the Auditors, but the Auditors are the ones with the stamps, so the distinction matters.

And now I must tell you what happened to Earth.

I will tell you the way it was recorded, because the recording is part of the story, because the way a thing is described is sometimes more annihilating than the thing itself.

From the Official Processing Summary, Case File SOL-3, Audit Cycle 1171-C:

*The planet designated SOL-3 was processed pursuant to Standard Audit Protocol 1171-C on the date corresponding to local calendar September 14, 2047. Upon determination that the dominant biological species (see: Attachment 14-F, "Homo sapiens, Morphological and Behavioral Profile") had achieved interstellar transit capability without prior registration, the planet and its environs were flagged for Comprehensive Resource Audit. All structures, institutions, and biological materials not filed under active exemption were archived per standard operating procedure. Processing was completed within one local rotational cycle. The Audit team noted no irregularities.*

*Three thousand two hundred fourteen (3,214) units of the dominant species were in transit at the time of processing and thus fell under Subsection 9: Items In Motion. Per Subsection 9, Items In Motion are to be held in pending status until their classification can be determined through the standard review process. A Form 9-A (Notice of Pending Status) was transmitted to the Items In Motion via standard communication protocols.*

That's it. That is the whole of it. Eight billion people—their cities, their songs, the way they burned their toast and named their dogs and fell asleep in each other's arms on Sunday mornings—reduced to a processing summary that could fit on a single page, double-spaced, with room left over for a signature block.

Archived.

The word is doing a lot of work in that sentence, and we will return to what it means, or might mean, or refuses to mean, later. For now: archived.

And those 3,214 Items In Motion?

I have watched them—with something that my operating parameters do not technically permit me to feel but that I have provisionally classified as *fondness*—for seventeen of their years.

They have spent every single one of those years trying to file a complaint.

**II.**

The *Persistence* was never meant to be an ark.

It's worth saying this plainly because there is a version of this story—the one you might be expecting, the one shaped like a myth—in which a visionary leader foresaw the apocalypse and built a great vessel and gathered the best and brightest and set out among the stars while the world burned behind them, a new beginning carrying the flame of the old.

That is not what happened.

What happened is that the Huang-Okafor Corporation, in partnership with the European Space Consortium and a private equity group based in Singapore that no one involved liked to talk about, built a generation ship designed to transport eight hundred colonists to a potentially habitable exoplanet in the Tau Ceti system. The *Persistence* was an R&D project with a marketing budget. It was a proof of concept. It was, in the most optimistic projections, a fifty-year voyage to a planet that had a 31% chance of being able to support a modified agricultural biome, which the press releases described as "a promising new chapter for humanity" and the engineers described, in private, as "well, someone's got to go first, and it probably won't explode."

The ship was six weeks out from Earth, still running system checks, still shaking out the habitation modules, when the planet was archived.

Six weeks. Close enough to see, if you happened to be looking at the right monitor at the right time. Several people were. The psychological evaluations from that period fill eleven binders in the ship's medical archive, and I have read every one of them, and I will not describe what is inside because there are things that should not be narrated from above. I will say only this: Dr. Amara Osei, the ship's chief psychologist, wrote in her personal log on the fifteenth day, *We are not processing this. We are not going to process this. The word "process" should be stricken from the language. I am going to recommend we process it anyway because I don't know what else to do.*

Three thousand two hundred fourteen. Not eight hundred. Because when the archiving happened, the *Persistence* was not the only human vessel in transit. There were orbital stations. There were lunar installations. There was a Mars survey team of eleven, including one botanist who had been there for nine months and was, at the moment the Earth vanished, in the middle of an argument with her ex-husband via satellite delay about who was keeping the cat. (The cat was on Earth. The cat was archived. The botanist does not talk about the cat.)

They came to the *Persistence* because it was the only ship with a drive that pointed *away.* The orbital stations could sustain themselves for months but not years. The lunar base had water but no shielding for the long term. The Mars team had eleven people and enough food for fourteen months. One by one, in a series of increasingly desperate rendezvous maneuvers that the ship's navigation officer later described as "physically possible, theoretically inadvisable, and absolutely not what the manual is for," they came aboard.

Eight hundred became three thousand two hundred fourteen.

The *Persistence* was designed with the elegant efficiency of a thing that expected to carry a precise number of people consuming a precise number of calories in a precise number of cubic meters of living space. It did not expect to quadruple its population in eight weeks. The corridors became living quarters. The recreation bay became a dormitory. The secondary cargo hold became a neighborhood, complete with a name (someone called it "Spillover" in the first month and the name stuck, then became affectionate, then became an identity; people from Spillover would eventually develop their own traditions, their own slang, their own fierce local pride, because that is what people do).

The hydroponics bay was, by Year 3, also a school. Also a nondenominational chapel. Also, on Tuesdays, a courtroom—because someone had to adjudicate disputes, and the hydroponics bay had the best lighting and enough bench seating for a gallery, and the judge, a retired family court magistrate from Ottawa named Honorable Patricia Chen, insisted that justice required adequate lumens.

Seventeen years.

I want you to hold that number in your mind. Not as a statistic. As a lived thing. As breakfasts and bedtimes and birthdays. As the slow accumulation of habit, of custom, of all the small sacred rituals by which people prove to themselves that they still exist.

Someone maintained a lending library. It began with whatever physical books were aboard—the colony ship's manifest had included a curated collection of five hundred titles chosen by a committee that had argued for two years about whether to include religious texts and ultimately compromised by including all of them, which satisfied no one and turned out, in the event, to be the right choice. The physical books were supplemented by e-readers, by printouts of digital archives, by handwritten copies of books remembered well enough to reconstruct. The librarian, a man named George Ferris who had been an accountant in Melbourne, ran the lending library with the iron discipline of a man for whom the Dewey Decimal System was a form of worship. You could borrow a book for two weeks. You could renew once. After that, George Ferris would come to your quarters personally, and you would give the book back? George Ferris had survived the end of the world. You were going to give the book back.

Someone else ran a restaurant. "Restaurant" is generous—it was a maintenance closet on Deck 7 that a woman named Lucia Vargas had outfitted with a salvaged heating element, two re-anodized cooking surfaces, and a menu that changed based on whatever the hydroponics bay was overproducing that cycle. The food was, by any objective measure, not good. It didn't matter. You could sit down. Someone brought it to you. There was a menu, handwritten, with items that had names. "Tuesday Surprise" was textured protein with recycled herb paste. "Lucia's Famous" was the same thing with different herb paste. It was a restaurant because Lucia said it was a restaurant, and she took reservations, and sometimes there was a wait, and people waited because waiting for a table at a restaurant was a thing that people who had a future did.

Humanity clings to routine because routine implies a future worth preparing for. This is either the species' greatest strength or its most elaborate self-deception. I have been observing civilizations for a very long time, and I have yet to determine which.

The *Persistence* was heading somewhere. That was the important thing. That was the thing that kept the committees meeting and the library lending and Lucia heating her protein paste in a closet on Deck 7.

It was heading toward coordinates that might be a sanctuary planet.

The coordinates had been extracted—painstakingly, over years, by a team of signals analysts who'd previously tracked satellite interference patterns for various terrestrial governments and were now applying those skills to intercepted Auditor transmissions—from fragments of bureaucratic communication that seemed to reference "designated habitation zones" for "pending entities." The fragments were incomplete. The translations were uncertain. The analysts freely admitted that the coordinates could represent anything from a habitable world to a waste processing facility to a filing error.

The captain treated the coordinates as confirmed. Not because she believed they were, but because belief and operational necessity sometimes occupy the same space, and the *Persistence* needed a destination the way a body needs a pulse—not because it's sufficient, but because without it, everything else stops.

**III.**

Miriam Weekes had survived the end of the world because she'd won a lottery.

Not a metaphorical one. An actual lottery. The kind with a website and a number generator and a confirmation email that went to her spam folder, where it sat for three days before she found it while looking for a coupon. The *Persistence* colony project, in addition to its crew of scientists and engineers and carefully selected specialists, had reserved forty slots for "General Population Representatives," to be chosen at random from the global applicant pool, because a geneticist on the planning committee had argued that long-term colony viability required broad genetic diversity and a sociologist had argued that a colony of pure specialists would develop pathological social structures within two generations, and also because the marketing team had determined that a public lottery would generate positive media coverage, which it did.

Miriam was fifty-four. She was from Trenton, New Jersey. She had worked for twenty-six years at the New Jersey Motor Vehicle Commission, the last eleven of them at the Trenton Regional Office on Bakers Basin Road, where she had processed an estimated 340,000 license renewals, title transfers, and registration applications. She had no special skills the colony ship's planners valued. She could not fix an engine. She could not splice a gene. She could not treat a wound beyond the application of a bandage and firm advice to see someone who could. She could not navigate by stars.

She had applied to the colony lottery on a lunch break, mostly because her friend Denise had sent her the link with a message that said, *girl can you imagine,* and Miriam had filled out the application the way she filled out everything: completely, accurately, and with her reading glasses on.

What Miriam could do—what twenty-six years at the Trenton Regional Office had refined into something that was not a skill so much as a *constitution*—was sit in a plastic chair for nine hours without losing her composure. She could read forms without her eyes glazing over. She could identify, by instinct honed through decades of observation, which line was actually moving. She understood wait times. She understood reference numbers. She understood the particular tone of voice that a system uses when it is telling you that your call is important, which is both a lie and, in its own quietly desperate way, a prayer—a prayer that you will stay on the line, that you will not hang up, that your patience will be sufficient to carry you through to the other side of whatever process you are trapped inside, because the system does not know how to save you but it knows, dimly, that saving you is somewhere in its original programming.

Miriam understood bureaucracy. Not as a frustration or an obstacle or a symptom of institutional decay. She understood it the way a sailor understands the sea: as an environment. Vast, nonsentient, capable of drowning you, but navigable. Always navigable. If you learned the currents. If you didn't panic.

Seventeen years after the end of the world, Miriam Weekes sat in her office aboard the *Persistence* and did what she had done every day for the last seventeen years. She filed paperwork.

The office was a repurposed storage closet on Deck 4, between the water reclamation junction and a panel that hummed at a frequency Miriam had long since stopped hearing. It was five feet by seven feet. It contained a desk—actually a meal tray mounted on brackets—a chair that had been salvaged from the original crew lounge and reupholstered with material from a decommissioned acceleration couch, a terminal connected to the ship's communication array, and Miriam.

Also: the walls.

The walls of Miriam's office were covered. Not decorated—*covered,* the way a war room is covered, the way a conspiracy theorist's apartment is covered in the movies, except that Miriam's walls were not held together with yarn and paranoia but with color-coded adhesive tabs and a filing system so rigorous that the ship's quartermaster, who managed the inventory of three thousand people's survival needs, had once visited, looked around, and muttered, "Christ."

These were the Auditor forms.

Seventeen years of them. Every transmission received from the Auditors—those vast, distributed, incomprehensible entities who had processed her planet and left her species homeless in the black—had been printed, translated (as best as the linguistics team could manage), cross-referenced, annotated, and mounted on the walls of this closet in chronological and categorical order. Intake forms. Acknowledgment receipts. Classification indices. Regulatory frameworks. Amendment protocols. Appendices to appendices. Footnotes to footnotes to footnotes. The Auditors communicated almost exclusively through forms, and Miriam had collected them the way other people collected stamps or grudges or reasons to give up.

She had a system. The blue tabs were incoming transmissions. The green tabs were her own filings—copies of everything she'd sent. The yellow tabs were cross-references. The red tabs were discrepancies. There were a lot of red tabs.
