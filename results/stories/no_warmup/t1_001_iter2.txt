# The Thirty-Seven Letters of Monk Hideo

### As Compiled by His Opponent

---

## Preface by the Compiler

I have been asked, in the years since I first allowed word of this collection's existence to reach beyond my household, why I chose to publish these letters. The question presumes I have chosen. Let me say instead that I have arrived, as water arrives at the sea—not by decision, but by the exhaustion of all other directions.

These letters came to me over a span of forty-one years, beginning in the autumn of the twenty-third year of Kanbun and ending this past winter. I received them. I kept them. I told no one. That these facts require explanation is itself a kind of explanation, though not the kind that satisfies.

I will offer what context is necessary and no more. Those who were present at the Enshū tournament of 1683 will need no reminder. Those who were not have likely heard some version of what occurred, though I have yet to encounter a version that is accurate. The tournament at Enshū was, at that time, the foremost invitational exhibition of classical jūjutsu in the Tōkaidō region. It drew practitioners from eleven schools. Participation was by reputation only, and the final match was considered—I do not exaggerate when I say this—a matter of cultural record. Champions carried their school's name into history or failed to.

Monk Hideo—he was not yet a monk then, merely Hideo, senior student of the Takagi-ryū—had won the exhibition three consecutive times. His technique was renowned for a quality I can only describe as *completion*. Where other fighters executed movements, Hideo arrived at outcomes. The distance between his intention and its realization was so slight that watching him fight produced in the observer a curious temporal confusion, as though the result had preceded the act. I say this not to flatter a man who is now beyond flattery but because the reader must understand the magnitude of what followed.

In the final match of his fourth exhibition, facing an opponent whose name is of no consequence, Hideo performed the formal bow. He settled into his opening stance—left foot forward, weight distributed in the Takagi fashion, hands open and low. His opponent mirrored the preparation of his own school. The referee raised his fan.

And then Hideo's eyes changed.

I must be precise, because precision is the only currency I have left. His eyes did not widen, nor narrow, nor shift focus. What changed was the quality of their attention. I have spent a lifetime studying the body's languages—the micro-adjustments of weight that betray a man's next movement, the almost imperceptible rotation of the hip that precedes a throw, the breath-Loss that signals commitment to a strike. What I saw in Hideo's eyes in that moment was *recognition*. Not of his opponent. Of something about his opponent. His gaze moved to a single point on the man's body—the left shoulder, or perhaps the junction where shoulder met neck—and then his entire bearing shifted in a way I lack adequate language to describe other than to say he seemed suddenly to be holding something he could not put down.

He straightened from his stance. He turned to the referee. He said, "I forfeit this match," in a tone appropriate to remarking on the weather. And he left.

The silence that followed was, I think, the loudest sound I have ever heard.

His opponent stood alone on the mat for what seemed an impossible duration. The referee conferred with the judges. The championship was awarded by default. The crowd dispersed in a state of bewildered agitation that would, in retrospect, never fully resolve—not in the weeks that followed, nor the months, nor the decades.

What the forfeit meant, no one could say. Hideo offered no explanation. He departed Enshū that evening and was ordained at Jōkō-ji monastery within the year. He never competed again. The theories propagated by others—that he had been bribed, that he had suffered a sudden illness, that he had experienced a religious awakening on the mat, that it was an act of protest against the tournament's organizers—I will not dignify with rebuttal except to say that none of them account for what I saw in his eyes.

His opponent—the man who received the championship—built what the world considered a distinguished career. He opened a school. He taught students who became masters. He was regarded as the man who had stood where Hideo fell. This is not untrue. It is also not the whole of the truth, but I will leave the nature of its incompleteness to the letters that follow.

The first letter arrived at my home six months after the tournament. I almost did not open it. I have no account to give of why I kept it, nor of why I kept every letter that followed, nor of why I am now, having reached the age where a man's remaining seasons can be counted on his fingers, placing them before the world.

I said at the outset that I would offer what context is necessary and no more. I have, perhaps, offered more than is necessary. But a man assembling a document like this must be permitted his unevenness. These are Hideo's letters. My annotations are included only where I judge them useful to the reader's understanding. This is not my story. If there are places where it appears to become my story, this says more about the nature of correspondence than about my intentions.

I ask the reader to proceed with patience. The letters were written slowly, over the span of a life, and they are meant to be read the same way.

## The Letters

---

### Letter One

*Received: Third month, twenty-fourth year of Kanbun (1684). Bearing the seal of Jōkō-ji monastery. Written on plain paper in a hand of notable discipline.*

---

I write to you with no expectation of reply and, I confess, with no clear expectation of my own purpose. A man who has newly entered monastic life is advised to release his attachments in an orderly fashion, as one empties a house room by room. I find instead that certain rooms resist. Their doors are stiff. One hesitates at the threshold, not from cowardice but from the suspicion that the room is larger than it appeared from the hallway.

Jōkō-ji sits in the mountains north of Mino province, above a river whose name changes three times as it descends to the valley. The monks here number fourteen. Our days follow a structure that would bore you to read and bores me to describe, though I have come to understand that boredom is merely the mind's protest at being denied the narrative it expected. When the expectation dies, the boredom becomes something else. I have not yet determined what.

I practise still—not jūjutsu, or at least not what you would recognize by the name. The abbot permits me use of the lower courtyard at dawn. I move through forms. It is like speaking a language in a country where no one understands it: the words retain their shape but lose their direction. I do not know if this is grief or progress.

You fought well at Enshū. My congratulations are late but, I hope, not so late as to be unwelcome. I watched your earlier matches from the assembly and noted several developments in your technique since the previous year's exhibition—the drop-step entry you have integrated into your hip throws is particularly refined. You commit to it with an honesty I admire. A man's technique is his signature, and yours has grown more legible.

The monastery's cook makes a buckwheat porridge each morning that I believe violates several precepts regarding attachment to suffering. I mention this because you should know that spiritual elevation is not without its costs.

I will close by saying only that certain things we carry—seen but not acted upon—weigh differently than the things we have done. I have not yet learned whether the monastic life lightens this particular burden or merely provides a silence large enough to contain it.

I do not expect a reply. I write because the alternative is not writing, and I find I am not yet accomplished enough in detachment to prefer it.

Hideo

---

**Compiler's note:** *I opened this letter at my desk in the early evening. I read it twice. I set it in the alcove where I kept correspondence requiring response, though I had no intention of responding. Three days later, I moved it to the chest where I kept private papers. It has remained in my possession for forty-one years. I will not speculate on why.*

### Letter Two

*Received: Ninth month, twenty-fourth year of Kanbun (1684).*

---

The maples along the temple path have begun their turn. I write about this not because I imagine it holds interest for you but because I have discovered that the observation of color requires a patience I formerly lacked. When I fought, I saw the world in terms of movement and structure—where a body was, where it would be, what it concealed and what it offered. Now I am learning to see what does not move. A leaf in the process of changing color is, of course, in motion—but at a pace that humiliates the eye. You must look and look and then look the following day and confess that the change occurred in the interval of your absence, as if the leaf preferred to do its work unobserved.

I trust your school prospers. I have heard—monks are not so isolated as the world imagines—that you have begun accepting students. This is correct. A man of your ability has an obligation to transmit what he knows. The alternative is to let knowledge die in the body that holds it, which is a form of avarice the warrior traditions rightly disdain.

I have been meditating on the question of transmission. When a master demonstrates a technique, what exactly passes from his body to the student's eye? The movement itself cannot be transferred—each body must generate its own. What passes, I think, is something closer to *permission*: the student sees that the movement is possible and is thereby authorized to discover it in himself. This is why the quality of a demonstration matters so much more than its technical accuracy. A perfect technique performed without presence teaches nothing. An imperfect technique performed with total commitment teaches everything.

I say this knowing that your technique, in the years I observed it, was neither imperfect nor lacking in commitment. But transmission is a subject I return to often, here in this place where I have no one to transmit to and nothing, any longer, to transmit.

The buckwheat porridge continues. I endure.

Hideo

### Letter Three

*Received: Second month, twenty-fifth year of Kanbun (1685).*

I excerpt here the relevant passage, as the bulk of this letter concerns technical matters of interest primarily to practitioners—a detailed analysis of hip rotation in throwing techniques and a spirited, if one-sided, critique of the Yagyū school's approach to weapons transfer. The letter's closing paragraph, however, I reproduce:

---

...and so the question of what one generation owes the next, in matters of martial knowledge, remains unresolved by theory. I suspect it can only be resolved by practice, and practice is, as I have said, indistinguishable from living.

I think sometimes of the debts we carry without having agreed to their terms. You have, I imagine, experienced this—the sense that something is owed, though no creditor has presented himself and no contract was signed. I do not mean financial debt, of course. I mean the obligation that arises when one man knows something about another that the other does not know about himself. Whether this constitutes a debt at all, and if so in which direction it runs, is a question I return to with the regularity of the tides and with comparable progress.

The river below the monastery froze for the first time in living memory this winter. Walking on its surface was an act of faith I was not capable of. Several younger monks crossed without hesitation. I watched from the bank and felt something I initially mistook for wisdom but have since identified as age.

Hideo

---

**Compiler's note:** *He wrote twice a year in those early years, with a consistency that suggested ritual. I noted the arrival of each letter with the same complex of sensations one feels upon hearing a distant temple bell—awareness, orientation, and the faint vertigo of being placed, without consent, in relation to something.*

*I did not write back. I considered it. Each time, I found that the act of composing a response required me to adopt a posture—toward Hideo, toward the tournament, toward myself—that I could not yet identify, much less sustain.*

### Letter Four

*Received: Eighth month, twenty-fifth year of Kanbun (1685). This letter I summarize, as it is brief and largely concerns Hideo's account of an alteration to the monastery's daily schedule. One passage warrants inclusion:*

---

...I have taken to practising in the evenings as well. The abbot watches but says nothing. I believe he understands that a man does not choose his forms of prayer.

In the lower courtyard, when the light is failing, I sometimes find myself executing techniques against an absent partner—not a visualized one, mind you, but an absence with specific dimensions. Each absent partner has his own height, his own weight, his own tendencies. Some of them are compilations. Some are precise. I do not choose which absence I train against on a given evening. The body decides. I have noticed that one particular absence recurs with a frequency that suggests unfinished business, though I could not say whose.

Hideo

---

**Compiler's note:** *That year, my school received its first invitation to demonstrate at a regional exhibition. I declined, citing a knee injury that was real but insufficient. I spent the evening of the exhibition in my study, rereading the four letters I had by then accumulated. This is, I believe, the first time I read them consecutively rather than as isolated arrivals. The experience was different in a way I could not then articulate. I mention it only because it seems relevant to the nature of what this collection became.*

### Letters Five and Six

*Received in the first and eighth months of the twenty-sixth year of Kanbun (1686). I reproduce them here in abridged form.*

Letter Five addressed the concept of *zanshin*—the sustained awareness maintained after a technique's completion. Hideo's treatment was scholarly, drawing from Yagyū Munenori's writings and from his own training at Takagi-ryū. He argued that *zanshin* was not, as commonly taught, an addendum to technique but its actual substance—that the strike or throw exists only as a vehicle for the awareness it produces, the way lightning exists to produce thunder. "What remains after completion," he wrote, "is not a residue but the thing itself. The technique is the husk. The awareness is the grain."

Letter Six turned to the relationship between repetition and understanding:

---

...A form practised ten thousand times does not merely improve. It deepens. But depth, I have learned, is not always a virtue. There are martial artists—I have known several, and you have too—whose mastery of a technique becomes so complete that the technique disappears from their consciousness entirely. They execute it as the heart beats: perfectly, automatically, and without the slightest awareness of its mechanism.

I once considered this the highest level of achievement. I am no longer certain. When an action passes below the threshold of awareness, it also passes below the threshold of examination. It becomes, in a profound sense, *invisible to the person performing it*. And what is invisible cannot be questioned, adjusted, or corrected. It can only be *seen from outside*—by a training partner, by an opponent, by a teacher whose eye has not yet been dulled by the same habituation.

This is, I think, the strongest argument for the value of fresh perspective in martial training, and it is also, I think, the reason we resist it so fiercely. To be seen clearly is to be made aware of what we have ceased to see in ourselves. This is a gift. It is also an intrusion. I confess I do not know which aspect should take precedence.

Hideo

---

**Compiler's note:** *I note, for the reader unfamiliar with the technical context, that by this period I had been teaching my own method for three years. My school's curriculum was, naturally, built upon the techniques I had refined over a career of competition and practice—techniques that were, by this point, so deeply embedded in my body's knowledge that I could not have altered them even if I had wished to. I did not wish to. I mention this to provide context, not commentary.*

### Letter Seven
