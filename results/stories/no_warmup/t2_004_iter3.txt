## ACT ONE: "THE THESIS"

---

**[COLD OPEN]**

**[Handheld footage. A kitchen — modern, sunlit, a brownstone somewhere in the Northeast. The camera operator is slightly too close. Two people sit at a farmhouse table that is clearly very old, scarred in a way that suggests it has survived not just use but transport, many times, across great distances. MARGARET CHEN (née Greenbriar) sits with her hands folded. She appears to be in her early thirties: dark auburn hair, sharp jaw, the kind of stillness that reads as either composure or suppressed violence. JAMES CHEN sits across from her. He also appears to be in his early thirties: black hair, an easy smile that he deploys like a shield, a scar across the bridge of his nose that has been there, unchanging, since 1846. Between them: two mugs of tea, a plate of untouched biscuits, and approximately one hundred and seventy-two years of marriage.]**

**JAMES:** The thing you have to understand is that she's genuinely impossible to live with. I say that with — look, I say that with enormous love.

**MARGARET:** He doesn't.

**JAMES:** I *do*. I say it with love and also with the hard-won clarity of a man who has been told, to his face, at breakfast, what he said in his sleep on a Tuesday night in 1903. Not the gist. Not the general theme. The *words*, with accompanying commentary on my *inflection.*

**MARGARET:** You said, and I'm quoting: "Mmmph. No. Harriet. Not the blue one." You were dreaming about our neighbor in Concord, Harriet Walsh, with whom you had, I should note for the camera, a highly suspicious friendship involving an excessive number of shared afternoon constitutinals—

**JAMES:** *Walks.* They were *walks.* She had a terrier with a bad hip and I—

**MARGARET:** —and I wouldn't bring this up at all, except that when I mentioned it the next morning — the following morning, not a hundred years later, the *very next morning* — you said, "I have no idea what you're talking about, I never dream." And then in 1967, in that horrible flat in Reykjavik with the blocked chimney, you said — entirely unprompted while we were arguing about whether to buy a television—

**JAMES:** Oh, *Christ.*

**MARGARET:** —you said, "I've always been a vivid dreamer, you know that about me." And I said, "On November fourteenth, 1903, you stated definitively that you never dream," and you said—

**JAMES:** I said, "Maggie, for the love of God, it's a *television.*"

**MARGARET:** You said, "A man is allowed to change."

**JAMES:** A man IS allowed to change!

**MARGARET:** Then change! You said that exact phrase — "A man is allowed to change" — in 1903, 1967, and then last Tuesday, when I pointed out that you've started putting the forks in the knife slot of the drying rack, which you *also* did in the Buenos Aires house from 1948 to 1953, and when I asked you to stop you said—

**JAMES:** "A man is allowed to change." *(He is trying very hard not to smile.)* In my defense, I have been alive for a very long time and have developed strong opinions about cutlery organization.

**MARGARET:** You haven't developed strong opinions. You've developed the *same* opinion. Repeatedly. And then denied each previous instance.

**JAMES:** Well, maybe my strong opinion is that forks are wrong and so are you.

*(Silence. Margaret's mouth does something complicated. James watches her with an expression that a casual viewer might mistake for antagonism but that the camera, at this distance, reveals to be something much more dangerous: total, unwavering attention. He is watching to see if she will laugh. He wants her to laugh. He has wanted her to laugh for a hundred and seventy-two years and he has never once stopped wanting it, and she knows this, and she knows he knows she knows, and the whole thing is unbearable and also the entire point.)*

**MARGARET:** *(a sound escapes her that is not quite a laugh but is adjacent to one — a sharp exhale through the nose, an almost imperceptible shake of the head)* Forks are wrong.

**JAMES:** Forks are *deeply* wrong.

**MARGARET:** You are the most exhausting man who has ever lived. And I mean that with the full weight of history because I *remember all of it.*

**JAMES:** *(grinning now, openly, recklessly)* See, this is what I'm saying. Impossible.

**[INTERVIEW — DR. AMELIA THORNTON. A university office, book-lined. She is in her mid-fifties: wire-rimmed glasses, gray threading through locs pulled back loosely, the particular steadiness of someone who has spent decades listening professionally. She speaks directly to the camera, which is positioned where an interviewer would sit. She has the air of someone who has rehearsed this opening mentally many times but is choosing, just now, to abandon the rehearsed version.]**

**DR. THORNTON:** I need to tell you why I'm making this film now and not ten years ago when I first began the research, or twenty years ago when I first became aware of their case, or frankly at any other point in a career that has been building toward this project whether I knew it or not.

In January of 2023, Margaret Chen walked into my office at Columbia. Unannounced. No appointment. She sat down in the chair you're sitting in now and said, "Dr. Thornton, my husband and I have been married for one hundred and seventy years, and I need someone to explain to me why I haven't strangled him in his sleep." And then she paused and added, "He wouldn't die, of course. But the gesture would be cathartic."

*(She removes her glasses. Cleans them. Puts them back on.)*

I'm a clinical psychologist with a specialty in powered-individual relationship dynamics. I've published extensively. I've consulted on policy. I've seen, in my practice, nearly every configuration of powered and unpowered partnership you can imagine. But I had never — no one has ever — encountered a case study like this.

Margaret and James Chen have been continuously married since August 7th, 1852. That is not a typographical error. They have never divorced. They have never formally separated, although they have — and Margaret will correct me if I get the count wrong, and she will, because she gets everything right, that is her entire situation — they have lived apart on fourteen distinct occasions ranging from three months to eleven years. They have always come back.

*(She leans forward.)*

This is not a love story. I want to be very clear about that from the beginning because people will try to make it one, and Margaret will be furious, and James will pretend not to care, and both of those responses will be lies, just different kinds of lies. This is also not a tragedy, although it contains more grief than anything I've encountered in my professional life.

This is a *marriage.* The longest confirmed human marriage in recorded history. And I believe it has something to tell us — all of us, powered and unpowered, mortal and otherwise — about what it means to choose another person. Not once, in a church, on a summer afternoon. But again and again and again, across centuries, in the face of every possible reason not to.

*(Pause.)*

My own marriage ended eleven years ago. Seventeen years, respectable by normal standards. My ex-husband said I was "constitutionally incapable of letting things go." He was probably right.

When Margaret Chen sat in that chair and told me she remembered every word her husband had said in a hundred and seventy years of marriage, I thought: Oh. *Oh.* I know you. I don't know you on that scale, but I *know* you.

That's why I'm making this film now. Because she asked. And because I think I understand the question.

**[ANIMATED EXPLAINER SEQUENCE — Clean, minimalist, in the style of Kurzgesagt. A calm British voiceover narrates over simple, elegant graphics.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):** In roughly four to six percent of the global population, a secondary set of genetic expressions activates during puberty, producing what are commonly referred to as "powers." This has been documented for as far back as written records exist. The Oracle at Delphi was almost certainly powered. So was, on the less glamorous end, a fourteenth-century Bruges dyer named Pieter Claes, whose sole ability was an involuntary luminescence that made his left ear glow faintly green.

Powers are not chosen. They cannot be predicted, replicated, transferred, voluntarily suppressed, or turned off. They range from the profoundly useful to the magnificently pointless, and their distribution appears to follow no genetic, geographic, or demographic logic whatsoever.

**[Graphic: A bell curve showing the range of powered abilities, from "devastating" to "inconvenient." Arrows point to various positions. Margaret's "eidetic-absolute" memory and James's "terminal-resilience" are both plotted far to the right.]**

Society has, over centuries, adapted. Legal frameworks governing powered individuals date to at least the thirteenth century and have been revised, with varying degrees of rationality, ever since. In the Victorian era — particularly relevant to our story — powered individuals were subject to a complex web of registration requirements, social stigma, and what historian David Kwan has called "the prestige problem": if your power was useful enough, you were celebrated; if it was merely strange, you were suspect.

The key fact for our purposes is simple: *powers are permanent.* If you can fly, you will never stop being able to fly. If you can taste emotions, you will taste them forever. If your memory is perfect, it will be perfect when you are twenty and it will be perfect when you are two hundred and everything you have experienced will be present and accessible at all times, every joy and every wound equally vivid, the happiest day of your life sitting right beside the worst, and you will never, not ever, be able to forget.

And if you cannot die — you just can't. That's it. That's the condition.

## ACT TWO: "THE COURTSHIP"

---

**[INTERVIEW — DR. THORNTON]**

**DR. THORNTON:** To understand the marriage, you have to understand the two people who entered it. And to understand those people, you have to understand what their powers did to them *before* they found each other.

I want to start with Margaret.

---

**[ARCHIVAL SEQUENCE. Daguerreotypes, letters displayed in careful gloved hands, journal pages in handwriting that has remained precisely identical for 190 years. A few early photographs, faded but legible. Margaret's voice — from a modern interview, but reading from her own journals — plays over the images.]**

**MARGARET (V.O.):** I was thirteen years old and I was sitting in the parlor of our house on Beacon Street and my mother was telling Mrs. Hartwell about the Lowells' dinner party, and she got the date wrong. She said it was a Thursday. It had been a Wednesday. And I knew it had been a Wednesday because I could suddenly — it was like a door opening — I could see the evening. Not remember it the way you remember things, like a smudge, an impression. I could *see* it. The candles. The fish course. The particular stain on Mr. Lowell's left cuff. My mother's laugh when she'd had too much wine — that specific laugh, not her polite one, the real one.

I corrected her. She said I was mistaken. I told her the stain was on Mr. Lowell's left cuff, shaped like Italy. She went very quiet and then she sent me to my room and I heard her crying through the floor.

That was the first day. I had every day before it, too, suddenly clear and full, but that was the day I understood what it meant.

**DR. THORNTON (V.O.):** Margaret Greenbriar was born in 1830 to a middling-prosperous Boston family. Her father, Elias Greenbriar, was a clerk in a shipping firm. Her mother, Constance, was a homemaker with social aspirations that modestly exceeded their means. Margaret was the second of four children, the eldest girl, and by all accounts — including her own, which are the most reliable accounts in existence — she was clever, willful, and what her mother called "contrary."

The power manifested fully by fourteen. Absolute eidetic memory: not merely photographic, but total sensory recall of every moment from birth onward, with no degradation, no distortion, no mercy.

**[INTERVIEW — DR. SARAH OKONJO, neuroscientist, Johns Hopkins. She speaks with careful precision.]**

**DR. OKONJO:** People misunderstand what this means. They think it's a gift. A party trick. "Oh, you remember everything, how wonderful, you must have done well in school." And yes, fine, she has perfect recall of every text she's ever read. But what people don't grasp — what's clinically significant — is the *involuntary* nature of it.

You and I, when we remember something painful, we remember a *version* of it. Softened. Compressed. The emotional charge fades. This is not a bug; it is a profoundly important feature of human cognition. It's how we survive. It's how we forgive. It's how we sleep at night.

Margaret Chen has never experienced that softening. Not once, in almost two hundred years. Every cruel thing said to her exists in her memory at full volume, full color, full emotional resolution. She can tell you what she felt when her mother slapped her at age seven — not a reconstruction, but the *actual sensation,* re-accessible at will or, more to the point, *not* at will. The memories surface. They intrude. They sit alongside the present moment with equal weight.

It is, in my professional assessment, a form of permanent, perfect, inescapable PTSD — if the trauma is simply *being alive and paying attention.*

**[Back to Margaret's journal entries.]**

**MARGARET (V.O.):** By sixteen I had stopped correcting people. By seventeen I had learned to perform forgetting — to say "Oh, was it? I don't quite recall" when I recalled everything, because people don't want to be around someone who remembers. It makes them feel watched. It makes them feel *kept.*

I wanted — I wrote this when I was nineteen, and I can see the page now, the ink blot near the margin where I pressed too hard — I wrote that I wanted to be a teacher. I wanted to go west. I wanted to see the Pacific Ocean and I wanted to find a place where nobody knew me and I could pretend to be someone who forgets.
