# The Commissar's Confession

---

## I.

The paper was the color of straw and it smelled of nothing. That was the detail that would stay with him later—that such a document should carry no scent at all. No ink-bloom, no mildew, no trace of the hand that wrote it or the room in which it was written. He received it from the clerk at twenty past seven on the morning of 14 Messidor, and the clerk's fingernails were bitten to the quick and there was a spot of egg yolk on his cuff, and these were the things a man noticed when he had trained himself to notice everything except the thing in front of him.

*Citizens condemned by judgment of the Revolutionary Tribunal, to be conveyed this day to the Place de la Révolution:*

He read.

*Laserre, Michel. Age forty-one. Printer.*

Not the printer. The printer's brother. He smelled pipe smoke, heard a stammer on the letter *d*—Michel who could not say *dignité* without tripping on it, who had once laughed at himself for this and said the word itself resisted him because he had too little of it. A back room. November rain.

*Grandmaison, Hélène. Age twenty-nine. Seamstress.*

Who was not a seamstress. The word *seamstress* in the Tribunal's lexicon meant whatever the Tribunal needed it to mean, which was: a woman of no consequence. He saw Hélène's hands, which had never held a needle that he'd witnessed, folding paper into eighths with the swift competence of someone accustomed to making things small enough to conceal.

*Duclos, Armand. Age fifty-seven. Former curé.*

An exhalation. Not from his mouth but from somewhere lower and older, the chest perhaps, the diaphragm—a place from which sound escapes not as speech but as pressure released. Duclos who had abandoned his collar in the second year but never his habit of blessing bread before he broke it, a motion of the hand so small and quick it could be mistaken for brushing away a crumb, and perhaps by now he had convinced himself that this was all it was.

*Vernet, Sophie, née Auchard. Age thirty-three. Wife of the printer Vernet, deceased.*

He stopped reading.

Or rather: his eyes stopped, and his mind continued, because the mind does not obey the same instructions as the eye. His mind said her name and then her name again and then folded the name into a drawer that did not lock, had never locked, and he felt the drawer slide open again even as his eyes resumed their descent down the list, past Jourdain (age twenty-two, student—God, twenty-two), past Masson (age sixty, no profession listed, which meant the Tribunal had not bothered to invent one), past Clément, past Roux, past the two whose names he did not know and whose faces he therefore could not yet see—

Nine.

Nine names.

He counted them again because counting was the kind of work the mind could do without breaking, and the number held: nine. He looked at the names he did not know—*Perrault, Denis* and *Barbet, Louise*—and felt nothing for them and was grateful and then was not grateful, because to feel nothing for them was already the practice of the machine, and he was not yet at the machine, not yet, the morning was still young and the light was still gray and there was still time to be a man who felt things before the day required him to stop.

Nine names. And three that were not there.

He searched the list again, though he had already memorized it, because the absence of a name was harder to confirm than its presence. You could see what was written. You could not, with the same certainty, see what was not. But they were not there: not Desforges, not the Clary woman, not Aubry. Three names that lived in a different list, the one he carried in no document and in no pocket—the one written on the soft tissue of his amygdala in an ink that could not be read by anyone who opened his skull, though men in this city had lately tried that method of reading.

They were still hidden.

Unless the list was incomplete. Unless a supplementary order was coming. Unless this document, straw-colored and scentless, was the first half of a test whose second half would be administered at the scaffold.

"Citizen Maret."

His name. Not his real name—none of them used real names anymore; reality had become a luxury—but the name sewn into his commission, the name the Republic knew, the name that sat in ledgers and on warrants and that would, presumably, appear in whatever archive survived, if any archive survived, if the Republic did not in its final convulsion eat its own records as it had eaten everything else.

"Citizen Maret, do you accept the charge?"

The clerk. Egg yolk on his cuff. Eyes like a dog's—not cruel, not kind, simply attentive to the hand that fed him, following it in case it moved toward his bowl or toward his throat.

"I accept the charge."

His voice was level. He had checked it as a carpenter checks a plumb line—by feel, by practice, by the knowledge of what true sounded like even when nothing was true. Three years of this. Three years of saying *I accept* and *it is done* and *the Republic is served*, and each time the words came out in the same plain carpenter's tone, and each time something behind the words—a small animal, soft-furred and wordless—flinched and went still.

"You are to collect the condemned from the Conciergerie at nine hours and proceed directly to the Place. The executioner has been informed. A file of guards will be at your disposal."

"Understood."

"Citizen Robespierre has expressed—"

The clerk paused here, and in the pause Maret heard the architecture of power: how a name, spoken by a man with egg on his cuff in a room that smelled of damp plaster, could fill a silence with the density of a wall. The clerk was afraid to finish his own sentence. The clerk did not know what Robespierre had expressed because Robespierre expressed things the way weather expressed things—through pressure changes felt in the bones—and whatever had come down from the Committee had been transmitted through enough intermediaries that the original expression had been distorted, compressed, made both vaguer and more menacing by each retelling.

"Citizen Robespierre has expressed his confidence that you will discharge this duty with the rigor the present moment demands."

*His confidence.*

"Please convey to the Committee that I am sensible of its trust."

This was the correct sentence. He knew the sentence the way a musician knows a passage—not by thought but by the body's memory of having performed it. His hand did not tremble. He had trained the tremor out of his left hand in the first year by holding a glass of water each morning and watching the surface until it was still. By the second year the exercise was unnecessary. By the third year the stillness was so complete he sometimes pressed his fingertips to his jugular simply to confirm that blood still moved in him, that the stillness was discipline and not death.

But this morning, as the clerk folded the warrant back into its dossier, Maret felt the fingertips of his right hand—not his left, the trained one, but his right—make a small involuntary motion against the seam of his breeches. A rubbing. The kind of motion a child makes against a blanket's edge. It lasted less than a second and no one saw it and it meant everything.

He was afraid.

Not the clean fear of discovery, which he had carried so long it had become a kind of companion, almost warm or at least familiar—but a different fear, shapeless and total, the fear of a man who has just been told to destroy the reason he has for doing anything at all.

## II.

The morning of 14 Messidor was the color of a headache. Gray sky, gray stone, gray light that seemed to come not from above but from the walls themselves, as though Paris were phosphorescent with its own decay. He walked north along the Rue Saint-Honoré and the street was already alive—or what passed for alive in the Year II. A woman selling turnips from a handcart, her voice a thin blade cutting the fog. Two national guardsmen leaning against a shuttered shopfront, their muskets held with the bored negligence of men who had learned that the Republic's enemies could be anywhere and therefore were, practically speaking, nowhere, and that vigilance sustained at sufficient duration becomes indistinguishable from sleep. A dog, ribs visible beneath fur the color of wet newsprint, nosing at something in the gutter that Maret did not look at because there were certain things in the gutters of Paris in Messidor that responded to scrutiny by becoming more specific, and specificity at this hour was a luxury he could not afford.

His boots on the cobblestones. Left, right. Left. A rhythm. The rhythm was the spine of the day and he clung to it, because as long as his body moved forward the mind was given permission to be still—or not still, not that, the mind was never still, but *contained*, kept within the corridor of purpose rather than released into the rooms on either side where the furniture was draped in white cloth and the shapes beneath were familiar and terrible.

Left, right. Left.

The fog had a taste. He had noticed this about Parisian fog in the Year II: it tasted of lye and river water and, faintly, impossibly, of copper, as though the blood that had been spilled in such quantities over these cobblestones had entered the water table and risen as vapor and now clung to the tongue of anyone who breathed. This was not rational. He knew it was not rational. The Seine carried its dead downstream to the sea and the fog was fog and the taste was the taste of his own mouth, unwashed, coffee-less—there was no coffee anymore, or rather there was, at a price measured in denunciations per ounce—and the copper was the copper of his own bitten cheek, because he had bitten the inside of his cheek without noticing, a habit from childhood that surfaced in moments of—

He passed a wall.

On the wall, in letters that had been bold red and were now the dark brown of old blood (and here the metaphor collapsed into literalism and he could not tell if the paint had faded or if the letters had been written in what they appeared to have been written in, and he did not stop to determine this, left right, left)—in these letters was written: *LIBERTÉ, ÉGALITÉ, FRATERNITÉ OU LA MORT.*

Or death.

The *ou* was the largest word. Someone had understood, consciously or not, that the hinge of the phrase was not any of the virtues but the conjunction that connected them to their alternative. *Or.* The smallest word performing the labor of the entire Revolution. Because the Revolution had always been an *or*—a fork, a blade, a binary. You were for it or against it. You were the Republic or you were its enemy. You were breathing or you were not. The middle ground, the place where a man might stand with one foot in conviction and the other in doubt—that ground had been salted years ago. Nothing grew there. He had tried.

And here, now, with the word *LIBERTÉ* fading on a wall in the fog, his mind performed its first unauthorized departure from the corridor—slipped sideways through a door he had not opened and into a room he had not entered in months, years possibly, a room where the light was different because the light was the light of 1789 and everything in 1789 was gold.

---

Not gold. But that was what memory made of it because memory was a forger and its specialty was light. The light of 1789 was ordinary sunlight falling on ordinary stone, but it fell on faces that were open in a way that no face in Paris was open anymore, and this openness—this willingness to be seen hoping—was what memory gilded and sold back to him as gold.

He had been twenty-four. A clerk in the courts, which meant he spent his days translating the misery of the poor into the language of the law, which was to say translating French into French, common suffering into Latinate abstraction, and he had been good at this because his mind was structured for it: the capacity to hold two versions of a thing simultaneously, the real and the official, was a talent that expressed itself first as legal competence and later, much later, as espionage, though at twenty-four he did not know the word *espionage* except as it appeared in adventure novels he considered beneath him.

The day—one specific day, though memory had compressed a season into a single afternoon—he stood in the Palais-Royal garden and listened to a man whose name he later learned was Desmoulins speak from atop a table, and the speech was mediocre and the rhetoric was borrowed and none of this mattered because what mattered was the crowd's face, which was one face, the face of a body discovering that it was a body and not a collection of limbs, and that face was his face too, and for the first and perhaps last time in his life Claude Maret—not yet Maret, still his birth name, a name that sat now in a sealed file in a room he had never seen—was not watching himself from the outside but was *inside*, was the thing and not its observer, was the raw material of history and not its clerk.

He believed.

This was the fact that no subsequent disillusionment could annul, only overlay: he had believed. Not in Desmoulins, who was a stammering showman, nor in any specific program or party or constitution, but in the proposition that the suffering of the ordinary—the turnip-seller, the seamstress, the clerk himself—was not a permanent feature of the world like weather but a human construction that could be dismantled by human hands. He believed this with the whole of his body. His blood believed it. The backs of his hands tingled as though suffused with some new and warmer species of circulation, and when the crowd surged he surged with it and when the crowd roared he did not roar because he was not a roaring man but he *moved his lips*, and the words were there even if the sound was not, and the words were *justice* and *equality* and *at last, at last, at last*.

At last.

Three years ago. A century.

He did not become a spy because he stopped believing. He became a spy because the others stopped believing first and did not notice. Or rather: they continued to use the words—*liberté*, *égalité*, the full catechism—but the words had been hollowed out, scraped clean of their original contents and refilled with something denser and more useful: power. He could not identify the precise moment of substitution. It was not a moment. It was a gradient, a slow replacement, the ship of Theseus rebuilt plank by plank until the vessel was unrecognizable and yet everyone on board insisted it was the same ship, the same voyage, the same destination, and if the destination now appeared to be a scaffold rather than a sunrise, well, the enemies of the people had made it so, the aristocrats, the Girondins, the Hébertistes, the—the list of enemies grew and the ship sailed on and the word *justice* meant *punishment* and the word *equality* meant *suspicion* and the word *freedom* meant *obedience* and he stood on the deck and recognized nothing except the ocean, which was blood, which had always been blood, which was the one honest thing left.

The moderates found him. Or he found them. The sequence was unclear because in those first clandestine months everything happened sideways—a phrase dropped in conversation, an emphasis that lasted half a second too long, a silence shared in a room after someone else had left, a silence that said *you too? you also see it?* These were the passwords of the second revolution, the invisible one, the revolution within the Revolution that had no army and no tribunal and no guillotine and whose only weapon was information passed from hand to hand like a note in a schoolroom, folded small, written in the lightest possible ink, and always, always at risk of interception.

He was recruited because he was placed. A clerk who had become an inspector who had become a commissar—the Republic promoted competence with the frantic enthusiasm of an organism that was consuming its own functionaries faster than it could produce them—and his position gave him access to lists, to names, to the machinery of accusation. He could see who was about to be arrested. He could sometimes intervene. Not often. Not heroically.
