# Blood Moon

The wolves begin again—three short howls, then silence, then one long mournful note that seems to unravel across the sky like thread pulled from old cloth.

Yennefer doesn't look up from the body.

The village elder lies in the mud outside his own doorway, drained so completely that his skin has achieved the particular translucence of old parchment held to candlelight. She crouches beside him, two fingers pressed to his throat out of professional habit rather than any genuine hope, and studies the twin punctures at his neck with the detached attention of someone cataloguing a rare specimen.

"Messy," she says to no one. "Whoever did this was showing off."

"Guilty."

She rises and turns in a single fluid motion, chaos energy already coiling between her fingers, violet light threading the dark like something alive.

He's leaning against the mill wall with the particular ease of a man who has never once in his existence needed to worry about what might be leaning against a wall beside him. Red coat. Ridiculous hat. Eyes that catch the blood moon's light and give nothing back.

"You've been watching me work," Yennefer says. It isn't a question.

"I've been watching you *think*." The smile beneath the hat brim is all teeth. "There's a difference. Most people just look at a corpse and feel something tediously human. Fear, grief, outrage. You looked at him like a puzzle. I found that refreshing."

"I'm delighted my intellectual process entertains you." She doesn't release the chaos. "You killed him."

"I killed all of them." He gestures broadly toward the village, and she becomes aware, with the cold precision of a woman who has survived three centuries by noticing things, that the settlement behind her is entirely silent. No dogs. No crying children. No snoring drunks. "The elder was last. He ran the furthest. I do appreciate a man who tries."

The chaos in her hands brightens.

He watches it with something that might be hunger, or might be aesthetic appreciation—the distinction, she suspects, doesn't much matter to him.

"Go ahead," he says softly. "I'm curious what you'll reach for first."

She reaches for nothing. She closes her hands and the light dies, and she watches his expression shift—just slightly, just enough—toward something more genuinely interested.

"You're not afraid," he observes.

"I'm three hundred and some years old. I've negotiated with demons who make you look like a parlor trick." She tilts her head. "I'm also not stupid enough to throw power at something that wants me to throw power at it. What are you, exactly? You're not a common vampire. Common vampires don't *talk* like that."

"Like what?"

"Like they're *bored*."

The word lands and she watches it land, watches something shift behind those terrible eyes. He pushes off from the wall and she holds her ground—holds it consciously, deliberately, because she has learned that the moment you step back from something like this, you have already lost the only negotiation that matters.

He circles her slowly. She turns with him, keeping her face toward his, and the wolves howl again—irregular, distant, as though even they are uncertain of the rhythm tonight.

"The bird of Hermes is my name," he says, almost conversational, "eating my wings to make me tame."

"Ripley's *Compound of Alchymy*." She raises an eyebrow. "Fourteenth century. You're old."

"Older than that." He stops circling. They stand perhaps four feet apart, and she can feel the wrongness of him now, the way the air near him seems slightly colder than physics requires, the way the moonlight doesn't quite sit right on the red of his coat. "You smell like lilac."

"And you smell like a grave." She meets his gaze without flinching. "What do you want? You didn't stay to be thanked."

"I want to know what you are." He says it with a directness that cuts through the theater of him, and she understands suddenly that this is the real conversation—that everything before was him deciding whether she was worth having it with. "You're not a witch. Not precisely. The energy you were holding had a different quality. Structured and wild simultaneously. Like something that learned order from the inside of chaos."

Yennefer is quiet for a moment.

"Sorceress," she says. "And you're right that it's different. We work with what's already present in the world. We don't summon. We *negotiate*."

"Negotiate," he repeats, and something in his voice suggests he finds the concept genuinely novel. "With what?"

"With everything." She gestures at the air around them. "The fabric of things. It has preferences. You learn them."

He stares at her for a long moment. The moon is very red and very full and the wolves have gone silent again.

"Show me something," he says.

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because you'd like it too much." She holds his gaze. "And because I don't perform for monsters who've just slaughtered a village to alleviate their boredom. I have some standards."

"Even out here? Alone? With me?"

"*Especially* then." She lets a beat pass. "You're looking for something that can end you. I can see it. It's the only reason you're still talking instead of killing." She watches his face. "I'm not it. But I'm also not nothing, and I think you've realized that, which is why we're still having this conversation."

The silence stretches between them like a held breath.

Then he laughs—genuine, low, surprised out of him.

"You're remarkable," he says.

"I'm aware." She turns away from him, which is either the bravest or most foolish thing she's done in decades, and begins walking toward the tree line. "Don't follow me."

He doesn't.

But she hears him behind her, still laughing softly, as the wolves begin their broken chorus again—and she doesn't look back, because some negotiations you win only by refusing to check whether you've won.
