# Gone South
## Chapter One: The City That Care Forgot

The guy who dropped me off said it like he was doing me a favour. *"This is as far as I go, kid."* Then he pulled away before I even had my backpack fully out of the truck bed, and I stood there on the sidewalk watching his taillights disappear into a tangle of one-way streets, thinking: *okay. Okay. This is fine.*

It is not fine.

It's seven-thirty in the morning and New Orleans smells like last night's beer and something sweet underneath it, like rotting flowers, like a party that forgot to end. The street I'm standing on is narrow and the buildings lean toward each other overhead, wrought iron balconies draped with ferns and Mardi Gras beads that nobody bothered to take down. It's beautiful in a way that makes me feel lonelier, somehow. Beautiful things do that sometimes.

My phone buzzes. Mom. The third time in an hour.

I watch it ring and I think about her face two nights ago — not the angry version, which I expected and had mentally rehearsed for, but the other version. The one where she went very still and quiet and said, *"Taylor, I don't know what you want me to say to that."* Like my existence was a question she hadn't studied for.

I let it go to voicemail. Battery at 12%. I can't afford the conversation.

I start walking because standing still feels like surrender.

---

By nine o'clock I've figured out a few things. French Quarter. That's where I am. Tourists exist here in a separate dimension from everyone else — they move in slow herds with frozen daiquiris, already, at nine in the morning, god — and then there are the locals who navigate around them like water around rocks. I try to move like a local even though I have no idea where I'm going.

I'm hungry. I have forty-three dollars and some change, which sounded like enough last night when I was stuffing it into my jacket pocket and climbing out my bedroom window, but now feels like a joke with no punchline.

I find a bench near a fountain in a square full of oak trees, their roots buckling the pavement like they're trying to escape too. I eat the last granola bar from my bag and think about what I actually have: forty-three dollars, a change of clothes, my dead-end phone, a journal I've been writing in since I was twelve, and a name I gave myself six months ago that nobody has used yet except me, alone, in the dark.

*Taylor.* 

Not the name on my school ID. Not the name Mom said when she went quiet. *Taylor.* I'd picked it because it didn't belong to anyone's expectations.

"You got any cash?"

I look up. There's a girl — or someone around my age, it's hard to say — sitting on the other end of the bench. I didn't hear them sit down. They're wearing a denim jacket covered in patches, hair shaved close on one side and long on the other, dark circles under sharp eyes. A backpack between their feet, beat-up and overstuffed, same as mine.

"Not really," I say. True enough.

"Same." They nod, like we've agreed on something. "You just get here?"

"That obvious?"

"You've got the look." They gesture vaguely at my face. "Like you're doing math. Everyone does the math when they first get here. *How long can I make this last.*"

I don't say anything. I'm not sure how much to give away to a stranger on a bench, even a stranger who clearly knows something I don't.

"I'm Rue," they say. "Like the herb. Or like regret. Depends on the day."

"Taylor."

"Where from?"

"Tennessee."

Rue makes a sound that isn't quite a laugh. "I did Tennessee once. Knoxville. Rough."

"Yeah." I look at them sideways. "You're not from here either?"

"Nobody's from here," Rue says. "That's the thing about this city. It collects people." They pause. "How old are you?"

There it is. The question with teeth.

"Old enough," I say.

Rue looks at me for a long moment with those sharp eyes and I feel assessed, categorized, filed. Then they nod again, like they've decided to let it go, and something in my chest loosens slightly.

"There's a place," they say, "on Frenchmen Street. They feed you if you help with setup before the lunch crowd. No questions."

"Why are you telling me that?"

Rue shrugs. Picks at a patch on their jacket — it says *STILL HERE* in block letters. "Because someone told me, once. That's how it works."

---

We walk together, which I tell myself is purely practical. Rue knows where they're going. I don't. That's all it is.

But here's what I notice, and what I can't stop noticing: the way Rue moves through this city like they own a small piece of it. Confident but watchful. They know which streets to take and which ones to avoid and they say *hey* to a woman sitting outside a laundromat who says *hey* back without looking up. There's a whole infrastructure here I can't see yet, a map drawn in a language I don't speak.

I think about home. I think about my bedroom ceiling, which I stared at for an hour before I climbed out the window, memorizing it like I knew I was leaving. I think about my little brother, who is nine and doesn't understand most things but had knocked on my door that afternoon and slid a drawing of a dinosaur under it without saying anything. The dinosaur was labelled *Taylor* in his handwriting.

I'd almost stayed.

Almost.

My phone buzzes again. I look down.

*Dad* this time.

I turn the screen face-down in my pocket and keep walking, two states from everything I know, next to a stranger named Rue who might be an answer or might be another question entirely.

The city opens up ahead of us, loud and strange and indifferent.

I am still here.
