# The Dragon's Appetite

The mangoes had been rotting for three days now, piled outside Sung's fruit stall in a blackening heap that nobody seemed inclined to move. I had come to mark my mornings by that smell — sweet and fetid together, the stench of something that had been beautiful before it turned. It clung to the back of my throat as I ducked beneath the low lintel of Fong Street and entered the quarter proper, my naval coat folded under my arm, replaced with the plain linen jacket of a merchant's clerk.

I was due aboard the *Resolute* at eight bells. It was already past nine.

"Lieutenant." 

Old Chen materialised from a doorway as though he had been poured from the shadow itself. He was perhaps sixty, perhaps eighty — his face gave nothing away except a permanent expression of mild amusement at the world's follies, which I suppose included me.

"You are late today," he said, in the careful English he had assembled over thirty years of trading with men like me. Men who needed things.

"I was delayed."

"Yes." He fell into step beside me without invitation. "Commander Ashworth was asking after you at the Factories this morning. I heard this from Peng, who heard it from the boy who carries water to the English rooms."

I stopped walking. "What did Ashworth want?"

"I cannot say what a man wants. Only that he was asking." Chen tilted his head. "You have the look today, Lieutenant. The hungry look."

"I have money." I patted my coat. This was partially true. I had what remained after yesterday, which was not enough, which was why I had risen before dawn and walked to the Portuguese quarter to find Sebastião.

Sebastião had given me a letter to deliver to a merchant in the flower district — a letter I had not opened and did not intend to open, because not opening letters was how a man maintained the useful fiction that he remained a person of basic decency. He had paid me four Spanish dollars for the service. The letter was already delivered. The four dollars were already insufficient.

"Come," said Chen. "Fong is open."

---

The den occupied the upper floor of a building that sold rope and sailcloth at street level, which struck me, in my more sardonic moments, as an apt arrangement. The stairs were steep and dark and I knew every one of them by the sound they made beneath my boots — the third creaked, the seventh had a softness that suggested rot, the ninth was solid as a promise.

Two men were already lying on the low platforms when I entered, their breathing slow and oceanic. One I recognised as a Dutch factor from Macao who had once lectured me about the moral degradation of the opium trade at a dinner in the English Factory. I did not disturb him.

Fong himself was a small, neat man who wore the same grey jacket every day and kept his accounts in a ledger with the meticulous care of a bank clerk. He looked up when I entered.

"Mr. Merrick." He never used my rank. I had asked him once whether this was deliberate. He had said he did not find ranks interesting.

"Fong." I sat across from him at the low table. "I have three dollars and forty cents."

He wrote something in his ledger. "The price is four dollars."

"It was three-fifty last week."

"Last week was last week." He looked up at me with eyes that held no particular cruelty, only the flat arithmetic of commerce. "The chests coming upriver from Whampoa are taxed heavier now. The Commissioner's men are — energetic, this month."

"I can have the remainder by evening."

"Yes," said Fong. "You can have the pipe by evening also."

I looked at my hands on the table. They had begun their trembling about an hour ago, a fine vibration like a compass needle seeking north. Fong watched them with the same detached interest he might give to weather.

"There is a thing you could do," he said, after a moment.

"Tell me."

"There is a man — English, a supercargo from one of the country ships — who owes money to a friend of mine. Not a large sum. Eighty dollars. He is staying at the Inn of the Three Bridges, near the river gate." Fong set down his pen. "My friend would like this man to understand that the debt is remembered. Only to understand. No violence — violence is complicated and my friend is a simple man."

"You want me to frighten him."

"I want you to *visit* him," Fong said, with gentle precision. "An English naval officer, in his coat, paying a social call. This is a very different thing from frightening."

The coat under my arm suddenly felt heavier. I thought about Ashworth asking after me at the Factories. I thought about the *Resolute* sitting in the Pearl River with my name in the watch-bill and my cabin empty. I thought about the seventh stair with its suggestion of rot.

"One pipe now," I said. "The visit, then the second."

Fong considered this with the gravity it apparently deserved. Then he nodded and called something through the curtain behind him.

---

I lay back on the platform and let the smoke find me, which it always did, which was the one reliable thing left in my life. The ceiling above was water-stained, mapped with dark continents I had memorised over months of study. The Dutch factor breathed beside me like a tide.

I thought about the supercargo at the Inn of the Three Bridges. I thought about what my father had said when I received my commission — *the Navy will make a man of you, James* — and what he might say now, watching his man conduct business in a rope merchant's attic while the fleet waited.

The mango smell reached me even here, sweet and black and persistent.

I closed my eyes. The dragon settled its weight across my chest, and I was grateful, and I was not yet ready to think about what that meant.
