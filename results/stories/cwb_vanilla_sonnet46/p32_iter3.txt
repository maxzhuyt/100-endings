# The Morska Conspiracy
## Part III: The Underhold

The door swung inward on silent hinges — too well-oiled, Aya noted, for a room supposedly left to rot and ruin. The smell hit her first: tallow candles, coal smoke, and beneath it something older. Brine and iron and age.

The underhold was lit.

A dozen lanterns burned in their brackets, casting the vaulted stone chamber in amber and shadow. The artifact — the *Scythan Coil*, that beautiful, terrible thing — sat untouched in its cradle at the room's far end, behind its lattice of warding-glass and iron chain. Between Aya and it stood Maren Voss, guild-mistress, decidedly unshackled, with four of her heaviest bodyguards arranged at her flanks like teeth in a jaw.

Voss was a small woman, which had always been her most dangerous quality. She wore her silver hair pinned back with a stiletto. Her coat was good wool, her boots were better leather, and in her right hand she held a flintlock revolver with the casual authority of someone who had never once missed.

"Close the door," she said pleasantly.

Simeon's pocket watch ticked. Tick. Tick. Tick.

Aya did not close the door. She did not move at all. Beside her she felt Katra go very still in the particular way that meant she was calculating distances, and Simeon's breathing had changed — shallow and fast, the sound a man made when he was doing arithmetic on his own mortality.

*She was never arrested,* Aya thought, with the cold clarity that came to her in moments of genuine peril. *The raid was theatre. She drew us out.*

"I said," Voss repeated, with the same pleasant tone, "close the door."

One of the guards moved. Not toward the door — toward *Aya*. She had time to take one step backward before the hand closed on her collar, and then she was being walked forward into the room with her heels dragging against the stone, and she heard Katra's sharp intake of breath and Simeon's muttered *bloody hell* as the remaining guards swept in behind them.

The door closed.

Tick. Tick. Tick.

The guard's hands found the edges of her mask — the thing she had worn every night for three years, the small dark mercy of anonymity — and tore it free.

The cold air of the underhold touched her face.

Voss looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind those pale eyes. Not surprise. Never surprise. Recognition, perhaps, or the satisfaction of a theory confirmed.

"Well," the guild-mistress said softly. "There it is."

*They will know by morning.* The thought arrived with the mechanical certainty of a guillotine dropping. *Father will know. The whole of the Morska quarter will know. Lady Aya Vanthorpe, of the Vanthorpe banking house, playing thief in the dark — and worse. So much worse than thief.*

Her mother's face surfaced, unbidden. The rope. The crowd.

She made herself breathe.

"Madame Voss," Aya said, and was quietly grateful that her voice came out level. "I see the evening's intelligence was somewhat selectively distributed."

"I do find," Voss replied, "that a well-placed lie separates the ambitious from the merely greedy." She tilted her head. "You are ambitious, aren't you, girl. I've watched you for some time."

"Then you'll know," Aya said, "that killing me would be a considerable waste."

Katra made a small, pained sound. Aya did not look at her.

"Is that a negotiation?" Voss sounded almost amused.

"It's an observation." Aya kept her eyes on the guild-mistress and kept her hands very still at her sides. She could feel the immateria at the edges of her perception — that cold dark sea that her mother had taught her to swim before she'd learned to read — but she did not reach for it. Not yet. Not with four guns in the room and Simeon and Katra breathing beside her. "You have the Coil. You have the guild. You've demonstrated, this evening, that you have rather more control of the situation than any of us credited you with. What you don't have," she continued carefully, "is what I represent."

Tick. Tick. Tick.

"Which is?" Voss asked.

"The Vanthorpe name. The Vanthorpe accounts." Aya let that sit for a moment in the lantern-light. "My father is not a sentimental man, but he is a practical one. He will pay a very great deal to avoid the particular scandal of his daughter's — extracurricular activities — becoming public record."

"And if I simply make those activities *public*?" Voss said. "If I walk your unmasked face through the Morska quarter at dawn and let the broadsheets do the rest?"

*Then I hang,* Aya thought. *As she did. In the same square, perhaps, if they want the poetry of it.*

"Then you have a corpse," Aya said aloud, "and a dead hostage pays no ransom. Whereas a live one, properly handled, could fund your operation for the better part of a decade." She paused. "I am also," she added, with great deliberateness, "considerably more useful alive than dead in other respects. You know what I can do, Madame Voss. You've seen it."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the deep stone above them, the city breathed and groaned, its great brass machinery ticking over in the dark.

Simeon's watch ticked.

Voss lowered the revolver by perhaps two inches. Not a concession — a consideration.

"Your friends," she said.

"Are mine to vouch for."

"That is not," Voss said gently, "what I asked."

Aya looked at Katra then — Katra, who had followed her into every bad idea for seven years, whose dark eyes were steady and furious and frightened all at once. She looked at Simeon, pale beneath his collar, one hand pressed flat against his waistcoat over the watch as though he could muffle it.

*I'm sorry,* she thought. *I'm so sorry.*

"Name your terms," Aya said.
