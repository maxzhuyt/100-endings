# Blood Moon

The cart wheel lies half-buried in mud near the village gates, one spoke snapped clean, the rest splayed like a dead man's fingers. Whatever family abandoned it left in a hurry. The village beyond is quieter still.

Yennefer notices the wheel first.

She crouches beside it, pressing two fingers into the dark earth. The mud smells wrong—copper underneath the rot, underneath the rain. She's followed this particular wrongness for three days through pine forest and river crossing, and now it deposits her here, at a gate hanging loose on one hinge, at a village that has forgotten how to make noise.

She straightens. Adjusts her black riding coat. Steps through.

---

He's already inside, of course.

She sees the red before she sees him—an impossible red, the color of something that should be bleeding but isn't. He stands in the village square with his back to her, head tilted toward the well, and even from forty paces she can feel the wrongness radiating off him like heat off a forge. Not magic. Something older than magic. Something that magic learned to be afraid of.

"You're late," he says, without turning.

"I wasn't aware we had an appointment." She keeps walking. Her boots find the cobblestones deliberately, each step measured. She will not slow down for him. "Though I suppose whatever killed these people didn't wait for me either."

He turns then, and she catalogs him the way she catalogs everything—the wide hat, the orange glass over eyes that aren't eyes, the smile that has too much history in it. Tall. Theatrical. The guns at his hips are absurd in their size, which means they are not absurd at all.

"I killed these people," he says pleasantly.

Yennefer stops. Ten paces between them.

"How forthcoming of you." She lets her gaze move across the square—doors hanging open, a child's shoe near the well, no bodies visible but the smell makes bodies unnecessary. "Vampire?"

Something shifts in that terrible smile. Interest, maybe. She files it away.

"You say it like a diagnosis," he observes.

"I say it like what it is." She looks back at him directly, violet eyes steady. "You don't smell like the others. The ones I've handled before." The others smelled like grave soil and desperation. He smells like nothing at all, which is somehow worse. "You're older."

"Considerably."

"And you killed an entire village." She tilts her head. "For what? Entertainment?"

"They were harboring something." He waves one gloved hand, dismissive. "Something tedious. I handled it. The collateral was—" the smile widens, "—thorough."

The chaos sits in her chest like a coal, and she lets it sit there. She has stood before kings who thought their power made them untouchable. She has refused to flinch at things that flinched the world. She does not reach for her magic yet because reaching would tell him she's afraid, and she is not—

She is, a little.

She doesn't let that matter.

"You're not going to run," he says. It isn't a question. His head tilts, mirroring her own gesture back at her, and she finds it irritating in a way that clarifies her thinking. "The others ran."

"I'm not sweet," she says, "and I'm not a maiden in distress in need of saving." She takes one step forward. "What I am is the person who was hired to find out what's been emptying villages along this road. I've found it." Another step. "Now I'm deciding what to do about it."

The air between them changes. She feels him *reading* her the way she reads a spell diagram—looking for the structure underneath, the load-bearing points. She lets him look. She's spent centuries building a face that shows exactly what she chooses to show and nothing else.

"You have power," he says, and his voice drops into something that isn't quite human register. "Real power. Not the parlor tricks the witches in this century manage." He takes a step of his own, and the shadows near his feet behave incorrectly. "Where does it come from?"

"Sacrifice," she says simply. "Same place all real power comes from."

That lands. She watches it land. His smile doesn't disappear but it changes shape, becomes something almost genuine, and she thinks: *there*. There is the thing underneath the theater.

"The bird of Hermes is my name," he says softly, almost to himself, "eating my wings to make me tame."

She knows the text. She's read stranger things in stranger libraries. "You're bound."

"Leashed." The word carries dimensions of feeling she doesn't have time to map. "There's a difference."

"Is there." She holds his gaze. "I've worn chains that called themselves something else too."

The silence that follows is the most honest thing that's passed between them. She watches him decide something—watches it move across that ancient, performed face—and she keeps her chaos gathered and ready without letting it show in her hands.

"You're not going to try to kill me," he says finally.

"I'm not suicidal." She lets the corner of her mouth move. "And you're not going to kill me."

"What makes you certain?"

"Because I'm the most interesting thing you've encountered in—" she considers him, "—how long?"

The smile returns, different again, layered in a way she'll be thinking about later, in some other town, in some other disaster. "You're arrogant," he says.

"Thoroughly," she agrees.

She turns and walks back toward the broken gate, toward the abandoned wheel with its dead-finger spokes, toward the road that leads away from whatever this village used to be.

"Sorceress."

She doesn't stop walking.

"Same place all real power comes from," he repeats, tasting the words. "We'll meet again."

"I know," she says.

She doesn't look back. Not because she's afraid of what she'd see—but because she knows, with the particular certainty of someone who has survived centuries of terrible things, that she'd find him smiling.

And she might smile back.
