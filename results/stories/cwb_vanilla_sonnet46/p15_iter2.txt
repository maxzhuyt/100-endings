# Therapeutic Boundaries

The table is too narrow.

That's the first thing I think when Mara tells me to lie down on it—that whoever designed physiotherapy tables had never considered what it means to be six-foot-two and built for contact, that they'd imagined some smaller, more manageable body. I climb onto it anyway, paper crinkling under my weight, and stare at the ceiling tiles while she washes her hands at the sink. The diffuser on the windowsill is pushing lavender into the room, which does nothing except make the antiseptic smell worse by contrast, like someone sprayed perfume over a wound.

"Left knee," she says, as if I don't know which one. As if I haven't spent four months being defined by it.

"Yeah."

She pulls a rolling stool to the foot of the table and sits, and I feel the warmth of her hands before they actually make contact—that half-second of proximity that the body registers before the brain does. She cups my heel in her left palm and rests her right hand flat across my shin, just below the patella, and the touch is so clinically deliberate that it almost works. Almost reads as nothing.

"I'm going to take you through passive flexion first," she says. "Tell me where the resistance starts."

I used to run routes with Mara. We used to train in the same facility, same pre-dawn hours, same brutal conditioning coach who made us do lateral ladder drills until we vomited. She was a sprinter—short distances, explosive—and I was always trying to keep up with her, which she found funny and I found motivating, which is maybe the same thing. She tore her Achilles at twenty-six, rehabbed it, and then made a decision I still don't fully understand: she stepped sideways. Got her certification. Built something new with the same hands that used to grip starting blocks.

I didn't make a decision. The decision was made for me, by a defensive lineman's knee and the particular angle of a fall.

She begins to flex my leg, slowly, bending it at the knee and drawing my heel toward my body. The movement is smooth and controlled and it doesn't hurt until it does—somewhere around seventy degrees, the scar tissue inside the joint starts pulling, a deep internal drag that isn't quite pain yet but is the announcement of it.

"There," I say.

She stops. Holds the position. "I'm going to maintain this for thirty seconds. Breathe."

I breathe. The stretch burns at the posterior capsule of the knee, that dense fibrous tissue that tightened through months of immobilization, and I focus on the ceiling tile directly above me and not on the fact that her thumb is tracing small, slow circles just below the joint line, which is a therapeutic technique called soft tissue mobilization and is not anything else.

"You're guarding," she says.

"I'm not."

"Your quad is firing. You're trying to resist the flexion with your quad."

She's right. I can feel it—the involuntary contraction, the muscle bracing against the movement as though it can protect the joint by refusing to let it move, which is exactly the wrong instinct and exactly what my body keeps doing. I force the muscle to release and the stretch deepens immediately, the burn spreading upward into the joint itself, and I make a sound I don't intend to make.

"Good," she says quietly. "That's the actual end range. Stay there."

Her thumb keeps moving. It's clinical. I know it's clinical. The pressure she's applying is breaking up adhesions in the soft tissue around the medial joint line, and the slight pain of it is productive pain, therapeutic pain, the kind that means something is being corrected. I know all of this. I have been in enough treatment rooms to understand the vocabulary of it.

It doesn't help.

"Thirty seconds," she says, and lowers my leg back to the table. She doesn't release my heel immediately. She holds it for a moment longer than necessary—or maybe exactly as long as necessary, and I'm miscounting—and then sets it down with a care that is almost unbearable.

She stands, moves to my side, and I watch her consult her notes without looking at me. Her hair is shorter than it was when we trained together. She's wearing compression leggings and a clinic polo and she looks exactly like what she is, which is a professional in a professional setting, and I resent her for that with a specific and irrational intensity.

"Roll onto your side," she says. "I want to work the IT band."

I roll. She positions herself behind me and I feel her press her forearm into the outer thigh, just above the knee, using her body weight to apply sustained pressure into the iliotibial band—a thick rope of connective tissue running from the hip to the shin that has been screaming since the surgery. The pressure is deep and uncomfortable in the way that only bodywork can be, a pain that sits right at the edge of what's tolerable, and she leans into it incrementally, reading my body's response.

"Talk to me," she says.

"About what."

"About the pain level. One to ten."

"Six," I say. Then: "Seven."

She holds it. The pressure doesn't ease. "You used to push through sevens like they were nothing."

"That was different."

"Was it?"

The lavender from the diffuser drifts over me and I close my eyes and feel her forearm working slowly up the band, millimeter by millimeter, and I think about what it means that she gets to ask me that question. That she gets to stand behind me in a room that smells like flowers and antiseptic and be the one who decides how much is enough.

"It was different," I say again, "because it was going somewhere."

She pauses. The pressure lifts slightly—not fully, just enough.

"So is this," she says.

She leans in again. I breathe. The ceiling tile has a water stain in the upper left corner, shaped like nothing I can name.
