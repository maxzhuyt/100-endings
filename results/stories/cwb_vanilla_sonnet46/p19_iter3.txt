# The Dragon's Meridian

The morning gun from *Andromache* woke me before I was ready to be woken.

I lay still on the narrow cot in my rented room above Fong's tea house, listening to the shot echo off the Pearl River and dissolve into the noise of the waking city — the creak of sampans, the particular hollow percussion of wooden pails, a woman somewhere below arguing in Cantonese with the velocity of someone who had been arguing since before sleep. My mouth tasted of old pennies. I turned my head and coughed into the pillow until my ribs ached, and when I pulled the linen away there was a small dark stain that I folded inward before I rose.

My uniform coat hung on the back of the door. I put it on without looking at myself in the glass.

---

Fong was sweeping the lower room when I descended, moving dust from one province of the floor to another with a broom that had lost half its bristles. He was a small man of perhaps sixty, with a face arranged in permanent mild disappointment, like a magistrate who had long since stopped expecting the truth.

"You sleep late," he said, without looking up.

"I slept adequately."

"You cough in the night. My wife hears it."

"Sea air," I said. "Plays havoc with the chest. I've had it since the Azores."

He swept. "Your friend came this morning. The Portuguese one."

"Da Silva?"

"He waited one hour. He says you owe him for last Tuesday."

I felt the familiar arithmetic begin in my chest — not quite panic, something more chronic than that. "Tell him I'll have it by Thursday."

Fong finally looked at me. His expression hadn't changed precisely, but something behind it had shifted, the way light shifts in a room when a cloud moves over the sun. "I will tell him," he said. "You want tea?"

"No. Thank you, Fong."

Outside, the Thirteen Factories district was already thick with the day's commerce. I walked with my hands clasped behind my back in the posture I had learned gave the impression of a man going somewhere purposeful, a man with dispatches, with authority. A pair of Company clerks passed me near the American hong and touched their hats. I returned the gesture and kept walking.

---

I found Achmed at his usual station near the river steps, crouched beside a basket of lychees he had no serious intention of selling. He was a Malay of indeterminate age who had arrived in Canton on a country ship three years prior and simply never departed, accumulating instead a network of small usefulness that made him invaluable to men like me.

"Lieutenant," he said. He didn't stand.

"I need to see Chen tonight. Can you arrange it?"

He selected a lychee and turned it in his fingers, examining it for flaws. "Chen is careful now. There was trouble last week, you heard? A Company man, they say he talked to the Hoppo's people."

"I'm not a Company man."

"No." He bit into the fruit. "But you wear the coat."

I looked down at my uniform as though I'd forgotten I was wearing it. The brass buttons caught the morning glare off the river. "Tell him it's Merrick. Tell him I need the usual quantity and I'll bring coin this time, not paper."

Achmed chewed thoughtfully. "There is something else Chen wants, maybe. A favour."

"What kind of favour?"

"A letter. Official paper, naval stamp. Something that says a certain cargo passed inspection without inspection."

The river traffic moved behind him — a string of flower boats heading toward Whampoa, a salt junk low in the water, a pair of small boys sculling a raft of river grass. Ordinary life, proceeding without me.

"I'll need to know the cargo," I said.

"You won't want to know the cargo."

I coughed then — a long, rattling series that bent me forward and drew looks from two Lascar seamen passing on the steps. I pressed my fist to my mouth and waited for it to subside. When I straightened, Achmed was watching me with the particular expression of a man calculating the remaining useful life of a tool.

"Sea air," I said.

He said nothing.

"Tell Chen yes," I said. "Tonight. The usual place."

---

I spent the middle hours of the day doing what I was supposed to do, or a reasonable approximation of it. I attended briefly at the factory offices and signed three documents that Hargreaves, the flag secretary, pushed toward me without explanation. I walked the length of the anchorage and noted in my log that all vessels were present and correctly moored, which was true. I spoke for eleven minutes with Captain Elliot's aide, a young man named Forsythe who had the enthusiastic vacancy of someone recently arrived from England, for whom Canton was still a spectacle rather than a condition.

"Remarkable place, isn't it, Merrick?" he said, gazing out at the river with his hands on his hips. "Quite unlike anything."

"Quite," I said.

"You've been here, what, three years now?"

"Four."

"And you're still finding it remarkable, I imagine."

I looked at the river, at the brown water moving south toward the sea, carrying silt and refuse and the reflections of ten thousand lanterns not yet lit. Somewhere in the city behind us, in a room I would reach by a route I had memorised in the dark, there was a pipe and a lamp and approximately four hours of something that was not peace but was at least the absence of the alternative.

"Every day," I said.

Forsythe smiled and clapped me on the shoulder. "That's the spirit."

The afternoon gun sounded. My cough came again, brief this time, almost polite. I covered it with my hand and tasted copper and salt and something underneath those things that I had stopped trying to name.

I turned from the river and walked back into the city.
