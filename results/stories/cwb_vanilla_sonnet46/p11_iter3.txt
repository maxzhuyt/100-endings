# Overthinking It

---

The periodic table on the wall has 118 elements and I have memorized all of them and I still cannot figure out how to say *hi* to Marcus Webb without my voice doing that thing where it goes up at the end like a question mark that wasn't supposed to be there.

Hi?

Hi.

*Hi.*

I've been running the variations since Tuesday.

He's sitting across the lab table from me right now, two feet and four inches away — I measured with my eyes, which I've trained to be fairly accurate — copying something from his textbook into his notebook with this very particular focused expression where his left eyebrow goes slightly higher than his right. I've noticed this before. I've noticed this approximately eleven times. I am not going to think about the fact that I've noticed this eleven times.

I draw a small sketch in the corner of my lab notes. A boy with one eyebrow higher than the other. Tiny. Barely there. I shade it with the side of my pencil and then I put a circle around it and then I think about whether anyone could look at that circle and understand that the circle means *this is a separate thing from the science notes, this is just a doodle, this means nothing,* and I decide that circles are ambiguous enough. I'm safe.

Priya is on my left. I draw her next — quick, easy, the little bun she always wears, two loops for her glasses. Priya is my actual best friend and I can talk to her without rehearsing and right now I am pointedly not looking at her because she knows about Marcus and she has this expression she makes, this tiny suppressed smile, and if she makes that expression right now I will have to move to a different country.

Devon is on Marcus's left. Devon is easy to draw because he has extremely round cheeks. I give him round cheeks and I feel a little guilty and then I draw his cheeks slightly less round and I feel better. Devon has already said four things today and none of them were embarrassing and I keep thinking about that like it's a superpower. *Four things. Unprompted. Just said them.*

"So for the hypothesis," Marcus says, and I look up, and he's looking at me, and I feel my face do approximately seventeen things in the span of one second.

"Yeah," I say.

*Yeah.* That's what I said. *Yeah.* After eleven variations of *hi* and three days of rehearsed hypothesis discussions, I said *yeah* like I was agreeing that yes, there is indeed a hypothesis, yes, science contains hypotheses, good point.

"Do you want to write it, or should I?" he asks.

Okay. Okay. This is a normal question. This is a question I prepared for, actually, because I imagined this exact scenario on Wednesday night while I was supposed to be sleeping. In my Wednesday night version I said something like *I can write it if you want to talk through it first,* which is a perfect sentence because it is collaborative and it shows I'm engaged but not controlling and it opens a conversation without demanding one.

"I can write it," I say. "If you want to talk through it first."

He smiles. Not a big smile. Just the normal small version, the one where one corner goes up a little more than the other. I have also noticed this before. I am not going to count how many times.

"Yeah, that works," he says.

I write *Hypothesis:* at the top of a clean line and then I hold my pencil and I wait and I think about how I am holding my pencil. I'm holding it normally. I think. I adjust my grip slightly and then I think about whether adjusting my grip looked weird, whether it looked like I was *thinking* about how I was holding my pencil, which would be a strange thing to visibly think about, and now I've been holding my pencil above the paper for probably four seconds without writing anything.

I write a small wave in the margin. Just a line. It doesn't represent anyone. It represents the general sensation of drowning.

"So if we increase the concentration," Marcus starts, and he's leaning forward a little, and I can tell he's actually interested in this, which is one of the things — I'm not going to list the things. There are too many things and listing them is not useful right now.

I write down what he says. My handwriting is very neat under pressure. This is something I've noticed about myself. When I'm nervous my handwriting gets smaller and more precise, like my body is trying to take up less space in every possible way simultaneously.

Priya passes me a note. I open it under the table.

*You're doing great,* it says, with a tiny star.

I fold it back up. I look at her. She's already looking at her textbook, very studiously, the way you look at a textbook when you are actively trying not to make a suppressed smile. I love her. I will tell her that later when Marcus is not two feet and four inches away.

"Does that make sense?" Marcus asks.

He's asking me. He's asking if the hypothesis makes sense to me, which means he wants to know what I think, which means my opinion has been requested, which means this is a moment where speaking is not only acceptable but actually required.

"Yeah," I say. "I mean — yeah, I think so. It's a good hypothesis."

*It's a good hypothesis.* He didn't ask if it was good. He asked if it made sense. I have introduced an unsolicited compliment about his hypothesis and now I need to either die or recover and I don't know how to do the second one.

"Thanks," he says, like it was a normal thing to say.

Maybe it was a normal thing to say.

I draw a very small sun in the margin, right next to the wave. I don't shade it. I leave it open.
