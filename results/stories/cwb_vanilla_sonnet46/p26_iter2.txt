# The Weight of Fabric

The briefing documents had described Tessavar as a colony in *administrative tension*—that careful bureaucratic phrase which meant, Maren had learned, that people were angry and someone was afraid. What the documents had not described, could not describe, was the smell of the lower markets at the fourth hour after dawn: salt-brine and something sweetly ferrous, the breath of a world that had been breathing without them for three hundred years.

She had left the delegation compound before the others woke.

This was, she understood perfectly well, a violation of protocol. She understood it the way one understands a word in a language not one's own—correctly, technically, without the emotional weight the native speaker carries. The protocol existed to protect envoys from *incidents*. Maren had decided, somewhere between the orbital descent and the compound's sealed corridors, that she was more afraid of arriving at a negotiation having seen nothing true.

The cloak was a mistake. She had worn it against the morning chill, which was real enough—Tessavar's axial tilt gave its equatorial settlements a cold dawn—but the Tessavari did not appear to believe in cloaks. They wore close-fitted garments that left their arms bare to the shoulder, and in some cases more than that, and they were built for this: the Coastal Tessavari, the *Selhari*, had a subcutaneous fat distribution that read, to Maren's eye, as simply different, a body solving a problem her own lineage had never faced. The Highland Tessavari, the *Drevanu*, were taller and more angular, with the particular stillness of people whose ancestors had learned that stillness conserved heat.

Everyone looked at her cloak.

Not rudely. Not even with hostility, though the briefings had warned of hostility. They looked the way one looks at something that raises a genuine question.

A child—Selhari, she thought, by the breadth of the shoulders even at that age—pointed and said something to its parent. The parent responded without looking down, eyes still on Maren, and the tone was not unkind. Explanatory. *That is what they wear, where they come from.* Or perhaps: *That is what cold looks like, when you have never learned to carry it inside you.*

She walked for two hours. She bought something from a market stall that turned out to be a fermented root pressed into a cake, and ate it standing, and the vendor watched her with what she chose to interpret as professional interest. The market was not segregated—Selhari and Drevanu moved through it together—but they clustered, and the clustering had a grammar she could not yet read. There were stalls where only one type gathered. There were intersections where they passed through each other like weather systems, without stopping, without speaking.

The official position was that Tessavar's *subspeciation*, as the documents termed it, was a social construction amplified by political actors. Maren had studied enough history to know that this was the kind of true statement that explained nothing.

---

Commander Velsh was waiting in her quarters.

He was a compact man who had learned to make rooms feel smaller than they were. He did not raise his voice. He said: "You were observed leaving the compound perimeter at 0340 hours."

"Yes," Maren said. She removed the cloak and folded it over a chair. The room was warm. "I was."

"The Minister of Science has requested a preliminary meeting. Given your—" he paused in a way that was itself a kind of speech "—initiative, Command has decided you should conduct it alone."

She recognized the punishment dressed as opportunity. She accepted it as both.

---

Minister Orvaine was a Drevanu woman of perhaps sixty years, which on Tessavar, with its slightly elevated radiation environment and the dietary customs of the Highland lineage, might mean something different than sixty years meant elsewhere. She was tall even seated. Her office contained no decorative objects, only documents, and this told Maren something about what Orvaine wanted visitors to think of her.

"You walked the Selhari market this morning," Orvaine said, without greeting. "The vendor at the third row, fermented *kassi*—she is an informant for the Coastal Governance Council. You should know that."

"Thank you," Maren said. "Should I know that you know that?"

A pause. Orvaine's eyes were the particular pale grey-green of Highland genetics, and they were, Maren thought, genuinely interested. "Sit."

Maren sat.

"Your briefing documents," Orvaine said, "will have told you that I am a political appointment. That I am not a scientist. That my function here is—" she turned a hand over, a gesture of elegant dismissal "—decorative. Managerial."

"They implied it."

"And what do you make of that?"

"I make of it," Maren said carefully, "that you have held this posting through three changes of administration, two significant incidents of civil disruption, and one renegotiated trade compact. Which suggests that either you are very good at decoration, or the briefing documents have a limited understanding of what science policy actually requires."

Orvaine said nothing for a moment. Outside, the morning was fully arrived, and through the high narrow window the light of Tessavar's sun—slightly cooler than Sol, slightly more generous in certain wavelengths—fell across the documents on the desk and made them look like artifacts.

"The Offworld Commission," Orvaine said at last, "wants us to adopt a unified census taxonomy. One species. Administrative simplification." She said *administrative simplification* the way one says the name of an illness. "Tell me what you think that means to a Selhari child who has never been cold a day in her life, and a Drevanu elder who has never once been warm."

Maren looked at the light on the documents. She thought about the child in the market, pointing at her cloak. The parent's explanatory tone.

"I think," she said slowly, "that it means something different to each of them. And I think you know exactly what it means to both."

Orvaine smiled. It was not a warm smile, but it was, Maren thought, a real one.

"Good," the Minister said. "Then we can begin."
