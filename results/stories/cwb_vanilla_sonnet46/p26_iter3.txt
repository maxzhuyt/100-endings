# Offworlder

## Chapter Seven: The Unscheduled Hours

The briefing documents had described Heven's Third District as "commercially active but socially stratified, with interlineage tensions requiring careful navigation." What they had not described—what perhaps no document could—was the smell of it: fermented grain and hot iron and something floral that had no name in any catalog Sera Voss had studied.

She had left the consulate compound at the fourth hour, when the duty officer changed shifts and the gap between one bureaucrat's attention and another's opened like a door left ajar.

---

The market occupied six city blocks in a configuration that suggested it had grown rather than been planned. Stalls pressed against one another with the comfortable intimacy of old argument. Sera moved through them slowly, keeping her hands visible, her pace unhurried—the posture of someone with nowhere particular to be, which was, for the first time in eleven months, true.

The fabrics came first.

They hung from iron frames and wooden poles and, in one ambitious stall, from the branches of a tree that had been permitted to grow up through the market's roof. The colors were wrong in a way that took her a moment to identify. Not wrong—*other*. The dye sources on Heven had diverged from Earth's palette over two centuries of isolation, and what she was seeing were pigments extracted from organisms that had no terrestrial analog: a blue so deep it seemed to contain its own gravity, derived, she would later learn, from the ink-sac of a burrowing creature called a *veleth*; a yellow-green that seemed to pulse at its edges, pressed from the bark of the *sorrow tree*, so named not for sentiment but for the sound its wood made in wind.

The woman tending the stall was Shoreline lineage—Sera recognized the broader orbital ridges, the adaptive skin pigmentation that ran toward deep ochre in response to Heven's UV spectrum, the slightly increased lung capacity visible in the width of the chest. She was perhaps sixty years old and she watched Sera with the patient assessment of someone who had watched many people look at her fabrics.

"Offworld?" the woman said.

"Is it obvious?"

"You looked at the blue first. Everyone from offworld looks at the blue first. They've never seen it."

Sera bought a small square of the blue fabric—more than she should have spent from her personal allowance, less than it was worth—and the woman folded it in paper that crinkled like dried leaves.

---

Further in, the market changed character. This was Inland lineage territory, marked not by any sign but by the shift in faces, in the particular way conversations paused when she passed. The Inland lineage had adapted to Heven's interior plateau over five generations: taller, with eyes that had shifted toward a gray-hazel to manage the different light frequencies of the high altitudes, with a social structure that the briefing documents called "hierarchical" but which, watching the market operate, Sera would have called *ceremonial*. Every transaction involved a small formal exchange before the commerce began—a phrase, a gesture, a moment of acknowledged recognition that said: *I see you as a person before I see you as a buyer.*

She tried it, imperfectly, at a food stall. The vendor—young, patient with her fumbling—corrected her gesture without condescension and then sold her something wrapped in a flat bread that tasted of smoke and something almost sweet.

She ate it standing, watching the market move around her.

The tensions the briefing documents described were real. She could feel them in the way Shoreline and Inland vendors arranged themselves, in the careful geography of which stalls neighbored which, in the small negotiations of space that happened constantly and mostly without words. But tension was not the same as violence, and proximity was not the same as hatred. What she was watching was something more like two rivers running parallel, aware of each other, occasionally turbulent at the borders, but moving in the same direction.

The documents had not said that.

---

The duty officer who met her at the consulate gate said nothing. The silence was its own communication.

Commander Yael Ostrova was waiting in the situation room, which was either a coincidence or a statement. Sera suspected the latter.

"You were unscheduled and unaccompanied for four hours and seventeen minutes," Ostrova said. She did not look up from the display she was studying.

"Yes."

"Protocol exists for reasons that are not arbitrary."

"I know."

"Do you." Ostrova set down the display. She was a compact woman from the Martian administrative class, with the particular stillness of someone who had learned to conserve energy in environments that demanded it. "You will meet with Minister Davan Cosse tomorrow morning. He has requested a preliminary consultation regarding the lineage census proposal."

"The census is the central point of contention."

"Which is why you will meet with him, and why you will do so without first taking unauthorized walking tours of the districts whose political representation the census would restructure." A pause. "The blue fabric was an unnecessary expenditure."

Sera said nothing. There was nothing useful to say.

---

Minister Cosse received her in a room full of windows and very little furniture—a power arrangement, she recognized, designed to leave visitors nowhere to settle, nowhere to feel at home. He was Inland lineage, tall, with the gray-hazel eyes and the formal bearing of someone who had made ceremony into armor.

"You walked the Third District yesterday," he said, before she had offered a greeting.

"I did."

"Without escort. Without notification." He let this sit between them. "What did you find?"

She understood, then, that this was not a reprimand. It was a test. He had known she would go. He had perhaps expected it. The question was whether she had seen anything, or only looked.

"I found," she said carefully, "that the briefing documents were written by someone who had not eaten in the market."

Something shifted in his face. Not quite a smile—something more considered than a smile.

"Sit down, Envoy Voss," he said. "We have a great deal to discuss."
