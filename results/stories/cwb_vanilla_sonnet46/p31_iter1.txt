# Gone South
## Chapter One: The City That Care Forgot

The saxophone finds me before I find it.

It's coming from somewhere down the block, this low, aching sound that curls through the morning heat like smoke. I stop walking and just *listen* for a second, because it's the most beautiful thing I've heard in maybe my entire life, and also because my feet hurt, and also because I genuinely do not know where I am going.

New Orleans, technically. That's where I am. Beyond that, I got nothing.

---

The guy who drove me here — some long-haul trucker named Dale who smelled like beef jerky and had a Jesus fish on his dashboard and asked me approximately forty-seven questions about my parents — dropped me on the corner of Canal and something with a "Good luck, kid" that felt less like encouragement and more like a warning. I watched his rig disappear into traffic and felt the city close around me like a mouth.

That was two hours ago. Maybe three. I lost track.

I've been walking since then, because walking feels like doing something, even when you're doing nothing. The French Quarter is waking up slow, like a person with a serious hangover. Delivery trucks double-parked on narrow streets. A woman in a bathrobe sweeping a stoop. Pigeons. Wrought iron balconies dripping with last night's Mardi Gras beads even though it's not Mardi Gras, it's just apparently *always* like this here.

I kind of love it. I hate that I kind of love it.

I'm supposed to be devastated right now. I *am* devastated. But there's something about being somewhere nobody knows your name, nobody knows what your mom said or what your dad's face did — that silence in his face was the worst part, worse than words — that makes you feel weirdly, guiltily *alive*.

The saxophone gets louder as I turn down a side street, and then I see him: older Black man, maybe sixty, sitting on an overturned milk crate with his eyes closed, playing something that doesn't have a name but should. A handful of tourists photograph him without stopping. A little kid drops a dollar in his case. He doesn't open his eyes for any of it.

I sit down on the curb across from him and eat the last granola bar from my backpack.

Forty-three dollars and some change. That's what I have. That's the entire financial situation.

*Okay, Taylor. Think.* 

I'm thinking. I'm thinking that 48 hours ago I was sitting at our kitchen table telling my family something true about myself for the first time in sixteen years, and my mom had started crying in a way that wasn't sad-crying, it was *angry*-crying, which is a specific kind of crying I know very well, and my little brother Jordan had gone completely still the way he does, and my dad had just — left the room. Just stood up and walked out. Like I'd said something boring. Like I'd asked about the weather.

I'm thinking that I don't regret it. The telling. I think.

I'm thinking that I have forty-three dollars.

---

"You got somewhere to be?"

I look up. There's a girl standing over me, maybe my age, maybe a little older. She's got dark circles under her eyes and a septum ring and a backpack that looks as beat up as mine. Her hair is half-shaved on one side, grown out on the other, like she started a project and abandoned it. She's looking at me the way I probably look at math tests: like she's trying to figure out if I'm a problem she can solve.

"No," I say.

"You look lost."

"I'm not lost. I know exactly where I am." I gesture vaguely at the street. "I'm *here*."

She almost smiles. Almost. "First day?"

I don't answer, which is an answer.

She drops down beside me on the curb without being invited, which I note. The saxophone shifts into something slower, something that sounds like 3am.

"I'm Reese," she says.

"Taylor."

"You run or you get kicked out?"

The question is so blunt it takes me a second. "Run," I say. Then: "Both, kind of. It's complicated."

"It's always complicated." She pulls a water bottle from her bag and drinks, doesn't offer me any, then seems to think better of it and holds it out. I take it. "How long you been on the street?"

"I'm not on the street," I say, too fast.

Reese looks at me sideways. She has very dark eyes, the kind that are doing a lot of thinking behind them, and I get the sudden uncomfortable feeling that she can see the granola bar wrapper in my hand and the forty-three dollars in my pocket and the whole entire disaster of the last 48 hours written somewhere on my face.

"Okay," she says, easy. Not pushing.

We sit there for a minute. The musician finishes his song and starts another one, something with more urgency to it now, something that sounds like a question.

"There's a shelter," Reese says, casual, looking straight ahead. "On Tulane. They don't ask much. It's not — " she pauses, choosing words carefully, "— it's not perfect. But it's something."

"I'm fine," I say.

"I know you are."

She says it like she means it, which is strange, because she doesn't know me. And something about that — the simplicity of it, someone just *saying* a thing like that without needing anything back — makes my throat do something embarrassing.

I look away. Study the cobblestones. Count the pigeons.

"How long have *you* been here?" I ask.

Reese is quiet for a beat too long.

"A while," she says.

The saxophone plays on. The city hums around us, indifferent and gorgeous and enormous. Somewhere, my phone is full of messages I haven't opened.

I'm here. I'm *here*.

Whatever that means.
