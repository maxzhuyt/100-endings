# The Underhold

The door swung inward without resistance.

That alone should have been warning enough.

Aya stepped through first, one hand braced against the frame, the other pressed flat to the hidden seam of her coat where the summoning chalk sat wrapped in oilskin against her ribs. The underhold breathed cold air at them — the particular cold of deep stone, of places that had not known daylight in living memory. Tallow candles burned in iron cressets along the walls, dozens of them, far too many for a room that was supposed to be empty. Their light moved like something nervous.

*Too many candles,* she thought. *Someone expected to see clearly.*

Katra came in behind her, close enough that Aya could feel the tension radiating off her like heat from a forge. Simeon brought up the rear and pulled the door shut with a soft, final click that seemed, in the silence, enormously loud.

The underhold was a vaulted chamber carved from the bedrock beneath the harbour district, its ceiling lost in shadow, its walls sweating with the cold moisture that seeped through from the tidal channels beyond. Chains hung from iron rings. Old ones. The floor was dark stone worn smooth by decades of foot traffic and, in the centre, set into a plinth of pale scythan marble that had no business being this far north, sat the artifact.

It was not unguarded.

Six men stood in a loose crescent before the plinth, each one carrying the particular stillness of men paid to be patient. They were not guild muscle — not the rough-edged dockworkers and failed soldiers Aya had grown accustomed to navigating. These men wore matching coats. Their weapons were good. One of them held a clockwork lantern that ticked softly in the silence, its beam cutting a precise line across the stone floor like an accusation.

And behind them, seated in a chair that had been carried down here with the deliberate theatricality of someone who had planned this at considerable leisure, was Maret Voss.

Guildmaster Voss was a woman of perhaps fifty, with the sort of face that had been handsome once and had since become something more useful — angular, watchful, every line of it carved by calculation. Her silver hair was pinned with the same practical severity Aya had always observed from a careful distance. She wore no mask. She had never worn a mask.

She was supposed to be in a cell in the Morska civic gaol.

She was, demonstrably, not.

"I had wondered," Voss said, without rising, "whether you would come tonight or wait until morning." Her voice was the same as Aya had always known it — measured, unhurried, the voice of a woman who had never once in her life been surprised by anything she hadn't arranged herself. "I confess I had wagered on morning. You are either bolder than I credited, or less patient."

Beside Aya, Simeon made a sound very low in his throat. Katra had gone absolutely still in the way she did when she was calculating exits.

*There are no exits,* Aya thought. *She's had hours. She's closed them all.*

"Guildmaster." Aya kept her voice level. The mask was still in place — the plain dark leather she had worn every night for eighteen months, the one small mercy remaining to her. "I see the civic gaol's hospitality left something to be desired."

"The civic gaol," Voss said, "serves its function. As does a false arrest, when one wishes to draw out the ambitious." She tilted her head, studying Aya with the unhurried attention of a naturalist examining a specimen. "I have been watching your particular ambition for some time, little shadow. You move well. You summon better." The word landed in the room like a stone dropped in still water. "That is not a guild skill."

The man with the clockwork lantern stepped forward.

It happened quickly, and then it was over. Aya's arm was wrenched behind her back with professional efficiency, and before she could reach the chalk, before she could think the first syllable of anything useful, the mask was pulled from her face.

The cold air of the underhold hit her skin.

The candlelight was very thorough.

She heard Katra draw a sharp breath. She heard Simeon say, very quietly, *"Christ."*

Voss rose from her chair then, slowly, and crossed the floor toward Aya with her hands clasped behind her back. She stopped close enough that Aya could see the precise colour of her eyes — grey, the grey of harbour water in winter — and examined her face with an expression that was not quite satisfaction and not quite surprise and was somehow worse than either.

"Aya Venmoor," Voss said. "Lord Venmoor's daughter."

The name fell on the room like a sentence.

*There it is,* Aya thought, and felt something cold and absolute settle in her chest. *There is the end of everything.*

"Well." Voss turned away, unhurried, returning to her chair as though the matter were already concluded. "This does complicate the evening."

"It complicates yours considerably more than mine," Aya said.

Voss glanced back at her.

"You want to kill us." Aya kept her chin level, kept her voice from shaking through an act of will that cost her considerably. "That's tidy. That's what I'd do. But I am Lord Venmoor's only living heir, and my father is a man who asks questions until he gets answers, and he has the ear of three magistrates and the Harbour Comptroller." She paused. "A dead thief in your underhold is an inconvenience. A dead *Venmoor* is an inquiry."

From somewhere beyond the walls, out in the dark water of the harbour, a foghorn sounded. Once. Twice. A third time, long and low and mournful, the sound of ships navigating by sound alone through waters too black to read.

Voss was quiet for a moment.

"You're proposing," she said at last, "that I find you useful."

"I'm proposing," Aya said, "that you already do."
