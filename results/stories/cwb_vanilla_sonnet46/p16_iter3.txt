# Hell is Other Demons

The morning briefing ran long, as it always did on Tuesdays.

Lucifer sat at the head of a conference table carved from a single piece of petrified despair—a gift from the Third Circle, who had excellent taste and terrible timing—and listened to Mammon explain, for the fourteenth consecutive week, why the Greed Department was technically *over* budget.

"The irony," Lucifer said, "is not lost on me."

Mammon shuffled his papers. He had seventeen fingers on his left hand and used all of them nervously.

The rest of the morning was administrative. There was a dispute between two mid-level torment coordinators over territorial jurisdiction in the Glutton Flats. There was a memo about the sulfur smell—*not* the good sulfur smell, someone had written in the margins, *the other one*—emanating from Corridor Nine. There was, as always, the matter of the waiting list, which had grown again overnight by a number Lucifer found both impressive and faintly depressing.

He initialed things. He stamped things. He condemned a demon named Frix to three centuries of filing for gross incompetence, which Frix seemed to feel was unfair, which suggested Frix had not yet grasped the fundamental operating philosophy of the institution.

By midday, Lucifer had developed a headache behind his left eye that no amount of divine origin could quite explain away.

---

Lilith arrived at half past two, which meant she had not planned to arrive at all and had ended up here anyway, which was the only way she ever arrived anymore.

He heard the heels first. Then the smell of something burning that was not brimstone—something earthier, like autumn leaves and bad decisions. Then she was simply there, the way weather is simply there, occupying the doorway of his private office with her scorched leather purse hanging from one shoulder and an expression that communicated, with extraordinary efficiency, that she was not here because she wanted to be.

"You changed the door," she said.

"I change the door every century. You know where the office is."

"I know where the office is because I *haunt* this place involuntarily, not because I memorized your renovation schedule." She came inside anyway. She always came inside. "You've got Mammon's fingerprints all over this budget again."

"Mammon has seventeen fingers. It's statistically inevitable."

She sat in the chair across from his desk—the good chair, the one she had always taken, the one no one else sat in because something in the room's architecture made other visitors instinctively choose the worse option. She dropped her purse on his desk with a sound like a small rockslide.

The purse was ancient and terrible and smelled of a hundred mortal cities. A receipt from a petrol station in Aberdeen stuck out of the front pocket. There was a sound from inside it, very faint, like something small and ceramic rolling against something small and metallic.

"What's in there now?" he asked, despite himself.

"A snow globe from Reykjavik. A bus token from somewhere that doesn't have buses anymore. Three fortune cookie fortunes I disagreed with." She paused. "A jawbone."

"Human?"

"Does it matter?"

It didn't, particularly. He looked at her instead, which was the more dangerous activity. She had done something different with her hair, or possibly the universe around her hair, and she was wearing the red she wore when she was angry about something she hadn't decided to be angry about yet.

"You're not here to kill anyone," he said.

"I haven't ruled it out."

"But it's not the *primary* purpose of the visit."

She said nothing, which was its own kind of answer. She picked up the snow globe from the purse—he hadn't seen her reach for it—and turned it over in her hands. The snow inside was not white. He chose not to ask.

"The Reykjavik one," she said. "There's a bakery there. On a street that floods every March. They make this bread—" She stopped. Put the snow globe down. "It's nothing."

"You went to Iceland."

"I go lots of places."

"You went to Iceland and you found a bakery and you thought about—"

"I didn't think about anything."

The office was very quiet for a moment, in the particular way that places are quiet when they are actually extremely loud on a frequency just below conversation.

"I've been," he said carefully, "finding the administrative work somewhat—"

"Don't."

"I wasn't going to say anything sentimental."

"You were constructing the scaffolding for something sentimental. I could see the load-bearing beams going up." She looked at him with those eyes that had looked at him since before the concept of *looking* had been fully worked out. "You're lonely."

"I run Hell."

"Hell is full of people. That's rather the point of it." She stood, which meant the visit was ending, which meant nothing had been resolved, which was the natural state of things between them. She picked up her purse. Something inside it shifted with a sound like small bones. "The bread was good," she said, not looking at him. "That's all I meant."

"Lilith."

"Tuesday," she said.

"What?"

"I'm usually not doing anything Tuesday." She said it the way one might announce a minor change in weather. Informational. Non-committal. Geologically significant. "If the door's the same."

"I'll keep the door."

She left without saying goodbye, because she never said goodbye, because goodbye implied an ending and they had never quite managed one of those.

Lucifer sat for a long time after, in the quiet that was not quiet.

Then he picked up the snow globe she'd left behind—deliberately, he knew, she never forgot anything—and turned it over. The dark flakes settled slowly around a tiny church.

He put it on the windowsill, where he would see it.

He had a lot of Tuesdays.
