# The Last Outpost — Chapter One: Dreads Askew

The thing about asteroid belt evenings is that they don't really exist. No sun to set, no sky to bruise purple and orange. But we invented our own version here at the Outpost — Davo dims the overhead panels to about sixty percent around the nineteenth hour, and something in the human and near-human brain just *accepts* it. Evening. Here we go.

I'm pulling a second pour for Mick Oradeen, a silicate miner with hands like geology, when the stranger comes in.

I notice the dreads first. They're the kind that move on their own — bioluminescent tentacle-locs, probably gene-modded, probably expensive once upon a time — and right now they're doing that anxious writhing thing, coiling and recoiling against each other like they can't agree on a direction. The stranger is humanoid, slight, wearing a jacket that's seen three too many pressure-seal failures. They navigate the crowd without looking at it, which takes a particular kind of practice, and they find the far end of the bar the way a stone finds the bottom of a well.

Quiet. Fast. Inevitable.

"Vega whisky, neat." A pause. The dreads knot briefly around each other. "Better make that a double."

I already have the bottle out. That's not intuition — that's just bartending.

"Vega's a good choice," I say, setting the glass down and pouring two fingers, then two more. "Means you've either had a very good day or a very bad one."

"Does anyone order Vega whisky after a good day?"

"Honestly? No."

I leave the bottle nearby, which is either generosity or professional pessimism, and I start wiping the section of bar between us. It's already clean. It's always already clean. But my hands need something to do when my ears are working this hard.

The stranger drinks. Not desperately — carefully, like the whisky is information they're processing. The dreads settle by maybe ten percent.

Around us, the Outpost does its evening thing. Mick Oradeen has found someone to argue with about silicate yields. The Pellucid Sisters — three merchant women who share a ship and apparently a single collective opinion about everything — are debating something in their layered harmonic language over by the viewport. Farther back, in the booth I privately call the Confessional, two people who are definitely not supposed to be meeting are definitely meeting.

Normal. All of it beautifully, boringly normal.

"You work here long?" the stranger asks.

"Long enough that I stopped counting," I say. "Nets."

"Sorry?"

"My name. Nets."

The dreads do something that might be a nod. "Sable."

"Just passing through, Sable, or are you docking a while?"

Another careful sip. "That depends."

I wipe the same spot. "On?"

"On whether a thing I'm looking for is actually here." Sable sets the glass down and looks at me directly for the first time. Their eyes are interesting — irises with that fractured amber quality that usually means Kepler-heritage, three or four generations back. "I was told the Outpost keeps records. Transaction logs. Cargo manifests going back, what — fifteen years?"

"Davo keeps records," I say. "Davo keeps *everything*. It's either his greatest virtue or his most alarming quality, depending on the day."

"Can I talk to Davo?"

"You're talking to me first." I say it pleasantly, but I say it. Sable seems to understand the structure of this, because they don't push.

"Fair," they say.

I pour them a small, unsolicited top-up, which is my version of *keep going*.

"I'm looking for a cargo shipment," Sable says. "Registered under a shell company — Auric Meridian Holdings. It would have come through here about eight years ago, maybe nine. Biological material, listed on the manifest as agricultural samples." They say *agricultural samples* the way you'd say *that's not blood, it's paint*. "I have reason to believe the listing was fraudulent."

"Biological material gets flagged," I say carefully. "Customs runs a secondary scan on anything organic."

"I know." Sable's jaw tightens. The dreads do a slow, unhappy spiral. "Which means either customs cleared it legitimately, or customs was—" They stop.

"Helped along," I offer.

"Yes."

I keep wiping. The spot is practically a philosophical concept at this point, a meditation on cleanliness. I'm thinking about eight years ago, which is two years before I arrived here, which should mean this has nothing to do with me, which is the kind of logic that has historically preceded things having everything to do with me.

"What kind of biological material?" I ask.

Sable looks at their glass. "People," they say quietly. "Children, specifically. Eleven of them. They were mine to protect, and someone moved them through a legitimate cargo hub in a fraudulent shipment, and I have spent—" Their voice does something complicated and then rights itself. "I have spent a long time following a very cold trail to this particular rock."

The Outpost hums around us. Mick Oradeen laughs his gravel laugh. The Pellucid Sisters harmonize briefly in disagreement.

"I need those records," Sable says. "And I need to know if anyone here remembers that shipment. Someone always remembers something."

They're right about that. That's the thing about a place like this — it's a hub, which means it's a memory. Eight years of miners and merchants and people passing through, and the Outpost absorbed all of it, the way stone absorbs heat.

The trouble is, I know who keeps the oldest memories here. The ones that predate the official logs.

And I know why asking him is going to be complicated.

I set down my rag. Pick it up again.

"Davo," I say finally, "closes his records office at twenty-two hundred."

Sable glances at the clock panel above the bottles. "That's twenty minutes."

"Then you should probably finish that drink," I say, "and I should probably figure out how to explain to my boss why I'm bringing a stranger into his records office after hours." I pause. "He doesn't like strangers."

Sable looks at me. "But you're going to help me."

I pick up the rag again.

"I haven't decided yet," I say, which is a lie, and probably both of us know it.
