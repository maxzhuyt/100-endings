# Gone South
## Chapter One: The City That Care Forgot

The strap slides off my shoulder again.

I hitch it back up and keep walking, like I know where I'm going, like I have a single solitary plan beyond *not Tennessee*. The air here is a living thing — thick and green-smelling and hot in a way that's completely different from home. Home hot is dry and mean. This hot is *wet*. It wraps around you like it's trying to tell you something.

It's eight in the morning and New Orleans is already awake and already strange.

Marcus, the truck driver who'd picked me up outside of Memphis, had been decent enough. Didn't ask too many questions. Dropped me at a gas station on the edge of the French Quarter with a twenty-dollar bill and something that was almost an apology in his eyes. "End of my route, kid," was all he said. I'd watched his taillights disappear and felt the specific terror of having actually done the thing you planned to do.

The strap slips again.

*Fix the damn strap, Taylor.*

I stop outside a bakery. Through the window I can see a woman arranging pastries in a glass case, moving them with the focused tenderness of someone handling small animals. My stomach makes a sound like a dying engine. I haven't eaten since a gas station hot dog somewhere in Mississippi, which felt disgusting going down and feels worse in retrospect.

I have forty-three dollars. The twenty from Marcus, plus twenty-three of my own. I do the math the way you do when you're trying not to panic: forty-three dollars, no phone service because Mom pays that bill and I am *not* calling Mom, no ID that reflects anything true about me, and a backpack containing: two changes of clothes, a journal, a phone charger, my sketchbook, and a photograph I almost left behind but didn't.

I didn't leave it behind.

I keep walking.

---

By ten o'clock I've established several facts about the French Quarter. One: it smells like beer even when it's not serving beer yet. Two: the buildings are genuinely beautiful in a crumbling, complicated way, all iron lacework and peeling paint, like they're gorgeous and they know it and they're tired of trying. Three: I fit here approximately zero percent.

Everyone seems to know the rhythm of this place and I don't know the first beat.

I find a bench in a small square — Jackson Square, a sign says — and sit down hard. A man nearby is playing saxophone, something slow and liquid, and pigeons are doing their stupid pigeon business around his feet. I pull out my sketchbook because it's the one thing I know how to do when my brain gets too loud.

The too-loud thing it keeps doing is replaying Dad's face.

Not the anger. The anger I'd prepared for, sort of. It was the *confusion* that broke something in me — that specific look of a person encountering a word they don't have a definition for. Like I'd walked into the kitchen and announced I was a different species.

*I don't understand what you're telling me*, he'd said.

*I know*, I'd said. *I know you don't.*

I'm drawing the bakery woman from memory — the careful way she moved — when a voice says, "That's pretty good."

I look up.

The person standing there is maybe my age, maybe a year older, with close-cropped hair and a nose ring and the particular quality of stillness that belongs to people who've learned to take up as little space as possible. They're wearing a flannel shirt in this heat, sleeves down, which is a choice that registers somewhere in the back of my mind.

"Sorry," they say, not sounding sorry. "I'm Reyna."

"Taylor."

Reyna sits down on the other end of the bench with the casual confidence of someone who does this all the time, and I realize with a slow cold certainty that they probably do. There's a backpack at their feet too. Older than mine. More defeated-looking.

"You got that brand-new runaway energy," Reyna says, not unkindly. "It wears off."

"I'm not—" I start.

Reyna raises one eyebrow.

"Okay," I say. "Yeah."

"How long?"

"Since last night."

Something moves across Reyna's face. Not pity exactly. Recognition, maybe. "You eat?"

"Not really."

"Come on then." They stand up, sling the beaten-up backpack over one shoulder. "There's a place on Decatur that does biscuits. Woman who runs it doesn't ask questions."

I should not follow a stranger. I know this. I have been told this my entire life by people who were, at that exact moment, the main danger in my life. I look at Reyna — at the careful stillness, the sleeves-down flannel, the way they're not quite making eye contact, like they're offering something and trying not to look like they're offering it — and I make a judgment call.

I stand up. The strap slides off my shoulder.

"Your buckle's broken," Reyna says.

"I know."

"I've got a safety pin somewhere."

We walk. The saxophone follows us for half a block then fades into the general music of the city, which has its own bass note running under everything, some frequency I can feel in my sternum. I think about the photograph in my bag. Me and my cousin Deb, two summers ago, both of us laughing at something off-camera. I look happy in it. I look like a person who doesn't know yet what they are.

I know now.

That's the thing nobody tells you about finally saying the true thing out loud: even when it burns everything down, even when you're two states away with forty-three dollars and a broken strap and no plan —

You still know.

And knowing is not nothing.

Reyna stops outside a narrow doorway and looks back at me. "You coming?"

"Yeah," I say. "I'm coming."
