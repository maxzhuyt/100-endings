# Útiseta

I still don't know what we woke up that night. I'm not sure *woke* is even the right word. Maybe we only woke ourselves.

---

Astrid came to my window at eleven-thirty, the way she always did, tapping three times with a birch twig she kept specifically for the purpose. I'd been lying on top of my duvet in my coat, not sleeping, watching the ceiling and listening to the forest press its weight against the house. We were three kilometres outside Björkheden, which was itself barely a name on a map, and the darkness there had a density I'd never felt in Stockholm. It wasn't the absence of light. It was the presence of something else.

She had the bag already packed. That was Astrid's way—she prepared everything, told you about it afterward, and by the time you understood what you'd agreed to, you were already walking.

"Bring the shears," she said, before I could speak.

"What shears?"

"Kitchen shears. Anything iron."

I took the scissors from my desk instead. She looked at them and decided they would do.

The mound was twenty minutes through the treeline, following a path that Astrid navigated without a torch. I used mine. The beam caught the birches in intervals, white trunks appearing and vanishing like figures stepping back into darkness, and the frost on the ground threw the light back in a way that made everything look wet and recently disturbed. Thick mist clung to the edges of the burial mound when we reached it, glowing under moonlight, pooling in the low ground around its base as though the earth itself was exhaling.

It was older than the village. Older than the church, certainly. Astrid had told me it was Bronze Age, though she said it with the casual authority she used for everything her grandmother had taught her, the same tone she used when explaining which boy in our class was afraid of his father, or how to make someone dream about you. I had stopped questioning her sources.

"We sit on top," she said. "Backs together. You face north."

"Why north?"

"Because that's where they come from."

She said *they* the way you'd say *rain*. A fact of weather.

From her bag she produced two things: a length of cord she wound loosely around both our wrists—*not tight*, she said, *it's not binding, it's connecting*—and a small clay dish she set in front of us containing dried juniper and something else I didn't identify then and haven't since. She lit it with a match. The smoke rose straight up in the still air, thin and pale, and the smell was resinous and old and wrong in a way I couldn't articulate. Not unpleasant. Wrong in the way that a sound can be wrong—a frequency that arrives before you consciously hear it.

"Your grandmother taught you this," I said. I needed to hear her confirm it again.

"She learned it from hers." Astrid settled her back against mine. "Stop talking now. You have to be quiet long enough that you stop thinking about being quiet."

The rules, as she'd explained them on the walk: we sat until we received something—a word, an image, a feeling that arrived from outside ourselves rather than from within. We didn't move from the mound. We didn't speak unless spoken to, which she said with no irony at all. The iron was for afterward, held in the hand when we stood, to close whatever had opened. If I felt something touch my shoulder, I was not to turn around.

"What happens if I turn around?"

She hadn't answered that one.

I don't know how long we sat. Twenty minutes, perhaps. Perhaps longer. The cold came up through the mound in a way that seemed independent of the air temperature, entering through the base of the spine, and I became aware of the ground beneath me as something layered—frost, then soil, then older soil, then whatever the mound contained at its heart, which I tried not to think about and therefore thought about constantly. The mist thickened at the edges. The treeline, which had been distinct, softened.

I heard the sound first as wind. Then as something moving through the undergrowth. Then, and this is what I cannot explain away regardless of how many times I've tried, as breath. Slow and rhythmic and located—not surrounding us, but positioned. To the north of us. Equidistant from the treeline as we were, which meant it was in the open field, which meant there was nothing there to make that sound.

Astrid's back stiffened against mine. She had heard it too.

"Don't move," she said, very quietly, and her voice had changed. The casual authority was gone. What replaced it was something I had not heard from her before: the careful steadiness of someone who has just realised the situation is not what they described.

The breathing continued. It did not approach. It did not recede. It simply persisted, patient and even, and the mist at the mound's base thickened further until I could no longer see the ground below us, only white, only that cold luminescence, and the mound felt suddenly very much like what it was—a deliberate elevation, a constructed prominence, something built to be seen from a distance and to see from.

Astrid pressed the scissors into my hand. "Now," she said. "Stand up now."

We stood together, backs still touching, and I gripped the scissors until my knuckles ached. The sound stopped the moment we rose. The mist did not thin. We walked back through it and then through the treeline and then through the dark, and Astrid did not speak again until we reached the edge of my garden, where she turned to me with an expression I had never seen on her face and said:

"We won't do that one again."

She meant it as reassurance. It wasn't.
