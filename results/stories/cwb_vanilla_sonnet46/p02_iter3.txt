# The Lighthouse Keeper

---

**14th September 1973**

Dear Mum,

Got here Tuesday. The boat that dropped me was away before I'd even set my bag down, which tells you something. The light itself is fine, everything works, the logbook from the last keeper is neat enough. Man called Forsyth. He didn't leave a note or anything, just the logbook and half a tin of condensed milk.

The lamp mechanism needs greasing every six hours or it starts to drag. That's the main job, really, that and the log. Wind from the northwest most days. Visibility poor.

I'm fine. The pay is good.

Your son,
Duncan

---

**2nd October 1973**

Dear Mum,

Thanks for the parcel. The shortbread didn't survive the crossing but I ate it anyway. Tell Mrs Gillies I said thank you.

To answer your question, no, there's no one else here. That's the nature of it. There was a two-man posting at Rubha Mòr but they've cut costs, so it's just me and the mechanism and the log. I write the same things every day. Wind direction. Visibility. Vessels observed. Some days there are no vessels.

The fog comes in some mornings so thick you can't see the water below. You can hear it though. It's not the sound you'd expect. It's not dramatic. It's more like breathing.

Tell Dad I'm managing fine.

Duncan

---

**19th November 1973**

Mum,

Sorry for not writing sooner. There was a problem with the lamp housing, a fracture in the outer glass, and I had to manage it myself because the supply boat wasn't due for three weeks. I used the repair kit and it held, but for four nights I wasn't sure it would. You keep the light going because that's what you do, but I won't pretend those were easy nights.

I've been thinking about the flat in Greenock. Whether it's still available. I'm not saying I want to leave, I'm just thinking about it.

The condensed milk ran out.

Duncan

---

**3rd December 1973**

Mum,

I know what you're going to say and you're right. I signed the contract and I knew what I was signing. I'm not a boy.

The fracture was this. The outer glass on the lamp housing is about two inches thick and it cracked from the top right corner down to roughly the middle. Not a clean break, more like a split in ice. The housing is what protects the mechanism from the weather and if weather gets into the mechanism, the mechanism fails. If the mechanism fails, the light fails. If the light fails, ships navigate by dead reckoning in waters that have put ships on rocks since before anyone was keeping records of it.

So I taped it. Marine epoxy first, then aluminium tape over that, then I sat with it for an hour to make sure it was setting in the cold. Then I watched it every night until the boat came and the proper repair was done.

I'm not telling you this so you'll worry. I'm telling you because you asked what I actually do here and that's what I actually do.

Duncan

---

**28th January 1974**

Dear Mum,

I've been reading. There's a box of books left by previous keepers, going back years. Someone called McAulay left a complete set of Walter Scott, which I've been working through. Someone else left a book about migratory birds with pencil annotations. I don't know who made them. They're very precise, the annotations. Whoever it was knew what they were looking at.

I've started making my own notes. Not in the logbook, that's official. In a separate notebook. Things I see. There's a seal that comes to the eastern rocks most afternoons. I've been watching it for six weeks now. It's the same one, I can tell by a mark on its left side. It doesn't do anything in particular. It just comes and sits on the rock for a while and then it goes.

I don't know why I'm telling you that.

Duncan

---

**15th March 1974**

Mum,

Spring is different here. I wasn't expecting that. I thought it would just be the same place with more light. But the birds come back and the water changes colour, it goes from grey-black to something greener, and the whole thing feels less like an endurance and more like a place.

I've been thinking about Forsyth, the man who was here before me. I looked back through his logbooks, they keep them all. He was here for four years. His entries are very spare, just the facts, but you can read between them if you pay attention. There's a period in his second winter where the entries get shorter and shorter, down to just a few words each day. Then in March they start to expand again. Same as mine, I think, though I didn't notice it happening.

I'm not saying I'm glad to be here. I'm saying I think I understand it better.

Duncan

---

**7th August 1974**

Dear Mum,

I've applied for the posting at Rubha Mòr. It's two men, like I said. I'll hear in October.

I was watching the seal this afternoon and I thought about something you said when I was small, that patience was just attention with nowhere to be. I don't know if you remember saying that. You probably don't. You said a lot of things.

I think I know what you meant now.

The lamp mechanism is running well. The logbook is up to date. Visibility today was excellent, the best I've seen, and I could make out the hills on the mainland, just the tops of them, sitting above the water like they were floating.

I wrote it in the official log and then I wrote it again in my own notebook, and that felt like the right thing to do.

Your son,
Duncan
