# Lost & Found in Osaka

The song stopped her dead.

Yuki had been walking with her head down, earbuds in, mentally cataloguing the synthesizer modules she'd seen in the third shop on the left — a Korg Minilogue, slightly scuffed, priced lower than it should have been — when the sound cut through everything. She pulled one earbud out. Then the other.

Electric guitar. Live. Somewhere ahead in the crowd.

She followed it the way you follow a smell, unconsciously, weaving between salary workers and tourists and a group of middle schoolers in matching caps. The melody resolved itself as she got closer. *Tank!* The Cowboy Bebop theme, but played faster than the recording, more aggressive, the jazz phrasing bent into something that felt almost punk. Whoever was playing it was doing things in the bridge that Yuki was fairly certain weren't in the original arrangement.

She found the source near the arcade entrance. A girl, maybe seventeen or eighteen, sitting on an upturned milk crate with a beat-up Telecaster plugged into a small battery amp. She had short hair dyed the particular orange-red of autumn maples, a denim jacket covered in patches, and the expression of someone who was both completely present and completely elsewhere. A cardboard sign beside her open guitar case read: *Requests welcome. No Maroon 5.*

Yuki stopped at the edge of the small crowd that had gathered. She stayed when the others drifted away.

The girl finished with a flourish — a run up the neck that shouldn't have worked but did — and looked up. She caught Yuki staring.

"You've been standing there for three songs," the girl said. Not accusatory. Amused.

Yuki felt heat climb her neck. "Sorry. I — sorry."

"Don't apologize. I like an audience that actually listens." The girl set her guitar across her knees. "You know what that last one was?"

"*The Real Folk Blues.* Also Bebop. You changed the chord in the second chorus. The — it goes to a flat seven instead of the—" Yuki stopped. "Sorry. That's weird to say."

"That's *not* weird to say, that's the most interesting thing anyone's said to me today, and a man earlier told me his entire theory about vending machines being a government surveillance program." The girl grinned. She had a small gap between her front teeth. "You play?"

"No. I do — I mean, I work with sound but not like—" Yuki gestured vaguely at the guitar. "Synthesizers. I build patches. Program sequences. It's not the same."

"It's completely the same."

"It's really not."

"It's sound design. You're shaping sound. I'm shaping sound." The girl shrugged. "The instrument's just the argument you make." She tilted her head. "I'm Hana."

"Yuki."

"You live here, Yuki? You don't look like a tourist."

"Namba. Twenty minutes on the subway." She paused. "You?"

"Kyoto, technically. But I come down on weekends." Hana patted the milk crate affectionately. "This spot gets good foot traffic. And the takoyaki place around the corner knows my order now, so."

Yuki almost smiled. "How long have you been doing this?"

"Busking? Two years. Playing? Since I was eight." Hana plucked a single string, let it ring out. "You know what I was trying to do with that flat seven, by the way? In the Bebop chorus?"

"Make it feel unresolved. Like the show."

Hana pointed at her. "Exactly like the show. Nobody ever — okay, you're the first person who's gotten that without me explaining it." She looked at Yuki with new attention, the way you look at something you've just recalibrated your opinion of. "Have you eaten?"

Yuki blinked. "What?"

"Food. Eaten any today. There's a kaitenzushi around the corner that's actually good, which is rarer than you'd think in a tourist district. Come with me." She was already unclipping the cable from her guitar.

"You don't have to—"

"I know I don't have to. I want to." Hana began packing up with the efficient speed of someone who'd done it a thousand times. "Unless you have somewhere else to be?"

Yuki thought about the Korg Minilogue. It would still be there tomorrow.

"No," she said. "I don't."

---

The restaurant was narrow and bright, the conveyor belt making its patient loop past red-cushioned stools. Hana grabbed a plate of salmon before she'd fully sat down.

"Okay," she said, "synthesizers. What kind of music do you actually make?"

"Ambient, mostly. Textural stuff." Yuki watched a plate of tamago pass and then took it. "I got into it through anime soundtracks, actually. The way composers like — some of them layer synthesis with traditional instruments and the result is this kind of—" She stopped. "I'm doing the thing where I talk too much about one subject."

"You've said maybe two hundred words total. You're fine." Hana ate her salmon. "Which soundtracks?"

"The one I keep coming back to is *Haibane Renmei.* Yoshitaka Amano did the—"

"*Yoshitaka Amano.*" Hana put her chopsticks down. "The *Haibane Renmei* soundtrack."

Yuki looked up. "You know it?"

"The track *Free Bird* — not the Lynyrd Skynyrd one, the actual album track — I learned to play it on guitar when I was fifteen and I cried the entire time." Hana shook her head slowly. "Nobody knows that soundtrack. I have recommended it to people and watched their eyes go completely blank."

"The way it uses silence," Yuki said, and her voice had changed slightly, the hesitation smoothed out of it. "It uses silence like an instrument. The space between the notes is doing as much work as the notes."

"That's what I want to do," Hana said, pointing at her again. "That's the whole thing. That's what I'm trying to figure out how to do with a guitar." She leaned her elbows on the counter. "Okay. Tell me everything you know about how he built those textures."

Outside, Den Den Town hummed and flickered. The conveyor belt made its quiet loop.

Yuki told her.
