# DEAR SISYPHUS
## *A Column for the Troubled, the Confused, and the Romantically Catastrophic*

---

The boulder, as it happened, was having a cooperative morning.

This was not unusual. People assumed the boulder was the problem. The boulder was never the problem. The boulder was just a boulder. It was the hill that had opinions.

"Next one," said Sisyphus.

Crow ruffled through the satchel with his beak, extracted an envelope, and tore it open with the practiced efficiency of someone who had been doing this for three months and had developed strong feelings about wax seals.

---

*Dear Sisyphus,*

*My husband leaves his sandals directly in the center of the doorway every single evening. I have mentioned this four hundred and twelve times. He says he "forgets." We have been married thirty-one years. Is he doing it on purpose? Should I leave him?*

*—Worn Down in Corinth*

---

"Hm," said Sisyphus.

He pushed.

The hill made its familiar sound, which was less a sound and more a philosophical position.

"Four hundred and twelve times," he said, not unkindly. "She's been counting."

Crow said nothing, which was his primary qualification for the job.

"Write this down."

---

*Dear Worn Down,*

*He is not doing it on purpose. This will be more frustrating to hear than if he were. A man doing something on purpose can be negotiated with. What you have instead is a husband whose mind, the moment it crosses the threshold, simply releases the sandals as a completed thought and moves immediately to dinner.*

*The question you're really asking is not about sandals. You've been asking it for thirty-one years and he keeps answering it by staying.*

*Move the sandals. Not as defeat—as a private joke between you and yourself. You are the only one sophisticated enough to understand it.*

*Warmly,*
*Sisyphus*

---

"Adequate," said Crow, which from Crow constituted a standing ovation.

The boulder reached the summit.

Sisyphus stood for a moment at the top of the hill, as he always did, looking out at the grey and amber distances of the underworld, which were really quite beautiful if you'd stopped expecting them to be something else. Then the boulder went back down, and so did he, and by the time he reached the bottom Crow had the next letter ready.

---

*Dear Sisyphus,*

*I am a middle manager at a grain distribution concern. My subordinates respect me, my superiors tolerate me, and I have just realized, at the age of forty-four, that I have no idea what I actually want from life. I thought I would feel more like myself by now. When does that happen?*

*—Adrift in Administration*

---

Sisyphus read this one twice.

"Ah," he said, with the warmth of someone recognizing an old acquaintance at a market.

---

*Dear Adrift,*

*It doesn't happen. That is the good news, though I understand it won't feel that way immediately.*

*The self you're waiting to feel like is assembled continuously, not delivered. You've been building it all along—in the tolerating and the being-tolerated, in the grain, in the management. The sensation you're describing, that gap between who you are and who you expected to be, is not a sign that you've failed to arrive. It's the sensation of being a person.*

*What do you do on the afternoons when no one needs anything from you? Start there. Not because it will answer the question, but because it's a better question.*

*Yours,*
*Sisyphus*

*P.S. Forty-four is not late. Forty-four is when people stop performing the answer and start looking for it.*

---

It was at approximately this moment that Hermes appeared, because Hermes always appeared at approximately this moment, which is to say whenever he was least needed.

He materialized with his customary elegance on a nearby rock, examined the satchel of letters, examined Crow, and allowed his expression to convey that he had opinions about both.

"This," he said, gesturing at the general enterprise, "seems beneath you."

"Mmm," said Sisyphus, pushing.

"You're a figure of *tragedy*. An emblem of futile human striving. There are *paintings*."

"There are," Sisyphus agreed pleasantly.

"And you're writing an advice column."

"Dictating. Crow writes. He has better penmanship."

Crow looked at Hermes with the expression of a professional who has been interrupted.

"I'm simply saying," Hermes continued, in the tone of someone who had prepared this speech and intended to finish it, "that this is not a dignified use of an eternal punishment."

Sisyphus paused at the top of the hill. Below him, the boulder waited with its characteristic patience.

"Hermes," he said, "how many people did you help today?"

A pause.

"I delivered messages."

"Mm. I'll put you down for none, then."

The boulder went down. Sisyphus followed it. Hermes, after a moment of divine indignation, vanished, which was the most graceful exit available to him.

"Next," said Sisyphus.

---

*Dear Sisyphus,*

*My neighbor's goat has eaten my prize cabbages three years running. My neighbor claims the goat "wanders." I have begun to suspect the goat is being directed. How do I handle this without starting a feud?*

*—Cabbage-less in Boeotia*

*P.S. The goat looked at me.*

---

*Dear Cabbage-less,*

*The goat is not being directed. Goats do not require direction; they have internal purpose that puts most humans to shame.*

*Speak to your neighbor not about the goat but about the fence. Fence conversations are practical. Goat conversations become personal. You want a fence, not a confession.*

*As for the look: goats look at everyone like that. It is not personal. It is simply their face.*

*Regarding the cabbages—plant twice as many as you need. This is not advice about the goat. This is advice about everything.*

*Cheerfully,*
*Sisyphus*

---

Crow sealed the letter, tucked it under his wing, and made a note in the ledger he kept for his own purposes, in a column labeled *PRACTICAL.*

The hill waited.

The boulder waited.

Sisyphus rolled up his sleeves, settled his hands against the familiar stone, and pushed—already thinking about the next letter, the next small human tangle he might help someone through.

It was, he had decided, good work.
