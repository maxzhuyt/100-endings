# The Lighthouse Keeper

---

**14th September 1973**

Mum,

Got here Tuesday. The boat that brought me out was called the *Mhairi Jean* and the man who drove it never said a single word the whole crossing. Forty minutes. I counted the gannets instead of talking. Got to eleven before the Mull of Kintyre came round the headland and I lost count.

The light itself is fine. Older than I expected. The previous keeper left a tin of shortbread which I've been rationing. There's a logbook going back to 1889 and some of the handwriting in it is beautiful, like the men had nothing to do but practice their letters. Maybe that's what I'll do.

It pays well. That's the main thing.

Your son,
Duncan

---

**2nd October 1973**

Duncan,

Glad you arrived safe. Your Auntie Fiona said you were mad to take it and I told her you were always one for your own company. Your father was the same. I've sent a parcel with the oatcakes you like and a new pair of socks as the ones you left behind had holes in both heels.

Write when you can.

Mum

---

**19th October 1973**

Mum,

The parcel came out with the supply boat. Thank you for the oatcakes. I've been eating them with the last of a cheese that went slightly wrong, which sounds worse than it is.

The Mull of Kintyre is visible on clear days, just sitting there like a dark thumb pressed into the water. On bad days there's nothing. Just grey into grey. The fog horn runs for hours and I've started to feel it in my back teeth.

I'm sleeping fine. Tell Auntie Fiona.

Duncan

---

**8th November 1973**

Mum,

Something happened that I want to tell you about plainly so you don't worry. There was a storm for four days, the worst since I arrived, and on the third night I went up to check the lamp mechanism and I stood at the top and I looked out at where the water was, though I couldn't see it, and I felt something I don't have a good word for. Not frightened. More like I'd been removed from everything that knew my name. Like I was already gone.

I went back down and made tea and it passed. I'm telling you because I don't want to keep things from you, not because there's anything wrong. There isn't. I'm fine.

The logbook from 1889 mentions a keeper called Archibald Weir who grew tomatoes in the lamp room. I find that very funny for some reason.

Duncan

---

**20th November 1973**

Duncan,

I read your letter three times. I'm not going to pretend I'm not worried because I am, I'm your mother. But I also know you're not a dramatic person and if you say you're fine I'll believe you.

Your cousin Robert is getting married in February. I told him you probably can't come and he said he understood. I'm not sure I do entirely but I told him you understood.

Are you eating properly? Not just oatcakes.

Mum

---

**3rd January 1974**

Mum,

Happy New Year, late. I watched it come in from the gallery. Clear night, cold enough to sting. I could see lights on the mainland and I thought about everyone there having their parties and I felt fine about it, which surprised me.

I've been reading everything in the station. There's a box of paperback westerns left by someone and I've read six of them. They're not very good but they're company of a sort. The men in them are always riding toward something or away from something and never just standing still, which I think about.

I've started writing things down in a notebook. Not a diary exactly. More like observations. The way the light moves across the water at different hours. The birds. There's a pair of fulmars nesting on the north face of the rock and I've named them, which I'm aware is not entirely sane.

Duncan

---

**28th February 1974**

Mum,

Robert's wedding was lovely. Everyone asked after you. Mrs. MacAllister said you were wasted out there and I said you were doing important work and she said yes but still. I think she meant well.

I've been having trouble sleeping since Christmas. Nothing serious. Just the house feels large with only me in it.

What are the fulmars called?

Mum

---

**15th March 1974**

Mum,

Archibald and Weir. Obviously.

I'm sorry about the sleeping. Have you tried the radio on low? That used to work for me in the flat in Greenock when the street noise stopped and the quiet got loud.

I want to tell you something and I need you to hear it properly. I've been offered a second year. Same posting. I said yes yesterday and I've been sitting with it since, checking whether I made the right call, and I think I did. Not because it pays well, though it does. Because I'm not finished here yet. I don't know what I'm doing exactly but I know I'm doing it, and that's more than I could say when I was working the yards.

The Mull of Kintyre was pink this morning at six. Just for about four minutes. I wrote it down.

Duncan

---

**2nd April 1974**

Duncan,

I heard you. I think I understand, or I'm trying to.

Your father used to go out early on Sundays before the rest of us were up and walk down to the river and just stand there. I never asked him why. I think I was afraid he'd say he didn't know, and that would have frightened me. Now I think not knowing why is sometimes the whole point.

Come home when you can. Not because anything's wrong. Just because I'd like to see your face.

The socks I'm sending are thicker this time. I asked the woman in the shop specifically.

All my love,
Mum
