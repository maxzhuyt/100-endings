# Chapter Seven: The Weight of Azra

The transfer vessel hummed with the particular indifference of machinery that had no opinion about its cargo.

Arthur Neegan sat in a bolted chair and watched the small display screen cycle through its diagnostics — pressure nominal, trajectory nominal, reactor output nominal — green text scrolling against grey steel with the bureaucratic patience of a system that had never once been asked what it thought about any of this. He found it oddly comforting. Somewhere between the third and fourth readout, he had decided that the screen was the most honest thing aboard.

He had been given forty minutes to pack. The retinue — six of them, in the dove-grey uniforms of the Antares Diplomatic Corps, with the twin-star insignia pressed in silver at their shoulders — had waited outside his prefab with the particular politeness of men who are accustomed to not needing to ask twice. Their commander, a lean woman with close-cropped hair and the careful eyes of a surveyor, had handed him a document on a glass tablet.

*Request for Consultation*, it read. *Voluntary.*

He had looked at the six men behind her.

"Of course," he had said.

---

The vessel broke Martian orbit with a shudder that Arthur felt in his back teeth, and then the rust-coloured curve of the planet tilted away through the porthole and was replaced by the simple, absolute black of the transfer corridor between worlds. He had made this crossing twice before — once arriving, once returning briefly to settle his mother's estate on Callisto. Both times it had felt like erasure. The self you were on one world had no jurisdiction out here.

A door slid open. The woman with the careful eyes entered and sat across from him without preamble.

"Mr. Neegan. I'm Coordinator Vass. I apologise for the abruptness of the invitation."

"You'll forgive me if I don't quite believe the apology."

She almost smiled. "The party you're meeting asked me to convey that your cooperation is genuinely valued. That word — *genuinely* — was specified."

"Who is the party?"

"You'll have that answer in approximately four hours." She stood. "There's food in the forward compartment. The coffee is adequate."

The door closed. The diagnostics screen cycled. *Trajectory nominal.*

---

The station had no name on any chart Arthur had been given access to, which told him something. It occupied a gravitational null point between Mars and the belt — a location chosen, he suspected, less for convenience than for the elegant ambiguity of its jurisdiction. It was large enough to suggest permanence and spare enough to suggest that permanence had not been the original intention.

He was shown through three corridors and two pressure doors and into a room that surprised him.

It was warm. There were actual plants — small ones, in ceramic pots along a shelf — and the lighting was the amber of late afternoon rather than the surgical white of institutional spaces. A man sat at a round table with two cups already poured. He was perhaps sixty, with the broad, unhurried face of someone who had learned that patience was simply leverage applied slowly.

"Mr. Neegan." He gestured to the chair opposite. "Please. The coffee here is better than adequate, whatever Vass told you."

Arthur sat. He took the cup. The coffee was, in fact, excellent.

"You have me at a disadvantage," Arthur said.

"Temporarily." The man folded his hands on the table. "My name is Councillor Dresh. I represent certain interests within the Consolidated Outer Systems Assembly — which is to say, I represent the interests of approximately nine hundred million people who will, within the next decade, find themselves either protected or endangered by what sits underneath your mining plot on Mars."

Arthur set down his cup. "That's a large number of people to attach to a survey claim."

"It's a large deposit of Azra." Dresh's expression did not change. "Our geophysical models suggest you are sitting atop the largest single vein yet discovered. Forty times the yield of the Schiaparelli extraction site. Perhaps more." He paused to let that settle, the way a good engineer lets a structure bear its own weight before testing the joints. "You understand what that means for drive technology."

"I understand what it means for weapons technology," Arthur said.

"Yes." Dresh seemed to appreciate the correction. "Which is precisely why I wanted to speak with you before certain other parties did." He turned his cup slowly in its saucer. "The Terran Directorate has filed three separate legal challenges to your claim in the past eleven days. The Meridian Extraction Consortium has, we believe, retained two lobbyists and one less reputable type of operative. And the Antares Corps" — he glanced toward the door through which Coordinator Vass had presumably retreated — "is nominally neutral, which in practice means they are available to whoever makes the more persuasive argument."

"And your argument?" Arthur asked.

"Simple." Dresh looked at him directly for the first time. "Sign a development accord with the Assembly. We fund extraction, we control distribution, and we guarantee — legally, structurally, with teeth — that Azra from your vein does not enter the weapons market for a period of no less than thirty years. You receive a percentage that will make you, by any reasonable measure, extraordinarily wealthy. And nine hundred million people in the outer systems do not spend the next decade looking down the barrel of something that accelerates radioactive decay to weapons-grade output."

The diagnostics screen on the wall cycled through its green text. *Pressure nominal. Reactor output nominal.*

Arthur looked at the plants on the shelf. Someone had watered them recently. Someone had brought soil up from somewhere and decided that this cold, jurisdictionally ambiguous station needed something alive in it.

"And if I decline?" he said.

Dresh picked up his coffee.

"Then someone less scrupulous than me will be having this conversation with you very shortly." He sipped. "And their coffee, I assure you, will be considerably worse."
