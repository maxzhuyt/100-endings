# Gunpowder Rendezvous

The bar doesn't have a name. Just a neon sign outside shaped like a fish that lost half its letters to weather, so it reads **ISH** in dying pink light. Spike finds a stool at the far end of the counter and orders something brown without specifying further. The bartender, a man with the face of someone who has heard everything, pours without asking twice.

Spike sets his lighter on the bar. It's a Zippo, once silver, now wearing a ring of rust around the hinge where it's been dropped in water more than once. He doesn't light anything with it. Just turns it between his fingers the way some men worry rosary beads, the motion unconscious, habitual, devotional in its own secular way.

He has the photograph in his jacket pocket. Target: one Hector Vargas, mid-level arms broker, currently believed to be moving through this particular port city with a briefcase and a death wish. The bounty is enough to cover three months of fuel, Jet's medical supplies, and Ein's food, with maybe enough left over for something that isn't canned bell peppers. He'd rather not split it.

He's already clocked the woman at the other end of the bar.

---

She arrived six minutes before he did, which means she's better at arriving than he is, which means she's had more practice being somewhere first. She's working on her second drink when he sits down, and she doesn't look at him directly, but he has the distinct impression she's already counted his exits.

The dragon on her arm catches the light when she lifts her glass. The twin holsters are worn and oiled and completely unselfconscious, the way a carpenter might wear a tool belt. She's smoking a cigarette down to nothing with the focused efficiency of someone who treats pleasure like a job.

She has the same photograph in her pocket. He'd bet his good eye on it.

---

"Vargas," she says, without preamble, without looking at him. Her voice has the texture of gravel in a tin can.

"Never heard of him," Spike says.

She finally turns. Her eyes do a quick professional inventory of him — the slouch, the suit that costs nothing, the gun at his hip, the way his left eye doesn't track quite the same as his right — and whatever calculation she runs, she doesn't share the result.

"You're the bounty hunter," she says. "The one they call Spike."

"People call me lots of things."

"I'm Revy." She says it like a period at the end of a sentence that doesn't invite questions. "The bounty is mine."

"Funny," he says, and signals for another drink, "I was just thinking the same thing."

---

They drink. The bar fills up around them and then empties again, the tide of other people's bad decisions washing in and out, and they remain at their respective ends of the counter like two weather systems that haven't decided yet whether to merge or destroy each other.

She moves to the stool beside him somewhere around the third drink. He doesn't comment on it.

"You were Red Dragon," she says.

He picks up his lighter. Sets it down. "Was."

"I've worked with Lagoon Company out of Roanapur." She says it the way someone might lay down a card, not to win, exactly, but to show they're playing. "I know what that tattoo means. The one you're covering with that ugly suit."

"You've been looking."

"Occupational habit." She steals one of his cigarettes without asking, which he finds more interesting than annoying. "How's the eye?"

"Decorative."

She almost smiles. It's a complicated almost — something that lives in the same neighborhood as amusement but keeps different hours.

---

They find Vargas at half past eleven. He's in a back room playing cards badly, sweating through a linen shirt, and he makes the mistake of going for his weapon when the door opens. Revy puts a bullet through his card hand before he clears the holster. Spike catches him when he falls, which is either courtesy or efficiency depending on how you look at it.

The argument about the bounty happens in the alley outside, in the rain, which is the appropriate setting for arguments that don't have clean resolutions. Her guns are out. His gun is out. They're standing close enough that the rain is creating a shared puddle between their feet.

"Fifty-fifty," he says.

"Seventy-thirty. I shot him."

"I caught him. Bounty's worth more alive."

"Sixty-forty."

"Done," he says, which surprises her, he can tell, because she was still calculating.

---

The motel is two blocks away and neither of them suggests it and both of them end up there. The room has one chair and one bed and a window that looks at a wall. Revy sits on the bed with her boots still on. Spike takes the chair, lights a cigarette finally, watches the rust on his lighter catch the lamplight.

Her guns are on the nightstand. His gun is in his lap. Neither of them has put anything away.

"You're going to leave in the morning," she says. It isn't an accusation. It's the kind of statement that has already made its peace with itself.

"So are you," he says.

She looks at him with something that might be the wreckage of a softer expression — something that got built once and didn't survive whatever came after. He recognizes the architecture. He's seen it in mirrors.

"I'm not a good person," she says.

"I've already died once," he says. "So."

The rain against the window fills the silence between them, which is a silence that contains a question neither of them asks because they both already know the answer isn't something you can spend or holster or walk away from clean.

The cigarette burns down.

The guns stay on the table.

*Whatever happens, happens.*
