# My Apocalypse Buddy

The thing about Chewy was that he had survived the collapse in better shape than most humans, which said something either very complimentary about animatronic stuffed koalas or very uncomplimentary about humans, depending on your philosophical disposition.

Midas had found him in the ruins of what the faded signage suggested had once been a "Family Entertainment Megaplex," which was either a place where families went to be entertained on a mega scale or some kind of pre-collapse religious institution. The distinction, at this point, was largely academic.

Chewy's fur had gone the colour of old dishwater. One glass eye was slightly more optimistic than the other, perpetually aimed skyward as though expecting better weather. Every forty-seven minutes, with the mechanical reliability that had outlasted civilization itself, he emitted a fragment of whatever advertising gospel had been encoded into his small grey chest.

"Right," said Midas, spreading the hand-drawn map across the bonnet of the dead car they used as a planning table. "The granary."

"*Taste the rainbow,*" said Chewy.

"The granary," Midas repeated, in the tone of a man who had learned to absorb non-sequiturs the way deserts absorb rain—completely and without visible effect. "Betterman says there's three months of grain in there, possibly four if we're not greedy, which we won't be because we're practically saints."

Chewy regarded the map with his asymmetric gaze.

The granary was, by Midas's reckoning, approximately four kilometres northeast, past the collapsed overpass, through the market district where Geraldine Muck operated her informal toll system, and around the edge of what everyone now called the Smelly Part, for reasons that rewarded neither investigation nor explanation.

The plan was elegant in its simplicity. Walk there. Get grain. Walk back. Midas had constructed this plan over two days, which was two days more than he usually spent on plans, and he felt justifiably proud of it.

He had not accounted for the goat.

---

The goat appeared approximately eleven minutes into the journey, at the point where the road curved past the old pharmacy. It was a large goat, the colour of bad intentions, with the eyes of something that had made peace with chaos and found the arrangement agreeable. It was standing in the middle of the road eating what appeared to be someone's boot.

"We go around," said Midas.

The goat looked at him.

"We definitely go around," said Midas, going around.

The goat followed.

It continued to follow them through the market district, which complicated the negotiation with Geraldine Muck considerably, as Geraldine had a longstanding and loudly expressed position on goats, their character, their ancestry, and their fundamental unsuitability for civil society. The toll, which had begun as two tins of preserved peaches, escalated through a series of goat-related grievances to four tins, a length of copper wire, and a formal written apology for the goat's behaviour, which Midas composed on the spot with the resigned fluency of a man who had written many apologies for things that were not, strictly speaking, his fault.

"*You're worth it,*" said Chewy, from under Midas's arm.

"Thank you," said Geraldine, unexpectedly mollified.

---

The granary, when they reached it, was locked. This was not surprising. What was surprising was that it was locked from the inside, which suggested either tremendous foresight on the part of whoever had locked it, or that someone was currently inside, which proved, upon closer inspection, to be exactly the case.

"There's someone in there," said Midas.

The goat attempted to eat the door.

The someone in there turned out to be three someones: a pair of brothers named Ott and Ott (they were, they explained without apparent embarrassment, both named Ott, following a clerical error at birth that had never been corrected and had eventually achieved the status of tradition), and a woman named Priscilla who had locked herself in three weeks ago following a dispute about turnips that had, in the telling, achieved an almost mythological complexity.

They were not, technically, hostile. They were, however, not inclined to share, which amounted to the same practical outcome.

Negotiations lasted forty minutes. Chewy contributed "*New formula, same great taste*" at the thirty-two minute mark, which broke the tension sufficiently that Priscilla laughed, and laughing Priscilla was, it emerged, a substantially more reasonable Priscilla than non-laughing Priscilla.

They left with one sack of grain. It was not three months' worth. It was not four months' worth. It was, Midas calculated, walking back through the market district with the goat still in attendance, approximately eleven days' worth if everyone was careful and nobody had seconds.

He chose to regard this as a partial success. Partial successes were, in his experience, the primary currency of the post-collapse world, and he had developed a connoisseur's appreciation for them.

---

They made it back to the Smelly Part before the rain started, and through the Smelly Part before Midas's left boot sole finally surrendered its long argument with structural integrity, and past the old pharmacy before the goat, apparently satisfied that it had discharged some obscure goatly obligation, departed without ceremony into a side street and was gone.

Home base was a former insurance office, which Midas found appropriate in ways he couldn't entirely articulate. He set the grain in the corner, hung his wet coat on the filing cabinet, and lowered himself into the salvaged armchair with the careful deliberateness of a man whose body had begun submitting formal complaints.

Chewy sat on the desk, one eye skyward, one eye watching the door.

Forty-seven minutes after they'd arrived home, right on schedule, he said: "*Because you deserve the very best.*"

Midas looked at the single sack of grain. At his bootless foot. At the map, which had gotten wet and was now largely fictional.

"Sure," he said. "Absolutely."

Outside, it continued to rain, which was at least consistent.
