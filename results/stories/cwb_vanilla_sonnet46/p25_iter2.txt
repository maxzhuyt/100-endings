# The Azra Gambit
## Chapter Seven: The Weight of Courtesy

The collar was strangling him.

Arthur Neegan tugged at it for the fourth time in as many minutes, earning a flat look from the nearest guard — if *guard* was the right word for a man in diplomatic whites who hadn't touched his sidearm once, yet somehow communicated its presence with every breath he took. The transfer vessel hummed around them, a sound Arthur associated with expense. Everything here was expensive. The upholstered seats. The recycled air, which smelled of something floral rather than the iron-and-sulphur tang he'd spent eleven years breathing on the Martian surface. Even the silence felt costly.

He looked out the porthole. Mars had already shrunk to a rust-coloured disc, indifferent to his departure. Somewhere down there, Claim 7-Neegan sat unattended, its survey flags snapping in a wind that nobody breathed. He wondered if they'd already sent someone to look at it.

He suspected they had.

The insignia on the guards' lapels caught the cabin light — a stylised compass rose bisected by a diagonal bar, rendered in silver thread. Antares Diplomatic Corps. Arthur had seen the emblem twice before: once on a wanted bulletin in Schiaparelli Station's commissary, and once on the side of a gunship that had escorted a corporate convoy through the Hellas Basin at a speed that suggested diplomacy had very little to do with the matter.

*Diplomatic in name only*, the saying went. He hadn't invented that saying. Plenty of people had, and several of them were no longer around to be credited for it.

---

The vessel docked with a station Arthur didn't recognise — which itself told him something. He knew every registered waypoint between Mars and the Belt. This one sat at a Lagrange point, running dark, its exterior lights reduced to the legal minimum. Inside, it was another matter entirely.

They walked him through corridors of pale composite and indirect lighting, past sealed doors that hummed with the particular frequency of serious computing infrastructure. A planet's ransom in processing power, dedicated to something he wasn't permitted to see. He filed the observation away and kept walking.

The room they brought him to was small, deliberately so. A circular table, two chairs, a carafe of water that was almost certainly Earthside-filtered. One wall was transparent, looking out onto open space. Mars was not visible. That, too, felt deliberate.

The man waiting for him stood as Arthur entered, which surprised him. He was perhaps sixty, with the lean, careful build of someone who exercised not for vanity but for the same reason a machine requires maintenance. His suit was dark, unmarked by any insignia Arthur could identify.

"Mr. Neegan." He gestured to the opposite chair. "Please. Sit."

"I'd rather know who I'm sitting with first."

The man smiled. It reached his eyes, which made it more unsettling, not less. "My name is Councillor Veth. I represent certain interests that have been watching the Azra situation with considerable attention."

"Certain interests." Arthur sat. "That's a category that currently includes about half the solar system."

"Fewer than you'd think." Veth poured water into both glasses, a domestic gesture that felt rehearsed. "The *serious* interests are rather fewer. Perhaps a dozen parties with the resources and the foresight to understand what your discovery actually means." He paused. "Do you understand what it means, Mr. Neegan?"

"I understand that my claim sits on top of a vein that preliminary survey puts at roughly forty times the largest previously recorded deposit." Arthur picked up the glass. The water was cold. Genuinely cold, not the tepid approximation the Martian settlements managed. "I understand that Azra accelerates radioactive decay by a factor that current physics is still arguing about in very loud rooms. And I understand that whoever controls enough of it controls the next generation of drive technology, which means they control the transit lanes, which means they control—"

"Everything," Veth said quietly. "Yes. You understand quite well."

"I'm an engineer, not a politician. I understand *problems*."

"Then let me present you with one." Veth leaned back, and for a moment the careful warmth left his face, replaced by something more honest and considerably less comfortable. "There are currently four separate parties moving to acquire your claim by legal means. Three of them have already filed counter-survey challenges with the Colonial Mineral Authority. The fourth has taken the precaution of purchasing two members of the Authority's review board." He let that settle. "There are two further parties who have dispensed with legal means entirely."

Arthur set down his glass. "And you?"

"I am here to make you an offer before the others make you something worse." Veth folded his hands on the table. "We want a partnership. Genuine equity. You retain thirty percent of the claim, with full legal protections underwritten by parties with the standing to make that guarantee meaningful. You continue as chief engineer — your knowledge of the site is, frankly, irreplaceable at this stage. In exchange, we manage the political environment."

"Manage it."

"Vigorously."

Arthur looked out at the stars. Eleven years ago he'd come to Mars with a surveyor's licence, two duffel bags, and a conviction that the frontier still meant something. That a man could find a thing and keep it. He'd been wrong about most things since then, but he'd kept the conviction going through sheer stubbornness.

"And if I decline?"

Veth's expression didn't change. "Then we wish you well, return you to your claim, and watch with genuine regret as the less courteous parties conclude their business with you." He paused. "We are, Mr. Neegan, the most polite option currently available. I say that not as a threat, but as a piece of engineering data. A fact of the system as it currently operates."

Arthur tugged at his collar one last time.

"Tell me," he said slowly, "about these legal protections."

Veth smiled again, and reached beneath the table for a second carafe.
