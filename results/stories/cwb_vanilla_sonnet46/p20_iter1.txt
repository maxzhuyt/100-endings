# Gunpowder Rendezvous

The jukebox bleeds the same three chords into the smoke-thick air for the fourth time running. Nobody complains. The kind of people who drink at the Rusty Anchor don't complain about anything they can't solve with a bottle or a bullet.

Spike Spiegel folds himself into a corner booth like a man made of loose joints and bad decisions, one arm draped over the seatback, a cigarette burning slow between his fingers. The bounty sheet sits on the table beside his beer, creased from his jacket pocket. Forty thousand woolongs. Enough to keep Jet from delivering another lecture about fiscal responsibility. Barely.

He spots her the moment she walks in.

Not because she's beautiful, though she is, in the way that a loaded weapon is beautiful—functional and dangerous and pointed at something. He spots her because she moves through the room like she's already decided everyone in it is a problem she hasn't gotten around to solving yet. Dragon tattoo. Twin holsters. Shorts that have no business being in a bar this far below sea level.

She spots the bounty sheet before she spots him.

Her eyes track to his face a half-second later.

"You've got to be kidding me," Revy says.

"Funny," Spike says, taking a long drag. "I was thinking the same thing."

---

She sits across from him because there's nowhere else to sit where she can watch the door, and watching the door is non-negotiable. She orders whiskey, neat, and drinks half of it before she says another word. The jukebox starts its loop again. The same grinding guitar riff, the same half-broken drumbeat.

"Lagoon Company," he says. It isn't a question.

"Bebop." She says it like she's tasting something she hasn't decided about yet. "Heard of you. Thought there'd be more of you."

"There usually is. Tonight I'm economizing."

"The target's mine." She sets her glass down with a precision that suggests she's thought about breaking it. "I've been tracking Vargas for six days. Six days of this shithole city and its shithole weather and bars exactly like this one."

"Seven days," Spike says pleasantly. "I've been here seven."

The look she gives him could strip paint.

He signals the bartender for two more drinks, which she doesn't thank him for but doesn't refuse either. The cigarette burns to his fingers and he stubs it out, immediately lights another. She watches this with something that might be professional recognition—she's already on her third since sitting down.

"Split it," he says.

"I don't split."

"Neither do I, usually. But forty thousand divided by two is still enough for what I need."

"What do you need?"

"Food, mostly. You?"

Something shifts in her expression. Not softening—Revy doesn't soften. But a hairline fracture appears in the hostility, and through it comes something that might, in different light, look like amusement. "Bullets," she says. "Always bullets."

The jukebox grinds through its chorus again.

---

By the third round, the bounty sheet has migrated to the seat beside Spike, face-down, as if they've agreed without agreeing to stop looking at it for a while. Revy has her boots up on the table. Spike has his eyes half-closed in that way he has that makes people think he's not paying attention, which is exactly when he's paying the most.

"You're watching me," she says.

"I watch everyone."

"Most people who watch me like that end up needing medical attention."

"I'm not most people." He exhales smoke toward the water-stained ceiling. "You've got good hands. I noticed when you came in. The way you cleared the holster on your right side when the guy by the bar moved too fast. Didn't even look."

Revy is quiet for a moment. "You noticed that."

"Jeet Kune Do. You read the body before the body knows what it's doing."

She studies him with a directness that most people find uncomfortable. Spike finds it restful, in a strange way. No performance in it. No calculation beyond the purely tactical. "You're not what I expected," she says finally.

"What did you expect?"

"Someone easier to hate."

The jukebox starts again.

---

They find Vargas at midnight, which is almost anticlimactic. He's three blocks away, drunk and stupid, and between the two of them the whole thing takes forty seconds and leaves Spike with a split lip and Revy with a new crease in her tank top from where a bullet grazed the fabric without touching skin, which she seems to regard as a personal insult directed at her wardrobe.

The motel is the closest thing to neutral ground they can find.

One room. One bed. This fact sits between them like a third party with opinions.

Revy leans against the wall opposite the door, both Cutlasses drawn, not pointed at him exactly but not pointed away either. Spike stands by the window, cigarette lit, jacket still on. His hand is near his Jericho. Neither of them has made a decision yet about what kind of night this is.

"Half the money," she says.

"Half the money," he agrees.

Neither of them moves.

The problem, Spike thinks, watching the smoke curl toward the water-stained ceiling, is that he recognizes her. Not her face. The thing underneath it—the particular exhaustion of someone who's been running from something long enough that the running has become the point. He knows that shape. He's lived inside it.

The problem, Revy thinks, watching him watch the ceiling, is that he's not afraid of her. People are always afraid of her. It's the thing she depends on, the distance it creates. He just looks at her like she's interesting. Like she's *worth* looking at.

"Whatever happens," he says, to no one in particular.

"What?"

"Nothing." He finally turns to look at her directly, and something in his mismatched eyes is almost gentle. "You hungry?"

The guns don't go away.

But she says, "Yeah."

The jukebox, three blocks over, starts its loop again.
