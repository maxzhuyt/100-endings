# Darkroom Revelations

The red light makes everyone look like they're confessing something.

I've thought about that before, in other darkrooms, other semesters. But tonight it feels specifically true, specifically aimed at the boy standing three feet away from me, watching a photograph materialize in the developer tray like he's witnessing something sacred. Which, I suppose, he is. That's the thing about Elliot. He treats ordinary moments like they deserve reverence.

He's humming. Something soft and tuneless, or almost tuneless—there's a shape to it that keeps almost becoming a real song and then dissolving. His fingers hover at the edge of the tray without touching the chemical bath, and the red light catches the silver ring on his thumb, the smudged liner he always wears slightly imprecise, like he applied it in low light or applied it deliberately imprecise. I've never been able to tell which.

I've been cataloguing him for four months. That's the honest version of what I've been doing.

I'm not proud of the cataloguing, exactly. It's not my most dignified impulse. But Elliot arrived in Experimental Photography in September wearing a shredded fishnet top over a long-sleeve thermal and carrying a camera bag held together with electrical tape and band patches, and something in my brain just—flagged him. Filed him under *pay attention to this one.* I'm twenty-five. I've been in this program long enough to know which students are interesting and which students are performing interesting. Elliot was the real thing. Quiet in a way that had weight to it.

"It's coming," he says, and his voice has that quality it always has, like he's slightly surprised by good things.

The image is one of his—a double exposure, two negatives sandwiched. A hand holding a flower, overlaid with the same hand holding nothing. The emptiness and the fullness occupying the same space. It's his whole thesis in one frame, honestly, though he hasn't said that out loud yet.

"It's good," I say. And then, because I'm me and I can't help it: "The registration is perfect. How did you get the alignment that precise?"

"Luck, mostly." He glances at me sideways. "And I did it like six times."

I smile. I'm standing close enough to smell the faint chemical sharpness of the developer on his hands, underneath something warmer. I've been managing my proximity to Elliot all semester with what I consider impressive discipline.

He goes back to humming.

The thing is, I have a type. I've always had a type, and I've always been clear about it, which is something I pride myself on. I like defined edges. I like people who know what they are. I've dated women who were very specifically women, men who were very specifically men, and I've liked the clarity of that, the way desire had clean lines I could follow. I'm a person who takes charge. I need to know the territory I'm navigating.

Elliot is not clear edges. Elliot is a double exposure.

And I have been quietly, inconveniently losing my mind about him since October.

"Can I ask you something?" he says.

"Yeah."

He doesn't ask it immediately. He uses the tongs to lift the print, move it to the stop bath, and I watch his hands work with the careful attention he gives everything. The humming stops.

"Do you ever feel like—" He pauses. Starts again. "Like you've been performing a version of yourself for so long that you can't tell anymore if you're performing or if that's actually you?"

The question lands somewhere specific in my chest. I think about the version of Dani I've been performing in this darkroom for four months—the one who is casually interested, professionally collegial, definitely not cataloguing.

"Yeah," I say. "I know that feeling."

He nods slowly. The print moves to the fix bath.

"I've been thinking a lot about—" Another pause, longer. The red light makes his face look like something being developed too. Like he's in the process of becoming visible. "About gender stuff. My gender stuff. I don't know if I'm—I don't know what I am, exactly. I've been trying on different words and none of them fit perfectly and some of them fit *mostly* and I just." He exhales. "I don't know why I'm telling you this."

"Because it's a confessional in here," I say. "The red light."

He looks at me then, really looks, and something in his expression does something complicated to my composure.

"Yeah," he says quietly. "Maybe."

I want to ask questions. I want to take charge of this conversation, map it, understand exactly where he is and what he needs and how to respond correctly. That's my instinct. Take the territory, know the terrain. But I can feel that instinct as the wrong one, feel it the way you feel a wrong note. This is not my territory to take.

"That sounds like important work," I say instead. "The trying on."

"It's exhausting."

"I bet."

"Does it—" He stops. Looks at the print in the fix bath. "Does it change anything? For you. About working together."

The question is careful. Tentative in a way that makes me understand he's braced for something. I think about what answer he's braced for, and it makes something in me go protective and fierce and tender all at once, which is a combination I don't have a lot of experience with.

"No," I say. And then, because honesty seems to be what this red light demands: "It makes me more interested in your work, actually. Which I didn't think was possible."

He's quiet for a moment.

"Just my work?" he says, and there's something in it—not quite teasing, not quite serious. Something in between, something new.

The photograph floats in the chemical bath. A hand holding everything and nothing simultaneously.

"We should get the print to the wash," I say.

But I'm smiling, and he can see it, and the red light holds us both in its slow, developing patience.
