# Hell is Other Demons

The ash had been falling since Tuesday.

Not dramatically—not the great choking curtains of cinder that preceded a soul-harvest or a minor apocalypse—just a fine, grey drizzle of it, settling on the obsidian windowsills, collecting in the gutters of the Pandemonium administrative district, dusting the shoulders of the damned as they shuffled to their various appointments with suffering.

Lucifer found it peaceful, actually. He stood at the floor-to-ceiling window of his office on the nine-hundredth floor and watched the ash fall and tried not to think about Lilith.

He failed, as he had been failing since approximately the fourteenth century.

---

His nine o'clock was a demon named Furfur, who managed the department responsible for generating mild but persistent anxieties in the living—the low-grade dread of unanswered emails, the specific misery of remembering something embarrassing from 2003. Good work. Important work. Furfur had submitted a budget proposal requesting seventeen additional staff.

"Denied," said Lucifer, without looking up from the proposal.

"My lord, the department is critically understaffed. The modern era alone has produced—"

"Hire three. Fire four. Net result: a department that is slightly anxious about its own continued existence." Lucifer smiled pleasantly. "Consider it immersive professional development."

Furfur left. The ash continued to fall.

---

At half past eleven, Lilith arrived.

She did not knock. She had never knocked. Knocking implied a threshold she was willing to respect, and Lilith had not respected a threshold since before the invention of doors.

She was wearing grey—the colour of the ash outside, which Lucifer suspected was not coincidental—and she was carrying a manila folder, which meant this was *business*, which meant he was not allowed to feel the particular loosening in his chest that he felt whenever she walked into a room.

"You have a soul escaping through sector nine," she said. "Again."

"Good morning, Lilith."

"It's eleven-thirty."

"Good morning."

She dropped the folder on his desk with the casual violence she applied to most things. "The containment wards are failing. I've told you this. I told you in August."

"You told me in August that I was a narcissistic relic with the administrative competence of a particularly dim seraph. The ward thing may have been in there somewhere."

"I said both things. They're not mutually exclusive."

He looked at her then, which was always a mistake, and always worth it. Four billion years old and she looked like a thunderstorm that had learned to wear a face. He had loved her before love had a name. He had driven her out of Eden and she had laughed at him while she left, and he had found that he loved her more for it, which was the central inconvenience of his existence.

"I'll have the wards seen to," he said.

"See that you do." She picked up the folder again, then didn't leave.

This was new. This had been happening for several weeks now—the not leaving.

"The ash," she said, looking at the window.

"Yes."

"It's been falling since Tuesday."

"I know."

"Do you know why?"

"I have theories."

She turned from the window. Something moved across her face that she immediately put away somewhere he couldn't see it. "You look terrible," she said.

"Thank you."

"When did you last sleep?"

"I'm the Lord of Hell, Lilith. Sleep is—"

"When."

He considered lying. He was very good at lying—had been, in fact, present at the invention of it—but she had known him since before the Fall, and she could read him the way old sailors read weather. "Thursday," he said.

She made a sound that was not quite sympathy and not quite contempt, but occupied the narrow, complicated territory between them. "I'll send someone to look at the wards," she said. "My people. Yours will make it worse."

"My people are perfectly—"

"Your people recently spent three weeks accidentally torturing each other instead of the damned because someone filed the rosters in the wrong cabinet."

"That was a clerical error."

"That was your cabinet."

She left. He watched the ash fall and felt the office become, in a way that had nothing to do with temperature, slightly colder.

---

The afternoon brought the usual horrors. A meeting with the Council of Sins, who were bickering about jurisdictional overlap between Sloth and Despair—a distinction that Lucifer maintained was largely academic. A call from an archangel, which he let go to voicemail. A damned soul who had somehow gotten his direct line and wanted to appeal their sentence; Lucifer listened for four minutes before transferring them to the Department of Appeals, which did not exist, and which therefore had an excellent hold time.

At dusk—Hell had a dusk; it was the colour of a bruise—his assistant brought him coffee and a note.

The note said: *The sector nine ward is fixed. Don't touch it. —L.*

Beneath that, in smaller writing, as though added quickly and without full commitment: *There's a place in the fourth circle that does the lamb. If you wanted. Thursday.*

He read it twice. He set it down. He picked it up and read it again.

Outside, the ash fell in its quiet, relentless way, grey and soft and endless, settling over Pandemonium like a blanket, like a question, like the particular silence that follows an argument that hasn't quite finished yet.

He wrote back: *Thursday.*

Then, after a moment: *You should know the coffee here is still terrible.*

Her reply came forty minutes later, which meant she had waited forty minutes before sending it, which meant she had written it immediately.

It said: *I know. I made it terrible. In the fourteenth century. You never noticed.*

He laughed. It echoed strangely in the empty office, bouncing off obsidian and old stone, and the ash kept falling outside, and Hell turned slowly on its axis, and somewhere in sector nine a ward held firm, and Lucifer Morningstar, for the first time since Thursday, felt something that was almost, almost, like rest.
