# Darkroom Revelations

The red light makes everything look like the inside of something alive.

I've thought that before, standing in here alone, but tonight the observation feels different because Elliot is beside me and the developing trays are full and neither of us has said anything for almost four minutes. I've been counting. Counting is what I do when I'm trying not to say the first thing that comes into my head, which is usually the wrong thing, or the right thing delivered too hard, like a fist through drywall.

I watch the image surface in the chemical bath. His image. A self-portrait he shot on medium format, face half-obscured by the sleeve of an oversized jacket—vintage, I think, some dead man's corduroy—and the composition is genuinely extraordinary. He has an eye that I find irritating and magnetic in equal measure. The kind of eye you can't teach.

"It's coming up nice," I say, because four minutes is my limit.

"Yeah." He leans closer to the tray, and his shoulder almost touches mine. Almost. "I wasn't sure about the exposure."

"You were right to trust it."

He makes a small sound. Not quite agreement. Something more private than that.

My phone vibrates in my pocket. Marcus, probably, or Jade wanting to know if I'm coming to the thing at the thing. I leave it. The darkroom has its own jurisdiction. What happens under red light operates by different rules, and I learned a long time ago not to import the outside world into spaces that are trying to be sacred.

Elliot reaches into the tray with the tongs and I watch his hands, which are fine-boned and deliberate, the nails painted black but chipped now, grown out. He's been wearing his hair differently this semester. Longer. Tucked behind one ear tonight, and there's a small silver hoop I don't remember seeing before catching the red light.

I have a type. I know my type. I've always known my type with the kind of certainty that felt like self-knowledge but might just have been habit. Defined. Legible. People who know what they are and wear it like armour. I am, myself, exactly that kind of person. Dani, 25, assertive, certain, occasionally exhausting. I take up the space I'm given and then a little more.

Elliot takes up almost none.

And yet.

"Can I ask you something?" he says, still looking at the photograph.

"Yeah."

"Do you ever—" He stops. Starts the tongs moving again, unnecessary agitation of the image. "Do you ever feel like the thing you're making is more honest than you are? Like the work knows something you don't?"

I look at him directly. He's not looking back.

"All the time," I say. "That's why I make work."

He nods slowly. The photograph is ready and he lifts it, holds it up toward the safelight, and his face in the image stares back at us both, half-hidden, half-revealed, and I feel something shift in the room's atmosphere the way pressure shifts before weather.

"I've been thinking," he says, "about—" Another stop. Longer this time. I wait. I am not naturally a waiter but I make myself still, the way you go still around something you don't want to frighten. "About who I am. Like, underneath the—" He gestures vaguely at himself. The jacket. The rings. The careful assemblage of Elliot. "I don't know if I'm—I don't know what I am, actually. Gender-wise. I've been sitting with that for a while."

The red light hums. Somewhere in the building a door closes.

"Okay," I say. Carefully. Not my usual volume.

"Is that—" He finally looks at me, and his eyes in this light are very dark and very uncertain. "Is that weird? To tell you?"

"No." I mean it completely. "Thank you for telling me."

He exhales. Sets the photograph in the rinse tray. His shoulders drop a half-inch, some small release of tension, and I feel an unfamiliar instinct rise in me—not the usual move-in, take-charge, fix-it instinct, but something quieter. Something that wants to hand him the space rather than fill it.

"I don't have language for it yet," he says. "I just know that some days I look in the mirror and I feel like—like the image doesn't match the negative. You know?"

I know the metaphor because he's speaking my language, and something about that undoes me a little.

"That's a good way to put it," I say.

"I look at my work and I think—she's in there. Or they. Or something that isn't quite what people see when they look at me." He picks up the tongs again, fidgets with them. "I haven't said that out loud before. The *she* part."

She.

I sit with that. I sit with what it does to the architecture of the attraction I've been quietly constructing all semester, the one I thought I understood, and I find that it doesn't collapse. It just—reconfigures. Becomes something I don't have a name for yet either, and maybe that's okay. Maybe we're both standing in the darkroom watching something develop that we can't quite see the edges of.

"I'm glad you said it," I tell her—and I let the pronoun land gently, a question and an offering at once—"out loud. Here."

Elliot looks at me. Something in her face opens.

"Yeah?"

"Yeah."

My phone buzzes again. I don't move.

The image floats in the rinse water between us, that half-hidden face, and the red light holds us both in its strange, forgiving warmth, and I think: *this is what the work knows before you do*. This exact moment. The negative and the print finally beginning to match.

I pick up my own tongs. I have photographs to develop.

We have time.
