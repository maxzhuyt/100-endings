# Overthinking It

Okay. Okay, okay, okay. He just sat down. That's fine. That's completely normal. People sit down all the time. It would actually be *weird* if he didn't sit down, so this is good. This is the best possible outcome. He sat down, he put his notebook on the table, he said "hey" — he said *hey* — and I said "hey" back, which, yes, was the correct response. A-plus. Gold star. Nobody would look at that exchange and think *something is wrong with that girl.*

Although I did say it kind of flat. Was it flat? It might have been flat. Or maybe it was too bright, like aggressively cheerful, like I'd been waiting. Which I hadn't been. I mean I had, but not in a way that should be detectable by the human eye.

The project sheet is right here. I should look at the project sheet. Looking at the project sheet is a completely legitimate thing to do because we are here to do a project, and the sheet contains information about the project, and I am a person who does projects.

*Thermal energy transfer in everyday materials.* Okay. I actually know a lot about this. I read ahead in the textbook in September, not because I'm the kind of person who reads textbooks in September — I'm not, I just — it was there, and I was bored, and now I have opinions about thermal conductivity that I have never shared with another living person. This is potentially useful. I could say something smart. I could open with something smart and he would think, *oh, she knows things,* and that would be a good foundation for —

No. No, leading with a thermal conductivity fact is not a normal thing to do. That's the kind of thing I do in my head, in conversations that exist only in my head, where the other person always responds perfectly because I wrote their lines. In those conversations I'm very charming. In those conversations I say things like "well, the thing about copper is —" and he laughs in a specific warm way and asks a follow-up question.

In real life I once tried to say "well, the thing about" and then forgot what the thing was about and said "never mind" and walked away.

He's uncapping his pen. I should also uncap my pen. We should both have uncapped pens. This is a moment of pen-uncapping synchronicity and I am going to capitalize on it by —

*Oh god, there's that smell again.*

Ammonia. It's been hovering at the edge of everything since third period, this sharp chemical ghost that gets into the back of your throat and sits there. Someone must have cleaned the floors last night and the ventilation in this room is genuinely criminal. I keep losing my train of thought to it. It smells like the inside of a very clean mistake.

Right. Okay. He's looking at the project sheet. I should say something. I should initiate. Mrs. Callahan said we have forty minutes and I have now spent approximately four of them narrating my own paralysis, so.

Option one: *"So I was thinking we could divide it into sections."*

Practical. Efficient. Maybe a little cold? Like I'm conducting a business meeting. Like I have a briefcase. Like I am thirty-five years old and this is a quarterly review. Scratch it.

Option two: *"Have you done anything like this before?"*

Oh, that's terrible. That sounds like a first date. That sounds like I googled "conversation starters" and picked the worst one. Which I did, actually, once, for a different situation, but that's not the point. The point is no.

Option three: Say nothing and just start filling in the worksheet and let the silence do whatever the silence wants to do.

This is my most frequent strategy and I've gotten very good at it. The problem is it reads as either deeply focused or deeply hostile and there's no way to control which one people decide.

Option four: *"The copper thing is interesting."*

Wait, why did I say that. I haven't said that yet. I'm thinking about saying that. The copper thing. Which I would then have to explain. And then he would look at me and I would have to continue existing in front of him while explaining the copper thing, and my voice might do that thing where it gets too fast, where I can hear myself accelerating like I'm trying to outrun the embarrassment of my own sentence —

He just wrote something on the worksheet. He wrote *materials: aluminum, copper, plastic* and then he looked at me and said, "We should probably pick a fourth one. What do you think?"

He asked me what I think.

He asked me what *I* think.

This is a direct question. I have been asked a direct question. This is actually the ideal scenario because I don't have to initiate, I just have to respond, and I know the answer, I genuinely know the answer, I have thought about this exact thing —

"Wood," I say. "Wood has really low thermal conductivity so it would give you a good baseline for comparison. Like a control, almost."

He's nodding. He wrote it down. He wrote *wood* on the worksheet and he's still nodding and he says, "Yeah, that's smart, actually."

That's smart, actually.

I look back down at my own paper. I write *thermal energy transfer* at the top in my neatest handwriting, which is a thing I do when I need to look like I'm doing something while my entire nervous system recalibrates. The ammonia smell drifts through again and I breathe through my mouth and think about copper and aluminum and wood and the specific way he said *actually,* like he meant it as a compliment and not a qualifier.

*That's smart, actually.*

I have forty more minutes. I should not think about that sentence for all forty of them.

I'm going to think about that sentence for all forty of them.
