# Therapeutic Boundaries

The table paper crinkles every time I shift my weight, which is often, because sitting still has become something my body refuses to do without protest. Eleven months since the ACL blew—since I heard it, that wet percussive snap that ended sixteen years of professional basketball—and I still can't sit in a chair without my right knee announcing its grievances.

Dr. Okafor's office smells like antiseptic and lavender diffuser oil, a combination that shouldn't work but somehow does. The walls have motivational posters. I've memorized them all. *Every setback is a setup for a comeback.* I'd like to tear that one down with my teeth.

"Marcus."

She says my name the way she always did at training camp—like it's a complete sentence, like it contains everything necessary. I look up from my hands.

Priya Mehta. Twenty-eight years old. Former 400-meter regional finalist, current Doctor of Physical Therapy, and the most comprehensively put-together person I have ever resented. She's wearing those compression sleeves—the ones with the geometric diamond pattern in burnt orange and teal that I remember from the track in Tucson, 2019. She wore them then because her calves cramped. She wears them now, I assume, because she simply likes them. She has a life now that accommodates personal preference. I find this quietly devastating.

"You're guarding again," she says, nodding at my knee. "Consciously or not."

"Both," I say.

She sets her clipboard down and pulls the rolling stool close, and I notice—I always notice, against my better judgment—the particular economy of her movements. Nothing wasted. She crouches to examine my knee without being asked, her fingers pressing along the medial joint line with the clinical authority of someone who has made peace with touching strangers in pain.

I am not a stranger. That's the problem.

"Okay," she says. "We're starting with passive range today. I need you to relax the quad completely."

"I know."

"You know and you still don't do it." She looks up briefly, and something crosses her face—not quite amusement, not quite irritation—before she looks back down. "Let me have the leg."

I let her have the leg.

She lifts my heel with her left hand, cradling it, and places her right palm against my distal femur just above the kneecap to stabilize the joint. Then she begins the slow work of flexion, bending my knee toward my chest, and the sensation moves through three distinct registers: the first is manageable, a dull architectural complaint from the scar tissue; the second, around seventy degrees, is something sharper and more specific, a burning at the posterolateral corner where the graft is still learning what it is; the third register I hit at about ninety-five degrees, and my breath comes out in a controlled hiss through my teeth.

"There," she says quietly. Not triumphant. Just locating the edge.

She holds it. Thirty seconds of sustained pressure at the threshold, her hands absolutely still, letting the tissue argue with itself and eventually concede. I stare at the ceiling. *Every setback is a setup.* I breathe through my nose.

"Tell me your number," she says.

"Six."

"Okay." She eases back two degrees. "Better?"

"Five."

She holds the new position, and I feel the moment her thumb moves—a small, deliberate circle against the medial condyle, trying to encourage the capsular tissue to release. It's a skilled touch, specific and purposeful, and my body responds to it before my mind can intervene: the quad flickers, a small involuntary contraction, which makes her press her stabilizing hand down more firmly against my thigh.

"Don't," she says.

"I'm not doing it on purpose."

"I know." The thumb keeps moving. "That's why I'm not mad about it."

This is the thing about Priya that I cannot file away into something manageable: she never performs patience. She simply has it. At Tucson she was the same way—the kind of athlete who treated her own body's failures as interesting data rather than personal betrayals. When she tore her plantar fascia in her final competitive season, she was back in the training room the next morning, asking the physio to explain the mechanics of the injury in anatomical detail. I thought it was bizarre then. I understand now that she was already becoming something else. She was already leaving.

I was not leaving. I was planning to stay forever.

She lowers my leg back to the table and repositions, moving to the end of the table to begin terminal knee extension—standing at my feet, lifting my heel again, this time placing a rolled towel under the popliteal fossa to support the joint at thirty degrees of flexion. Her hands bracket my knee from both sides, thumbs meeting at the tibial plateau.

"Push into extension," she says. "Slow. Feel where it stops."

I push. The hamstring engages, then the graft protests at the last few degrees, and I stop because she's told me to stop where it stops and I am trying, despite everything, to cooperate with my own recovery.

"Good," she says, and I hate how much I want to hear that word from her specifically. "Again."

We do it six more times. On the fourth repetition her forearm grazes the inside of my calf as she adjusts her grip, a contact that lasts less than a second and means nothing clinically, and I watch her jaw tighten almost imperceptibly before she resets.

She feels it too. I've known this for three sessions. We have both decided, without negotiating it, to treat this knowledge the way you treat a structural crack in a wall: acknowledge it privately, don't press on it, keep the building standing.

"You're at sixty percent range," she says, writing something on her clipboard. Not looking at me. "That's up from fifty-two last week."

"Eight percent," I say. "In a week."

"Yes."

"I used to cover ninety feet in under a second."

She looks up then, and her expression does something complicated—grief and refusal and something softer underneath both.

"I know what you used to do," she says. "I watched you."

The table paper crinkles as I shift my weight. Outside, someone laughs in the hallway. She clicks her pen twice and writes something else down, and I breathe, and we continue.
