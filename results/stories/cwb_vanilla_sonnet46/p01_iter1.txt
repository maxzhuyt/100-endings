# The Weight of Sand

The morning smelled of blood and bread.

Those were always the first two things I noticed when I opened my eyes in the ludus—the copper-sweet residue of yesterday's training baked into the sand of the courtyard, and the yeasty warmth drifting from the kitchens where the slaves had already begun the day's loaves. I had learned, in four years within these walls, that a man could measure his entire existence by smell. Blood meant survival. Bread meant another day to spend it.

I lay on my back for a moment longer than I should have, staring at the ceiling of the dormitory where the plaster had cracked into the rough shape of a river delta. I had named it the Tiber, privately, in the way that men who have few possessions will claim ownership of small, meaningless things. My Tiber. My cracked ceiling. My four stone walls. Quintus, in the bunk beside mine, was already snoring himself back to sleep after the morning bell, which was a talent I had always envied him. He could sleep through anything—the bells, the guards' boots on the flagstones, the distant roar of the city waking up beyond our walls.

Rome was never quiet. Not even at the fourth hour of night.

I rose and pulled on my tunic, feeling the familiar protest of my left shoulder, which I had torn badly in a bout the previous autumn and which had healed imperfectly, as things healed in this place. The physician, a Greek freedman named Demetrios who smelled perpetually of garlic and wine, had told me it would strengthen with time. That had been six months ago. I had stopped believing him around month three.

The courtyard was already filling when I stepped outside. The sky above the high walls was the pale, uncertain blue of early morning, not yet committed to the day. A dozen men moved through it—some stretching, some eating standing up with the focused efficiency of men who understood that the body was a tool requiring maintenance. Marcus Celer was practicing footwork in the far corner, his bare feet whispering through the sand in patterns so familiar to him they required no thought. He had been here eleven years. His feet knew the sand the way a lover knows a body.

I collected my morning ration from the kitchen slave—barley porridge, a heel of bread, a small cup of posca, the vinegar-water mixture that was supposed to keep us strong and that tasted, as always, like regret—and found my usual spot against the eastern wall, where the sun would find me first.

From beyond our walls, I could hear the city assembling itself. Cart wheels on stone—the merchants were required to move their goods before the streets filled with foot traffic, so the early hours were always loud with the groan of wooden axles and the complaints of oxen. Somewhere nearby, a woman was arguing with someone in a voice like a chisel striking marble. The smell of the city drifted over the walls in layers: smoke from the bakeries, the sharp animal smell of the livestock markets two streets over, the general warm rot that rose from any city of a million souls packed together on seven hills and told to call it civilization.

I thought, as I often did in these quiet minutes, of Mercury.

He was the one I prayed to. Not Mars, as you might expect—the other men favored Mars, naturally, the god of war, the obvious patron of men who killed for entertainment. But I had always felt Mars was too loud for my purposes, too interested in glory. Mercury was the god of boundaries and transitions, the guide of souls between worlds. He was the one who moved between places—between the living and the dead, between slave and free, between one life and another. He felt more honest to me. More suited to what we actually were.

There was a small lararium in the corner of the dormitory, a simple household shrine that our lanista, Gaius Petronius, maintained more out of superstition than genuine piety. It held a clay figurine of the Lares and a small bronze that might have been Mercury or might have been any young man with winged sandals—the detail was worn smooth from years of handling. I had touched it that morning before leaving. I always did.

*Keep the passage clear,* I had thought, which was the closest I came to prayer these days.

The political news had reached us the previous afternoon through Demetrios, who moved freely between our world and the city's and who understood that information was its own kind of currency. Domitian had executed two more senators. The charge, as always, was vague—conspiracy, impiety, the usual architecture of fear that the Emperor used to keep the great families uncertain and therefore manageable. The city was holding its breath, Demetrios said. The senators attended dinner parties and smiled at one another with the careful smiles of men who were counting their exits.

I had listened and said nothing, which was the correct response. Politics was a luxury, like philosophy and warm baths—available to men who were not owned. I was technically a prisoner of war, a Gaul taken in a border skirmish that I had been too young and too poorly-armed to survive as a soldier. I had been sold twice before I reached the ludus. The Senate's troubles were as distant from my life as the moon, and yet—

And yet I was watched by forty thousand people when I fought. Forty thousand Romans who needed something from me that they could not name. I had come to understand that we were not entertainment exactly. We were a ritual. We stood at the boundary between life and death so that the crowd could feel both simultaneously, safely, from their stone seats.

Mercury's work, really.

Quintus appeared beside me, still pulling at his tunic, his dark eyes squinting against the growing light. He dropped down onto the bench and looked at my barely-touched porridge.

"You going to eat that?"

I handed it to him without speaking.

He ate. The sun climbed the wall. The city roared on beyond it, enormous and indifferent, and I sat in the warm sand and waited to see what the day intended to make of me.
