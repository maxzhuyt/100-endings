# The Weight of Salt

The morning smelled of bread and blood, the way it always did.

I lay on my pallet long after the others had risen, listening to the sounds of the ludus coming alive around me — the scrape of sandals on stone, the low muttering of men who had learned to speak quietly even when no guards were near, the distant clang from the smithy where Titus was already sharpening something that would eventually find its way into someone's body. My body, perhaps. Someone else's, more likely. I had survived eleven fights. I had learned not to think too carefully about the arithmetic of that.

The cell was small enough that I could touch both walls if I stretched my arms wide, which I sometimes did in the night, not from any desire to measure the space but simply to confirm it was still there. The plaster was cool and slightly damp, the way stone stays damp in Rome even in summer, holding the memory of old water deep inside itself. Someone before me had scratched marks into the wall near the floor — I had always assumed they were counting days, though there were so many of them that the counting must have eventually stopped meaning anything.

I rose and dressed in the grey tunic they gave us, coarse wool that itched at the shoulders, and I went to find salt.

This was my ritual, and I kept it without apology.

Before every fight — before I even touched my training weapons, before I ate, before I spoke to any man — I found salt and I held a pinch of it on my tongue until it dissolved completely. My mother had told me once, back in Thrace when I was young enough to believe everything she said, that salt was the oldest gift the gods had made to men. That it preserved what was good and drove out what was corrupt. She pressed it into wounds when I fell from climbing trees. She scattered it across the threshold of our house each new month. When my father died, she placed a small clay bowl of it beside his body so that whatever waited for him on the other side would know he came from a household that honored the proper things.

I did not know if I still believed in the gods my mother believed in. Rome had so many gods that a man could lose himself trying to keep track of their preferences. But I believed in salt. I believed in the small, stubborn gesture of it, the way the brine spread across my tongue and made everything sharp and immediate and real. It reminded me that I had a body worth preserving. It reminded me that I intended to keep it.

Castor, one of the Gauls who trained in our cohort, saw me at the supply room and said nothing, which was the courtesy we extended to one another's private rituals. He had his own — he touched the scar on his left forearm three times before stepping onto the sand, murmuring something in his own language that none of us had ever asked him to translate. Barro, the enormous Nubian who had been here longer than anyone, simply stood still for a long moment with his eyes closed, his lips not moving. Whatever he was doing in that silence, it seemed to work. He had survived twenty-three fights.

We ate in the common room as the sun climbed higher and the city outside our walls began its daily roar. Rome was never quiet — I had learned this in my first weeks here, when the noise of it had seemed impossible, inhuman, the sound of a thing too large to be governed by any ordinary logic. Carts rattling over stone, vendors calling out in a dozen languages, the bells from temples, the arguments, the laughter, the crying of children, the occasional trumpet from some military procession moving through the streets on business that had nothing to do with us and everything to do with the world we were sealed away from. The Emperor Trajan was somewhere in that city today, or perhaps not — there were rumors he was moving troops again, extending the reach of Rome into Dacia, into Parthia, into whatever remained of the world that had not yet been folded into the empire's enormous, indifferent embrace.

I thought about that sometimes. The empire. The way it had reached all the way to Thrace and found me, a farmer's son, a young man who had made the particular mistake of fighting back when the soldiers came through our village looking for men who looked like they could be made useful. I had broken a soldier's nose. He had laughed about it afterward, which was almost worse than if he had been angry. *This one*, he had said to the others, pointing at me with the easy authority of a man accustomed to pointing. *This one has something in him.*

Something. I had spent four years trying to determine what that something was, whether it was worth having, whether it was the thing keeping me alive or the thing that would eventually get me killed in front of fifty thousand people who wanted their afternoon to be interesting.

After breakfast, we trained. I worked the wooden post until my shoulders burned and my hands bled slightly at the old calluses, and Draco, our lanista, walked the perimeter watching us with his flat professional eyes, occasionally stopping to correct a stance or berate someone for laziness. He was not a cruel man by the standards of his profession. He was simply a man who had organized his entire understanding of human beings around their capacity for violence, which in Rome was not considered an unusual way to think.

The sun reached its height and the heat settled over the ludus like a hand pressing down.

Tonight was not my night to fight. Tomorrow was not either. In three days, the games would begin for the festival, and my name was on the list that Draco kept rolled in his office, and I knew it, and every man in this place knew it, and we all continued doing what we were doing because there was nothing else to do.

I touched my tongue to the last trace of salt and tasted the sea, very far away.
