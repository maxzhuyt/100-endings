# Pattern Recognition

The WiFi password is taped to the hostess stand. Plaintext. Horizontal strip of masking tape, black Sharpie, twelve characters — *Riverdale2019!* — visible to every person who walks through the door. I clock it in the half-second between pulling the door open and stepping inside. I don't mean to. It just *happens*, the same way you can't stop yourself from reading a word once your eyes land on it.

*Predictable substitution pattern. Probably the owner's neighborhood. Exclamation point appended because some forgotten IT policy demanded a special character. I could be on their network in thirty seconds. I won't be. But I could.*

I find our table. She's already there, which means she's punctual, which is good, which means I'm slightly late, which is bad. Her name is Priya and her profile said she works in biotech and likes "nerdy stuff, hiking, and aggressively mediocre wine." I had assessed a 34% probability this date would survive appetizers.

I'm revising that estimate upward.

---

She's funny. Like, *actually* funny, not performing-funny. She makes a self-deprecating joke about her parking and I laugh before I can evaluate whether I should, which is rare. I'm usually three beats behind on genuine responses because I'm busy running parallel processes — cataloging exits, noting the waiter's body language, wondering why the table by the window has been empty for forty minutes despite a twenty-minute wait list.

*Corner booth. Reserved, maybe. Or the AC vent above it runs cold. Check the ceiling tile — yes, moisture stain. Nobody wants to sit under a slow leak. Smart crowd.*

"You okay?" Priya asks.

"Sorry. Yes. Completely here." *Mostly.*

The conversation finds its footing slowly, the way conversations do when two people are both trying too hard and both aware they're trying too hard. We talk about her work, my work — I say "cybersecurity consulting" which is true and vague and sounds less alarming than the fuller version. We talk about the restaurant, the neighborhood, the parking, circling the drain of small talk, and I'm starting to feel the familiar low-grade despair of connection-by-checklist.

And then she says, almost apologetically, like she's confessing something embarrassing: "Okay, random, but the art on that wall kind of looks like a Magic card."

I stop.

"Which set?" I ask.

She looks at me. Something shifts in her face — a door opening. "You play?"

"I *played*. Past tense, theoretically. I have four Commander decks that suggest otherwise."

She laughs — real, surprised, delighted. "I have *seven*."

*Probability of date survival: recalculating. Recalculating. Recalculating.*

We spend forty minutes in the pure unself-conscious joy of it. She runs a Meren reanimator deck and has opinions about it that are specific and correct. I confess my Zur the Enchanter phase with appropriate shame. We argue briefly and companionably about whether Stax is a legitimate playstyle or a personality disorder. She says personality disorder. I say both. She points at me and says "fair," and I think: *this is the thing. This is what it's supposed to feel like.*

I am, improbably, having a good time.

I am, improbably, not running threat assessments.

---

Then her phone buzzes on the table and she flips it face-up to check the notification, and she unlocks it, and I see it.

The pattern.

An L-shape. Starting top-left, straight down, straight right. Three points. The simplest possible gesture, requiring almost no fine motor engagement, traceable in about a quarter-second by anyone sitting within conversational distance, which I am.

I know I should let it go. I know this the way I know I should stop picking at loose threads on my jacket — consciously, clearly, and too late.

"That's not a great unlock pattern," I say.

She looks up. "Sorry?"

*Walk it back. You can still walk it back.* "Your pattern. It's very — it's a common one. Easy to observe."

There's a pause. The specific kind of pause that functions as a yellow warning light.

"Did you just... watch me unlock my phone?"

"I didn't *mean* to. It's just — L-shapes are in the top five most common patterns, and from this angle it was—" *Stop talking. You are actively making this worse.* "I'm sorry. It's a reflex. I look for vulnerabilities. Occupational thing."

"In my *phone*."

"In everything." I hear how that sounds. "That's — I don't mean it in a creepy way. I mean I walked in here and immediately knew the WiFi password from the hostess stand, and I noticed the couple by the bar has been arguing since before we arrived based on their posture, and the waiter is right-handed but keeps leading with his left which suggests a recent injury, and I—" 

I stop.

She's looking at me with an expression I'm actively trying to classify. It's not quite horror. It's not quite fascination. It's somewhere in the middle, in the territory of *I have just learned something significant about this person and I am deciding what to do with it.*

"You're like a... threat detection system," she says slowly. "That learned to walk around in a person."

*That is the most accurate description of me anyone has ever offered on a first date or possibly ever.*

"I'm working on the person part," I say.

Another pause. She picks up her wine glass. She takes a drink. She sets it down.

"Okay," she says. "What's the password?"

"Riverdale2019! Capital R, exclamation at the end."

She types it in. Connects. Looks up. "The waiter's injury — left or right wrist?"

"Left. Probably a sprain, not a break. He's using it, just favoring."

She nods slowly, like she's running her own calculations. "Change my pattern. To what?"

*Oh.* "Something asymmetrical. Longer. Don't start from a corner."

"Show me after dinner?"

I look at her. She's smiling, slightly. Not the polite smile from forty minutes ago. Something drier and more real.

"Yeah," I say. "Okay."

*Probability of second date: recalculating.*
