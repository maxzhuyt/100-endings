# Útiseta

I still don't know what we woke up that night. I'm not sure I want to.

---

Astrid had explained it to me the way she explained everything—leaning against her locker with that half-smile, like she was sharing a recipe. *Útiseta.* Sitting out. You go to a burial mound, she said. You go at midnight. You sit on the earth above the dead and you keep very still and you ask your question, and something answers. Her grandmother had done it. Her grandmother's grandmother. It was old, she said, older than the church that sat in the center of our village like a white stone dropped from a great height.

"What kind of question?" I'd asked.

"Any kind." She'd shrugged. "Last time I asked about Erik Lindström. Whether he'd kissed Maja at the summer party."

"Had he?"

She'd smiled wider. "He told me himself, three days later. Couldn't stop talking about it."

This was how Astrid worked. She always had an answer that was almost an answer. She'd been the one to show me the candle game in September, the one to teach me the rhyme you said over a photograph. I was new to Norrland, new to the dark and the cold and the silence that pressed against the windows at night, and Astrid was the only person who seemed genuinely interested in me. I told myself it was all theater. Teenage games. The kind of thing girls in Stockholm did with Ouija boards at sleepovers, just dressed in older clothes.

I told myself that.

---

We met at the edge of the village at eleven-thirty. Astrid had a canvas bag over her shoulder and she'd braided her hair in a way I hadn't seen her wear it before—two tight plaits, pinned flat. She handed me a bundle without explanation. A piece of undyed wool, folded into a rough square.

"Sit on this," she said. "Not directly on the earth. Not tonight."

"Why?"

She was already walking. "Because you're not ready to sit directly on the earth."

The mound was twenty minutes into the trees, up a slope where the pines grew close and the ground between them was carpeted in something dark and soft that I couldn't quite identify in the dark. My phone torch made everything worse—turned the trees into flat cutouts, made the shadows behind them absolute. I turned it off. Astrid hadn't used hers at all.

She knew the way without light. That bothered me more than anything else.

The mound was lower than I'd expected. Maybe a meter and a half of raised ground, oval-shaped, the grass on top longer than the grass around it. Even in November, even dead and frost-bitten, it was longer. Astrid set her bag down at the base and began laying things out with the efficiency of someone unpacking a lunch.

A small stub of tallow candle. Not wax—she'd been specific about that when she'd described it to me, and I'd thought she was being dramatic. Now I understood she'd simply been accurate. A piece of iron, a nail, old and reddish. Three small stones she'd carried from somewhere I didn't ask about. She arranged these at the cardinal points around the mound's crown.

"No circle," she said, reading something in my face. "That's for keeping things out. Tonight we're asking something in."

I almost left then. I want to remember that I almost left.

We climbed to the top of the mound and sat cross-legged on our wool squares, facing each other across the candle she'd sheltered between her palms to light. The flame was very small and very steady, which was wrong, because the wind was moving through the pines around us in long slow waves, and each wave brought a sound that was almost—*almost*—like syllables. Not words. The almost-before-words. The sound of something trying to arrange itself into meaning.

"Ask your question," Astrid said. Her voice was different up here. Quieter and more careful, the way you speak in a library, or a hospital.

"I don't have one."

"You do."

I thought about Marta Björk, who had been cruel to me in a specific and surgical way since my first week at school. I thought about what I'd wanted to know about her, the shape of her life that I might press on. I thought about Erik Lindström, and whether Astrid had really just *guessed*.

I didn't ask my question out loud. I thought it very clearly, the way Astrid had told me to, and I directed it downward, through the wool, through the frost-hard earth, into whatever dark and patient space existed beneath the mound.

The wind moved through the pines.

I don't know how long we sat there. Long enough that my feet went numb and the candle burned to half its length. Long enough that the almost-syllables in the pines started to feel less like wind and more like something that was *learning* the shape of language, practicing, getting closer.

And then I felt it. Under my hands, through the wool, through everything—a vibration. Rhythmic. Patient. Like breathing, but too slow to be breathing. Like a heartbeat, but too slow to be a heartbeat.

Like something very old, turning over in its sleep.

I looked at Astrid. Her eyes were open and fixed on the candle flame and her face had lost its half-smile completely. She looked, for the first time since I'd known her, genuinely frightened.

"Astrid," I whispered.

"Don't," she said. "Don't speak now."

"What is—"

"*Don't.*"

The candle went out.

The wind moved through the pines, and this time I heard it clearly—not words, not quite, but the *shape* of a word. One long, low, patient sound that rose and fell and rose again.

We ran. We left the stones, the iron nail, the tallow stub.

Astrid never mentioned it afterward. Not once. And Marta Björk, who had been cruel to me in a specific and surgical way—

She stopped. Completely. The following Monday.

I never asked Astrid why.
