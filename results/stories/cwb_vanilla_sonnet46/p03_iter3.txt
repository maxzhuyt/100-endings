# The Wrong Kind of Quiet

The bell above the door didn't so much ring as wheeze, and Maddox Cole — who had, in the last calendar year, graced the covers of *Empire*, *GQ*, and the front page of the *Daily Mail* three times — stumbled into Penmorfa Books looking like a man who had just been chased by wolves.

He had been chased by something worse.

"Shut the door," he said, to no one in particular, then shut it himself and pressed his back against it. Through the rain-blurred glass, two figures with cameras jogged past without stopping. He exhaled.

"We're open, by the way," said a voice. "In case you were wondering. No need to ask."

He turned.

Behind the counter, a woman was looking at him over the top of a hardback, with the particular expression of someone who had been enjoying five minutes of peace and was conducting a calm audit of exactly how much it had been disturbed. She had dark hair escaping from a knot, ink on her left index finger, and the air of a person who was not, even slightly, impressed.

"Sorry," Maddox said. He smiled. It was the smile — the one that had made a film critic in Los Angeles describe him as *'disarmingly, almost unfairly attractive.'* "Bit of a situation outside."

"Mm." She turned a page.

He looked around the shop. It was narrow and warm, crammed floor to ceiling with books in a way that suggested organisation had been attempted and then affectionately abandoned. Rain tapped at the window. Somewhere, a radiator ticked.

"You're not going to ask what kind of situation?"

"Not particularly, no."

Maddox blinked. He drifted toward the counter with the practiced ease of a man who had learned, very young, that moving toward people usually worked in his favour. "I'm Maddox Cole."

"I know who you are." She didn't look up. "You're blocking the Welsh Poetry section."

He stepped aside. "You recognised me."

"You're very tall and you've been in several films. It's not detective work." She finally lowered the book. Her eyes were sharp and brown and thoroughly unimpressed. "I'm Rhiannon. This is my shop. Are you going to buy something, or are you just using it as a bunker?"

"Both, potentially." He leaned an elbow on the counter. "What are you reading?"

She held up the cover. *The Complete Works of R.S. Thomas.*

Something shifted in his expression — not the performance of interest, but the real thing, arriving uninvited. "Seriously?"

"Why *seriously?*"

"I've been trying to get through *Pietà* for three weeks. It keeps stopping me dead. I get four lines in and I have to put it down."

Rhiannon was quiet for a moment. She set the book on the counter. "Which four lines?"

He straightened, and for the first time since entering the shop, looked slightly uncertain. "*I have nowhere to go. The hand I put out / to feel the pulse of the wind / encounters nothing—*" He stopped. "Then I just sit there feeling a bit bleak."

"That's the correct response," she said. Something in her voice had changed — not warmer, exactly, but less armoured. "Most people find him too austere."

"Most people haven't spent a lot of time alone on film sets at three in the morning."

"Is that where you read poetry? Film sets?"

"Is that strange?"

"It's not what I pictured."

"What did you picture?"

She picked up her book again, but he noticed she didn't open it. "Someone reading scripts, mostly. Possibly looking at himself in mirrors."

Maddox laughed — a real one, shorter and less polished than his usual. "I do that *after* the poetry." He glanced along the shelves. "Do you have *Not That He Brought Flowers?*"

"Third shelf, poetry section. Left side, near the bottom. Behind the Gillian Clarke."

He found it, crouching down with a slight lack of grace that he didn't seem to notice or perform. He pulled it out and stood, turning it in his hands with a kind of quiet attention that Rhiannon watched for a moment longer than she meant to.

"You can sit, if you need to wait them out," she said, with the tone of someone making a practical concession rather than an invitation. "There's a chair by the window. It's got a cat on it, but she'll move."

"What's the cat's name?"

"Saunders."

"After Saunders Lewis?"

"After no one else." She allowed the smallest possible smile. "She's very nationalist. She'll judge you if you don't speak Welsh."

"*Dw i'n dysgu,*" Maddox said, with an accent that was earnest and imperfect.

Rhiannon stared at him. "You're learning Welsh."

"I'm filming outside Abergavenny for six weeks. It seemed rude not to try." He paused. "That's probably the most impressive thing about me. More impressive than the films."

"It is, actually." She said it before she'd decided to, and something crossed her face — a brief, involuntary candour. She looked back down at her book. "The chair's by the window."

He took the Thomas collection with him. Saunders the cat regarded him with slow yellow eyes, then stood, stretched with magnificent indifference, and relocated to a shelf between Penelope Fitzgerald and some local history.

The shop went quiet, except for the rain and the radiator.

After perhaps ten minutes, Rhiannon looked up. He was reading — genuinely reading, one foot propped on the chair's edge, the book held close in the grey window light. He hadn't checked his phone. He hadn't looked at his reflection in the glass.

He turned a page and said, without looking up, "The paparazzi have gone, by the way. You're not obliged to keep harbouring me."

"I'm aware," she said.

He looked up then, and she was already looking back at her book.

Outside, the rain continued its quiet, indifferent work on the slate rooftops of Penmorfa, and neither of them moved to leave.
