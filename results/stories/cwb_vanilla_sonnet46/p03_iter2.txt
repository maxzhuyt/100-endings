# Love in the Limelight

## Chapter Seven

The bell above the door didn't ring so much as shriek — someone had come in fast, shoulder-first, and the little brass fitting cracked against the frame hard enough to make Meredith look up from her stocktaking.

The man had his back to her, peering through the gap in the door he'd left open two inches, watching the street. He was breathing hard. He was also, she noticed, absurdly good-looking, which felt like an irrelevant detail and yet somehow wasn't.

"We're open," she said. "Since you're already in."

He turned. The face resolved into someone recognisable — not personally, but in that strange way of famous people, as though you'd met them somewhere you couldn't quite place. She placed it after a moment. Rhys Calloway. She'd seen two of his films without particularly meaning to.

"Brilliant," he said, flashing a smile of almost architectural perfection. "Lovely shop."

"You haven't looked at it."

He glanced around, as if to correct this oversight. "Lovely now that I have."

"There are paparazzi outside," she said. It wasn't a question. She'd seen the cluster of them from her window twenty minutes ago, migrating up St. Mary Street like a slow and purposeful weather system.

"Paparazzi is a strong word."

"What word would you prefer?"

"Enthusiastic members of the public."

"With cameras."

"People have hobbies." He let the door close and turned to face her properly. He had the particular ease of someone who had learned, through long practice, to make every room feel like it had been waiting for him. "I don't suppose you'd tell them I'm not here, if they come in?"

"I'd suppose I'd tell them the truth."

He paused. It was a very brief pause, barely a stutter in the performance, but she caught it. "Which is?"

"That a man came in, bought something, and left."

"I haven't bought anything."

"Then you'd better," Meredith said, and returned to her stocktaking.

She heard him move into the shop — the particular sound of someone navigating a small space they hadn't expected to have to navigate. *Chapters & Dust* was not a large bookshop. It was a deep one, floor-to-ceiling shelves receding into the back like a paper forest, the kind of shop that required commitment.

"Do you know who I am?" he asked, from somewhere in Biography.

"Yes."

"Just checking." A pause. "Most people—"

"I imagine most people make rather a lot of it."

"They're usually pleased to meet me, yes."

"I'm sure that's very gratifying." She ticked something off her clipboard. "The Welsh fiction is on your left if you want something to read while you hide."

"I'm not hiding. I'm browsing."

"Mmm."

She heard him pull a book from the shelf. Then another. She didn't look up, which cost her slightly more effort than she would have liked to admit. There was something almost funny about it — Rhys Calloway, who had apparently once shut down a street in Los Angeles for a film shoot, standing in her 400-square-foot shop on a Tuesday afternoon in Cardiff, leafing through a Caradog Prichard novel.

"This any good?" he called.

"*Un Nos Ola Leuad*? It's one of the greatest Welsh-language novels of the twentieth century."

"Is it in English?"

"That's the translation. Yes."

"Right." Another pause. "Are you Welsh?"

"My shop is called *Chapters & Dust* and I'm standing in Cardiff. What do you think?"

"I think you're not answering the question."

She looked up at that. He'd appeared at the end of the shelf, leaning against it with the book in one hand and an expression that was different from the first one — less performed, more curious. It was a better look on him.

"Merthyr, originally," she said. "You?"

"London. But my mother's from Abergavenny."

"That explains the *S4C* documentary, then."

Something crossed his face — genuine surprise, and under it, something more uncertain. "You watched that?"

"My mother did. She cried. She's very easily moved by things."

"And you're not."

"I'm moved by books," Meredith said, "which is professionally convenient."

He was quiet for a moment. Outside, she could hear the distant sound of voices on the street, the particular frequency of people looking for something. He glanced toward the window, then back at her, and she had the odd sensation of watching someone remember where they actually were.

"Can I ask you something?" he said.

"You've been asking me things."

"Something different." He set the Prichard down on the nearest surface — not a shelf, just a surface, which she would have to correct later. "Do you actually not care, or is it a thing you do?"

"I'm sorry?"

"The — " he gestured, vaguely, at her. "The not being impressed. Is it real or is it a bit?"

Meredith set her clipboard down. She looked at him steadily, this man who was used to being looked at in a very different way, and felt — despite herself, despite every sensible instinct — the low, inconvenient pull of someone who had just, accidentally, asked her a real question.

"It's real," she said. "Though you're more interesting than I expected, if that helps."

"It does, actually." He said it quietly, and the smile that followed was smaller than the first one and considerably more dangerous. "That's the nicest thing you've said to me."

"It's the only nice thing I've said to you."

"I know. That's why it counts." He picked the book back up. "I'll take this."

She moved to the till. He followed, and stood across the counter from her, and the shop felt, suddenly, about half its already-modest size.

"Meredith," he said, reading her name badge.

"That's what it says."

"I'm Rhys."

"I know who you are."

"You said that before." He handed over the book. "I'm choosing to find it charming."

"You can choose whatever you like," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt, which was, she decided, a small and private victory.

The bell above the door shrieked again as a photographer leaned in.

Neither of them looked away from each other.
