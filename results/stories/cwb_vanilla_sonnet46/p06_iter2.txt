# Carnival of Dangerous Intentions

The shooting gallery smelled of gunpowder and salt air. She stood at its edge, just beyond the amber spill of the overhead bulbs, watching the other women lean into the counter with their escorts behind them, guiding their wrists, whispering instructions that had nothing to do with aim. She understood that particular theater. She had performed it herself, once, with someone whose name she no longer permitted herself to say.

The fortune teller's booth was a few yards to her left — a cramped, curtained structure draped in burgundy velvet gone thin at the folds. A hand-lettered sign read *Madame Ouspenskaya, Revelations by Appointment or Impulse*. The palmistry charts tacked to the outer panel were water-stained and faded, their diagrams of the hand divided into territories like a contested map: the Mount of Venus, the Girdle of Venus, the Plain of Mars. She found herself studying the lines of her own palm without meaning to, tracing the crease that supposedly governed love, which, according to the chart, ran dangerously close to the one governing ruin.

She pressed her hand flat against her skirt.

*He won't come*, she told herself. *Men like that never come when you actually want them to. They appear only when you've decided to stop wanting them.*

But she had not stopped wanting him. That was the problem she had been refusing to name since Tuesday.

She heard him before she saw him — a low, unhurried voice cutting through the mechanical clatter of the gallery, saying something to the barker that made the barker laugh in spite of himself. Then he was beside her, not having approached so much as materialized, the way certain problems do.

"You came," he said. Not a question. Not quite a statement. Something in between, like a card placed face-down on a table.

"I was in the neighborhood." She kept her eyes on the gallery.

"Coney Island." He seemed to taste the words. "On a Thursday evening. In those shoes."

She looked down involuntarily. Her heels were impractical and she knew it. She had known it when she put them on.

"What's wrong with my shoes?"

"Not a thing." He was watching her with those eyes she had been trying to reconstruct all week and failing — too dark to read, too steady to dismiss. "They're the shoes a woman wears when she wants to be looked at but intends to deny it."

*The audacity.* She felt the color rise in her throat. "You don't know anything about me."

"I know you've been standing here for at least ten minutes." He glanced toward the fortune teller's booth. "I know you looked at that chart long enough to memorize it. And I know you haven't told anyone you're here."

The last part landed differently than the rest. She didn't ask how he knew. She wasn't sure she wanted the answer, and she wasn't sure the answer would be innocent.

"You're very confident for someone who doesn't know my name," she said.

"Tell me your name."

"I didn't say I would."

A slow smile crossed his face — not warm, exactly, but interested, the way a chess player looks when the other side makes an unexpected move. He was younger than she was, she thought. Perhaps by three years. Perhaps by five. It ought to have made him easier to manage. It did not.

"All right," he said. "Keep it. I'll learn it eventually."

*The presumption of that sentence.* She turned it over in her mind the way you turn over something you've found on the ground, uncertain whether it's valuable or dangerous, suspecting it might be both.

"I should go," she said, and made no move to go.

"The fortune teller is open," he said, nodding toward the booth. "We could let her settle it."

"Settle what?"

"Whether you should stay or go." He said it lightly, but his eyes were not light. "You seem like a woman who prefers to have someone else make the difficult decisions."

That stung because it was partially true and entirely unfair. She had made every difficult decision in her life alone, and badly, and without anyone's help, and she resented the implication enormously.

"I make my own decisions," she said.

"Then make one."

The midway noise swelled around them — a child shrieking with delight somewhere, a barker calling the odds on something, the mechanical groan of a ride gathering speed. The bulbs overhead hummed their yellow hum. She was aware of the warmth of him, which was closer than it should have been, and of the fact that she had not stepped back.

*What are you doing?* The question arrived in her own voice, precise and unhelpful. *You know what this is. You have seen this before. You have been this before, on the other side of it, and you know exactly where the current goes.*

She knew. And she stayed.

"One reading," she said. "Then I'm leaving."

"Of course," he said, and there was that smile again — not triumphant, which she could have resisted, but something quieter and more dangerous. *Certain.*

He held the curtain aside for her. The gesture was courteous. The gesture was also a threshold, and they both understood it as such. She ducked beneath the burgundy velvet into the dimness beyond, catching the scent of incense and old wood and, beneath it, the sea.

Inside, a small woman with enormous rings on every finger looked up from her table without surprise, as though she had been expecting exactly this — exactly them.

"Sit," said Madame Ouspenskaya. Her eyes moved between them with professional calm, and then settled on the woman's face with something that was not quite pity and not quite warning.

"Give me your hand," she said. "The left one. The left tells me what you were born with." She paused. "The right tells me what you've done with it."

Behind her, she heard him settle into his chair without a word.

She placed her left hand on the table.
