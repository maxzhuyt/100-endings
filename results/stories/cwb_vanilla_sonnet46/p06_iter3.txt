# Carnival of Dangerous Intentions

The shooting gallery smelled of gunpowder and rust, and beneath that, drifting from somewhere further down the boardwalk, the spun-sugar sweetness of cotton candy. The scent caught her unexpectedly, the way it always did — a door swinging open onto a room she couldn't quite enter. A summer, somewhere. A hand holding hers. The memory dissolved before she could press it flat and examine it, leaving only the feeling: something warm, something lost.

She straightened her gloves.

The boardwalk was filling now with the evening crowd, the kind of people who came to Coney Island after supper — couples, mostly, the women in light dresses with their hair pinned back against the heat, the men with their jackets slung over one shoulder. Everyone moving with that particular looseness that came from proximity to the ocean, as though the salt air had unfastened something in them. Ruth watched them and felt, by contrast, very tightly fastened indeed.

She was thirty-one years old. She was a widow of four years. She had absolutely no business being here.

*His locks.* There it was again, that ridiculous phrase, surfacing like something buoyant she kept trying to push underwater. She had read too much of her mother's poetry as a girl, that was the trouble. It had given her a vocabulary for feelings she was supposed to have outgrown.

She heard him before she saw him.

"You came."

He was leaning against the wooden frame of the gallery, one shoulder dropped, watching her with an expression she couldn't immediately categorize. Not quite amusement. Not quite relief. Something that contained both without committing to either. He was younger than she'd let herself remember — twenty-four, perhaps twenty-five — and the carnival lights were doing him no favors in terms of her composure, gilding those dark curls, throwing shadows that had no right to fall where they were falling.

"I was in the neighborhood," she said.

"In the neighborhood." He smiled at that, slowly. "Coney Island."

"I like the ocean."

"So do I." He pushed off the frame and fell into step beside her without asking permission, which she found both irritating and — she would not examine what else. "I wasn't sure you would come back. You left very quickly, the other night."

"I had a train to catch."

"You told me you lived in Brooklyn."

She had told him that. She remembered the precise moment she had told him that, standing at the edge of the carousel in the blue-gray dusk, and the way his eyes had moved to her face as though she had said something far more interesting than her borough of residence. She had caught the train to Manhattan and sat in the rattling car for forty minutes before she realized she was going in the wrong direction.

"I also," she said carefully, "don't know your name."

"Daniel." He said it without the ceremony she might have expected, as though the information were incidental. "And you're Ruth. I remembered."

"You have a good memory."

"Only for certain things."

They walked. The boardwalk boards were warm through her shoes, the day's heat stored in the wood. Somewhere to their left, a barker was calling out for takers at the ring toss, his voice carrying that particular carnival cadence — rhythmic, slightly hypnotic, designed to make the ordinary transaction of handing over money feel like participation in something larger. She understood the mechanism and found herself susceptible to it anyway.

*This is what you are doing,* she told herself. *You are being worked, like a crowd.*

"You're quiet," Daniel said.

"I'm thinking."

"About what?"

She glanced at him sideways. "About whether this is a good idea."

He stopped walking. She took two more steps before she stopped as well, and turned. He was looking at her with that unclassifiable expression again, and she noticed — not for the first time, but more acutely now — that his stillness was unusual. Most young men his age were in constant motion, full of nervous energy, always reaching for a cigarette or adjusting their hat. Daniel simply stood.

"And?" he said.

"The jury is still out."

"What would help them reach a verdict?"

*Nothing you could offer,* she thought, *that wouldn't complicate the deliberations considerably.* What she said was: "I don't know anything about you."

"You know my name. You know I'm at Coney Island on a Thursday evening in July, which tells you something about my character, or my employment situation, or both." He tilted his head. "You know I came back to the same spot two nights running, hoping you would too."

The cotton candy smell came again, stronger now — a cart passing nearby, the vendor calling out prices, and with it that fractured almost-memory: a fairground, or something like one, and laughter she could almost hear. She had been very small. Someone had been holding her hand. She didn't know why she couldn't place it; she knew every room of her childhood, or thought she did.

"That's not nothing," she said, meaning the two nights running.

"No," Daniel agreed. "It isn't."

The carousel music started up from somewhere behind them, that slightly mournful calliope sound that she had always thought was the most honest thing about carnivals — the way the cheerfulness was just one register above a minor key. She was aware of the distance between them, approximately three feet of salt-warm air, and she was aware of being aware of it, which was worse.

"I'll have a coffee with you," she said. "There's a place at the end of the boardwalk."

"Just coffee."

"Just coffee."

He nodded, and something shifted in his expression — not victory, not quite, but its quieter cousin. He fell into step beside her again, and she looked straight ahead at the lit-up length of the boardwalk, all those buzzing bulbs strung against the darkening sky, and thought: *I know exactly what I'm doing.*

She was not at all sure that was true.
