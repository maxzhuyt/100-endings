# Chapter Two

The guide's name was Pelletier, and he was the kind of man who filled silence the way water fills a boot—completely, naturally, without apparent effort. JT had known him since childhood, had listened to him talk through entire meals, entire evenings, entire days afield, his voice a comfortable drone about weather patterns and animal sign and the particular behavior of bulls in rut. Pelletier talked the way other men breathed. It was simply what he did.

Which was why the quiet that surrounded him now felt so wrong.

JT noticed it first at dinner that second night. They'd come back to the lodge with one animal between them—his father's bull, tagged and hung in the cold shed out back—and Pelletier had cooked the meal as he always did, moving around the small kitchen with practiced efficiency, setting plates down without ceremony. But he hadn't said a word about the hunt. Hadn't asked where they'd been, what they'd seen, whether the sign was good or the rut was moving. He'd just cooked and served and then retreated to the far end of the table, working his fork through his food with the mechanical focus of a man trying very hard to think about nothing.

JT watched him from across the table while his father and Mike argued pleasantly about bullet placement. His father was in good spirits. Whatever had happened in the clearing seemed to have passed through him like weather, leaving no mark that JT could see. Mike was the same—laughing at his own jokes, tearing bread, entirely himself. JT had been watching them both since the clearing, searching for some residue of whatever he'd witnessed, and finding nothing. This bothered him more than he could say.

"Good meat," his father said, nodding toward the stew. "Pelletier, you've outdone yourself."

Pelletier looked up. He smiled, and it was a real enough smile, but it didn't quite reach his eyes, which were doing something JT couldn't name—not fear exactly, more like the careful stillness of a man who has decided not to look at a particular corner of a room.

"Season's been strange," Pelletier said, and then seemed to regret saying even that much. He looked back down at his plate.

JT's father waited for more. When it didn't come, he exchanged a glance with Mike—*old Pelletier, getting eccentric in his age*—and returned to his bread.

But JT kept watching.

---

He caught Pelletier alone the following morning, before first light, when the lodge was still breathing the slow breath of sleeping men. JT had come down for water and found the guide standing at the kitchen window with a mug of coffee, staring out at the tree line, which was barely visible as a darker darkness against the pre-dawn sky.

Pelletier heard him on the stairs and turned, and for just a moment—just a fraction of a second—his face arranged itself into an expression JT would spend a long time trying to forget. It was gone before JT could be certain he'd seen it at all. By the time the guide's face settled into its familiar arrangement, it was merely tired. Merely old.

"Couldn't sleep either," JT said, because it seemed like the thing to say.

Pelletier nodded, turning back to the window. "Coffee's hot."

JT poured himself a mug and stood beside the older man, looking out at the same darkness. The woods were quiet in a way that felt considered. No wind. No owls. Just the particular silence of things that have stopped moving because they've heard something approach.

"You've been quiet," JT said carefully.

"Man gets quiet as he ages."

"Not you. You've never been quiet a day in your life."

Pelletier said nothing for a moment. He drank his coffee. Outside, something shifted at the edge of the tree line—a branch moving without wind, or maybe just JT's eyes making shapes from shadow. He blinked and it was gone.

"When's the full moon?" JT asked. He didn't know why he asked it. The question arrived in his mouth fully formed, as though it had been waiting there.

The mug stopped halfway to Pelletier's lips.

"Three days," the guide said. His voice had changed slightly—flattened, the way a voice flattens when a man is working to keep it steady. "Give or take."

"We're planning to stay through the weekend."

Pelletier set his mug down on the counter with a small, precise click. He turned to look at JT directly, and this time whatever was in his eyes stayed there long enough to be read. It wasn't fear. It was something more considered than fear. It was the expression of a man who has done a private calculation and arrived at an answer he doesn't like.

"You should think about leaving before then," Pelletier said. "All of you."

JT waited.

"The season's been strange," the guide said again, as though the phrase were a door he kept approaching and then stepping back from. "The animals. The way they've been moving. Or not moving." He paused. "You understand what I mean."

JT thought about the first caribou, watching them with that patient, waiting calm. He thought about the clearing. He thought about his father's hand, trembling, reaching toward the muzzle of a bull that should have been gone before they'd even raised their rifles.

"Tell me what you know," JT said.

But Pelletier had already picked up his mug and was moving toward the hallway, the conversation apparently concluded by some internal rule JT wasn't privy to.

"I know the moon," the guide said, without turning around. "And I know these woods. And I know that some years, the season ends before the hunters are ready for it to."

He disappeared up the stairs.

JT stood alone in the kitchen, looking out at the tree line, which was beginning now to resolve itself from the darkness as the sky went gray and thin at its edges. The woods looked exactly as they always had.

That was the part that frightened him most.
