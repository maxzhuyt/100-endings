# The Weight of the Trident

The morning began the same way every morning began — with the smell of other men's sweat.

I lay on my wooden bunk in the pre-dawn dark and listened to the barracks breathe around me. Forty men packed into a long stone room, their sleeping sounds rising and falling like the tide against the Ostian shore. Coughing. Muttering. The wet, rhythmic grinding of Barca's teeth from the bunk above mine, a sound I had come to hate with a quiet, practiced hatred over the course of three years. Beyond the barred window, Rome was already moving. It never truly slept. I could hear the rumble of the supply carts that were banned from the city streets during daylight hours, the distant bark of a dog, the occasional shout of a carter cursing his oxen. The eternal machinery of the city, grinding on without pause or sentiment.

I reached under my bunk and found the trident by touch alone. My fingers closed around the shaft — Gallic ash wood, wrapped in leather cord at the grip, worn smooth where my palms had shaped it over months of handling. Three tines of iron, each one filed to a point I could have threaded a needle with. I did not need to see it. I knew it the way I once knew my mother's face, by something deeper than sight.

They called me Nereidon. The crowd's name for me, not my own. My own name was Caius, and I had been a fisherman's son from Brundisium before debt made me desperate and desperation made me foolish. The lanista had seen something in my arms and shoulders when he came recruiting at the debtor's prison, and he had paid what I owed and brought me here, to this barracks beside the great training ground, and explained to me very simply that I now belonged to him.

The retiarius fights with a net and trident. It is considered by many in the crowd to be the lowest class of gladiator — effeminate, they say, because we wear no helmet, no armor on the chest, only a shoulder guard and a leather arm wrapping. We are meant to be seen. Our faces are exposed so the crowd can watch us suffer or triumph in equal measure. Some of the other men looked down on me for it. Barca, a Thracian who fought with a curved sica and a small shield, had called me a fisherman once in the yard, meaning it as an insult.

He was not wrong. That was the thing.

The trident was the tool of my father's trade before it became the instrument of my survival. When I first picked one up in training, my hands had remembered something my mind had tried to forget — the angle of a throw, the feel of resistance, the particular patience required to wait for the right moment. My father had been a patient man. He had taught me that the sea does not reward panic. I had taken that lesson and applied it to the sand of the arena, and I had survived eleven fights, which was eleven more than most men expected of me when I first arrived.

I rose before the horn sounded and walked out into the training yard.

The sky above Rome was the color of a bruise healing — purple fading to a sickly yellow at the horizon. The air smelled of bread from the bakeries on the Via Sacra, and beneath that, the permanent undercurrent of the city: smoke, animal dung, the faint sweetness of rotting vegetables from the markets, the mineral smell of the aqueduct water that fed the fountain in our yard. I stood at that fountain and splashed my face and looked up at the walls that surrounded us.

They were not, technically, a prison. This was what the lanista, Quintus Fabius, liked to remind us. We were under contract. We were professionals. He said these things and smiled his merchant's smile, and I thought about the guards on the gate and said nothing.

Rome was celebrating something. It was always celebrating something. In three days there would be games at the amphitheater to mark some anniversary of the Emperor Domitian's reign — I had lost track of which one. The Emperor's need for spectacle had grown more extravagant with each passing year, fed, I suspected, by the growing whispers against him in the Senate. I had heard the guards talking. A lanista hears everything, because no one thinks to guard their words around property. Domitian was afraid. Fear in an emperor was a dangerous thing for everyone below him, which was everyone.

The games would be large. Which meant the crowd would be large. Which meant the pressure on each man in this yard would be immense and specific and personal.

I began to work through the forms alone, the trident moving in slow arcs through the cool morning air. The net hung coiled at my hip, lead weights sewn into its edge, and I felt its familiar drag as I moved. The practice was meditative. My breathing slowed. I thought about my father, who had died of a fever when I was sixteen, leaving me the boat and the debts attached to it. I thought about how differently a life can turn. I thought about the crowd that would fill those stone seats in three days, senators and slaves and merchants and their wives, eating their sausages and placing their bets, looking down at us the way a man looks down at figures on a game board.

I did not hate them for it. That had surprised me, when I discovered it. I had expected to find hatred and found instead something more complicated — a kind of dark understanding. The crowd needed us to be either heroes or monsters so they did not have to think about what we actually were.

What we actually were was men. Men who had run out of other options.

The horn sounded. Around me, the barracks began to wake. Another day had begun its slow, inevitable work upon us all.
