# Darkroom Revelations

The red light makes everyone look like they're confessing something.

I've thought about this before—how the darkroom strips away the performance of daylight. How people stand differently in here, shoulders dropped, voices quieter, like the safelight has some agreement with the subconscious. I've done some of my best thinking in darkrooms. Some of my worst decisions too, if I'm being honest.

Tonight it's just Elliot and me, and the chemical smell of developer, and the slow emergence of images in the tray between us.

---

I noticed him in September. Hard not to—he walked into Kowalski's experimental photography seminar wearing a sheer black top under a crushed velvet blazer, silver rings stacked on every finger, hair falling across his face in this careful-careless way that suggested he'd thought about it without trying to look like he had. He sat in the back. He didn't speak for two weeks. When he finally did, it was to quietly dismantle the entire class's reading of Francesca Woodman, and everyone just sort of *stopped*, and I thought: *oh, you're interesting.*

I'm not usually patient with interesting. I tend to move toward things I want. It's a character trait, or a flaw, depending on who you ask.

But something about Elliot made me slow down. Maybe because he seemed like something that could startle easily. Not in a fragile way—more like a deer that's actually watching you the whole time, deciding.

---

"Can you hand me the tongs?"

His voice is soft in the red dark. I pass them without looking up from the tray, where one of our test prints is developing—a double exposure he shot of a fire escape overlaid with what I can only describe as his own hands, reaching.

"This one's working," I say.

"Yeah." He leans over the tray beside me, and I'm aware of how close that puts him. He smells like cedar and something synthetic, like fabric from a thrift bin that's been washed too many times. "I wasn't sure about the exposure time."

"Your instincts are good."

A pause. He makes a small sound that isn't quite agreement.

We work in the comfortable silence we've built over three late sessions now. I've learned his rhythms—when he's thinking versus when he's uncertain, the difference between his quiet and his *quiet*. I've been careful not to push. This is new for me. I keep catching myself about to say something directive and swallowing it back down.

*Give him room*, I keep thinking. *Just give him room.*

---

It happens when I reach across him for the stack of photo paper.

I catch it wrong—my elbow, his arm, the box tilting—and the lid comes off and the paper fans out across the counter directly under the white work light I'd forgotten to switch off when we came in.

"Oh, *shit*—"

We both lunge. Too late. Twenty sheets, exposed, ruined. I stand there holding the empty box feeling like an idiot.

"Dani." His voice is careful.

"That's sixty dollars of paper." I set the box down. "That's our buffer stock. We needed that for the final prints."

"We have enough for the prints we've planned. We'll just—we won't have room for mistakes."

"I *am* the mistake."

He laughs. It surprises both of us, I think—this genuine, unguarded sound in the red dark. "That's dramatic."

"I'm dramatic. It's a known quantity."

"I know." And the way he says it—not unkind, almost fond—does something to my chest that I don't immediately have a name for.

I start stacking the ruined paper to throw away, just to have something to do with my hands.

"Can I tell you something?" he says.

I stop moving. "Yeah."

He's looking at the developing tray, not at me. The fire escape print has gone dark and perfect. "I've been—" He stops. Starts again. "I've been thinking a lot lately. About. How I present. Who I am when I'm—just. Who I am."

The red light makes his face unreadable and completely readable at once.

"Okay," I say. Carefully. Like I'm not holding my breath.

"I don't think I'm—" He touches one of his rings, rotating it. "I'm not sure *man* is the whole story. For me. I've been sitting with that for a while and I haven't said it out loud to anyone and I don't know why I'm saying it now except that it's dark and you're—" He glances at me. "You don't perform anything. You're just *you*, all the time. I've been watching that all semester and I think it's made me braver."

I don't trust myself to speak for a moment.

Here's what I'm not saying: *I've been watching you too.* Here's what I'm not doing: filling the space with my certainty, my opinions, my six years of being out and confident and sure, because this isn't mine to be sure about.

"Thank you for saying it out loud," I finally say. "To me."

"Does it—" He hesitates. "Does it change anything? For you?"

And here is where I'm supposed to have an answer. Here is where Dani, who always knows what she wants, who takes up space and names things and moves toward them—here is where she should say something clean and true.

What I know: I'm attracted to him. I've been attracted to him. Whatever he is, whatever he's becoming, the specifics of that have never been the point.

What I don't know: what that means about me, about my own neat categories, about the story I've told myself about my own desire.

"No," I say. "And also—I'm figuring some things out too. Right now. In real time."

He looks at me.

The print floats in the tray between us, those reaching hands suspended in chemistry, caught mid-emergence.

"Okay," he says softly. "That's okay."

The red light holds us both.
