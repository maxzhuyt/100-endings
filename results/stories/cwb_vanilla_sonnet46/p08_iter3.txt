# Stolen Moments in Roma

The Trevi Fountain announced itself before Ben Hartley ever saw it — a roar of cascading water cutting through the mid-morning heat, tourists packed three-deep along the marble railing, coins glinting in the basin like scattered promises. Ben stood precisely four metres from the kerb, nose buried in his guidebook, mouthing the Latin inscription above Neptune's head with the quiet concentration of a man who had mistaken thoroughness for experience.

He never heard the motorcycle.

The Vespa shrieked past so close that the wing mirror clipped his guidebook clean from his hands, sending it spinning into a puddle of tourist-spilled gelato. Ben stumbled backwards into a Japanese couple, apologised to them in French, then apologised again in English, then simply held up both hands in a gesture of universal mortification.

"*Cretino!*"

The rider had stopped. She swung off the Vespa with the fluid economy of someone who had never once in her life been uncertain of her body, yanking off her helmet to reveal dark hair, darker eyes, and an expression that suggested she was deciding whether to be furious or entertained.

"You were standing in the road," she said, switching to English with the casual authority of someone who speaks four languages and is proud of none of them.

"I was on the pavement," Ben said.

"The *pavement*." She looked down at his feet. He was, in fact, standing in the road. "Ah."

"I may have drifted slightly."

"You drift like a ship." She retrieved his guidebook from the gelato puddle, held it between two fingers as though it were evidence. "*Roma per Principianti.*" She looked up. "Rome for Beginners."

"It's research," Ben said. "I'm an academic."

"Of course you are." She handed it back. Her name, she told him without being asked, was Alessandra. She said it the way Romans say everything — as though the word itself were a small, beautiful fact about the universe.

---

He should have walked away. He knew that. Instead he found himself accepting a coffee at a bar so narrow two people couldn't pass without turning sideways, watching Alessandra drink her espresso standing up, the way Romans do, the cup gone in two practiced sips.

"What do you study?" she asked.

"Classical architecture. The persistence of ancient form in modern urban space."

She stared at him. "So you look at buildings."

"Essentially, yes."

"I deliver things between buildings." She set down her cup. "We are not so different."

Ben laughed — a surprised, genuine laugh that seemed to startle them both slightly. Alessandra's mouth curved. She was watching him the way a cat watches something it hasn't decided whether to play with or ignore.

He noticed, without quite processing it, that when they left the bar, the ceramic espresso spoon had disappeared from his saucer and reappeared, somehow, in the breast pocket of her jacket.

---

"I will show you Rome," she announced outside, straddling the Vespa.

"I have a lecture to prepare," Ben said.

"Get on."

He got on.

She drove the way she apparently did everything — with complete disregard for conventional boundaries. She took him through the Trastevere backstreets, past laundry strung between buildings like festival bunting, past a *trattoria* where an old man was arguing passionately with a cat. She stopped at a gate in a garden wall, produced a key that Ben was fairly certain she hadn't possessed ten minutes ago, and led him through to a private courtyard where orange trees grew in terracotta pots and the city noise dropped away like a coat slipping from your shoulders.

"This is someone's property," Ben said.

"It is *everyone's* property," Alessandra said, lying back on a stone bench and closing her eyes. "That is the correct Roman philosophy."

"That is not the correct Roman philosophy."

"Ben." She said his name for the first time. It sounded different in her mouth — shorter, more definite, like a full stop. "Sit down."

He sat down.

---

They were crossing the Campo de' Fiori when a man called out her name. He was perhaps fifty, barrel-chested, with the weathered look of someone who had spent decades outdoors and several of those decades regretting specific decisions.

"*Alessandra, figlia del diavolo.*"

"*Tonio,*" she said warmly, kissing both his cheeks.

Tonio looked at Ben with the frank assessment of a man evaluating livestock. "English?"

"British, yes. Ben Hartley."

"You know this woman how long?"

"About three hours."

Tonio nodded slowly, as though this confirmed something he had long suspected about the British. He leaned toward Ben with the gravity of a man delivering scripture. "She took my grandfather's watch," he said. "Nineteen ninety-eight. We did not find it for six years. It was in the lining of her school bag." He paused. "She was eleven."

Ben looked at Alessandra.

"I was returning it," she said serenely.

"*For six years?*"

"It took time to find the right moment."

Tonio gripped Ben's arm. "Go to your hotel," he said. "Read your books. Eat pasta. Go home." He released him, kissed Alessandra on both cheeks again with the helpless affection of someone who has long since surrendered to a force of nature, and disappeared into the crowd.

Ben turned to Alessandra. She was already walking, hands in her jacket pockets, the afternoon light catching the angles of her face.

"Should I be concerned?" he asked, falling into step beside her.

"Everyone should be a little concerned," she said. "It keeps you alert."

---

They ate at a table outside a restaurant that Alessandra had chosen by the method of walking in, inspecting the kitchen without invitation, and declaring it acceptable. The owner had not objected. Ben was beginning to understand that very few people objected to Alessandra, not because she was intimidating, but because her certainty was somehow more contagious than any argument against it.

The wine was cold. The evening was warm. Rome turned amber around them.

"You'll go back tomorrow," she said. It wasn't a question.

"To my flat, yes. I'm here for three months."

She looked at him over her glass with an expression he couldn't parse — something between calculation and something softer, which she seemed to notice and promptly set aside.

"Three months," she repeated.

"Plenty of time," Ben said, and surprised himself by meaning it.

Alessandra smiled — not the sharp, testing smile of the morning, but something quieter, which she hid quickly behind her wine glass, as though she hadn't quite intended to let him see it.

Outside, a scooter alarm was going off.

Neither of them mentioned that it might be hers.
