# Breaking Formation

The bass hits me in the sternum before I even open the door.

Four speakers, one in each corner of the studio, and they've calibrated them so the sound arrives at the center of the room from all directions simultaneously—a sonic cage. I know this because I've been standing in the center for six minutes, trying to find the pocket in the beat, and there isn't one. The music doesn't breathe. It's a grid.

My name is Marcus Delaney Webb. I am twenty-one years old. I learned to dance in a parking lot on Hooper Avenue where the asphalt buckled near the drain and you had to account for the uneven ground or you'd roll an ankle, and that imperfection became part of the choreography, became *me*. I have never been to South Korea before this week. I have never worn shoes this clean.

"Again from the top," Instructor Choi says.

He is standing at the mirror wall with his arms folded and his expression is the same expression the mirror has—flat, reflective, returning everything you give it without comment. He is fifty-three years old and has produced nine groups that have charted globally and he has not smiled once in two days. What he has done is watch me with the specific attention of someone cataloguing errors.

The other six trainees snap back to their opening positions. Right foot back, weight on the ball of the left, arms at forty-five degrees from the hip, palms facing the floor. A formation. They hit it in unison with a precision that makes a small mechanical sound, like a lock engaging.

I hit it a half-beat late and my arms come up to sixty degrees because that's where my body wants them.

"Mr. Webb." Choi doesn't move. "You are not in position."

"I'm close—"

"You are not in position."

I drop my arms to forty-five and feel immediately like I'm wearing someone else's skeleton.

The track restarts. All four speakers. The sound pressure is a physical thing, a hand on the chest. It's a hundred and twelve beats per minute and it is perfectly, ruthlessly even—no swing, no push-pull, no *conversation* in the rhythm. Back home I could find the ghost beat underneath any track, the human error the drummer left in, the place where the producer's hand hesitated on the fader. I'd live in that space. That's where I'd be most myself.

This track has no ghost. This track was made by machines that don't hesitate.

The formation moves on the first count and I move with it—step right, lock the hip, bring the left foot in with a sharp *chasse* to center—and for four bars I'm with them, I'm actually *in* the grid, and I feel Choi's eyes change slightly, register something. Then the chorus hits and my body does what my body does.

I drop into a demi-plié that's six inches deeper than the choreography calls for, and my chest rounds forward, and my right arm pulls back in a wave that travels from my shoulder blade through my elbow to my fingertips, and for one full measure I am dancing. Not performing the choreography. *Dancing*. My weight shifts to the outside edge of my right foot and I let the momentum carry me into a half-spin that puts me facing the mirror instead of the front wall, and I see myself—

And I see the six other trainees, frozen in the correct position, watching me with expressions that are not unkind but are very clearly not surprised.

"Stop."

The music cuts. The silence is enormous.

Choi walks toward me with the measured pace of someone who has done this many times. He stops four feet away. This close I can see that his face isn't flat—it's controlled, which is different. There's something moving behind it.

"What you just did," he says, "was beautiful."

I blink.

"It was also wrong." He turns to the other trainees. "Take five minutes."

They file out and the room is suddenly just me and Choi and the four silent speakers and the mirror showing us to ourselves.

"You isolate beautifully," he says. "Your body segments independently. Your timing is internal, not metronomic, but it is real—it is *yours*. I have trained students for thirty years and what you have in your body cannot be taught." He pauses. "What can be taught is what you do not have. Precision. Uniformity. The ability to make your body a single instrument in an ensemble rather than a soloist."

"I know how to be in a crew," I say, and I hear the defensiveness in it.

"A crew follows the energy. An ensemble creates it *collectively*, identically, so that the audience receives one signal from many bodies." He points at the center of the room. "Stand there."

I stand in the center. The speakers are equidistant from me.

"The music will find you equally from all directions," he says. "This is intentional. You cannot lean into any one speaker. You cannot find a pocket because the pocket is everywhere and nowhere. That is the discipline. Not the steps. The *surrender* of preference."

He says the last two words like he knows what they cost.

And here's the thing I don't say out loud, the thing that's been living in my chest since the plane landed: I paid for these clean shoes with a scholarship that has my name on it, and my mother cried when I left, and the parking lot on Hooper is still there with its buckled asphalt and its ghost beats and if I walk out of this room I go back to it not as a dancer but as someone who was almost something.

But if I stay—

If I stay and I surrender preference, if I lock my arms at forty-five degrees and I kill the wave in my spine and I make my body a clean instrument in someone else's ensemble—

What comes home?

Choi is watching me work through it. He doesn't rush me.

I walk back to the opening position. Right foot back. Weight on the ball of the left. Arms at forty-five, palms down.

I don't know yet which kind of surrender this is.

The track restarts.
