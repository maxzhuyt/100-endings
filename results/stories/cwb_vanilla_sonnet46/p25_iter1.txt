# The Azra Gambit
## Chapter Seven: The Weight of Courtesy

The transfer vessel's air filtration system hummed with a persistent mechanical drone, the kind that burrowed into a man's skull after the first hour and took up permanent residence. Arthur Neegan had spent eleven years breathing recycled Martian air and had developed a particular sensitivity to the quality of filtration units. This one was military grade. The kind that scrubbed not just particulates but sound, emotion, and apparently, any lingering sense of personal autonomy.

He pressed his thumb against the porthole's cold alloy rim and watched Mars recede — a rust-coloured smear dissolving into the black. His plot was down there somewhere. His *claim*. Forty-seven hectares of red nothing that had turned, in the space of a fortnight, into the most dangerous piece of real estate in the inner system.

The escort had arrived at 0600, which told him something. Men who arrive at 0600 have rehearsed the hour. There had been six of them, in charcoal pressure-suits bearing the Antares Diplomatic Corps insignia — the twin-star emblem that every colonial administrator recognised and most had learned to distrust. Diplomatic Corps. The name had always struck Arthur as the kind of joke that wasn't meant to be funny. They carried sidearms that weren't standard issue for any diplomatic function he'd ever read about, and they spoke with the measured courtesy of men who understood that politeness, applied with sufficient pressure, was indistinguishable from a threat.

*"Mr. Neegan. You've been requested."*

Not summoned. *Requested*. The distinction had been delivered with a smile.

---

The vessel docked with something large. Arthur felt it in his teeth before he heard it — the deep, structural groan of mag-clamps engaging with a hull that had mass to spare. Through the porthole, a shadow consumed the stars. Not a station. A ship. Something governmental in its proportions, bureaucratic in its geometry: all flat planes and deliberate angles, designed to communicate authority rather than elegance.

They walked him through three pressure doors and two identity checkpoints before depositing him in a room that was aggressively, almost satirically, comfortable. Upholstered chairs. Actual wood panelling — synthetic, certainly, but the gesture alone represented an expenditure that made his throat tighten. A table with water in a glass pitcher, not a sealed pouch. *Glass*. He hadn't drunk from glass in four years.

He did not touch the water.

He waited eleven minutes — he counted — before the door opened.

The man who entered was perhaps sixty, with the careful posture of someone who had spent decades ensuring that nothing about his appearance communicated more than he intended. Grey suit, no insignia. Hair silver and precise. He carried a single data-slate and set it on the table between them as though placing a chess piece.

"Mr. Neegan." He sat without offering his hand. "My name is Councillor Veth. I represent certain interests that have been following the Meridian Basin surveys with considerable attention."

"Certain interests," Arthur repeated.

"The kind that prefer not to be named in rooms with filtration systems." A thin smile. "You understand the caution."

"I understand that six armed men pulled me off my claim at dawn."

"*Escorted*," Veth said, with gentle precision. "You were escorted. You remain entirely free to leave." He paused. "After we've spoken."

Arthur looked at the door. At the data-slate. At the water he hadn't touched.

"Then speak," he said.

Veth opened the slate and turned it to face him. A geological survey — *his* survey, the deep-resonance scan he'd filed with the Colonial Mining Authority three weeks ago. Except the version on Veth's slate had annotations Arthur had never written, extending the vein projections far beyond his own conservative estimates. The numbers were extraordinary. They were, in fact, almost incomprehensible.

"We believe," Veth said, "that your claim sits atop the largest single Azra deposit yet identified. Larger than the Schiaparelli fields. Larger, potentially, than all currently operational sites combined." He let that settle like sediment. "You appreciate what that means."

Arthur appreciated it very much. It meant that the arms race everyone pretended was a trade dispute had just found its fulcrum, and the fulcrum had his name on the title deed.

"What's the offer?" Arthur said.

Veth seemed pleased by the directness. "Extraction rights, in partnership with our consortium. You retain nominal ownership — the legal architecture will be elegant, I assure you. In return, you receive a settlement figure." He named it.

Arthur had grown up in the Dundee tenements. He had worked indenture contracts for seven years to purchase his claim outright. He was not a man easily impressed by numbers.

He was, however, a man who understood what a number that size was designed to accomplish. It was large enough to stop questions. Large enough to make a man grateful rather than curious.

"And if I've already had other conversations?" Arthur said carefully.

Something moved behind Veth's eyes. Brief, controlled, and — Arthur thought — genuinely interesting. "Then I would encourage you to consider the relative stability of the parties involved." He folded his hands. "The Azra question will be resolved, Mr. Neegan. The physics are already written. What remains negotiable is who benefits from the resolution, and who is — simplified — out of the equation."

*Simplified*. Arthur filed the word away.

"I'll need time," Arthur said.

"Of course." Veth rose, retrieving his slate with the same deliberate care with which he'd placed it. "You'll be returned to your claim within the hour. We ask only that you not file any amendments to your survey with the CMA before we speak again." He paused at the door. "The water is quite good, incidentally. Earthside filtered."

The door closed.

Arthur looked at the pitcher. At the glass. At his own reflection, distorted and small, in its curved surface.

He drank.

It tasted, he thought, exactly like a decision he hadn't made yet.
