# Stolen Moments in Roma

The Trevi Fountain announced itself in sound before sight — a roar of cascading water that Ben Hartley followed like a man in a trance, guidebook raised to his nose, completely failing to register the motorcycle until it was approximately three centimetres from his left hip.

The horn was operatic in its fury.

"*Madonna!*" The rider wrenched the bike sideways, boot scraping stone, and came to a stop that was more controlled than it had any right to be. She pulled off her helmet in one fluid motion, and Ben's first coherent thought — arriving somewhere behind his terror — was that she was extraordinarily beautiful and extraordinarily angry, and that these two facts were somehow inseparable.

"You — *stai camminando in mezzo alla strada come un fantasma!*" She gestured at the cobblestones beneath his feet, then at him, then at the sky, as though appealing to God directly.

"I'm — yes, I'm terribly sorry, I was reading, which was obviously — *mi dispiace molto*, I think that's right, *molto*, and I — is your bike damaged? Are *you* —"

"*Sei inglese.*" She said it the way one might say *sei pazzo* — you're mad.

"British, yes. Ben Hartley. I'm on sabbatical. I'm researching Baroque —" He stopped. She was staring at him with an expression that occupied the precise territory between contempt and fascination. "Sorry. That's not relevant."

"No," she agreed. "*Io sono Alessandra.*" She looked him over with the frank assessment of someone pricing antiques. Then, inexplicably, she smiled. "You dropped your book."

---

The guidebook had fallen open to a dog-eared page on the Palatine Hill. Alessandra studied it while Ben retrieved it, and by the time he straightened up she had apparently made some private decision, because she was strapping her helmet to the bike with the purposeful energy of someone who'd just changed plans.

"You want to see Rome?" she said.

"I've been seeing Rome, rather. For three weeks, actually."

"*No.*" She shook her head slowly, almost pityingly. "You've been reading about Rome. Different thing." She nodded at the seat behind her. "Get on."

"I don't think —"

"*Andiamo.*"

He got on.

---

She took him first to a courtyard off the Via del Governo Vecchio that appeared in no guidebook ever printed — a crumbling ochre space where an old man sold wine from unmarked bottles and a cat slept across the threshold like a furry doorstop. While Ben negotiated awkwardly for two glasses, Alessandra drifted to the old man's cluttered table of trinkets and back again with the unhurried grace of a tide going out. Ben didn't notice the small brass compass that had migrated to her jacket pocket. He was too busy mispronouncing *rosso*.

She watched him over the rim of her glass with something warm and unreadable.

"You study old things," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Baroque architecture, primarily. The relationship between physical space and emotional —" He caught her expression. "Yes. Old things."

"*Capisco.*" She tipped her glass toward the crumbling walls. "Rome is the oldest thing. You can't study it. You have to let it *happen* to you."

"Is that what you're doing? Happening to me?"

She laughed — sudden, unguarded, genuinely delighted — and he felt it somewhere inconveniently central.

---

By noon she'd talked her way past a locked gate at the Aventine using what Ben suspected was a combination of charm and outright fabrication, and they stood in the famous keyhole that framed St. Peter's dome in perfect alignment. She pressed close behind him to angle his eye correctly, her chin nearly at his shoulder, and said nothing for a full thirty seconds. For Alessandra, he was beginning to understand, silence was a form of punctuation.

"Beautiful," he said.

"*Sì.*" She stepped back. "Also, I know the man who owns this garden. He would absolutely not let us in."

"You told me you were his cousin."

"*Cugina acquisita.*" She spread her hands. "Cousin by marriage. Technically I've never been married, so —"

"That's not how that works."

"Ben." She said his name for the first time, and somehow it sounded different in her mouth — shorter, warmer, like something she'd decided to keep. "Nothing in Rome works the way it's supposed to. This is why it's lasted two thousand years."

---

The rain arrived without negotiation, the way Roman weather always does — one moment brilliant and the next a full theatrical downpour that sent tourists scattering like pigeons. They ran, Alessandra laughing and pulling him by the wrist, and collapsed breathless under the striped awning of a *gelateria* on the Via della Croce while the street turned to a river around them.

The owner, a stout woman with magnificent eyebrows, handed them each a cone without being asked. Alessandra paid with a note she produced from somewhere and waved away Ben's wallet.

They stood shoulder to shoulder, watching the rain.

"Stracciatella," Ben said, examining his cone with academic gravity. "I've been eating it wrong, haven't I. You're supposed to let it —"

"You think too much," she said, not unkindly. She was eating hers with total, unselfconscious focus.

"Occupational hazard."

She glanced sideways at him. Rain hammered the awning. Somewhere close, a Vespa horn sang its thin complaint. He was watching the street with an expression she hadn't expected — not the flustered tourist she'd nearly flattened that morning, but someone quietly, genuinely *here*, present in the way that Rome occasionally demanded of people whether they planned for it or not.

She looked away first.

"The rain will stop in ten minutes," she said. "In Rome it always does."

"And then?"

She considered this with the brass compass turning slowly in her pocket, the weight of it familiar and small and entirely hers now.

"*E poi vediamo,*" she said. We'll see.

She didn't sound like someone who was finished.
