# The Weight of Stone

The briefings had described Maerath as a colony in tension. They had not described the smell of it—salt and iron and something organic, almost sweet, like fermentation carried on the wind from the lower markets.

Sera Voss left the consulate before dawn, which on Maerath lasted three hours longer than she had been told to expect.

---

The streets of the Underbend were carved from the same pale limestone as everything else on this world, the stone worked so long by so many hands that it had taken on a quality of patience. Glyphs ran along the building faces at shoulder height—the old Earth myths rendered in Maerath's own idiom, strange and compressed. She recognized Prometheus in one sequence: the figure bound, the bird descending, but the fire in his hands already given away, already gone, the theft reversed so that he was being punished for a generosity rather than a crime. She stopped and looked at it for a long moment. The modification was not accidental.

The Underbend was Compact territory—the name the briefings used for the lineage that had adapted deepward, into the mines and the sealed atmospheric zones. They were shorter than the Highborn by nearly a head, broader through the chest, with the characteristic periorbital darkening that the medical files attributed to melanin redistribution. The files also described them as *insular, resistant to integration, prone to collective action*. Walking among them in the early market hours, Sera thought that what the files had actually meant was: they talk to each other.

A woman was selling preserved fish from a cart. She looked at Sera directly, without the performed indifference that characterized the official reception halls.

"Offworlder," the woman said. Not an accusation. A fact, offered plainly.

"Yes," Sera said.

"You want to eat something or you want to look at us?"

Sera bought the fish. It was very good.

---

She was back in her quarters before the second bell, but not before the consulate's monitoring system had logged her absence and routed a flag to Commander Eleth aboard the *Meridian*, still in high orbit, still watching.

The call came within the hour.

Eleth's face on the screen was the face of a person who had learned to express disappointment without raising her voice, which made it considerably worse than shouting. "You left the secured perimeter," she said.

"I walked through a market."

"Alone. Unlogged. In a district that the Ministry of Order has classified—"

"The Ministry of Order classifies everything below the second terrace as sensitive. That includes the public baths and a school for children under eight."

A pause. "Voss."

"Commander."

"You are here to stabilize a relationship, not to conduct your own ethnographic survey. The Highborn council is already watching you for any sign that we favor the Compact position. If they learn you spent the morning buying fish in the Underbend—"

"They will learn," Sera said, "that I eat breakfast."

Another pause, longer. "Minister Caul has agreed to meet you. Today, third bell. Do not be late, do not be clever, and do not buy anything on the way."

---

The Ministry of Science occupied a building that had once been a water temple. The glyph-work here was older and less revised: Athena in her full sequence, the owl, the olive branch, the moment of springing fully-formed from the skull of a father who had swallowed the mother first. Someone had added a small, careful annotation in modern Maerath script beneath the owl. Sera translated it as she waited: *What is born of consumption is still born.*

Minister Caul was a Highborn man of perhaps sixty years, which on Maerath with its longer calendar meant something closer to seventy-five in standard reckoning. He was tall even seated, with the long-fingered hands and slightly elevated cranial vault that the files described as the Highborn's primary adaptive signature—the result of generations in the upper atmospheric zones, where oxygen was thinner and the skull had, over centuries, made its own accommodations. He had the look of someone who had held power long enough to find it boring, which was the most dangerous kind.

"Envoy Voss," he said. "I understand you've already begun your cultural research."

So they did know. She sat down without being invited to. "I went to a market."

"In the Underbend."

"The fish was excellent."

He looked at her for a moment with the evaluating stillness of someone accustomed to watching people decide how frightened to be. "You're young for this posting," he said.

"The Compact requested a junior envoy. They said senior envoys tend to have already decided what they think before they arrive."

"And what do *you* think? Having eaten your fish."

She considered the question as though it deserved consideration. "I think the tension here is not between subspecies. I think it's between people who have been told the tension is about subspecies and people who are trying to live in a city."

Caul's expression did not change, but something behind it shifted, some interior adjustment. "That is a very tidy analysis for a morning's walk."

"The glyphs helped. The Prometheus revision in the lower market—the one where he's punished for giving rather than stealing. That's a significant reinterpretation. Someone spent time on it. Someone wanted it seen."

"Stone is permanent," Caul said. "People carve what they want remembered."

"Yes," Sera said. "Which is why I'm curious about what the Ministry of Science has been publishing about Compact respiratory adaptation. The last three papers argue it's degenerative."

The silence that followed was of a different quality than the ones before it.

"You read the journals," he said.

"On the transit. I had time."

Caul leaned back. The long hands folded. He was, she realized, deciding something—not about the political situation, but about her specifically, running some internal calculation whose variables she could only partially see.

"The fish vendor," he said finally. "What was her name?"

Sera had not asked. She said so.

He nodded slowly, as though this were the correct answer to a question she hadn't known she was being asked. "Come back tomorrow," he said. "And find out."
