# Stolen Moments in Roma

The Trevi Fountain roared behind him, indifferent to the crowd pressing against its marble edges, and Ben Hartley stood precisely where he should not have stood — in the mouth of the narrow Via della Stamperia, nose buried in *Eyewitness Rome*, trying to locate a footnote about Nicola Salvi's original commission. He heard the motorcycle before he saw it: a furious, wasp-like buzzing that crescendoed from around the corner with zero warning. He looked up. The bike filled his entire field of vision.

Then it stopped — ten centimeters from his left knee, close enough that he felt the heat radiating off the engine against his trouser leg.

*"Madonna mia!"* The rider yanked off her helmet in one fluid motion, and what emerged was a cascade of dark hair and an expression of magnificent, operatic fury. She was perhaps twenty-eight, with sharp cheekbones and eyes the color of strong espresso. Her delivery jacket was sun-faded red, and along the bike's left panel — a Ducati Scrambler, powder blue — ran a long, spiraling scratch, its edges buffed to a dull silver, the kind of wound a machine earns from something fast and probably inadvisable. *"Che cosa stai facendo? Sei pazzo? Stai in mezzo alla strada come un — come un —"* She seemed to be searching for a word catastrophic enough.

"I am — God — I'm so terribly sorry," Ben said. He had dropped the guidebook. He bent to retrieve it, stood up too quickly, and nearly headbutted her mirror. "I was reading, which I appreciate is — yes — that's rather the problem, isn't it. My Italian is, I'm afraid, not — *mi dispiace molto*. Molto, molto."

She stared at him. Something shifted in her face — the fury recalibrated into something more dangerous: amusement.

*"Molto molto,"* she repeated, tasting the words. *"Come un bambino."* Like a child.

"Yes, probably exactly like a child." He straightened his glasses. "Ben Hartley. Oxford. I'm on sabbatical. I study — well, it doesn't matter what I study."

*"No,"* she agreed pleasantly. *"It doesn't."* She looked him over with the frank, unhurried assessment of someone pricing antiques. "Alessandra," she said finally, as if granting a concession. "And you nearly died, Professor."

"I'm technically a Reader, not a—"

"Same thing." She kicked the stand down and swung off the bike in one motion, tucking the helmet under her arm. She retrieved a small parcel from the cargo box — someone's delivery, already forgotten — then glanced back at him. "You have a plan for today?"

He held up the guidebook.

She took it from his hand, considered it for a moment with an expression of gentle pity, and dropped it into a nearby waste bin.

"*Andiamo,*" she said.

---

He should not have gotten on the motorcycle. He knew this academically, the way he knew that Roman traffic operated on principles antithetical to the Highway Code, that Alessandra had not offered a spare helmet, that his department's sabbatical insurance almost certainly contained exclusionary language about exactly this sort of situation. He got on anyway.

She drove the way she talked — fast, elliptical, cutting through gaps that weren't gaps until they suddenly were. He gripped the rail behind him with both hands, refusing on principle to hold onto her, until she banked hard left around a delivery van and his arms made their own decision. She laughed — he felt it through his palms before he heard it over the engine.

She stopped first at a *bar* on Via dei Coronari, a place with no English menu and a barista who called her *tesoro* and didn't charge her. Ben watched her lean across the counter while the man's back was turned and pocket two sugar-wrapped chocolates from the dish beside the register. It was quick, nearly invisible — a magician's economy of movement. She passed one to Ben without comment, as if it had materialized from the air.

"Did you just—"

"Espresso," she said, pushing a small cup toward him. "Drink it standing. You're in Rome now."

He drank it standing. It was extraordinary.

---

The Aventine Hill came next — the Knights of Malta keyhole, through which Rome's three sovereign territories aligned in a single improbable view. Ben had read about it. He hadn't expected Alessandra to already be crouched at the keyhole when he turned around, nor to find, when he looked, that she'd somehow acquired a fresh rose, which she was holding against the iron gate with theatrical reverence.

"Where did that come from?"

"A man on the street."

"You bought it?"

She gave him a look of serene incomprehension.

They ate lunch at a *trattoria* in Testaccio where she knew the owner's nephew, and she ordered for both of them in a rapid Italian that included, Ben was fairly certain, a negotiation he wasn't privy to. A bottle of house white appeared. She poured generously. Under the pergola, with the afternoon light going amber and soft, she asked him — with genuine curiosity, for the first time — what he actually studied.

"The rhetoric of ruins," he said. "How we narrate decay. What we decide a broken thing means."

She was quiet for a moment. She turned her wine glass slowly.

"*Interessante,*" she said, and he couldn't tell if she meant it.

---

By evening they were sitting on the steps of Santa Maria in Trastevere, the piazza filling with the golden, unhurried noise of Roman dusk. Ben's jacket was gone — left, he vaguely recalled, at the *trattoria* — and he felt, without quite being able to explain it, lighter for its absence.

Alessandra was turning something small between her fingers. He looked closer. It was his pen — his good pen, the Montblanc he'd had since his doctorate.

"That's mine," he said.

"*Sì,*" she agreed, without returning it.

She looked at him sideways, and the corner of her mouth did something complicated.

"Same time tomorrow, Professor?"

He should have said no.

"I'll be at the fountain," he said.
