## Chapter Two

That night, dinner felt wrong in the way that only familiar things can feel wrong—like finding a word you've used your whole life suddenly looks misspelled, the letters rearranged into something foreign and almost threatening.

His father cooked the way he always cooked at the lodge: venison chili from a can dressed up with onions and dried pepper flakes, cornbread from a box, coffee so strong it left a dark ring inside the mug. The propane lantern hissed softly overhead. Outside, the wind moved through the spruce trees with a low, wet sound, like breathing through a cloth. Everything was exactly as it should have been, and yet JT couldn't shake the feeling that he was watching a performance—that the three of them were going through motions whose original meaning had been quietly hollowed out.

His father hadn't spoken about the clearing. Hadn't spoken much at all since they'd come back in. He sat at the head of the table eating steadily, eyes tracking somewhere just past the middle distance, and when Mike asked him about the shot he'd missed—not the clearing, just a doe Mike had spotted on the walk back—their father simply said, *"Wasn't the right one,"* and left it there, flat as a stone dropped in still water.

Mike had let it go. Mike always let things go with their father. JT envied that sometimes, the ease of it, the way his brother could read the temperature of a room and simply adjust. JT always ran too hot or too cold.

"You're quiet," Mike said, nudging JT's boot under the table.

"I'm eating."

"You've been moving that chili around for ten minutes."

JT looked down. He had been. He put a spoonful in his mouth and chewed without tasting it. "Long drive," he said. "Still tired."

Mike nodded, satisfied with that. Their father said nothing, just reached across the table for the cornbread and tore a piece off with his fingers. His hands, JT noticed, had stopped trembling. They were very still now. Almost deliberately still, the way a person holds a hand steady when they don't want anyone to know it's been shaking.

JT thought about asking. He'd been rehearsing the question in his head all afternoon, turning it over, looking for a version that didn't sound like an accusation. *Did you see that buck? The big one, in the clearing? What was that?* Simple enough. Innocent enough. But every time he opened his mouth, he felt the question dissolve, and what replaced it was the image of his father's face—those too-bright eyes, lips parted, reaching out toward the animal like a man approaching something sacred or something terrible, and the two might have been the same thing.

He kept his mouth shut.

After dinner, Mike washed the dishes and their father went to sit on the porch with his coffee, which was what he always did, and JT stood in the doorway watching him through the screen. The old man sat in the same wooden chair he'd sat in since JT was small enough to fall asleep against his shoulder, and he looked out into the dark tree line with an expression JT couldn't name. Not peaceful. Not troubled. Something in between, or something that had moved past both.

JT pushed through the screen door and sat in the other chair. The cold came down immediately, settling over his shoulders like a hand.

For a while neither of them spoke. An owl called somewhere deep in the spruce, one long note, then silence.

"Good first day," JT offered.

"Mm."

"That caribou this morning dropped clean."

"They usually do, up here." His father wrapped both hands around his mug. "When they're ready."

JT looked at him. "When they're ready?"

His father blinked, as if surprised by his own words. "When you get a clean shot," he said. "You know what I mean."

JT wasn't sure he did. He watched his father's profile—the deep-cut lines beside his mouth, the grey stubble, the jaw working slightly as though chewing on something he hadn't decided whether to swallow. He'd always thought of his father as a man who fit neatly inside the world, who understood its rules and kept them without effort. Watching him now felt like looking at a photograph that had begun, very slightly, to blur.

"Dad."

"Mm."

"In the clearing today. That buck—"

"Didn't see a buck in any clearing." His father said it quietly, without inflection, lifting the coffee to his lips.

The words landed strangely. Not defensive. Not dismissive. Just—stated. The way you'd state a fact about the weather.

JT opened his mouth and closed it again.

"Cold's coming in tonight," his father said. "Ground'll be hard by morning. Good for tracking."

And that was that. The conversation folded closed like a hand of cards laid face-down on the table, and JT understood somehow that turning them over would cost him something he hadn't yet calculated. He sat with his father in the dark for another ten minutes, listening to the trees, and then went inside and lay on his bunk staring at the ceiling while Mike snored softly in the other bed.

He thought about the buck. The flat dark eyes. The way it had stood so close, so willing, as if it had arranged to be there.

*When they're ready.*

Sleep came eventually, shallow and strange. He dreamed about the clearing—empty this time, just the pale oval of open ground ringed by dark trees—and in the dream he understood that something was standing at the edge of it, just beyond where the light reached, watching him with the patient and absolute stillness of a thing that had been waiting for a very long time.

He woke before dawn to the sound of his father already moving in the kitchen, boots on the floorboards, quiet and purposeful.

Getting ready to go back out.
