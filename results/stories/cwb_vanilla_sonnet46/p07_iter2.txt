# Lost & Found in Osaka

The notes hit her before she even turned the corner.

Mia stopped walking. Her canvas bag — heavy with a secondhand LFO module she'd found three stalls back — swung against her hip as she stood there on the pavement, people flowing around her like water around a stone. She knew the melody. *Cruel Angel's Thesis*. But not like this. Whoever was playing had taken the familiar architecture of it and done something violent and loving to it at the same time, bending the notes, letting them bleed.

She followed the sound.

The girl was maybe seventeen, eighteen, stationed near the arcade entrance with a battered Telecaster and a small battery amp that had absolutely no business sounding that good. She had the kind of effortless posture that Mia associated with people who had never once worried about where to put their hands at a party. A tip jar at her feet held a modest nest of coins and a few folded notes.

Mia stood at the edge of the small crowd that had gathered, clutching her bag strap.

The song ended. People clapped. A few dropped coins. The crowd thinned almost immediately, the way crowds do, pulled back into the current of the arcade and the takoyaki stalls and the rest of the loud, breathing city.

Mia didn't move.

The girl looked up, fingers already picking out the opening of something new, and caught Mia staring. She raised an eyebrow.

"You want a photo or something?"

"No — sorry —" Mia looked briefly at the pavement. "I just. That was. The way you did the bridge. You changed the chord. You went to a flat seven instead of the — it was a flat seven, right?"

The girl's fingers stopped moving.

"Yeah," she said slowly. "It was."

"It made it sadder. Which is weird because the original isn't — I mean it's *about* something sad but it doesn't really *feel* —" Mia stopped herself. "Sorry. I'm bothering you."

"You're not bothering me." The girl tilted her head. "How do you know music theory?"

"I don't, really. I just. I make stuff. On synthesizers. So I read a lot."

"Synthesizers." The girl said it like she was tasting the word. "Like, old ones? New ones?"

"Both. Mostly old. I just bought a —" Mia reached into her bag and stopped, suddenly aware of how strange it would be to pull out a circuit board on a street corner. "Never mind. It's boring."

"If it was boring you wouldn't have a bag that looks like it weighs more than you do." The girl set her guitar across her knees and extended a hand. "Riku."

Mia shook it. "Mia."

"You're not from here."

"Kyoto. Day trip." Mia nodded vaguely toward the interior of Den Den Town. "There's a shop down that way that gets old Korg stuff sometimes."

"I know that shop. The guy who runs it has a ponytail and hates everyone."

"He sold me this for four thousand yen." Mia pulled out the module, held it up briefly, then put it away again as if embarrassed by it.

Riku leaned forward. "Wait, is that an LFO from an MS-20?"

Mia blinked. "You know what that is?"

"I know what that is." Riku sat back, grinning now, something shifting in her expression — the performance quality dropping away, replaced by something more direct and genuine. "My uncle had one. He let me take it apart when I was nine and then was very upset about that decision."

"Did you put it back together?"

"Mostly."

Mia almost smiled. It arrived on her face cautiously, like it wasn't sure of its welcome.

"So you play guitar and you know synthesizers," Mia said.

"I play guitar and I *destroyed* a synthesizer, which is different. You actually know what you're doing." Riku glanced at the tip jar, then back up. "I'm going to pack up in a minute. Have you eaten?"

"I had a convenience store rice ball on the train."

"That's not eating, that's surviving." Riku was already unclipping her guitar strap. "There's a kaitenzushi place around the corner. It's tiny and the salmon is genuinely very good and I go there so often the staff are starting to look concerned about me."

"Oh — you don't have to —"

"I'm hungry and you're interesting. Come on."

---

The restaurant was exactly as small as promised, a narrow room with a single looping belt and eight counter seats. They took the two at the far end. Riku ordered green tea without looking at the menu. Mia studied the laminated card with the focused attention she usually reserved for patch diagrams.

"What are you actually making?" Riku asked, loading a plate of salmon onto her tray. "With the synths. Like, what kind of music?"

"I don't know what to call it. It's kind of —" Mia picked up a plate of tamago, put it back, picked it up again. "There's this thing that happens when you modulate a signal at a frequency that's almost too slow to notice. It makes the sound feel like it's *breathing*. I'm trying to make whole tracks that feel like that. Like breathing. Or like being almost asleep."

Riku was quiet for a moment.

"That sounds beautiful," she said. Not the way people say things to be polite. The flat, factual way.

"It mostly just sounds like noise," Mia said. "But sometimes."

"Sometimes is enough to keep going."

Outside, without any warning, the sky cracked open.

The first firework was loud enough to rattle the window glass, and then three more followed in quick succession — gold, then white, then a cascading red that lit the whole street in borrowed sunset. The restaurant erupted briefly in noise, everyone turning, pressing toward the window.

Riku leaned across the counter to look. Her shoulder was warm against Mia's.

The sound faded. The smoke drifted.

"I didn't know there were fireworks tonight," Mia said.

"Neither did I." Riku settled back onto her stool, reaching for her tea. She was smiling. "Good surprise though."

Mia looked at her plate. Then at the window. Then, carefully, at Riku.

"Yeah," she said. "Good surprise."
