# Room 7B

## I.

The hum is in B-flat.

Not the B-flat of a piano, which is tempered and democratic, but the B-flat of sixty-four cable racks vibrating at the frequency of their collective impedance, a tone so low and so constant that new girls on the floor used to say they couldn't hear it and old girls said they couldn't hear anything else. Marguerite has worked the eleven-to-seven in Room 7B for nine years and she hears it the way she supposes the ocean hears itself, which is to say she hears it as the fundamental condition of being alive and awake and here, in this room, on this floor, at this hour, with these lamps.

The lamps. Sixty per panel and twelve panels on the B-board and each lamp capped in amber glass so that when the room is working—really working, a Friday night, the trunk lines hot with long-distance—the whole south wall breathes in a color that is not orange and not gold but something in between, the color of aeli's skin held up to a candle, the color of a word she has never had occasion to say to anyone. The lamps do not switch on. They _bloom_. A call arrives on a trunk line and the lamp does not click or snap but swells into itself, a soft thermal thing, as though someone on the other end of the wire had exhaled and the warmth had traveled the whole distance from Akron or Baltimore or wherever the call originates and arrived here, in this room, as light.

Helen is at position 7 tonight and Marguerite is at position 6 and their chairs are nineteen inches apart, which she knows because she once measured the space with a length of patch cord during a dead stretch at four in the morning, not for any reason she could have explained if asked, just as she could not explain why she knows that the gap between positions 6 and 7 is narrower than the gap between any other two positions on the B-board—some asymmetry in the original installation, a Bell System engineer's error in 1941 that no one ever corrected because it did not affect service. It affects nothing whatsoever. Nineteen inches. Their elbows touch when they reach for the same trunk jack, which is the third jack in the second row of the trunk-multiple block, the one they call "the bottle" because its collar is slightly wider than standard and it takes a particular angle of insertion—not straight but tilted maybe two degrees to the right—and Helen's way of tilting is different from Marguerite's, Helen's ring finger curling under the plug body to guide it, the nail of that finger kept shorter than her other nails, trimmed straight across and unpolished, because she learned years ago that lacquer causes the fingers to slip on the brass and she is not a woman who permits slipping, though tonight her hands—

Her hands are fine. Her hands are at this moment pulling a front cord from its shelf, the cord brushing the face of the board with the sound of a zipper opening, and she is answering with the words she has spoken perhaps three hundred thousand times—"Operator, what number please"—but the way she shapes the _what_ is hers alone, the _wh_ barely aspirated, almost French, which she is not, she is from Flatbush, but the sound is there and always has been and Marguerite has never mentioned it because to mention it would be to reveal that she has heard it, really heard it, the way you only hear a thing you have listened for.

Number please. The caller gives a number. Helen repeats it back. Her left hand is already moving to the trunk-multiple, the cord following her arm like an answer following a question, and the plug seats itself in the jack with the small thick click that Marguerite has heard so many times that she could identify it blind among a thousand other small thick clicks, Helen's particular pressure, Helen's particular angle, Helen's particular—

A lamp blooms on Marguerite's panel. She answers.

She reads the memo and does not read the memo. She is answering another call. "Operator, what number please." The caller wants Butterfield 8 and a seven-digit string and Marguerite's hands perform the connection while her mind stays where it stays, which is nineteen inches to her left, where Helen is asking a caller to please deposit twenty-five cents for the first three minutes and her voice on _please_ is the same as her voice on the _what_, something in it that does not belong to the Bell System, that was never trained into her at the school on Bethune Street where they taught you to sit up straight and speak into the mouthpiece and never use a contraction with a subscriber. Helen uses contractions. Helen says _don't_ instead of _do not_ and _we'll_ instead of _we will_ and once, during a lull at three in the morning in January, she leaned over—the nineteen inches collapsing to six, to four—and whispered _I'm so tired_ and the contraction of _I am_ into _I'm_ was the most intimate thing Marguerite had heard in her life, more intimate than anything she had heard in the years of her marriage before it ended, more intimate than the word _love_ which is a word she has heard many times because the callers say it, the callers say it to each other through the wires that pass through her hands and she patches them through, she is the medium through which the word travels, and on the nights when the word comes through the wire she can almost feel it in the cord, a vibration, a thermal thing, like the lamps.

I'm so tired.

That was February. Position 4 and position 5 that night, the wider gap, thirty-one inches (she measured later), and Helen had to lean further to close the distance and so the whisper had more intention in it, or Marguerite told herself it did, the way she has told herself many things over nine years, building an architecture of implication out of elbows and whispers and the shared grammar of the switchboard, a grammar in which "I'll take your overflow" means something and "Your cord's tangled with mine" means something and the act of reaching across another woman's position to pull a cord that is not yours, your arm crossing the plane of her body, is—

Is nothing. Is work. Is the ordinary choreography of two people who have sat side by side for three thousand shifts and whose bodies know each other the way a left hand knows a right hand, by proximity and practice, not by choice.

Helen pulls a cord.

Marguerite watches Helen pull a cord.

The lamps breathe.

## II.

She reads it standing in the stairwell between the seventh floor and the sixth, her back against the concrete wall where someone has scratched the initials D.C. + R.P. inside a heart, which she has looked at many times and which she looks at now while her eyes take in the mimeographed sheet thumbtacked to the corkboard beside the fire extinguisher, the ink still faintly chemical-smelling, still damp at the margins.

December 22, 1969.

She looks at the date and does not feel the thing that a person in a story is supposed to feel when she reads the date of an ending. She does not feel shock. She does not feel grief. What she feels is a kind of low-frequency hum, not unlike the hum of the cable racks, a B-flat of the interior, a foundational tone beneath which there is only silence and above which there is everything she has built.

She thinks of the board.

She thinks of position 6 and position 7 on a Tuesday in March of 1963, the night the blizzard hit and only four operators made it in and she and Helen worked the whole B-board between them, moving from position to position like dancers in a figure they had not rehearsed but already knew, Helen's hands always arriving at the right jack a half-second before Marguerite expected, which meant that Marguerite had been expecting, which meant she had been tracking Helen's hands even then, six years ago, when she was still married and Helen was still married and the word for what she felt was _colleague_ or _partner_ in the occupational sense, the Bell System sense, the sense in which two women who share a board are partners the way two cops who share a beat are partners, a word that means proximity and reliance and nothing else, nothing else, nothing—

She thinks of position 4, the night Helen was moved there for a week because June Kowalski was out with bronchitis, and the distance between position 4 and position 6 was two seats and Marguerite could not see Helen's hands without turning her head, which she could not do while working the board, which meant that for five nights she worked in a condition of reduced information—she could hear Helen's voice answering calls but could not see the curl of the ring finger around the plug body—and the week felt like weather, like a change in pressure, like stepping outside in January after being in a warm room, a somatic thing, an absence that registered not in the mind but in the skin of her left arm which had grown accustomed to the possibility of contact and was now exposed to thirty-one inches of open air and found the air insufficient.

She thinks of position 7 and position 8, the night Helen came back from her mother's funeral and said nothing about it, just sat down and put on her headset and answered calls for eight hours in a voice that was exactly the same as her ordinary voice except that the _what_ in "what number please" had lost its breath, the aspiration gone, just a hard _w_ followed by _at_, a consonant cluster with no air in it, and Marguerite heard it on the first call and understood everything and said nothing and at two in the morning reached across the nineteen inches and adjusted Helen's headset, which did not need adjusting, and Helen let her.

She thinks of all the positions. All the numbers. The board is a coordinate system and she has plotted every shift on it and what emerges is not a schedule but a map, a map of a country that will not exist after December 22, 1969.

She folds the notice in her mind. She goes back into the room.

Helen is already there. Helen is at position 7. Her headset is on. She is looking at the board, at the lamps. She does not turn when Marguerite sits down. She does not say, _Did you see the notice?_ She does not say anything. She answers a call.

"Operator, what number please."

The _what_ is aspirated. The breath is there. Marguerite sits down and puts on her headset and for a moment, before the first lamp blooms on her panel, there is the hum and only the hum, B-flat, the sound of the room being the room for one of the last times it will be the room, and she listens to it the way she listens to everything here, which is to say with her whole body, with the skin of her left arm, with the specific attention of a woman who knows she is building a memory in real time and has no camera and no tape recorder and no language adequate to the task except the language of the switchboard, which is a language of positions and trunk lines and the distance between chairs.

> **The New York Times — October 18, 1969**
> **PHONE COMPANY TO COMPLETE MANHATTAN SWITCHOVER**
>
> The New York Telephone Company announced yesterday that the conversion of its remaining manual toll switchboards in Manhattan to electronic switching equipment is expected to be completed by year's end, marking the close of an era that stretches back to the earliest days of the telephone.
>
> The last manual boards, located at the company's Sixth Avenue toll center in Greenwich Village, handle long-distance calls that for decades have been connected by operators who physically insert plugs into jacks on large switchboard panels. Under the new system, calls will be routed automatically by computer.
>
> "This is the natural evolution of the network," said Gerald F. Bremer, vice president for operations. "Electronic switching gives us the capacity to handle three times the call volume at a fraction of the cost. It's a tremendous step forward for the customer."
>
> Some of the operators at the Sixth Avenue center have worked the manual boards for 20 years or more. Under the terms of a union agreement, they will be reassigned to other facilities throughout the city. Company officials say retraining programs are in place to ease the transition.
>
> The conversion began with testing of the Centralized Automatic Message Accounting system on the building's lower floors in September and is proceeding on schedule. The final switchboard is expected to go dark shortly before Christmas.
>
> "It's progress," Mr. Bremer said. "You can't hold back progress."

## III.

November 4, 1969. A Tuesday. The eleven-to-seven.

She sees it happen at 11:47 PM. Helen's left hand reaches for the trunk-multiple block and instead of the third jack in the second row—the bottle—she seats the plug in the third jack in the _third_ row, which is the trunk to White Plains, and the call is from a subscriber in the Bronx asking for a number in Philadelphia, and if the patching error reaches the monitoring equipment on the sixth floor the shift supervisor will flag it and it will go in Helen's record, which already has two notations this quarter—one for a late arrival on October 9, one for an accuracy variance on October 16—and three notations in a single quarter triggers a formal review.

Marguerite's hands move before her mind gives them permission.

She reaches across the nineteen inches. Her right hand closes over Helen's left, lifts it—the fingers warm, the ring-finger nail short and straight, the skin over the knuckles dry and slightly rough from the winter air that has begun to blow through the gap in the seventh-floor windows, a gap that Building Services has been asked to seal three times and has not sealed because Building Services does not prioritize repairs on floors scheduled for decommissioning, and
