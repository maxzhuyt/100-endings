# The Splenic Artery

---

## I.

Drip rate was off. She could hear it — the interval between each fall into the graduated cylinder she'd fashioned from a sawed-off saline bottle was running 22, 23 drops per minute, and she needed 18. Sera reached above her head with her free hand, found the hose clamp by touch, and rolled the thumbscrew a quarter-turn clockwise. The arm from which the blood was being drawn — her left, the dominant one, which was a problem she had noted and cataloged and set aside — lay extended on a shelf of buckled steel that had, in the barge's former life, been a cargo rail. The 14-gauge catheter she'd placed in her own antecubital fossa was taped down with three strips of adhesive from a kit that had survived the capsizing in a waterproof pouch. The tape was already curling at the edges from the humidity. She would re-dress it in ten minutes. She would set no alarm because there was no device. She would count.

The man beneath the beam had a name — James Dak Kuol, South Sudanese national, age approximately thirty-four, alias "the asset" in the operational file she had been given at Myitkyina and had memorized and then burned — but she was not thinking about his name. She was thinking about his spleen. Specifically, about the splenic artery where it branched from the celiac trunk, which she had isolated sixteen minutes ago using her index and middle fingers because she had no vascular clamps and no hemostats and no Deaver retractors and no operative field that was not contaminated with the brown tidal water of the Kaladan River, which entered the inverted hull through a fracture in the port side and sat at her waist, warm as blood and smelling of diesel and alluvial rot.

She adjusted her position. Her right hip had gone numb against the steel. The numbness was not yet a clinical concern but would become one if it progressed to her lower extremities; she needed her legs. She needed everything she had. She inventoried: no second pair of hands, no suction, no electrocautery, no anesthetic — she had given the last 3 milliliters of ketamine to Kuol thirty minutes ago and it would be wearing off soon, and when it did his abdominal wall would clench and she would lose the operative window she had created by manually retracting his rectus with a bent piece of hull siding wedged between two bolts. No light except the waterproof penlight she held in her teeth, which gave her a cone of vision approximately six centimeters in diameter at the depth of his peritoneal cavity. The liver laceration she had packed with strips of her own shirt, cotton-polyester blend, not remotely sterile, packed in three layers and held with pressure from a rolled waterproof bag cinched against his right flank. That was holding. The spleen was the problem.

The spleen was always the problem. Grade IV injury, severe parenchymal disruption, the hilum involved. In a hospital she would take it. She would perform a splenectomy in eleven minutes — her personal best was nine, at Landstuhl, on a nineteen-year-old with a steering column through his left upper quadrant, and she had done it so cleanly that the attending had stood back from the table and simply watched the last four minutes without gloving in. But she could not take the spleen because she could not control the splenic artery without clamps, and she could not control the hilum without visualization, and she could not get visualization without suction, and the cavity was filling with blood — his and hers, now commingled, type O-negative flowing from her antecubital vein through six feet of IV tubing and a hand-pumped bulb into a 16-gauge catheter she had placed, by feel, in the exposed mesenteric vein just visible above the waterline in his open abdomen. She was transfusing her own blood into his venous system to replace what his spleen was pouring into his peritoneum. She was, in effect, circulating herself through him. The arithmetic was not favorable.

She checked her watch. Forty-one minutes since catheter placement. At her estimated flow rate of 18 drops per minute, calibrated at approximately 20 drops per milliliter of whole blood through this tubing gauge, she had delivered roughly 36 milliliters. Negligible, therapeutically. But the pump — the ridiculous, brilliant pump she had rigged from a blood pressure bulb and a one-way valve harvested from the saline bag — increased effective flow when she squeezed it. She estimated she had been delivering closer to 40 to 50 milliliters per minute during active pumping phases. Over forty-one minutes, with interruptions for surgical work, perhaps twenty-five minutes of active pumping. Call it a liter. She had given him a liter of her blood.

Her total blood volume, at her weight of fifty-seven kilograms, was approximately 3.8 liters. She had given away just over a quarter of it.

Class II hemorrhage. Heart rate elevation: present, 104 by radial pulse at last check, eight minutes ago. Narrowed pulse pressure: probable. Anxiety: she did not experience anxiety. She experienced triage. Mild confusion: no. Her mentation was clear. She could recite the branches of the celiac trunk — left gastric, common hepatic, splenic — as easily as she could recite her service number as easily as she could recite the names of every patient she had lost in fourteen years of operational surgery, which was none, which was zero, which was a number she held inside her chest like a stone polished smooth.

Zero.

She squeezed the pump. She counted drops. She did not think about the dark water at her waist or the sounds beginning to gather outside the hull.

Her pulse at last check: 104.

She checked it again.

98.

It had dropped. She noted this. She did not yet consider why.

---

## II.

The first engine was audible at distance — she estimated 300 meters downriver, maybe less; sound traveled deceptively across the delta, especially in the flat channels between mangrove islands where the water acted as a reflective surface and the dense root systems above the waterline absorbed the higher frequencies, leaving only the low thrum of outboard motors and, now, the percussive stutter of a second engine, this one smaller, probably a longtail. She cataloged the sounds as she worked. It was not a decision to catalog them. It was what her mind did when her hands were occupied: it sorted the auditory environment into categories of relevance, assigning each input a priority value. Engines: high. Birdsong: zero. The lap of river water against the hull: zero until it changed in volume or rhythm, at which point it would indicate tidal shift or wake from approaching vessels.

She squeezed the pump. She checked Kuol's pupils with the penlight — equal, round, three millimeters, sluggishly reactive. He was still under. His blood pressure she could not measure, but his radial pulse was present, thready, fast. She placed two fingers on his wrist and counted against the second hand on her watch. 128. Too fast. He was still bleeding. She was putting blood in and the spleen was letting it out. She was Sisyphus with a hand pump and a 16-gauge catheter.

She needed to pack the splenic hilum. Directly. She needed to get her hand into his left upper quadrant and apply manual pressure to the hilum while simultaneously packing the area around it. This would require her to release the makeshift retractor, which would mean the abdominal wall would partially close over her hand. She had done field surgery in confined operative windows before. In Helmand she had performed a damage-control laparotomy in the back of a Humvee traveling at forty miles per hour over a road that was not a road while a corpsman held a headlamp in his teeth and wept. She had done it. Patient survived. They all survived. Zero.

She was repositioning her right hand, working it along the lateral aspect of Kuol's abdomen toward the left upper quadrant, when the loudspeaker crackled.

The sound came from outside — from the water, from a vessel, amplified and distorted by the metal hull around her so that for a moment it was simply a wash of static and overtone, uninterpretable. Then it resolved. A voice. Male. Speaking English.

"Sera."

Not Dr. Voss. Not the surgeon. Not any of the operational cover names she had used in Rakhine or Kachin or the northern Shan corridor. *Sera.*

She recognized the voice on the second syllable. The way it aspirated the first vowel, drawing it out — *Seh-rah* — with a precision that was not the precision of a non-native speaker trying to get the pronunciation right but the precision of a man who had practiced saying this name, who had held it in his mouth like a thing he was tasting.

Colonel Kyaw Min Oo. Tatmadaw, 33rd Light Infantry Division, or formerly; the organizational affiliations had become irrelevant since the coup, since the fracturing of the military into regional fiefdoms. He had his own men now. He had his own delta. He had, apparently, a loudspeaker.

She did not stop working. Her right hand continued its path along the peritoneal gutter toward the spleen. She squeezed the pump with her left hand. She counted.

"I have been thinking," the loudspeaker said, "about the day we met. Do you remember, Sera? Maungdaw. The field hospital that was not a hospital — it was a school, I think, or it had been a school. There were drawings on the walls. Children's drawings. And you were angry because the table was not level. You made them put books under one leg. Medical textbooks. I remember thinking: she puts knowledge under the table so the table is flat. She is practical. She is someone who uses what is available."

Sera's fingers found the splenic hilum. She applied pressure. Kuol's body jerked — the ketamine was thinning. She leaned her weight into her right hand and held. Blood welled between her fingers, warm and dark and steady. She began packing around the hilum with her left hand, releasing the pump to do it, pulling strips of cotton from the diminishing supply of her shirt. Each strip she placed with the deliberation of a mason setting tile. First layer: directly against the bleeding surface, firm, overlapping. Second layer: perpendicular, compressive, anchored against the body wall.

"The shrapnel was here," the loudspeaker said, and she could hear him touching himself — the muffled sound of a hand against fabric, against thigh. "In the leg. You said it had traveled from the entry point — you said it was like a fish, swimming through the muscle. You were amused by this. You smiled. I think that was the first time you smiled. You had been in the country eleven days and no one had seen you smile, and you smiled because a piece of metal was behaving like a fish inside my leg."

She packed. She pressed. She counted seconds. Thirty seconds of direct pressure, then reassess. The water at her waist had risen an inch since the last time she'd checked, she noted. Tidal, probably. Possibly wake from the approaching boats. She filed this.

"The infection lasted nine days. You did not leave. Other doctors came and went — the man from Geneva, the woman from Ireland — but you stayed. I was delirious for two of those days. You told me later that I spoke to you in my delirium. You said I spoke in English, which I do not remember. You said I told you things about my childhood. You said you held my hand. Do you remember this, Sera? You held my hand."

Her pulse. She needed to check her pulse. She released pressure on the hilum — held it, she had been holding for twenty-eight seconds, close enough — and moved her left hand to her own neck. She found the carotid. She counted. The engines were closer now. She could hear, beneath the loudspeaker voice, a second sound: the hiss and snap of fire being set to dry mangrove, the crackle of accelerant in the root systems, and the reason was obvious — they were burning the channel margins to clear sightlines, to remove cover, to make the delta into a flat, legible theatre in which a capsized barge would sit like a coin on a plate.

Her pulse: 94.

It was still dropping. Class II hemorrhage did not produce bradycardia. Her heart should be compensating for the volume loss by beating faster, not slower. The drop from 104 to 98 she had noted; the further drop to 94 she now noted. She assigned it a provisional explanation — vagal response to the sustained physical effort of manual compression, combined with hypothermic effect of prolonged immersion in water at approximately 28 degrees Celsius — and she did not believe her own explanation, and she set the disbelief aside because she was working.

"I have looked for you for three years," the loudspeaker said. The voice was closer now. The same calm, the same educated cadence — Kyaw Min Oo had been schooled in Yangon and then in Singapore, she remembered; his English had the faintly British inflections of a Burmese military academy overlaid with the flattened vowels of Southeast Asian commerce. "I looked for you in Cox's Bazar. I looked for you in Dhaka. I was told you had gone to Syria, and I looked for you there too. I sent men. They found the hospital in Raqqa where you had worked but you were already gone. You are always going, Sera. You are always leaving. But you are never finished. I understood this about you. You are never finished because there is always another body, and you cannot leave a body unfinished. This is what makes you — you."

A command in Burmese, sharp, to someone else on the vessel. Then the voice returned, softer.

"I burned two villages to bring you here. You should know this. The convoy was not supposed to enter the delta. The road north was open. I closed it. The bridge at Kyauktaw — I closed that too. I needed the convoy to take the river, and I needed the river to bring you to me, and it did. I want you to know that this was not careless. I chose the villages carefully. They were already mostly empty. The people had been warned. Not all of them, but most. I am not a monster, Sera. I am a man who understands what it costs to bring something valuable to where it belongs."

She checked Kuol's pupils again. Still reactive. She removed the first packing layer and inspected the hilum. The bleeding had slowed from arterial pulsation to a steady ooze. This was progress. This was, within the constraints of the situation, a minor triumph. She repacked with fresh strips, working methodically, pressing each one into place with a fingertip, holding for five seconds, moving on.

"You are coming home," the loudspeaker said.

She said nothing. She had not spoken aloud since the procedure began except to dictate her inventory and to count drops, and she did not speak now. She packed the wound. She squeezed the pump. She counted.

Somewhere outside, mangrove was burning, and the smoke came in through the hull fracture with the water.

## III.

At approximately the fifty-five-minute mark — she was losing precision on her time estimates, which she noted — the visual anomaly began.

It presented first as a narrowing of her peripheral field, a contraction of the cone of awareness from roughly 170 degrees to perhaps 140. She tested it by shifting her gaze left and right while holding her head still, noting the point at which objects resolved from blur to form. The penlight was dimming — batteries, not optics — and the reduction in ambient light made the field-narrowing harder to quantify. She assigned it a provisional explanation: Class II hemorrhage trending toward Class III, estimated blood volume loss now approaching 1.2 to 1.5 liters, cerebral perfusion pressure declining. The tingling in her fingertips — bilateral, worse on the left, which was the donor arm — was consistent. She made a decision. She reduced the pump rate.
