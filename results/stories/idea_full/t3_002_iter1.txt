# THE LAST VALET

---

**DOCUMENT CLASSIFICATION: FORMERLY RESTRICTED DATA — PRESIDENTIAL EMERGENCY RECORDS**
**RELEASED UNDER EXECUTIVE ORDER 14XXX, MARCH 15, 2038**

**PROVENANCE:** National Archives and Records Administration, Record Group 457-C, Presidential Emergency Operations Center audio capture system (modified), Reels 4417 through 4471. Recovered 1994 from secondary storage at the Federal Records Center, Suitland, Maryland, during a routine inventory audit. Chain of custody established through GSA transfer manifest 72-PEO-9, signed by [REDACTED], December 1968.

**ARCHIVAL NOTE:** The following materials consist of seventy-three hours, fourteen minutes of tape audio captured between October 17 and October 20, 1967, from fixed microphones installed in the Presidential Emergency Operations Center beneath the East Wing of the White House. The recordings were assembled and annotated by Mr. James Paul Calloway, personal valet to the President, between November 1967 and January 1968, in a facility the location of which remains classified. Mr. Calloway's spoken commentary was recorded on a separate track and interleaved with the original bunker audio at Mr. Calloway's direction. The resulting compilation runs eleven hours, forty-one minutes. The following is a complete transcript.

The National Archives has made no editorial alterations. Bracketed notations indicating non-speech audio events — [TAPE HISS], [INAUDIBLE], [PAUSE] — have been added by the transcription staff for clarity.

This document should be understood as a primary source, not a vetted historical narrative. Portions remain under review.

**[TAPE HISS — 0:00:00]**

**[REEL HEADER TONE — TWO SECONDS]**

**[MR. CALLOWAY BEGINS]**

My name is James Paul Calloway. From April 1965 until October 20, 1967, I served as the personal valet to the thirty-sixth President of the United States, Lyndon Baines Johnson. Before that I was assistant valet, and before that I pressed shirts at the Willard Hotel, and before that I was a boy in Opelousas, Louisiana, who didn't know what a valet was. I say this so you understand. I am not a general. I am not a policy expert. I was never asked for my opinion on anything that did not involve shoe polish, trouser creases, the President's evening medications, or whether the blue tie or the gray one would photograph better under Senate hearing lights.

What I was, and what I remain, is the man who knew the President's body. His shoe size was 11D. His preferred toilet paper was — and he was specific on this point — Charmin, double-ply, which the residence staff special-ordered from a supplier in Green Bay, Wisconsin, because the standard government-procurement brand gave him what he called "a raw deal on both ends of the transaction." He weighed 227 pounds the morning of October 17, 1967. I know because I weighed him. I weighed him every morning. He liked to know the number. It was the first fact of his day.

I knew the sound he made before a nightmare. It was not a sound most people would notice. It was a change in his breathing — a very slight catch at the top of the inhale, like a man about to sneeze who doesn't sneeze. When I heard it, I would be ready. Sometimes he woke on his own. Sometimes I woke him. He preferred to be woken with a hand on his shoulder, not his arm. The arm startled him. The shoulder didn't.

I am explaining this because what follows requires you to trust me about things that no one else can verify, and I want you to trust me for the right reason. I am not the most important voice on these tapes. The President is the most important voice. The generals and the advisors and the communications officers are the important voices. I am the least important person in every room I describe. But I was in every room. I was always in the room. That was my job. To be in the room and to be invisible and to make sure the President's body was cared for so that the President's mind could do its work.

What you are going to hear is audio I have selected from seventy-three hours of continuous tape, recorded in the Presidential Emergency Operations Center during the seventy-two-hour period that began at 4:47 a.m. Eastern Standard Time on October 17, 1967, and ended — for all practical purposes — at approximately 4:00 a.m. on October 20, 1967.

I have organized this material thematically. I have cut what is redundant and what is merely mechanical — long stretches of dial tones, open-channel static, equipment noise. I have added my own spoken commentary where I believed it was necessary for a future listener to understand what was happening in the room, since the microphones captured sound but not sight, and since many of the sounds are ambiguous without context.

I did this work alone. No one supervised me. No one reviewed the final product. I was given the tape reels by a man whose name I will not say, in a room I will not describe, and I was told to "make a record." So I have made a record. It is the only record that exists of what happened below the White House during those three days, because everyone else who was in that room is dead, and the only account of their dying is what I put on these tapes and what I am telling you now with my own voice, in a room that is very quiet, at a time of night I will not specify, in the month of November of the year 1967.

I want to be clear about one thing before we begin. I am not asking you to feel sorry for anyone. What happened above us was worse than what happened below. I know that. Everybody in the bunker knew that. We were the lucky ones. We had seventy-two hours. Some of the people up above had four minutes.

So.

Let me tell you who was in the room.

---

## I.

---

We went below at 4:47 a.m. I know the time because the President asked me the time and I told him, and then he asked me again ninety seconds later and I told him again, and then he said, "James, what goddamn day is it?" and I said, "Tuesday, Mr. President," and he said, "Tuesday," as though I'd told him something about the nature of the universe that offended him personally.

The route down was not long. Elevator to the basement level, then a corridor, then the blast doors. I had been through it in drills. In the drills, the Secret Service timed us: ninety seconds from the residence bedroom to the sealed door, moving at what they called "an assertive walk." They did not want the President running. A running President created problems of optics and problems of cardiac risk in roughly equal measure, and the Secret Service was professionally responsible for preventing both.

That morning was not a drill, and we were not walking assertively. We were walking the way you walk when two men in suits are holding your elbows and a third is ahead of you and a fourth is behind you and all four of them are moving faster than you thought human beings could move without technically running. The President was in his pajamas. Blue cotton, monogrammed. I had his robe over my arm. I had his slippers in my hand. He was barefoot on the corridor tile and I remember thinking: his feet will be cold, and what I should have brought was the wool socks, the gray ones, in the second drawer.

The blast door was already open. The smell hit us first. Not a bad smell — a nothing smell. Recirculated air, concrete, the faint chemical tang of the CO₂ scrubbers that I later learned to hate. There were Marines on either side of the door and they did not look at us as we went through. They were looking at something behind us, or above us, or at a point in space that did not correspond to anything you could name.

The door sealed. It was not a slam. It was a slow hydraulic compression, a sound like the world clearing its throat, and then a series of metallic engagement sounds — locks, bolts, pressure gaskets — that took eleven seconds. I counted. You count things when you don't know what else to do.

**[MR. CALLOWAY NARRATION RESUMES]**

He was on the toilet within four minutes of arrival. I timed the door seal; I timed this too. You count. He stayed there, with brief interruptions, for roughly the next sixty hours.

I should describe the facility. It was smaller than most people imagine when they hear "presidential bunker." The main operations room was perhaps forty feet by thirty. Low ceiling — seven and a half feet, which made the President look even larger than he was, and the President was a large man. Fluorescent lighting, which gave everyone the complexion of someone who had been dead for a day and a half. A conference table that sat twelve, metal-legged, bolted to the floor. Along the walls, communications stations: banks of telephones, some with dedicated lines — the Moscow hotline, the SAC command line, NORAD, the Pentagon's National Military Command Center — others switchboard-routed. Two teletype machines, which clattered with such metronomic persistence that by the second day we had stopped hearing them, the way you stop hearing rain.

Off the main room, there were four ancillary spaces. A medical bay, roughly the size of a walk-in closet, which the Navy physician, Commander Edward Garrity, immediately claimed and organized with a competence that I found, in those first hours, almost indecently reassuring. A communications annex, where the more sensitive equipment lived — encryption machines, the hotline teletype, things that had blinking lights whose meaning I never learned. A supply room, stocked to sustain thirty people for thirty days: canned food, water rations, medications, blankets, a chemical toilet. And the restroom I have already mentioned, which had a regular flush toilet connected to the building's deep sewage line — a small luxury that the President treated as a strategic asset.

There were twenty-six of us.

I will name them all. I have thought about whether to do this. Some of their families may not know where they died, or how, and what I say here may cause pain that serves no purpose but the historical record. But I have decided that the historical record is purpose enough, and that being named is better than not being named, and that these people were in a room, and they were real, and I will not reduce them to a number.

At the conference table, the senior staff: Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara was not physically present; he was in the Pentagon's own bunker, connected by a dedicated line that worked for approximately fourteen hours before it didn't. Physically present were National Security Advisor Walt Rostow, who arrived with a leather briefcase containing documents he never opened and who sat at the table's head as though chairing a meeting that would eventually come to order; Deputy National Security Advisor Francis Bator, who was thirty-nine years old and who carried in his wallet a photograph of his twin daughters that he removed and placed on the table in front of him within the first ten minutes and did not touch again; and Special Assistant Jack Valenti, who should not have been there at all — he had officially left the White House staff eight months earlier — but who had been at the residence for a private dinner the night before and who, when the alert sounded, simply followed the President down without being told to leave, because Jack Valenti's gravitational center was Lyndon Johnson and nothing, not even the end of the world, altered that orbit.

The military contingent: Colonel Arthur Wellman, the senior military aide, who carried the "football" — the leather satchel containing the nuclear authentication codes — and who set it on the table next to Rostow's briefcase and never moved more than three feet from it for the rest of his life, which was fifty-one more hours; Major Thomas Dupree, Wellman's deputy, who had a habit of clicking his pen that irritated everyone in the room but that no one asked him to stop because the irritation was better than the silence between clicks; and Brigadier General Lewis Hendricks, the liaison to the Joint Chiefs, who was the calmest man in the bunker and who remained the calmest man in the bunker until the moment he died, at which point his calmness became indistinguishable from the kind of calm that is permanent.

Communications personnel — five of them. Lieutenant Commander Peter Ostrowski, Navy, who managed the hotline terminal and who in the first eight hours personally attempted over three hundred connections to Moscow and logged each failure in a green-covered notebook with handwriting so precise it might have been typeset. Sergeant First Class Michael Reeves, Army Signal Corps, twenty-four years old, from Decatur, Illinois, whose mother ran a laundromat and whose father had been dead for six years of a thing Michael called "the sugar disease," by which he meant diabetes. Reeves was the one who, when the blast doors sealed, said, in a voice I am certain he believed no one could hear, "Oh, Mama." He was the first to die, and I will come to that.

Airman First Class David Yoon, twenty-two, who operated the secondary teletype and who spoke, in addition to English, fluent Korean and passable Mandarin, a fact that became important later and that I mention now so you will understand its significance when the moment arrives. Petty Officer Second Class Robert Halloran, known to everyone as Bobby H., who managed the radio room and who had a gap between his front teeth you could slide a dime through, which I know because he demonstrated this at the President's request at approximately 6:00 a.m. on the first morning, during a four-minute window of relative calm, and the President laughed — the first and perhaps the last genuine laugh of the whole time.

The fifth communications person was Specialist Patricia Willard, Army, twenty-six, the only woman in the bunker, who was responsible for encryption and who wore her hair pinned in a way that must have taken ten minutes every morning and that never shifted by a single strand through three days underground, which I mention because it struck me, and because the small disciplines people maintain when everything else is falling apart are worth recording. She had been engaged to be married, three weeks out from her wedding. She told Commander Garrity this during her first medical check, and Garrity said, "Well, you'll have one hell of a story for the reception," and she looked at him and said, "Will I," not as a question, and Garrity never tried to comfort anyone with words again.

Commander Garrity: the Navy physician. Fifty-two years old. He had been the White House physician for three administrations and he moved through that bunker the way a man moves through a house he built himself. He knew where every first aid kit was, every water shutoff, every fire extinguisher. He had memorized the facility layout during a renovation in 1962 and could, and did, navigate it with his eyes closed when the lights went out for forty minutes on the second day. He carried his own stethoscope, his own blood pressure cuff, his own supply of potassium iodide tablets
