# Threshold

## I.

The corridor was hers the way a throat belongs to a breath.

Keva Sorellis pushed off the far bulkhead with the ball of her left foot, drove her shoulder into the rotation, and let the spin carry her seventeen meters down the vacuum-rated practice corridor without a single corrective twitch. The threshold-ball was already where she needed it to be — magnetized to the ceiling plate at the corridor's midpoint, waiting on its release timer — and when it dropped she didn't reach for it so much as arrive at the precise coordinates where reaching became unnecessary. The ball kissed the dead center of her forearm plate with a sound like a fingernail tapping glass, and she absorbed its momentum into a redirected arc that sent her body curling toward the opposite wall in a descending corkscrew so clean it could have been diagrammed for a textbook.

She caught the wall with both feet. Pushed again. Took the ball on the backhand plate this time, letting it roll the length of her arm and settle into the shoulder cradle before flicking it hard toward the scoring threshold — the luminescent rectangle painted on the corridor's endcap — and watching it pass through the plane with four centimeters of clearance on every side.

The scorer chimed. She didn't look at it.

Twice more she ran the sequence. The third time she added a feint — a half-rotation that would, in a match, force an opponent to commit to the wrong intercept angle — and redirected the ball behind her own back, between her knees, and into the threshold from an approach vector that most players couldn't visualize, let alone execute in full vacuum. Her breathing was metronomic. Her heart rate, visible on the biometric strip wrapped around her left bicep, read sixty-one. She was not trying. She was maintaining.

On the fourth pass she let the ball float and hung in the middle of the corridor, motionless except for the slow counter-rotation of her hips that kept her orientation stable. She pulled her mouthguard out, examined it, seated it again. Tore the tape from her left shin brace and rewound it — three wraps below the knee, two above the ankle, tension coming from the thumb, same as always. Then she drifted to the launch wall and found the ridge.

It was a manufacturing seam, maybe three millimeters proud of the corridor surface, running vertically near the standard push-off zone. She traced it with the fingers of her right hand, top to bottom, the way someone else might touch a talisman before walking through a door. Her fingertips knew it the way her forearm plate knew the ball. It was not superstition. It was sequence. The ridge was the last thing she touched before every drill, every match, every serious effort. It meant: the warm-up is over. It meant: become the thing you already are.

She pushed off the wall and ran the scoring sequence again. Sixty-one beats per minute. Four centimeters of clearance. The ball didn't know it was supposed to be hard to control because Keva's body had never told it so.

Everything she did in that corridor referred back to something else she had done in a corridor exactly like it. Every motion was a citation of a previous motion. She had been the top-ranked human threshold-ball player for two full seasons, and before that she had been the under-nineteen champion for three, and before that she had been a skeletal girl in a junior academy on Titania Station who could already do things in zero gravity that her coaches had to slow down on replay to believe. The progression was linear. The improvement was asymptotic. She was not getting dramatically better anymore because dramatic improvement requires a gap between what the body can do and what the game demands, and that gap, for Keva, had been closed for years.

She moved through the corridor the way water moves through a pipe it has carved for itself. Perfectly. Without remainder. Without the slightest indication that the pipe might someday need to carry something other than water.

## II.

The Pallith arrived in a single transit pod that docked at Bay 11 with a dull magnetic clang felt through three bulkheads. Keva was in the observation mezzanine above the staging area, cooling down, her towel drifting beside her in a slow helical rotation she didn't bother to arrest. Below, the bay doors opened, and what came through them was not a team in any sense her body understood.

There were nine of them. Or there were nine visible centers of mass — it was immediately difficult to say whether "nine" was the right number because the Pallith did not hold still long enough to be counted the way humans hold still. They poured into the staging bay in a loose, rolling formation, their exoskeletal limbs — four primary, two secondary, the secondary pair shorter and seemingly more dexterous — firing micro-bursts off every surface they touched. They were vacuum-native. The bay's pressurized atmosphere was, if anything, a concession to the human teams, and the Pallith moved through it with the visible impatience of creatures tolerating an inferior medium.

Their chromatophores started cycling the moment they cleared the pod threshold. Waves of color — deep violet to arterial red to a white so bright it looked like a malfunction — rippled across the smooth plates of their dorsal surfaces and down the articulated segments of their limbs. The patterns were fast, overlapping, call-and-response. One body would flare a sequence of gold pulses from its thorax and three others would answer in staggered cascades of blue-green, and then the entire group would shift into a synchronized rolling pattern of amber that lasted exactly two seconds before dissolving into nine different conversations happening simultaneously. It was language. It was obviously, unmistakably language — structured, responsive, dense with syntax — and it was completely, immovably opaque.

Keva watched. Around her on the mezzanine, other humans watched too: athletes from the Callisto bracket, a few coaches, a cluster of tournament officials with recording equipment. Everyone's eyes tracked the color the way a crowd tracks a series of fireworks — compulsively, with the persistent sense that comprehension was about to arrive and never quite arriving.

Below, the Pallith began to move through the bay in what might have been a warm-up.

They did not drill the way Keva drilled. There were no repetitions. No isolations of specific skills. Instead they seemed to be *interviewing* the space — launching off walls at odd angles, clustering in groups of two or three, dispersing, reforming, testing the bay's geometry the way a jazz ensemble might test the acoustics of an unfamiliar room. Their secondary limbs ran across bulkhead surfaces with a tactile attention that looked almost scientific. One of them — slightly larger than the others, with a dorsal plate pattern that was more matte than luminous — hung at the center of the bay and rotated slowly while the other eight moved around it in shifting configurations that had no repeatability Keva could identify. Each configuration lasted between four and ten seconds. None of them recurred.

Then, without visible signal, one of the peripheral Pallith launched the threshold-ball.

It was a standard tournament ball — magnetized composite, human-designed, the same ball Keva had been catching on her forearm plate for ten years — and the Pallith handled it like it was an afterthought. The ball moved from body to body in a chain of contacts so rapid that Keva initially lost track of it. It would appear in the grip of one Pallith's secondary limbs, vanish, reappear on the dorsal plate of another five meters away, vanish again, and materialize in what seemed like an impossible location — behind the cluster, above it, once apparently *inside* a gap between two bodies that had briefly interlocked their exoskeletal limbs in a way that created a kind of moving cage around the ball's trajectory. The ball was never still. The Pallith were never still. The entire bay had become a single kinetic system in which nine bodies and one ball were operating under rules that Keva's twelve years of threshold-ball training could not reverse-engineer.

And then the larger one — the matte-plated one who had been rotating at the center — did something that made Keva's hands tighten on the mezzanine rail.

It caught the ball on a primary limb, curled into a shape no human spine could approximate — a tight helical compression that turned its entire body into a loaded spring — and launched itself *backward*, away from the scoring wall, at what appeared to be full speed. Two of the peripheral Pallith immediately collapsed into the gap it left, interlocking limbs again, forming something that looked like a wall, or a gate, or a ramp. The large one hit them from behind. They *redirected* it — used its momentum, reshaped its vector — and it came off their joined bodies on a new trajectory, spinning now, the ball tucked somewhere inside its compressed form, aimed at the scoring threshold from an angle that was, by any human standard of the game, suicidal. You did not approach the threshold from a seventy-degree lateral offset while spinning. The ball could not be aimed with that much angular momentum acting on the thrower's body. It was a physics problem with no solution Keva's neuromuscular architecture could generate.

The Pallith uncoiled a meter from the threshold. The ball came out of its body — released from between two secondary limbs at the apex of the spin — and passed through the scoring plane so fast the threshold sensors registered the score before the flash of transit light had fully decayed.

On the mezzanine, someone's hand went to their mouth. Keva saw it in her peripheral vision. She didn't look away from the bay.

The chromatophores of all nine Pallith pulsed simultaneously: a single, uniform wash of deep violet that lasted exactly one heartbeat and then shattered into nine different cascading patterns, like an orchestra breaking from unison into polyphony. The large one drifted to the center of the bay again and resumed its slow rotation. The others re-formed around it in a new configuration.

Keva's biometric strip read seventy-four.

She had just watched something she could not have predicted, could not have defended, and could not have replicated. Not the scoring shot — she had seen difficult shots, she had made difficult shots, she had made shots that coaches called impossible — but the *architecture* of it. The way it required three bodies to build a single trajectory. The way the two wall-Pallith had known, without any visible signal Keva could detect, exactly when to interlock and exactly how to angle the redirecting surface. The way the large one had trusted the redirect so completely that it launched backward. Away from the target. Into the arms of its own team.

Nothing in the corridor she had just left — nothing in the closed, perfected, self-referencing system of Keva Sorellis doing what Keva Sorellis did better than any human alive — had prepared her for the possibility that the game could look like that.

The Pallith kept moving below. They did not repeat the sequence. They were not going to repeat the sequence. It had already become the past, and the swarm was in a new present, testing new geometries, cycling new colors, solving the bay's space in real time with a collective intelligence that did not need to rehearse because it was not drawing from a library of stored solutions. It was generating.

Keva floated on the mezzanine, her towel turning its slow helix beside her, and watched the colors she could not read.

## III.

Coach Anh Dao's operational berth was a two-meter closet off the main concourse, fitted with a fold-down display surface and a charging dock thick with tournament data cables. She worked the way she always worked: standing — or rather, anchored by a foot loop to the floor plate, her body canted at the persistent fifteen-degree lean that years of station gravity had written into her posture. She did not move more than she needed to move. She did not take up more space than the work required.

The bracket was on her screen. The tournament's algorithm had generated a seeded draw based on cumulative rankings: human players slotted by interstation record, Pallith players slotted by the hive-cluster delegation's own submitted rankings, cross-species matchups distributed to avoid first-round collisions between top seeds. The algorithm's recommendation had Keva facing a mid-ranked Ganymede player in Round 1, with the Pallith swarm-captain — the matte-plated one, the one the hive-cluster's submission designated with a glyph the tournament software rendered as *Olo-Set-Kashet* — seeded into Keva's projected quarterfinal.

Dao looked at this bracket for a long time.

Then she overrode it.

The tournament rules permitted coach-initiated seeding adjustments for any player within a two-seed range. Dao used the provision to move Olo-Set-Kashet into the first round. Into Keva's opening match. The adjustment was technically within parameters. It was also, by any competitive logic, insane. You did not spend your top seed's opening round on the most dangerous opponent in the draw. You built through the bracket. You gave your player easy early wins to establish rhythm, to warm the proprioceptive systems, to arrive at the critical matches with momentum and confidence intact. Every coaching manual written in the last forty years of interstation threshold-ball said this. Dao had helped write two of them.

She confirmed the override. The bracket reshuffled. Keva Sorellis versus Olo-Set-Kashet, Round 1, Bay 6, 0900 station time.

Dao closed the bracket screen and opened Keva's training queue. The queue was a sequenced list of drills that would load to the corridor's targeting system and guide the next day's preparation. Dao had built hundreds of these queues for Keva over five years. She could construct a pre-match drill sequence in her sleep: scoring progressions, defensive repositioning sets, the high-pressure sprint drills that simulated end-of-match fatigue conditions. She knew Keva's body the way a surgeon knows an anatomy. She knew exactly which drills would make Keva sharper, faster, more precise — more of what Keva already was.

She queued none of them.

Instead she loaded a single drill: Adaptive Response 7. It was an old exercise, legacy code from the original threshold-ball training libraries, rarely used in competitive preparation because it wasn't, in any traditional sense, a threshold-ball drill. AR-7 didn't train scoring. It didn't train defense. What it did was fire the ball from randomized positions at randomized intervals with randomized spin values, and the player's sole task was to make contact. Not to score. Not to redirect toward a target. Just to touch the ball. The drill penalized prediction — if the tracking system detected the player pre-positioning for a likely launch vector, it adjusted the vector away from them. The only way to succeed at AR-7 was to stop anticipating and start reacting. To empty the library. To reach for a ball you didn't know was coming, from a place you hadn't decided to be, with a body that had no plan.

Keva hated AR-7. She had hated it since the junior academy. She was markedly worse at it than at any drill that rewarded the things she was best at: pattern recognition, spatial memory, the fluid execution of rehearsed sequences under pressure. AR-7 penalized all of those. It was the one drill that made Keva Sorellis look ordinary.

Dao queued it as the only item in tomorrow's preparation. She tagged it with no annotation, no context, no explanatory note. The drill would simply appear on Keva's corridor display the following morning, unlabeled, unexplained — a single exercise where Keva's perfection would be a liability, loaded by a coach who had chosen not to say why.

Dao closed the training queue. She undocked from the display surface and pushed off toward the concourse. The route to Keva's recovery bay passed through the junction at Frame 40 — a thirty-second detour at most, a brief interruption, a chance to arrive, sit beside her, put a hand on the shoulder that was always tight after heavy drill days, and offer the animal comfort of simple physical proximity. To be present in the way coaches are supposed to be present
