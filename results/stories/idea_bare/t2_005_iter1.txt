# The Mother Tongue

## I.

The apartment smelled like boiled cabbage and Fabuloso, which was exactly how it was supposed to smell, though Katya wouldn't understand that for another three weeks.

She stood in the doorway holding a roll of garbage bags and a collapsible dolly she'd borrowed from the department administrator, who had hugged her and said, *Take all the time you need, hon*, which in administrative parlance meant five business days. The funeral had been Tuesday. It was now Thursday. Katya had forty-eight hours to reduce her mother's life to what could fit in a sedan.

The apartment was a one-bedroom in a low brick building on a street in Northeast Philadelphia where every third storefront sign was in Cyrillic. Katya's mother had lived here for twenty-two years, since leaving the rowhouse in Mayfair where Katya had grown up, the one with the cracked linoleum and the radiators that clanked like someone was trapped inside them. That house had been sold when Katya was in graduate school. This apartment was smaller but cleaner. Her mother had always been clean. Occupational reflex: thirty-one years in the laundry department of Temple University Hospital, feeding sheets into industrial mangles, her hands mapped with steam scars she dismissed as nothing, *nothing, nothing*, waving them like she was conducting a very small orchestra.

Katya turned on the overhead light. The bulb was the wrong wattage — too bright, the kind of bare fluorescence that makes everything look detained. She could see the grease shadow above the stove, the prayer corner with its brass-framed icon of the Theotokos and the dried palm cross from last spring's Verbna Nedilia. The shelf above the television held a photograph of Katya at her doctoral hooding ceremony, sandwiched between a ceramic rooster and a tin of Royal Dansk butter cookies that contained, as it had for Katya's entire life, sewing supplies.

She started in the kitchen because the kitchen was legible. She knew these objects. She'd bought half of them — the electric kettle, the silicone spatulas, the set of nesting bowls she'd ordered from Amazon two Christmases ago, which her mother had accepted with the particular grace of a woman who already owned perfectly good bowls. The pantry held Lipton tea (always Lipton, never anything Katya tried to introduce, no Earl Grey, no oolong, no loose-leaf anything), a bag of buckwheat groats, a jar of sour cherry preserves with a handwritten label, three cans of condensed milk. The refrigerator: half a loaf of dark bread, a heel of salo wrapped in newspaper, a Tupperware of borscht that Katya opened, smelled, and sealed shut again with the careful steadiness of someone defusing something.

She bagged the perishables. She boxed the dishes. She moved to the living room and stripped it with efficient sorrow — the afghans, the embroidered pillows, the back issues of *Ukrainian Weekly* stacked on the radiator cover. She was good at this. Not because she was heartless but because she was structured. Her mother had once said, not unkindly, *You have a brain like a filing cabinet, Katyusha. Everything goes in a drawer. Even feelings. Especially feelings.* This had been during their last real argument, seven years ago, when Katya had suggested that her mother might consider moving to a place with an elevator, and her mother had said, *I will die in this apartment,* and Katya had said, *That's not a plan, that's a threat,* and her mother had gone silent in the particular way that was not silence at all but a door slamming in a language Katya had never been taught.

She had died in this apartment. The coroner said it was quick.

The bedroom was small. A single bed with a white coverlet, tight as a drumskin. A wooden wardrobe. A nightstand with a reading lamp, a glass of water still half-full, a blister pack of omeprazole. Katya sat on the bed and drank the water because she was thirsty and because the alternative was to leave it there, and she couldn't leave it there.

She opened the wardrobe. Winter coats: a long wool one the color of ash, a puffy polyester anorak from Burlington Coat Factory, a rain slicker still in its packaging. Katya pulled them out. Behind them, the wardrobe was deeper than it appeared — the back panel had been removed, opening into a cavity that must have once been a linen closet, accessed from the hallway, now sealed off and repurposed. In it, stacked floor to almost-ceiling, were boxes.

Katya counted nineteen.

They were uniform — archival document boxes, the acid-free kind, each labeled on the spine with a strip of masking tape bearing a sequence of numbers in her mother's handwriting. No words. She pulled out the nearest one. It was heavy. Inside: notebooks. Composition books, the marbled black-and-white kind, filled margin to margin with handwriting in a script Katya did not recognize.

She sat on the floor of the bedroom with the notebook open on her knees, and for several seconds the only sound was the fluorescent light buzzing and the radiator conducting its invisible prisoner.

The script was not Cyrillic. It was not Latin, Greek, Georgian, Armenian, Arabic, Devanagari, Hangul, or any of the other writing systems Katya could identify on sight — and she could identify most of them on sight, because she was an associate professor of linguistics at the University of Pennsylvania, because she had a PhD from MIT, because she had spent her professional life studying the structural anatomy of human language and could read the IPA transcription of a click consonant the way a cardiologist reads an EKG. She turned the pages. The script was consistent — not the wandering invention of someone doodling, but a stable orthographic system with clear grapheme boundaries, apparent punctuation conventions, and what looked like systematic diacritical marking. She counted distinct characters. At least thirty-six. Possibly forty, depending on whether certain forms were allographic variants or separate graphemes.

She put the notebook down. She opened the next box. More notebooks Cassette tapes — Maxell UR 90s, the kind you bought in bulk at Walgreens, each labeled with the same numbering system. She opened a third box. Loose pages, hand-stitched into chapbooks with heavy thread, the kind of stitching her mother would have known from mending. A fourth box: a single volume, thick, bound in what appeared to be an old leather portfolio case. She opened it. Handwritten pages, double-columned. On the left, words in the unknown script. On the right, definitions — but not in English. Not in Ukrainian. In a mixture of both, with occasional annotations in what looked like French. Alphabetized by the unknown script's apparent ordering. Four hundred pages. Maybe more.

A dictionary.

Her mother — Halyna Markovych, born 1951 in a village outside Lviv, emigrated 1979, naturalized 1986, employed for three decades in the basement of a hospital feeding soiled linens into a machine — her mother had written a dictionary.

Katya turned back to the first notebook and opened it to a random page. She was looking at a block of continuous text — a paragraph, a passage, something with the density of prose rather than a word list. Her eyes moved across it, and she felt, against her will and underneath her grief, the old pull. The analytical reflex. The itch.

She noticed that certain character sequences recurred with high frequency at the beginning of words — possible prefixes, suggesting agglutinative morphology. She noticed that a particular character (a small, curved mark like a reversed comma) appeared intermittently above certain vowels and never above others, suggesting a tonal or phonological distinction that the base grapheme didn't capture. She noticed that what appeared to be verb forms — she was guessing now, but the positional patterns were suggestive — carried chains of suffixes that varied systematically, and she started counting the paradigm slots.

She put the notebook down. She picked it back up.

Her phone rang. It was her colleague Marcus, calling to check in, to say he was sorry again, to ask if she needed anything.

"My mother wrote a language," Katya said.

There was a pause. "Like — a diary in Ukrainian?"

"No. A language. A full language. I'm sitting in her bedroom looking at what appears to be a complete constructed language with its own orthography and at least four hundred pages of lexicon."

Another pause. "Jesus. Like Tolkien?"

"I don't know what it's like. I don't know what anything is like."

She stayed on the floor of the bedroom until the light outside the window turned the color of exhaust. She did not box the notebooks. She loaded them into her car in their archival boxes, one trip at a time, nineteen boxes, and drove to her apartment in West Philadelphia with the windows down because the air inside the car felt like it had been sealed in there for decades.

## II.

On Monday she went to the office because the alternative was to stay home with the boxes, and she wasn't ready to open them all. She opened them anyway. She opened them at her desk in the linguistics department, in the office she'd occupied for six years — the one with the spectrogram printout pinned to the corkboard and the complete set of *Language* journals on the shelf and the dying pothos on the windowsill that her graduate students took turns watering and that was dying anyway, as if out of principle.

She started with the dictionary.

The lexicon was vast. Not the two or three hundred roots of a hobbyist's project, not the curated aesthetic vocabulary of someone building a world for a novel. This was a working lexicon — thousands of entries, covering the mundane and the specific with the lopsided abundance of a real language. There were seventeen distinct terms related to types of bread. There were four words for silence, differentiated by what the dictionary's marginal notes (in her mother's cramped English) indicated were contextual distinctions: the silence of a room after someone has left it, the silence of a person who is choosing not to speak, the silence of a landscape after snowfall, the silence that precedes a lie. There was a rich vocabulary for weather, for cooking, for textile work, for the parts of the body, for emotional states that English would require circumlocution to describe.

Katya spent three hours on the kinship terminology alone. The system was bifurcate-collateral — it distinguished between maternal and paternal relatives with different root terms — but it also encoded something she'd seen in only a handful of documented systems: a distinction between kin who were *present* (alive, in contact, known) and kin who were *absent* (dead, estranged, lost, unknown). The language had, in other words, a grammaticalized way of saying *my mother's sister who is no longer here,* and the term for that was different from *my mother's sister who is still among us.* The absent-kin terms had their own morphological patterns, their own suffix chains. They were not simply negated forms of the present-kin terms. They were a parallel system. As though absence were not the mere lack of presence but a condition with its own architecture, its own rooms.

She worked through the verb morphology with a legal pad and a system of colored tabs, building paradigm tables the way she would for a field language. The verbs were agglutinative: a root plus a chain of suffixes encoding tense, aspect, mood, and — this stopped her — evidentiality.

Evidentiality. Grammaticalized source-of-knowledge marking. She knew the phenomenon well. Turkish had it. Quechua had it. Many Tibeto-Burman languages had it. In Turkish, for instance, the suffix *-mış* on a verb told you the speaker hadn't directly witnessed the event — it was reported, inferred, or dreamed. The absence of the suffix implied firsthand experience. Simple binary: I saw it, or I didn't.

Her mother's language did not have a binary system. It had — Katya counted five times, then six, then settled on seven — it had *seven* evidentiality distinctions. She laid them out:

1. Direct sensory witness (I saw/heard/touched this myself)
2. Inference from physical evidence (I see the tracks; therefore, something passed)
3. Reported by a known, named source (Mariya told me this)
4. Reported by an unknown or collective source (people say / it is said)
5. Culturally inherited knowledge (we have always known this / this is ancestral)
6. Knowledge obtained under duress or coercion (I was made to know this / I was told this by force)
7. Knowledge carried from a place that no longer exists (this comes from there, from before)

The sixth and seventh categories made Katya put her pen down. She had never encountered those distinctions in any natural or constructed language. The sixth — *knowledge obtained under duress* — was a grammatical marker for testimony extracted by force. The seventh — *knowledge carried from a place that no longer exists* — was a grammatical marker for diasporic memory, for information whose origin had been destroyed.

She picked up her pen. She put it down again. She pressed her fingertips against her eyelids until she saw the phosphene static, and then she kept working, because the work was the only container she had, and if she stopped working she would have to feel what was underneath the work, and what was underneath the work was a question she was not ready to ask, which was: *Mama, who were you?*

She spent the next four days mapping the morphological system. She skipped meals and forgot to water the pothos, which finally died and which her graduate student, Tomás, quietly removed without comment. She slept on the office couch. She played the cassettes on a Walkman she bought at a thrift store on Baltimore Avenue, lying on her back with headphones on, listening to her mother's voice reading aloud in a language she had never heard her speak.

Her mother's voice in this language was different. This was the thing Katya could not put in a paradigm table. In English, Halyna's voice had been careful, accented, slightly formal — the voice of a woman who had learned the language as an adult and never fully trusted it. In Ukrainian, which Katya spoke passably, her mother's voice had been warmer but still measured, the voice of someone performing a known role. On these tapes, in this language, her mother's voice was — the word Katya kept circling was *released*. Lower in register. More rhythmically varied. The prosody was different, not just in the predictable ways that different phonological systems shape intonation, but in some quality of *ease*, as though the vocal cords themselves had been clenched for decades and had finally, privately, on a Maxell UR 90, unclenched.

The diachronic variation was what broke through Katya's professional composure and into something rawer. The notebooks were dated — not with standard calendar dates, but with a numbering system she eventually mapped to years by cross-referencing with datable events mentioned in the margins. The earliest notebooks were from approximately 1981. The most recent was from 2023 — the year her mother died. Forty-two years of continuous writing. And the language *changed.*

She could see it. In the early notebooks, the phonological inventory was slightly different — certain vowels appeared in positions where later notebooks used different vowels, consistent with a chain shift. A voiceless velar fricative in early texts corresponded to a glottal fricative in later ones — the same kind of sound change that turned Middle English *niht* into Modern English *night.* The morphology simplified in some areas and complexified in others, exactly as natural languages evolved under the pressures of use and time. There were even what appeared to be *slang* annotations in later notebooks — informal variants marked with a small symbol that Katya interpreted as an indicator of colloquial register.

No one built this into a hobby project. No one sitting alone at a kitchen table decided to engineer naturalistic sound changes across four decades for the aesthetic pleasure of verisimilitude. This was not Tolkien. Tolkien was a genius, but Tolkien's languages were designed to be beautiful, to fit a mythology, to satisfy an artist's vision of internal consistency. This was something else. This was a language that *behaved as though it had been spoken by a living community across real time,* because — and here Katya felt the ground shift beneath her, the way ground shifts in dreams, not violently but with a sickening gentleness —

Because maybe it had been.
