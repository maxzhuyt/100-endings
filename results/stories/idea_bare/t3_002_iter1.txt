# The Language Beneath

## I.

The sandwich was bad. Not memorably bad, not bad in a way that would later lend itself to narrative, but bad in the particular way of faculty-lounge vending machines at three in the afternoon on a Thursday in March — the bread giving up, the lettuce already translucent, the cheese food product maintaining its shape out of spite. Maren ate it anyway. She was preparing testimony for the Kessler fraud case, twelve pages of phonemic analysis demonstrating that the handwritten codicil to Harold Kessler's will had been dictated rather than self-composed, that the syntactic fingerprint belonged not to the deceased but to his nephew, and the work was dull enough to require calories, even punitive ones.

Her office in the linguistics department at Chesham University occupied the end of a hallway that smelled, depending on the season, of radiator dust or mildew. The bookshelves held grammars of extinct languages arranged by date of last known speaker: Eyak, Ubykh, Dalmatian, a whole cemetery of tongues shelved in cream and oxblood spines. On the wall behind her desk, framed in black, was a spectrogram of the last living speaker of Klallam pronouncing the word for "river." A colleague had once called it morbid. Maren had called it precise. The spectrogram did not interpret, did not mourn. It recorded. That was what she admired about it. Language pinned under glass, still and measurable, finally safe from the indignity of being spoken.

The email came at 3:47 p.m. with a subject line that read **Fwd: Fwd: Fwd: Unusual Neonatal Vocalization — Linguistic Assessment Requested** and an attachment labeled **LUH_NeonatalWard_Rec7.wav**. The forwarding chain traced back through a postdoctoral researcher at Edinburgh she vaguely knew, a phonetician in Copenhagen she didn't, and an address at Landspítali National University Hospital in Reykjavik. The body of the email had been stripped to a single line at each forwarding: *"Can you take a look?"* then *"This is either a hoax or you need to hear it"* then, at the origin, a longer paragraph in Icelandic that her browser translated haltingly: *"Two-day-old female infant, no complications, no known abnormalities. Vocalizations began at approximately eighteen hours post-birth. Attending nurse reported structured, repetitive sound patterns inconsistent with typical neonatal crying or pre-linguistic babble. Recorded for documentation. We don't know what this is."*

Maren opened the file the way she opened everything — clinically, with her headphones on and her spectrographic software already running. Professional courtesy. She dealt in questioned documents and disputed recordings, not infant babble, but someone along this chain had thought a forensic linguist should hear it, and she could give it ninety seconds before returning to Harold Kessler's dead hand and his nephew's living greed.

The recording was forty-three seconds long.

The first thing she noticed was the absence of crying. Neonatal vocalizations at two days are vegetative — reflexive cries, coughs, the uncoordinated protests of a body discovering that the world is cold and loud and full of light. This was not that. The sound was small — impossibly small, the volume of a voice housed in lungs the size of fists — but it was *organized*. Phoneme clusters. Aspirated stops followed by vowel sequences, then what sounded, absurdly, like agglutinated suffixes. A pause. A repetition. Not identical but *varied*, the way a sentence is varied when its verb form changes to accommodate a shift in aspect.

She played it again.

The spectrogram on her screen drew itself in ridges of color — amber, green, deep vermillion — and the patterns were wrong. They were wrong the way a photograph of a skyline is wrong when you realize the shadows are falling in two directions. Neonatal vocal tracts cannot produce velar fricatives. They cannot manage glottal stops with that precision. The formant structure she was looking at required a laryngeal control that a two-day-old did not possess, should not possess, in the same way that a two-day-old should not possess the ability to sign a lease or drive a car. The physiology wasn't there. And yet the spectrogram insisted, calmly, in its colored ridges, that the physiology was precisely there.

She played it a third time with her hand over her mouth.

It was the verb form that broke her. Not the phonemic inventory, though that was remarkable — four distinct vowel qualities, a set of consonantal contrasts that mapped with unreasonable precision onto a system she had studied for her doctoral qualifying exams. Not the agglutination, though the suffixal chains were structurally coherent, each morpheme occupying the slot that the grammar demanded. It was the verb form. A conjugation in the *hamṭu* aspect — perfective, completed action — attached to a root she recognized. *ĝen*, to go. But the prefix chain included a dimensional marker she had seen only in the oldest attested tablets, the ones pulled from the deepest strata at Nippur, and even there it was contested, its function debated across forty years of Assyriology. She had written a seminar paper on it once. She remembered the argument she'd made: that the marker encoded something more complex than direction, that it implied a relationship between speaker and addressee that existed *across* temporal distance. Her advisor had written in the margin: *Interesting but unfalsifiable. You have no living informant.*

The infant in Reykjavik was using the marker correctly.

Not approximately. Not in a way that suggested statistical coincidence or auditory pareidolia or any of the other comfortable words she was reaching for. The infant was using it the way a native speaker would — if native speakers of Sumerian had existed at any point in the last two thousand years, which they had not, because Sumerian had died slowly and then completely, eaten by Akkadian and then by time, surviving only in the cuneiform pressed into clay like the footprints of something that had walked away and never come back.

Maren sat at her desk without moving. The spectrogram glowed on her screen. Outside her window the March sky was the color of wet paper, and a student was crossing the quad with a backpack and a phone, and the steam plant was pushing its white breath into the air, and everything was ordinary except for the fact that her field of vision had narrowed to a small, bright point and the sounds of the building — the ventilation, the hallway conversation two doors down, a photocopier chewing through paper — had receded as though someone had sealed her office in glass.

She did not have a word for what she was feeling. She considered the possibilities with the taxonomic instinct that was her deepest reflex: not wonder, not yet; not fear, though something in her hands had changed, a tremor she assigned to low blood sugar before acknowledging that she had just eaten. Professional excitement was closest but insufficient, like calling a hemorrhage "a circulation event." The recording sat in her headphones, finished, the spectrogram frozen on the screen in its ridges and valleys, and Maren realized she was waiting. For what, she could not have said. She pressed play again.

This time she listened with her eyes closed. The tiny voice filled her inner ear — so small, so impossibly precise, a mouth that had never tasted solid food articulating morphemes that had not been spoken aloud in two millennia — and she felt something shift in the architecture of her understanding, some load-bearing wall developing a crack that she could not see but could feel in the way the floor tilted. She was a woman in a beige office in a second-rate university in a world that was still, for the moment, the world she knew, listening to a dead language speak itself alive in the throat of a child, and she did not know — she did not have the grammar, the syntax, the morphology — for what this was doing to her.

The email from Edinburgh had included a note she'd skimmed past: *Five other recordings. Different hospitals. I'm told there are more.*

She read it again.

By Friday she had the other files. São Paulo. Osaka. Nairobi. Christchurch. Tromsø. She played them sequentially, then simultaneously, then in spectrographic overlay, and what she saw was what she had already known she would see and what she had been quietly, desperately hoping she would not. Six infants. Six vocal tracts, each distinct in its resonance and timbre, each impossibly capable. Six recordings of grammatically coherent Sumerian — and not merely coherent, not merely the same language, but the same *sequences*. Identical strings of morphemes, identically inflected, identically stressed, differing only in the acoustic signature of the individual child. As though six newborns on six continents had been given the same text to read.

Not given. That was the wrong word. There was no one to give it.

Maren sat alone in her office at eleven o'clock on a Friday night with the recordings playing on loop, the Sumerian filling the room like something exhumed — not a ghost, that was too easy, ghosts were the past insisting, and this did not feel like the past — but something more unsettling, something that carried the weight of patience, of a thing that had not died at all but only gone quiet, the way a frequency goes quiet when there is no receiver tuned to it, and had been waiting, all this time, inside the signal, inside the static, inside the silence of every infant's first breath, for someone to listen.

She did not sleep that night. She sat at her desk and she listened, and she could not say what she heard.

## II.

The name reached her the way disreputable names always reach respectable people — laterally, through a postdoc who knew a journalist who knew a bioinformaticist who had been fired from the same department as "the DNA language guy." Dr. Renn Okoro. Maren found his paper in sixteen seconds: it had been published in a mid-tier computational biology journal three years ago, retracted eight months later under pressure from peer reviewers who called it "irresponsible speculation grafted onto coincidental pattern-matching," and was now available only through his personal website, which had the desperate, overdesigned look of a man shouting into a void. The paper's title was "Structured Phonemically Consistent Sequences in Non-Coding Regions of the Human Genome: Evidence for an Embedded Linguistic System of Unknown Origin." She read it in her car in a parking structure because she did not want it in her browser history at work.

It was either the work of a brilliant mind or a broken one, and the distance between those two things was narrower than her profession liked to admit. Okoro had mapped nucleotide sequences in so-called "junk DNA" — the vast non-coding stretches that make up more than ninety-eight percent of the genome — to phonemic values using a conversion matrix he'd derived from, of all things, Zipf's law distributions in known linguistic corpora. The result, he claimed, was a phonemic inventory of twenty-three distinct sounds, a morphological system of agglutinative structure, and syntactic patterns consistent with a natural language — but a language that matched nothing in any linguistic database on earth. His reviewers had pointed out, with varying degrees of cruelty, that you could map anything to anything if your conversion matrix was flexible enough, that Zipf's law appeared in everything from city populations to the frequency of words in *Moby-Dick* and was therefore not diagnostic, that the human urge to find language in noise was so powerful it had a clinical name.

Maren read the paper twice. Then she opened her spectrographic overlays of the infant recordings and set them beside Okoro's published phonemic inventory.

Twenty-three sounds. Four vowels, nineteen consonants, including a set of velar and pharyngeal contrasts that Okoro had flagged as "typologically unusual but internally consistent."

The Sumerian phonemic inventory, as reconstructed by Assyriologists from cuneiform orthography and comparative Semitic data, included four distinct vowel qualities and a consonantal system featuring velar and pharyngeal contrasts that had been the subject of debate for half a century.

The overlap was not suggestive. It was not interesting. It was exact.

She called the number on his website from the parking structure with her engine running. He answered on the second ring. His voice was careful, guarded, the voice of a man who had learned that phone calls from strangers were more often traps than gifts. She identified herself by name and title and specialty, and there was a silence — long enough that she checked to see if the call had dropped — and then Renn Okoro said, "You've heard the recordings."

Not a question.

"Yes."

"And you've read my paper."

"Yes."

Another silence. She could hear him breathing. Then: "Don't call me from a university phone. Don't email me from a university address. And don't meet me anywhere near your campus. They're already watching the phenomenon, and anyone who connects it to my work is going to get very popular very fast with people you do not want to be popular with."

She almost hung up. The paranoia was a red flag, the kind of thing that separated rigorous outsiders from the genuinely unwell, and she had spent enough time in courtrooms to know how contaminating it was to be associated with a witness who sounded like this. But she did not hang up. She sat in her car in the concrete light of the parking structure and she thought about six babies on six continents speaking a language that had been dead for two thousand years, and she thought about the phonemic inventory on her screen matching the phonemic inventory of a disgraced geneticist's retracted paper with a precision that was either impossible or *exactly* as possible as everything else that had happened in the last five days, and she said, "Where?"

They met in the restaurant of an airport Marriott because Renn was already in transit — he had been in transit, he told her, for the better part of three weeks, moving between cities where new cases had been reported, gathering data from neonatologists who were willing to share recordings before their hospitals' legal departments shut them down. He was tall, narrower than his author photo, with the underfed, over-caffeinated look of a man running on something other than health. His shirt was wrinkled. His laptop bag bore the fraying insignia of the Max Planck Institute, where he had held a position until he hadn't. He sat across from her in a booth that smelled of reheated marinara and airport carpet, and he set his laptop between them like a barricade.

"How many cases?" Maren asked.

"As of this morning, forty-seven confirmed. Twenty-three more probable. That's just the ones I know about. The number's been doubling every eight days."

"And they're all—"

"Identical sequences. Identical morphology. I've had four independent Assyriologists verify it — anonymously, off-record — and they all say the same thing. It's coherent, it's meaningful, and it shouldn't exist."

"Then let's start with shouldn't."

He looked at her. His eyes were large, dark, and — she noticed this despite herself, despite the professional distance she was maintaining like a held breath — exhausted in a way that went beyond sleep. It was the exhaustion of vindication. He had been right. Being right had cost him everything, and now being right was going to cost more, and he knew it, and he was here anyway, in an airport Marriott, eating nothing, drinking bad coffee, because the alternative was to know what he knew and say nothing, and he had already proven he could not do that.

"The sequences in the genome," he said. "The ones in my paper. They're not random. They were not produced by mutation, drift, or any known evolutionary mechanism. The base-pair patterns are too precise, too uniform across populations. Whatever put them there did so *deliberately*, and did so ubiquitously — every population, every ethnic group, every lineage I've been able to sample. It's in the San Bushmen and the Finnish and the Sentinelese. It's in Denisovan-introgressed segments and non-Denisovan segments alike. It is, as far as I can determine, in *every human being alive.*"

Maren said, "That's a very large claim from a very retracted paper."

She heard herself say it and recognized the tone. It was the tone she used in cross-examination, the tone she used when a phonetician on the opposing side was overreaching, the tone she used to cut the legs from under someone else's certainty so she wouldn't have to examine her own.
