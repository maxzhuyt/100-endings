# The Frequency

## I.

The hum began on a Tuesday, or perhaps it had always been there and Tuesday was simply when Ruth's body decided to notice. It sat in the low register, beneath the refrigerator's compressor cycle, beneath the furnace clicking on at six and off at six-fourteen, beneath the particular silence of a house occupied by one person who has stopped expecting another. She felt it first in her back teeth — the left side, where Dr. Petersen had placed the amalgam fillings in 1996 and 2003 — a vibration like a tuning fork pressed to porcelain.

She checked the furnace. She unplugged the refrigerator and stood in the kitchen listening to the milk warm. She unscrewed the faceplate from the electrical outlet behind the toaster because she had once read about arcing wires and the sounds they make before they burn a house down. Nothing. She plugged the refrigerator back in. She bought earplugs from the CVS on Delancey, the foam kind that expand in the canal, and she slept with them for three nights, and the hum was patient, the hum was there when she woke, and by Friday she had made an appointment with Dr. Llewellyn, who looked in her ears with his otoscope and said the word tinnitus and printed out a sheet of paper with a web address for the American Tinnitus Association and a bullet-pointed list of coping strategies that included "white noise machines," "stress reduction," and "avoiding caffeine."

Ruth was seventy-one. She had worked in palliative care for twenty-three years, retiring at sixty-four, and in that time she had watched somewhere between four hundred and five hundred people die — she had never counted, which was perhaps a mercy she had extended to herself without knowing it. She did not romanticize death. She had cleaned the bodies of the dead, had changed the sheets beneath them while they were still warm, had held hands that gripped and hands that didn't and hands that were already the temperature of the room. She had managed morphine drips with a precision that was, in its own way, a form of love, though she would not have used that word. She would have said *competence.* She would have said *duty.* She had watched the pupils of the dying fix and dilate and she had noted the time and she had walked to the family in the hall and she had said the sentence — *I'm sorry, he's passed, I'm sorry, she's gone* — enough times that the words had worn smooth in her mouth like river stones, and she delivered them with a steadiness that families mistook for strength but was, she knew, simply the absence of surprise. Death did not surprise Ruth. Death was her colleague. They had worked the same shift for two decades.

So when she stood in her kitchen at 2:07 on a Saturday morning, holding a glass of water she didn't remember pouring, and the hum sharpened into something that was unmistakably a human voice — not her voice, not the television she hadn't turned on, not a neighbor's radio bleeding through the plaster — she did not scream, or pray, or call anyone. She stood very still. She held the glass. She listened.

The voice belonged to Dolores Gutiérrez.

Ruth knew this the way she knew the smell of her own house: not through analysis but through an animal recognition that preceded thought. Dolores Gutiérrez, sixty-seven, pancreatic, admitted March 2013, deceased March 29 at 4:16 a.m., Ruth present, Ruth holding the hand, Ruth noting the time. Dolores, who had been born in Puebla and come to this country at nineteen and worked in the laundry at St. Augustine's Hospital for thirty-one years and raised two daughters and buried a son and kept a garden of roses so precise and ordered that the neighborhood association had given her an award that she displayed on the mantelpiece beside a photograph of her dead boy at his first communion.

Dolores was not speaking to Ruth.

Dolores was not speaking to anyone.

What Ruth heard was not speech in any sense she understood. It was thought — raw, ungrammatical, compressed — the cognitive debris of a mind in its final seconds, a mind that did not know it was in its final seconds or perhaps knew and could not organize itself around the knowing. Dolores was thinking about the color of her daughter's hair. Not the older daughter, Rosa — Ruth knew this without being told — but the younger one, Marisol, whose hair had gone gray at the temples before she was forty, and Dolores was thinking *it's still black underneath, still black at the roots,* and she was thinking about the back door, *did I lock the back door, the back door sticks in the humidity you have to push the bolt,* and she was thinking about a yellow dress she had worn to a dance when she was sixteen that she had never told anyone about because the dance was where she had kissed a boy who was not the boy she married, and the thought had no shame in it, only a kind of bewildered tenderness, as if Dolores were holding the memory up to a light and seeing through it for the first time, and then —

Silence.

The hum withdrew to its low register. The refrigerator cycled on. The clock on the microwave said 2:09.

Ruth's hand opened. The glass fell. It did not shatter — it was thick-bottomed, a juice glass, the kind built to survive — but it struck the linoleum and cracked cleanly in two, and the water spread in a shape that looked, briefly, like a hand, and then like nothing, and then it was only water on a floor, and Ruth stood in the kitchen in her bare feet and her nightgown and she did not move for a long time.

She had been there. She had been *right there,* holding Dolores's hand at 4:16 in the morning, and what she had heard was the breathing change — the apneic pauses lengthening, the jaw going slack, the sound that families always describe as a rattle but which Ruth had always thought was more like a drawer that won't quite close — and then nothing. She had heard the body stop. She had noted the time. She had walked to the hall where Marisol was sleeping in a chair and touched her shoulder and said the sentence.

She had not heard the yellow dress. She had not heard the back door. She had not heard the black roots of her daughter's hair.

Ruth picked up the two halves of the glass and placed them on the counter and wiped up the water with the dish towel that hung from the oven handle and she sat down at the kitchen table and she pressed her palms flat against the wood and she waited.

Nothing came.

She waited until daylight grayed the windows and then she made coffee. Her hands were completely steady.

## II.

The next voice came the following evening, while Ruth was brushing her teeth. Arthur Devlin, eighty-four, congestive heart failure, 2016. He was thinking about a canoe. Specifically, the aluminum canoe he had left on the shore of Lake Wissota in 1972, upside down in the sand, and whether someone had found it and whether it had rusted from the inside out. He was thinking about the sound of aluminum on rock. He was thinking *I should have turned it over.* Then he was gone.

Two hours later, another: Margaret Soo-Hyun Linden, fifty-nine, glioblastoma, 2011, thinking in rapid alternation about the Krebs cycle — she had been a biology teacher — and about whether her husband would remember to descale the coffee maker, and about a sentence from a book she had read in college that she could almost but not quite recall, something about how time was not a line but a —

Gone.

By Thursday, Ruth had heard eleven voices. By the following Tuesday, the count was twenty-seven. They came at no predictable hour. They layered. They overlapped. She would be standing at the kitchen sink and Harold Combe's guilty muttering would dissolve into the teenage thinking of Jesse Quintero, who had died at nineteen of Ewing sarcoma and whose final thoughts were in Spanish and English simultaneously, the languages braided together so tightly that Ruth, who spoke no Spanish, could nonetheless feel the shift in texture when Jesse crossed from one to the other, like a hand moving from silk to linen. She would be trying to sleep and the retired teacher, Constance Belleweather, would arrive — meticulous, determined, still composing, word by word, the letter she had been working on when the morphine took her under. The letter was to Constance's sister. The letter was an apology. Ruth heard the same clause revised, abandoned, begun again, revised — *I should have told you that what I did was not about your husband, it was about — no. I should have told you that when I — no. I want you to know that the night of your — no* — and then nothing, mid-sentence, the mind extinguished between one word and the next.

Ruth stopped sleeping.

Not all at once, but in the gradual way that a faucet narrows when the pipes corrode: the intervals of silence grew shorter, the periods of reception longer, until by the second week she was getting perhaps ninety minutes of broken sleep between 4 and 6 a.m., when the frequency seemed to quiet for reasons she could not determine. She moved through her house in a state of continuous partial attention, one ear always tuned to the dead.

On the fourteenth day, she bought notebooks. Legal pads, college-ruled, three-packs from the office supply store. She bought a box of ballpoint pens. She sat at the kitchen table and she began to write.

She wrote down what she heard.

She wrote: *Dolores G. — 3/29/2013 — yellow dress, back door, Marisol's hair.* She wrote: *Arthur D. — 9/14/2016 — canoe, Lake Wissota, aluminum, regret re: not turning it over.* She wrote: *Jesse Q. — 11/2/2009 — bilingual, thinking about his mother's hands, the way she cracked eggs.* She wrote: *Constance B. — 6/8/2015 — the letter, unfinished, to her sister, the affair.*

She was clinical about it. She dated each entry. She noted the time of reception, the approximate duration, the emotional register — a term she borrowed from music, though she was not musical. She cross-referenced where she could: patient name, date of death, diagnosis, ward, attending physician, whether family had been present. She pulled out the boxes of old records she had kept in the garage — not official records, which belonged to the hospice, but her own notes, the small spiral-bound notebooks she had carried in her scrub pockets for twenty-three years, in which she had jotted times, dosages, observations. *Patient agitated at 3 a.m. Increased morphine by 0.5 mg/hr. Family called, daughter en route. Patient calm at 4. Patient expired at 4:16.*

She compared her old notes to the new ones.

They did not match.

Her old notes said: *Patient calm at 4.* Her new notes said: *Patient thinking at lightspeed about the door, the dress, the hair, the boy she kissed, the roots.* Her old notes said: *Patient resting comfortably.* Her new notes said: *Patient convinced the ceiling is his mother's kitchen and the water stain is the face of God and the IV tube is a snake and also a telephone cord and also the hand of someone he wronged in 1981 and he is trying to apologize to a water stain.*

She filled three legal pads. She filled seven. She tore the pages out and pinned them to the walls of the spare bedroom — the room that had been, briefly, a sewing room, and before that, longer ago, the room she had prepared for the child she did not have. She organized them by year. Then reorganized by ward. Then again by patient last name. She connected them with yarn — red for recurring themes, blue for names that appeared in more than one account, green for requests, which she defined as any thought that contained the structure of wanting. There was a great deal of green.

The room began to look like something between a detective's office and a chapel.

Ruth sat on the floor of it some nights, surrounded by the paper, listening. She heard a man named Edgar Wolfe, who had been a plumber, thinking about the threading on a pipe he had installed in 1988 and whether he had used the correct sealant. She heard a woman named Bettina Charles, who had been a pianist, hearing in her final moments not the Chopin nocturne she had played at her last recital but the sound of her granddaughter banging a pot with a wooden spoon, and Bettina was thinking *that's the tempo, that's correct, that's the right tempo*. She heard a man who had been a high school football coach thinking about a play he had called in 1975 that had lost a game and he was not thinking about the loss, he was thinking about the look on the quarterback's face — *Billy Pfeiffer, he was just a kid, I should have called the screen, I knew to call the screen, why didn't I call the screen* — and Ruth wrote it down, she wrote all of it down, and the wall grew denser and the yarn multiplied and still the voices came.

She had held these people's hands. She had adjusted their pillows. She had moistened their lips with small green sponges on sticks and she had told their families *she's comfortable,* *he's not in any pain,* *it's peaceful now.* And it was true about the bodies. The bodies were comfortable. The morphine saw to that. She was not a liar and she had never been a liar.

But she had described the surface of a lake to people who wanted to know what was at the bottom.

*He's resting,* she had said, standing beside a man whose mind was a hurricane of regret and sealant and threading and 1988. *She's at peace,* she had said, standing beside a woman who was composing and recomposing an apology she could not finish, could not get right, who died between *when I* and the next word.

Ruth filled fifteen legal pads in four weeks. She lost eleven pounds. Her hands developed a tremor that she addressed by switching from ballpoints to felt-tips, which required less pressure. She spoke to no one. She answered no phone calls. When her neighbor, Glenn, knocked to ask if she wanted tomatoes from his garden, she opened the door just wide enough to accept the bag and said, "Thank you, Glenn, I'm fine," and closed the door while he was still talking.

She was not fine. She was a receiver. The dead were pouring through her, and what they carried bore almost no resemblance to what she had told the living they were carrying, and this was not a fact she could set down.

## III.

The name appeared first in Harold Combe.

Ruth had marked Harold's entry early — he was one of the first voices she'd transcribed, the retired postal worker with the canoe at Lake Wissota. But on re-listening — and they did recur, the voices, cycling back at irregular intervals like radio stations drifting in and out of range — she caught something she had missed the first time, buried beneath the canoe and the aluminum and the regret. A name. A man. Harold was thinking about this man with a density of feeling that Ruth could not immediately categorize. It was not love. It was not hatred. It was the texture of a promise — something agreed to, something sealed — and it sat in Harold's dying mind like a stone in a shoe, a thing he could not stop stepping on even as the canoe and the lake pulled him toward whatever peace he was trying to assemble.

Ruth wrote the name down. She did not think much of it.

Three days later she was lying on the couch at 11 p.m., notebook open on her chest, when Yun-Seo Park arrived. Yun-Seo had died in 2013, ovarian cancer, age forty-nine. Ruth remembered her as quietly stoic, a woman who kept her curtain closed and her requests minimal. What Ruth heard now was not stoicism. It was a vast internal architecture of worry — her son's student loans, her mother's dementia, the specific brand of sesame oil she'd hidden in the back of the pantry because her husband bought the wrong kind and she didn't want to hurt his feelings — and then, surfacing through the worry like a shape through murky water, the same name. The same knot. Yun-Seo was thinking about a conversation she'd had with this man, and whatever the conversation had contained, it had laid something on her that she was still carrying as the anesthesia of her organs shutting down rearranged everything else into nonsense, because the name stayed. The name stayed sharp. The name was the last clear thing.

Ruth sat up.

She went to the spare bedroom. She found Harold's page. She found the name.
