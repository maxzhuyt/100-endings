# The Forger's Last Hand

---

## I.

The grocery list was in Eleanor Fitch's handwriting — tight, efficient, slanting eleven degrees to the left the way only a natural right-hander trained by nuns could manage. Eleanor had been dead for six years. I wrote her grocery lists on Tuesdays because Eleanor had always shopped on Tuesdays, and because the hand remembers what it loves. Milk, 2%. Pears, not too ripe. One tin of those Danish butter cookies she kept sewing supplies in but never sewing supplies. I could feel Eleanor's impatience in the downstroke of the *P*, the way she bore into the paper as though the act of writing a list was already taking time away from doing the things on it. I set down the pen.

I use Pilot Precise V5s for Eleanor. She was not a fountain pen woman. She would have found fountain pens theatrical, and she would have been right.

I held the list at arm's length. The slant was correct. The pressure was correct. The idiosyncratic way Eleanor formed her lowercase *k* — like a man tipping his hat — was correct. If you had placed this list beside one Eleanor wrote in 1987, a questioned-documents examiner with thirty years of experience would need magnification and at least an hour to see daylight between them. I know this because I am that examiner, or I was, before I retired, before the diagnosis, and I have studied more handwriting samples than anyone currently breathing in this city.

I put the list in my coat pocket and picked up my keys.

I was standing at the door when I noticed I was already wearing my coat. I had no memory of putting it on. The coat was buttoned to the collar, which meant I had been standing in the hallway for some time, because I always button from the bottom up and the collar is last, and this sequence takes me roughly twelve seconds, and twelve seconds is long enough that a healthy mind remembers doing it.

On the counter, beside my keys — which were also in my hand, so these could not also be my keys, except they were, a second set I did not remember placing there — was a yellow Post-it note in my own handwriting.

*You already ate. Don't buy more pears.*

I looked at the note for a long while. The handwriting was undeniably mine: the open-topped *a*, the slight rightward drift I've never been able to suppress, the ballpoint pressure of someone who learned to write before felt-tips softened everybody's hand. It was my writing to myself. A message from ten minutes ago, or an hour. A woman I had been leaving instructions for a woman I was becoming.

I put Eleanor's grocery list in the recycling bin and unbuttoned my coat. The unbuttoning took twelve seconds. I counted.

My hands were steady. I held them up in the light from the kitchen window and watched them for tremor. Nothing. The metacarpophalangeal joints were swollen — I am seventy-one — but they flexed cleanly, and when I picked up a pen and wrote my name in the air, the ghost signature was fluid, the muscle memory firing in the sequence I'd trained it to fire since I was nineteen and first understood that handwriting is not communication but *identity performed at the tip of a pen*.

The hands were fine. The hands were, if anything, better than they had been five years ago, as though the rest of me was withdrawing its resources from the outlying districts and concentrating everything in the fingers, the wrist, the forearm — the infrastructure of the craft. A besieged city pulling its troops back to defend the castle.

The castle was fine.

It was the city that was burning.

---

## II.

Here is what I have gathered. I will list it plainly, the way I would prepare a case file, because this is a case, my last, and the preparation must be impeccable.

**Paper:** Eleven sheets of Eaton's Corrasable Bond, 20 lb., purchased from a stationery dealer in Vermont who specializes in dead stock. The paper was manufactured between 1974 and 1982. It has the correct watermark, the correct rag content, the correct optical brightener profile — which is to say, almost none, because they hadn't yet started bleaching the life out of paper in the seventies, and when you hold a sheet of this up to UV light it stays dark and dignified, like a room lit by candles. Thomas used Eaton's. I know because I have four samples of his writing, all on Eaton's, all from the period between 1975 and 1981, which is the period in which his letters will have been written.

**Ink:** Parker Quink, blue-black. Thomas used a Parker 51 fountain pen — I have a photograph of him holding it, taken at a party in 1979, the pen clipped to his breast pocket like a medal. I acquired three bottles of Quink manufactured in the late seventies from an estate sale in Connecticut. The iron gall content in the vintage formula differs from the current formulation, and a gas chromatography test would show age-consistent oxidation patterns. I have tested this.

**Handwriting samples:** Four documents in Thomas's confirmed hand. A postcard to his mother, 1975. A signed lease, 1977. A note left on a refrigerator door, photographed by a friend, 1979. And a letter — the only letter — he ever wrote to me, in 1980, three lines long, which said he was leaving and which I have kept in a locked drawer for forty-one years, not because I still love him, or because I still hate him, but because it is the most comprehensive sample I have of his natural hand under emotional stress, and there is nothing more revealing than a man's penmanship when he is lying.

**Target:** Six letters, spanning 1976 to 1981. They will show a trajectory. The first letter, written shortly after Elena's birth, will be tentative, clumsy, a young man overwhelmed by fatherhood and terrified of his own inadequacy. He will write about holding the baby for the first time. He will describe her fingers. The subsequent letters will chart his attempts to stay connected after the separation — questions about her first words, her first day of school, her favorite color. He will get some details wrong, because a father writing from a distance would get details wrong, and perfection is the enemy of plausibility. The final letter, dated three months before the car accident, will be the one in which he says he is coming back. He will say he has made arrangements. He will say he wants to know his daughter. He will say: *I have wasted so much time already.*

This letter will be a lie inside a lie.

Thomas never wrote to Elena. Thomas never asked about Elena. Thomas went to Portland and started a contracting business and married a woman named Diane and had two sons he raised with evident competence and died on Route 26 in a chain-reaction collision during a February ice storm, and at no point in the intervening years did he attempt to contact the daughter he had left in a crib in a Baltimore apartment with a woman he had known for eleven months.

Elena knows this. She has known it since she was old enough to notice that other children had fathers and she had a silence where a father should be. She asked about him when she was eight. I told her the truth, or most of it. He left. He didn't come back. I told her it was not about her, which was also the truth, though the useless kind. She nodded and went back to her room, and something in her bearing changed that afternoon, a slight tensing across the shoulders that I have watched settle into permanence over thirty-three years.

She is a forensic document examiner. She works for the FBI's Questioned Documents Unit in Quantico. She is one of the best in the country, and she learned her craft at my kitchen table, copying letters when she was six, analyzing pressure patterns by the time she was twelve, reading human character in the variations of a signature the way other people read palms. I taught her everything I know.

This is the part I must be careful about.

The letters will be discovered among my belongings after my death. I have prepared a box — an old shoebox, the kind a person would use to store things they valued but could not bear to organize. Inside the box I will place the six letters alongside genuine documents: my birth certificate, my mother's recipe for pfeffernüsse, a theater program from 1973. The letters will be interspersed naturally. They will look like something I kept but never showed her. They will suggest that Thomas wrote, that I received the letters, and that I — for reasons I will leave to Elena's interpretation — never passed them on.

This means Elena will believe two things. That her father loved her. And that her mother withheld the evidence.

She will grieve him differently. She will be angry at me, posthumously, and her anger will be the kind that has nowhere to go once the person you're angry at is dead, which means it will fade, and what will be left underneath it is the warmth of knowing she was wanted. I will trade her good opinion of me for her good opinion of herself. This seems to me like a fair exchange.

I have perhaps ten months. Dr. Luehrsen does not say this in so many words — he speaks in gradients, in likelihoods, in language designed to preserve hope the way formaldehyde preserves tissue, by killing the living thing and keeping the shape — but I have read the literature, and I understand the trajectory. The fine motor control will hold longest. It is the judgment that will go first: the ability to step back from a letter and see it as a stranger would, to catch the anachronism, the tell, the faintly wrong thing. Even now there are moments when I look at a word I've just written and cannot say with certainty whether it is Thomas's hand or my own.

This morning I finished the second letter. I read it through twice, checked the pressure with a loupe, tested the ink's spread pattern against the 1977 lease sample.

It was good. It was very good.

But I am no longer sure I am the right person to judge.

## III.

Elena came on a Sunday, which was unusual. She normally visits on Saturdays, when the traffic on 95 is marginally less awful and she can make it back to Quantico by evening. The Sunday visit meant something. Either concern or surveillance. With Elena these have always been difficult to distinguish.

"I thought I'd help you sort through some of the office," she said, standing in my doorway with a canvas bag of cleaning supplies and a smile I could not read. "You mentioned last week the filing cabinets were getting out of hand."

I had not mentioned the filing cabinets. I was nearly certain I had not. But near certainty is a country I no longer fully inhabit, so I stepped aside and let her in.

She moved through the apartment with the brisk efficiency of a woman who has decided something. I watched her from the kitchen, where I was making coffee I knew she would decline — Elena drinks only tea now, has for years, though I sometimes forget and make the coffee and then remember when I see her face — and I noticed the way she looked at surfaces. Not at the surfaces themselves but at what was on them, the way a crime scene technician scans a room: systematically, left to right, top to bottom, missing nothing.

She was in the office for eleven minutes before she found it.

I had been careless. I know this now. The third letter — Thomas to Elena, dated April 1978, about her learning to walk — should have been in the locked drawer with the materials. It was not in the locked drawer. It was in the top tray of the filing cabinet, underneath a gas bill from November, where I had apparently placed it and forgotten, or placed it deliberately for a reason I could no longer reconstruct, or never placed it at all and was dealing with a gap in the sequence of events that my mind had papered over with plausible confabulation, which is the thing the brain does when it can no longer record: it *invents* the tape.

I heard the quality of the silence change.

There is a particular silence that descends when a person picks up a document that means something. I have heard it a thousand times in my career — in courtrooms, in police evidence rooms, in the moments before a signature is declared authentic or fraudulent. It is the silence of a mind that has stopped performing attention and has begun *paying* it: the real thing, the currency of the self, spent without reserve.

I walked to the office doorway.

Elena was standing by the filing cabinet, holding the letter. She held it correctly — by the edges, two fingers and a thumb, the way I taught her to handle questioned documents when she was thirteen. The letter was angled toward the window. She was not reading the words. She was reading the *hand*.

I watched her eyes.

They moved in the pattern I recognized: the global assessment first (slant, size, baseline alignment), then the local features (letter formations, connecting strokes, pen lifts). Her index finger hovered over a line but did not touch. She tilted the paper fifteen degrees, catching the raking light, which would reveal the ink's surface topography — the ridges and valleys that a ballpoint leaves but a fountain pen does not. Quink from a Parker 51 lays down a particular line, slightly wet, with capillary feathering at the terminals.

She brought the paper closer to her face. She was smelling it.

Old paper has a scent — vanillin, released by the slow decomposition of lignin. Artificially aged paper has a different scent: sharper, with chemical undertones. The Eaton's Bond was genuinely old. It would smell correct. But the ink was applied recently, and fresh Quink on old paper produces a faint interaction I could not fully suppress — a whisper of solvent that a trained nose might catch.

Elena lowered the letter. She looked at me.

"Mom," she said. "What is this?"

"What does it look like?"

"It looks like a letter from my father."

"Then that's what it is."

She looked at me for a long time. Her face did what Elena's face does when she is working: it went still, almost blank, all expression retreating behind the professional mask. I used to be able to see past that mask. Now, standing in the doorway with the light behind me and my daughter holding a dead man's words in a living woman's hand, I could not tell whether her stillness was shock or suspicion.

"I didn't know he wrote to you," she said.

"There are things I haven't told you, Elena."

This, at least, was true.

She looked at the letter again. I saw her thumb move — the faintest pressure against the edge of the paper, a tactile test for stock weight and grain. She would not learn much from this. The paper was genuine period stock. But the gesture told me she was already working, that the professional machinery was turning, that my daughter was holding something I had made and doing what I had trained her to do.

She folded the letter and put it in her coat pocket.

"I'd like to keep this," she said.

"Of course."

She did not ask if there were more. This omission was either tact or strategy. She left an hour later, the letter in her coat, her tea untouched, her face composed with a rigidity I recognized because I had built it into her, assembled it from demonstration and instruction over two decades of training a girl to see things as they were, not as she wished them to be.

After she left, I went to the filing cabinet and stood where she had stood. I looked at the drawer. I looked at the gas bill.

I went to the locked drawer in my bedroom and counted the materials. Five remaining letters. The handwriting samples. The ink. The paper.

Everything was in order.

The third letter was missing because my daughter had taken it. It was missing because I had left it where she could find it. It was missing because my discipline had lapsed, because the filing cabinet had felt, in some moment I could not retrieve, like a reasonable place to put a letter that should never have left the locked drawer.
