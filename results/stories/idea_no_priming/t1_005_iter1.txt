# The Court of Translucent Intent

## I. The Docketing of Fennick Grole

The bone lottery, it should be said at the outset, was not always conducted with bones. For a period of eleven years during the Second Adjudicatory Reform, the Court of Translucent Intent employed a system of lacquered wooden chits, each inscribed with a case number in ink derived from the pineal secretions of a specific breed of moth whose lifecycle had been engineered, at considerable public expense, to synchronize with the Court'sSessionTide calendar. The moths died out. The chits warped. A committee was formed, and the committee produced a report of two hundred and nine pages recommending a return to the ossuarial method, on the grounds that bones, unlike wood, did not absorb ambient psychic moisture, and that the public's faith in sortition was, in any case, strengthened by a process that appeared ancient and solemn rather than one that appeared to involve pulling damp sticks from a bucket. The committee's recommendation was adopted. The bones were sourced from the femurs of decommissioned court stenographers — a detail that is recorded in the procurement ledger and that no one, in the living memory of the current registrar's office, has ever questioned aloud, though it has been thought about, loudly and with some distress, by no fewer than four junior clerks during Sustained Civic Bond sessions, which is how we know.

It was from this lottery, on the morning of the eleventh of Ashmarch, that the number 1066-IV/ω was drawn.

The classificatory homunculus — a small, damp, diligent creature housed in a glass cabinet to the left of the Adjudicator's bench, whose sole function was the recitation of personnel credentials and whose sole pleasure, if it could be said to have pleasure, was the pronunciation of middle names — opened its translucent mouth and began.

"Fennick Ossuald Grole," it said, savoring the Ossuald. "Licensed Advocate, Third Tier, provisional. Academy of Procedural Telepathy, graduated forty-first of forty-three, notation: 'passed with reservations.' Caseload to date: Breem v. Breem, trespass-of-aura, residential; Cattergill v. The Municipal Oneiric Authority, boundary grievance, agricultural — " and here the homunculus paused with what might have been relish, though homunculi are not certified for relish — "involving a domestic fowl capable of projecting its dreams onto adjacent properties, settled out of session when the fowl died, see note. Supervisor evaluation from the Honorable Legate Durns: 'Enthusiastic but porous.' Disciplinary notation: one citation, minor, for involuntary lacrimation during a moot-court bonding exercise. Citation text: 'Mr. Grole was asked to perform a routine empathic cross-examination of a practice witness and instead absorbed the practice witness's childhood memory of a dog and wept for nine minutes. The practice witness was a construct. The dog was hypothetical. Mr. Grole was counseled.'"

The homunculus closed its mouth. It had enjoyed itself.

From the gallery — the Court's intake sessions were technically public, though in practice they were attended only by the registrar's assistants, a few insomniacs, and whatever low-level psychic parasites had drifted in through the poorly warded east doors — there was no particular reaction. The intake of Case 1066-IV/ω proceeded with the bureaucratic serenity of a river that does not know it is about to go over a falls, or does know, and considers falls to be someone else's department.

The formal charge was read by the Senior Registrar, a woman named Pellith Ohn, who had held the position for twenty-seven years and who read all formal charges in the same tone she used to read lunch menus and death certificates, which was the tone of a person who had long ago understood that language, in a court of law, was not a tool for communication but a tool for the *management* of communication, which is to say, for ensuring that what was communicated could, at a later date, be credibly denied to have meant what everyone present understood it to mean.

"The matter before the Court," read Pellith Ohn, "is brought by the Consolidated Guild of Oneiric Professionals, acting in its capacity as custodial guarantor of psychic propriety within the Public Oneiric Archive, and concerns one Maldren Voss, Archive Librarian, Second Classification, Incubus-Registered under Statute 7(d) of the Entities of Inherently Invasive Interiority Act, who stands accused on sixteen counts of Unlawful Psychic Intimacy, defined under Section 31(a)(iv) as the non-consensual ideation of a carnally saturant nature perpetrated upon the somnolent psyches of no fewer than thirty-one registered citizenry, said ideation being of a character, duration, and intensity sufficient to constitute, under the relevant statutory framework, a material and measurable corruption of the oneiric field, hereafter referred to as 'the Alleged Intrusion,' which is to say — "

She turned the page.

" — which is to say, that the Accused did enter the dreams of the named complainants and there perform, facilitate, induce, or otherwise occasion experiences of a nature that the complainants characterize as intimate, unsolicited, and psychically penetrative, this characterization being supported by sworn depositions taken under Second-Tier Telepathic Verification and notarized by the Office of Somnolent Affairs, copies of which have been filed with the Court in triplicate, see Appendix 7 through 22 inclusive."

What the charge meant, when one peeled back the statutory euphemism the way one peels back the dressing on a wound one has been told not to look at, was this: a quiet man who worked in a library was accused of climbing into people's dreams and doing things to them there. The word for what he was accused of doing — the plain, ugly, two-syllable word that lived beneath the phrase "carnally saturant ideation" like something dead beneath a carpet — was never spoken aloud in the Court of Translucent Intent. It did not need to be. The statute number was sufficient. Everyone knew what 31(a)(iv) meant, the way everyone knows what a locked ward means, or a closed coffin. The knowledge was carried in the number, and the number was clean, and the cleanness of the number was the point.

Fennick Grole was not in the room for any of this.

He was on the third floor, in the Advocates' cloakroom, trying to remove a stain from his only formal robe — he had spilled broth on it three days earlier, during a lunch he had eaten too quickly because he was re-reading the Academy's *Practitioner's Guide to Low-Stakes Telepathic Arbitration,* which was the only legal text he had ever found genuinely useful at trial and which contained, in its margins, his own annotations, written in the small, hopeful hand of a person who believed that preparation could substitute for talent — when the assignment notice arrived by pneumatic capsule and struck him in the sternum.

He opened it. He read it. He read it again. He looked up the statute number.

He sat down on the bench in the cloakroom, among the other robes, which smelled of camphor and other people's psychic residue, and he held the capsule in both hands, and he did not cry, because he had been cited for crying and was working on it, but his eyes became what a generous observer might describe as humid, and what his supervisor, Legate Durns, whose evaluation was now part of the permanent record, would describe as "porous."

Downstairs, the intake concluded. Pellith Ohn stamped the docket. The bone was returned to the drum. The homunculus settled back into its cabinet, contemplating, if it contemplated, the pleasant mouthfeel of *Ossuald.* Case 1066-IV/ω was now a living entry in the Court's SessionTide docket, assigned its chamber, its timeline, its Appointed Advocate. The system had processed the input and produced the output and all the necessary fields had been filled.

In the cell block beneath the courthouse, in a stone room lit by a single licensed luminescence that buzzed at a frequency calibrated to suppress psychic emanation, Maldren Voss sat on a bench not unlike the one Fennick sat on three floors above and several categorical worlds away, and waited for someone to come and explain to him what would happen next.

No one came, for a while. The system was, after all, still processing.

## II. "They Came to Me Because They Could Not Sleep"

The Preliminary Bond was mandated under Rule 14(c) of the Court's Advocacy Code, which stipulated that no Appointed Advocate could proceed to evidentiary session without first establishing a "baseline telepathic rapport with the accused sufficient to verify identity, assess cognitive capacity, and determine whether the accused's psychic field presents any occupational hazard to the Advocate that would necessitate reassignment or the provision of supplementary psychic protective equipment (see Form 77-B, 'Request for Cognitive Shielding, Standard or Reinforced')." The rule had been drafted by committee. The committee had been thorough. The committee had not, at any point in its deliberations, considered what it might feel like to open one's mind to another person in a stone cell while a registrar watched, because the committee was composed of administrators, and administrators do not feel things under Rule 14(c). They facilitate the feeling of things by others and note the results on Form 77-B.

The supervising registrar assigned to Fennick's Preliminary Bond was named Clottie Murg. She was seated on a stool by the cell door when Fennick arrived, already eating something from a waxed-paper sleeve — some kind of seed cake, dense and dark, which she consumed with the methodical, joyless efficiency of a person refueling. She did not look up when Fennick entered. She did not look up when he sat down across from the accused. She would not look up at any point during the session, though this would not prevent her from filing a complete and procedurally impeccable supervisory report, because Clottie Murg had supervised four hundred and eleven Preliminary Bonds in her career and could do it the way a farmer's wife can pluck a chicken — by feel, without interest, thinking about something else entirely.

Fennick sat. His formal robe still smelled faintly of broth. His hands were damp on the table, which was bolted to the floor, which was stone, which was warded, which was cold. Across from him sat Maldren Voss.

What Fennick had expected: the Academy's *Handbook on Entities of Inherently Invasive Interiority* devoted eleven pages to incubi, and the eleven pages were unanimous in their caution. An incubus's psychic field was described as "aggressively osmotic," "thermally seductive," and, in one passage that Fennick had underlined twice, "characterized by a superficial gentleness that masks a substrate of compulsive psychic predation, analogous to the bioluminescent lure of certain deep-sea organisms." The Handbook recommended full cognitive shielding, a pre-bond adrenaline tincture, and, in cases involving 31(a)(iv) charges, the presence of a Psychic Containment Officer. Fennick had requisitioned the shielding. It had not arrived. He had requisitioned the tincture. It was on back-order. He had requested a Containment Officer. The request had been returned with a note: "Containment Officers allocated exclusively to First-Tier Advocates per Budgetary Directive 9. Third-Tier Advocates (provisional) should refer to Chapter 12 of the *Practitioner's Guide*: 'Managing Psychic Overwhelm Through Breathing and Positive Self-Talk.'"

So Fennick sat across from an incubus in a stone cell with no shielding, no tincture, no Containment Officer, and a breathing technique he had practiced that morning while brushing his teeth and that had made him hyperventilate.

What Fennick had expected, in short, was an assault. A hot, dark, swarming thing — the psychic equivalent of a hand on his throat.

"Whenever you're ready," said Clottie Murg, not looking up.

Fennick opened the bond.

And there was quiet.

Not silence — silence is an absence, and this was not absent. It was full. It was the particular quality of quiet that exists in a room where someone has been reading for a long time: absorbed, self-contained, alive with the specific stillness of focused attention. Fennick had felt something like it once, as a child, falling asleep in a library while his mother worked — the hush that was not emptiness but *held breath,* the whole room listening to itself think.

Maldren Voss's mind was arranged like a library.

This was not a metaphor. Fennick understood this with the immediate, sensory certainty of someone who has stepped through a door and knows, before looking, that they are in a kitchen because they can smell bread. There were shelves. They were organized. There was a system — not the rigid, grid-like architecture of a trained legal mind, which Fennick had encountered during his Academy bond-practice sessions and which always felt to him like walking through a filing cabinet, but something older, warmer, more accretive: the organizational logic of a person who has spent decades learning what goes next to what and has come to understand the kinship between things not by category but by *resonance.* A memory of rainfall shelved next to a memory of applause, because they sounded alike. A patron's name filed next to the color of the light on the day they first came in, because that was how Maldren remembered them: not as a name but as a quality of afternoon.

Fennick moved through it. He was not supposed to linger — the Preliminary Bond was a verification procedure, not an exploration — but the architecture of Maldren's mind was so unexpectedly, so disarmingly *hospitable* that Fennick kept finding himself in the next room before he remembered he'd meant to stop. There was no resistance. There were no locked doors. There was, as far as Fennick could detect as he drifted deeper, no substrate of compulsive psychic predation whatsoever, and Fennick had been trained to detect it, and his training had included a vivid and scarring simulation involving the deep-sea organism metaphor rendered experientially, so he knew what it felt like, and this was not it. This was not even adjacent to it. This was — 

He found the memories.

They were not hidden. They were filed, carefully, under a heading that Fennick's psychic translation rendered as something like *Night Work* or *The Hours After Closing* — a soft-edged category, shelved in a part of the library that felt older and more worn, the way the most-used section of any library feels: the spines a little faded, the edges thumbed smooth.

A woman, middle-aged, still in her work clothes, sitting in the Archive reading room at a quarter past eleven. She had not come for a book. She had come because she could not sleep. She had not slept in nine days. Fennick could feel the texture of her insomnia — it was not the buzzing, caffeinated kind but the heavy, grey, waterlogged kind, the kind that feels like wearing a wet coat you cannot take off. She had come to the Archive because it was open late and because the librarian, she had heard, could help.

Maldren had helped.

Fennick experienced it at one remove — he was receiving Maldren's memory of the event, not the event itself — but even at one remove, the tenderness of it was almost intolerable. Maldren had opened the woman's dream the way one opens a window that has been painted shut: carefully, patiently, working around the edges where the old paint had sealed the frame to the sill. The dream underneath was not complicated. She was drowning. She had been drowning every night for nine days — the same grey water, the same grey sky, the same slow, heavy descent. Maldren had not removed the water. He had changed its quality. In his hands — in his mind — the grey water had become warm, and clear, and the drowning had become floating, and the sinking had become suspension, and the woman had slept. She had slept for eleven hours. She had woken and wept and come back the next night to thank him, and Maldren had filed the memory of her gratitude next to the memory of the floating, because they belonged together.

A child.
