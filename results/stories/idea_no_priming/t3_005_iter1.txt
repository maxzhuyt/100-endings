# The Throne of Residuum

## I.

The crown was a curtain rod bent into a circle.

Sable knew this because she had watched Fenwick bend it that morning, his knuckles white and his face doing something complicated that she had decided not to ask about. He had used pliers from the supply closet on the second landing, the one that smelled like fish even though no one had kept fish there in a long time, and the metal had fought him, and he had won, but only barely, and the result was not quite round. More like the shape a raindrop makes right before it lets go of a leaf. She had told him this and he had looked at her with that adult expression — the one that meant she had said something either very smart or very stupid and they were not going to tell her which.

Now she stood in the ground floor of the lighthouse, which was the throne room, which was also a room with chairs. There were fourteen chairs arranged in a half-circle facing the spot where she had been told to stand. Eleven of the chairs were empty. They had been set out with a precision that hurt to look at, each angled slightly toward the center, as if the people who were supposed to sit in them might still walk through the door any minute and would be disappointed by sloppy geometry. Name placards rested on three of the empty seats. Someone had crossed each name out with a single careful line — not scribbled over, not erased, just *marked*. Sable had read them all. Governor Harcastle. Admiral Lune. Secretary Pell. She had not known any of these people. She did not know what a secretary was for.

Of the three chairs that were occupied:

Fenwick sat in the closest one. He was the regent, which was a word Sable understood to mean "the person who was in charge before the person who is in charge now gets in charge." He had stopped blinking at the right intervals about a week ago. His eyes would stay open for thirty or forty seconds, long enough for them to go pink and wet at the edges, and then he would blink three times very fast, like a shutter trying to catch up. Sable had once tried to match his rhythm and had given herself a headache. He was tall, thin in the way that tall people get when they stop eating full meals, and his hands shook unless he was holding something. He held the curtain-rod crown now, and his hands were perfectly still.

Next to him sat Maren, who had been something military and was now something else that no one had explained. She wore a uniform that did not fit her anymore — it hung in places where it used to cling, and Sable thought privately that Maren was becoming a different shape than the person the uniform had been made for, like when you leave a candle near a window in summer. Maren's eyes worked fine. She blinked normally. But she never looked directly at anything she was talking about, as if her attention could only arrive at its target by sneaking up from the side.

The third was Old Partridge, who was not actually named Partridge and was not actually old in the way grandparents are old, but who had a quality of *doneness* about him, like bread that has stayed in the oven too long and has become something that is technically still bread but is mostly char and regret. He had been the Minister of Inland Development. Sable had asked him what that meant and he had said "I used to worry about roads" with a voice that suggested the roads had won.

These three people were her government.

Fenwick stood. His chair made a sound against the stone floor that was louder than it should have been because the room was so quiet that every sound was louder than it should have been. He held the crown in both hands and walked toward her, and his steps were the careful, considered steps of a person crossing ice, and the six paces between his chair and Sable's standing-spot took him long enough that she had time to notice a moth on the wall, watch it rearrange its wings, and decide it looked like a tiny paper airplane that had given up.

"Sable," Fenwick said.

"Yes."

"By the authority vested in me by—" He stopped. His jaw worked sideways. The words he needed were attached to institutions that did not exist anymore, chains of command that had been snapped link by link over the past year, and Sable could see him reaching for them the way you reach for a step in the dark and find only air. "By the authority," he said again, and let it hang there, unfinished, a bridge that went halfway over a river and then just stopped.

He lowered the crown onto her head.

It was too big. It slid down past her forehead and rested on her eyebrows, and for a moment the world was just a thin sliver of light beneath a ring of cold metal, and she thought *this is not how this works in stories,* and then she reached up with both hands and pushed it back, adjusting it with the firm, unsentimental competence of a person who has dealt with ill-fitting things before. She shoved a wad of her own hair — thick, dark, unwashed for three days because no one had reminded her — under the band above her left ear. This helped. The crown sat crooked but stable. She left it.

"Okay," she said.

Fenwick stepped back. He looked at Maren. Maren looked at a point slightly to the left of Old Partridge. Old Partridge looked at his hands.

"I have a decree," Sable said.

The word *decree* in her mouth sounded like a word she had learned recently and was trying at full volume for the first time, the way children try on swear words or scientific terms — with a hard, clean confidence that comes from not yet knowing the weight of the thing.

"A— yes. Of course, Your—" Fenwick stopped again. They had not discussed honorifics. There had been a system, once, for what you called the ruler of what was left of this country. But the system, like the country, had contracted into something that did not fit its old containers. "Of course."

"Bedtime," Sable said, "is nine o'clock."

The silence that followed was not the silence of people being polite. It was the silence of people whose machinery of response had encountered an input for which no pathway existed.

"For everyone," Sable added. "Not just me. Nine o'clock and you have to actually be lying down, not just in the room with the lights off looking at the ceiling, because that's not sleeping, that's just lying, and lying down is not the same as lying because one is with your body and one is with your mouth but they're both bad if you do them when you're supposed to be sleeping."

Old Partridge opened his mouth and then closed it again. It made a small sound, like a book being opened and shut.

"The dark hours are the bad hours," Sable said, and there was nothing in her tone that suggested she was making an argument. She was stating a fact the way she would state that water was wet or that you were not allowed to change the rules of a game after you were already losing. "So we should be sleeping during them. Not walking around and worrying. You all walk around and worry a lot at night and I can hear you through the floor, and your worrying is—" She paused. Not for effect. She was reaching for the word, and the word she wanted was not in her vocabulary, so she found its nearest neighbor. "Loud. Even when you're quiet, it's loud. So: nine o'clock. Lying down. And I'll handle the beam."

Maren said, very carefully, the way you say things when you are aware that the thing you are saying is insane and the fact that you are saying it is insaner: "Your Majesty, the strategic-planning sessions are conducted at midnight because—"

"Then do them at lunch."

"The intelligence reports—"

"Lunch."

"Our sentries—"

"They can be lying down *and* keeping watch. You can look at things while you're lying down. I do it all the time."

Sable said this with the unforced authority of a person who genuinely could not fathom the counterargument. She was not performing power. She was performing *sense*, which is a different thing, a thing that does not require volume or gravitas, only the unshakable conviction that the world should work a certain way and the mild surprise that anyone would suggest otherwise.

Fenwick, who had governed the remnants of a continent for nine months before this moment and who had watched the nightmare-creatures eat three provinces and the sanity of everyone he respected, looked at this child — this actual child, with a curtain rod on her head and a smear of something that might have been jam at the corner of her mouth — and said: "As you decree."

And then, outside, past the glass and the salt-stained walls, beyond the sweep of the beam as it turned its slow pale circle over the dark, something moved.

It was too large to be moving. Things that size should have been geography — hills, ridges, the shoulders of mountains. But it shifted, and the shifting was slow and tectonic and wrong, and it made a sound. Not a roar, not a scream. A deep, wet, pulling sound, like air entering a space that had been sealed for a very long time. Like lungs filling for the first time. Like a city — a whole city, with all its streets and squares and the empty sockets of its windows — remembering how to breathe.

Sable turned to look. The beam swept past, and for one rotation the light touched the shape at the far edge of the dark, and it was too big, and then the beam moved on and there was only the impression of mass, of patience, of something waiting without urgency because urgency implies the possibility of failure and this thing had never considered failure, not once, not ever.

"Also," Sable said, turning back to the room, "I want the chairs put away. The empty ones. It's not nice to save seats for people who can't come."

## II.

One hundred and thirty-seven steps.

Sable had counted them the first night, when Fenwick had led her up by the hand and shown her what must be done, and she had counted them every night since, not because she might miscount or because the number might change but because counting was the kind of thing you *did*. You counted steps. You counted days. You counted the things you still had, and you did it out loud, because out loud made it real, and real was important in a place where the edges of things had started to go soft.

"One," she said, and her shoe — too big, stuffed with newspaper at the toe, inherited from someone who didn't need shoes anymore — landed on the first step with a sound like a polite cough.

The staircase spiraled clockwise, which meant her right hand could trail along the wall and her left hand was in the air, which she did not like. She kept her left hand balled at her side. The wall under her right fingers was wet. Not dripping — more like sweating. The lighthouse was always sweating. It was old and made of stone and iron and it had been standing in the salt wind for longer than anyone could tell her, and its walls did the thing that cold glasses do in summer: they wore the weather on the outside of their skin.

"Two. Three. Four."

The interior smelled the way the interior always smelled: like rust and brine and — underneath those, if you breathed in hard, if you really *reached for it* — something warmer. Like the air above a stove when you've just blown out a match. Sulphur and heat and the smallest ghost of light. Sable thought of it as the lighthouse's own smell, the one it had even when you took away the ocean and the metal and the age. Its self-smell. She liked it. It made the stairs feel less cold under her newspaper feet.

"Fourteen. Fifteen. Sixteen."

The windows were set at irregular intervals — she had mapped this in her mind and the map was firm and reliable in the way that maps she made were always firm and reliable, because she built them slowly and checked them twice and once you had checked a thing twice it was true. Window at step twenty-two: faced north, showed the headland and the wreck of what had been a fishing village before the dark had eaten it from the inside out, though she did not think of it in those terms; she thought of it as "the broken houses," the way you'd think of a toy that had been sat on. Window at step forty-nine: faced east, showed the sea, which the nightmares did not cross or could not cross, and which was therefore the one direction that still looked like the world was supposed to look, gray and flat and honest. Window at step eighty-one: faced south, and she did not look out of window eighty-one.

She had looked once. She was not going to look again.

"Thirty. Thirty-one. Thirty-two."

Her legs were not tired yet. They would be. The tiredness lived somewhere around step ninety and it wasn't a leg-tiredness, not exactly; it was more like the feeling of carrying a heavy backpack for a long time, if the backpack was invisible and strapped not to her shoulders but to the inside of her head. A weight behind her eyes. A thickness. She had tried to explain it to Fenwick once and had said, "It's like when you try to remember a word and you *almost* have it and that *almost* lasts forever," and Fenwick had written this down in his notebook with his shaking hand and she had felt, briefly, like a specimen. She had not tried to explain it again.

"Fifty. Fifty-one."

The steps were not even. Some were taller than others, and some had cracks that caught the toes of her too-big shoes, and one — step sixty-three — had a chip missing from its left edge that was exactly the shape of Florida, which she knew about from a book she had read before the dark came, a book about geography that had belonged to her mother, who had belonged to the world before the world had belonged to the nightmares, and this was where she did not let her thoughts go, not on the stairs, because the stairs were for counting and counting was for keeping the inside of her head clean and organized, like a room you've tidied before company comes.

"Sixty-three," she said, and stepped over Florida.

"Sixty-four. Sixty-five."

The beam lived at the top. She could feel it now, the way you can feel the sun on your face even with your eyes closed — not heat, exactly, but *presence*. A pressure. An awareness that something above her was awake and waiting and in need of the specific thing she brought, which she could not have named if you'd asked her, except that it came from the same place inside her that made her want to count, that made her wave at things, that made her insist on the rules of games even when no one was playing.

"Ninety."

There it was. The backpack. The invisible one, strapped to the inside of her skull. She kept climbing.

"Ninety-one. Ninety-two."

Her breath came through her mouth now — not gasping, not ragged, just *open*, the way a runner's breath opens on a long hill. She was not a runner. She was a nine-year-old girl with newspaper in her shoes and a curtain rod on her head and she was climbing because climbing was her job, and she did her job because it was *hers*, and there was something in the way she said the numbers now — a little louder, a little firmer — that was not defiance but was in the same family as defiance, the way sparrows are in the same family as eagles but nobody would mix them up.

"One hundred."

Thirty-seven more.

The top of the lighthouse was a room made mostly of glass, or what had been glass — some panes had cracked and been patched with oilcloth and duct tape, and these patches were dark squares in the bright surround, like missing teeth in a smile. The beam mechanism sat in the center: a brass-and-iron apparatus that might once have been a Fresnel lens assembly but was now something else, something that had been *changed* by whatever force had also changed the world, so that its glass prisms had gone milky and warm and its iron frame hummed at a frequency Sable could feel in her back teeth. It did not rotate on its own. There was a crank, and the crank was stiff, and every night she turned it.

"One hundred and thirty-seven."

She stepped into the lamp room and let out a breath that lived somewhere deeper than her lungs.

The beam was dim. It was always dim when she arrived — banked, sulky, the pale color of watered milk. It came from the center of the lens assembly but it did not come from any bulb or flame or filament that she could see. It came from the glass itself, or from the air inside the glass, or from something that lived in the relationship between the glass and the iron and the particular quality of the silence at the top of 137 stairs. It was waiting for her.

She placed her hands on the crank.
