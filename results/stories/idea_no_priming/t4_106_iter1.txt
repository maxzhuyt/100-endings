# Parador and the Flood

## I.

He came in on the stolen shrimper at dusk, when the light was the color of a healing bruise, and the first thing he saw was the steeple of Saint Jude's rising from the water like a finger bone from a shallow grave. The town had no harbor anymore. The town had no streets. What it had was a brown, silted, lazily churning inland sea that stretched from the levee breaks to the east all the way to the limestone bluffs on the western edge, and in this sea, like the remnants of some botched civilization sunk for its sins, stood the upper halves of buildings, the crowns of oaks trailing Spanish moss in the current, electrical poles leaning at angles that suggested drunkenness or prayer.

The water was at fourteen feet.

Fourteen feet measured from the old flood markers on the courthouse steps, which were themselves submerged now to the ninth step. Fourteen feet meant the entire downtown commercial district was chest-deep at minimum, that the residential wards north of Canal were navigable only by flatboat, that the two-lane highway connecting the town to the parish seat had become a river with a current strong enough to roll a compact car. The Army Corps had sandbagged and retreated. FEMA had issued a declaration and retreated. The governor had toured by helicopter and retreated. What remained was what always remained in the lowest places: the people who could not leave, the people who would not leave, and the people for whom catastrophe was a liquidity event.

Parador tied the shrimper to a fire escape on what had been Decatur Street and stood on the deck and looked at the territory.

To the east, the ground rose. Not dramatically — nothing in this alluvial country was dramatic in its geology — but enough. Enough that the Tran compound, a cluster of commercial buildings and residences on the slopes above the old shrimp processing plant, remained dry on its upper floors. The Trans had been here three generations, had come as refugees and stayed as something more permanent, and their operation — gambling, protection, the movement of certain goods through the port — had survived hurricanes, indictments, and two internal wars of succession. Their territory was visible from where Parador stood: lights in the upper windows, a generator's thrum carrying across the water, and on the flat roof of the main building, a figure with a rifle, scanning.

To the west, the bluffs. Limestone shelves that had once been the fashionable part of town, where the antebellum homes stood behind iron fences and live oaks, and where the Delacroix family had, over four decades, converted a row of cotton warehouses into the infrastructure of a smuggling network that serviced the entire Gulf Coast. The Delacroix warehouses sat thirty feet above the old waterline. Even now, even at fourteen feet, the lowest of them was dry. The Delacroix family understood elevation the way old money always understood elevation — as birthright. They controlled what came in by water and what went out by road, and the flood had only concentrated their advantage: every boat in the parish that wanted to dock on dry ground docked at Delacroix wharfage, at Delacroix prices.

Between these two powers, the brown water moved.

Parador sat on the gunwale of the stolen shrimper and ate a can of Vienna sausages and watched the geometry of it: the high ground east, the high ground west, and the drowned flat country between. The water was the color of coffee with too much milk. It smelled of diesel and sewage and the particular sweetness of submerged vegetation rotting in the heat. The air was close, humid, ninety-one degrees at sunset. Somewhere nearby, something large and dead — a cow, perhaps, or a sofa that had absorbed enough water to develop its own ecosystem — bumped against a submerged car and kept moving.

He had come from Biloxi. Before Biloxi, Mobile. Before Mobile, a place he did not discuss except in fragments that shifted depending on the audience. He was — depending on the fragment — forty-six or fifty-two, born in Cartagena or Colón or Corpus Christi, educated by Jesuits or by the merchant marine or by eight years in Angola State Penitentiary. What was consistent: he was dark-haired and had a scar along the left jaw that pulled his mouth into an expression of faint, permanent amusement, and he spoke with an accent that moved like the water, picking up particulate from wherever it had been. He was not large. He was not armed. He had the shrimper, a canvas bag with clothes and papers, and a sense of the situation that was, as yet, only a sense — a feeling for the shape of the opportunity the way a dowser feels for the shape of the aquifer.

The next morning he motored the shrimper east and tied up below the Tran compound and waited until a man in a flat-bottomed johnboat came alongside and asked his business.

The morning after that, he motored west and tied up at the Delacroix wharf and waited until a woman with a shotgun asked his business.

To both, he said approximately the same thing, though not in the same language or in the same register: he was a man who moved freely on the water. He had a boat. He had no allegiances. He had information. What followed was the oldest transaction in the world — the exchange of a small, verifiable truth for a large, provisional trust. To the Trans he identified the location of a Delacroix supply cache in a flooded warehouse off Rampart — a cache he had spotted from the water, a real thing, a concrete fact. To the Delacroix family he identified the number and armament of the Tran sentries on the eastern perimeter — a count he had made from the shrimper, accurate, observable if they sent someone to check. Both sides checked. Both sides found him accurate.

The water held steady at fourteen feet. It had not rained in three days. The river was still high, the levee breaks still open, but the flood was paused, breathing, deciding.

Parador waited. A man in a boat amid a drowned city. One man, and the water, and the two hills on either side with their little kings.

*This is a story about the flood.*

## II.

Eight days in, and the scheme was running.

The first fabrication was architectural. Parador told Minh Tran — the eldest son, the operational head while his father recovered from a stroke in the upstairs bedroom — that the Delacroix family had purchased three cases of marine-grade plastic explosive from a contractor in Houma and was storing them in the old icehouse on Dauphine, above the waterline. The icehouse was real. The explosives were not. But the detail was correct in spirit: the Delacroix family *had* been buying from Houma, had *been* interested in demolition supplies, because half the parish was looking for ways to redirect the floodwater away from their own holdings and toward somebody else's. The lie was plausible because it was built on the scaffolding of a truth.

Minh Tran was thirty-four, precise, educated at Tulane before the family had called him home, and he ran his father's operation with the controlled intensity of a man who believed that rationality could be imposed on any situation given sufficient data. He wore wire-rimmed glasses and kept a laminated map of the flooded town on which he updated water levels twice daily with a grease pencil. He listened to Parador's report about the explosives and asked four specific questions — provenance, quantity, chemical composition, who had made the delivery — and Parador answered them all with the unhesitating fluency of a man who had been there, who had seen, who had no reason to lie because he had nothing to gain from the lie, he was simply a man on the water who noticed things and believed in being useful to his friends.

Minh sent two men on a skiff to watch the icehouse. They watched for two days and saw a Delacroix crew come and go — a crew that was, in fact, moving canned goods and bottled water from the icehouse to the bluff warehouses, an entirely innocent supply-chain operation that, viewed through the lens Parador had provided, looked exactly like men tending a weapons cache.

The second fabrication was financial. Parador told Marcel Delacroix — the family's comptroller, a fastidious man who wore linen suits even in the flood and traveled by pirogue with a ledger wrapped in plastic — that the Trans were counterfeiting currency. Specifically, that they had a small offset press on the third floor of their compound and were running off twenties to pay their people, because the flood had cut them off from their cash reserves in New Orleans and they were liquidity-starved. This was a beautiful lie. It was beautiful because the Trans *were* liquidity-starved — everyone with eyes could see that their purchases at the floating market near Saint Jude's had shifted from cash to barter over the past week — and because a counterfeiting operation would explain both the cash shortage and the Trans' apparent willingness to continue operating as if they had resources. Marcel Delacroix received this intelligence with the expression of a man who has confirmed a suspicion, which is the most dangerous expression in the world, because it means the lie has fused with a preexisting belief and can no longer be extracted without surgery.

Marcel sent word up the bluff. The word came back: watch and wait. Marcel, being Marcel, also adjusted his ledger — a column for anticipated losses due to counterfeit currency now circulating in the local economy, a column for the cost of deploying a detection protocol, a column for the potential leverage value of exposing the Trans' counterfeiting to the federal authorities still nominally operating out of the parish courthouse roof.

The third fabrication was personal. Parador told Minh that Marcel Delacroix had made inquiries — quiet, careful, through intermediaries in Baton Rouge — about purchasing the Tran family's shrimp-processing plant, which sat at the base of the eastern rise and was currently ten feet underwater. The implication: the Delacroix family was planning for the post-flood future, a future in which they would own both the high ground and the commercial waterfront, in which the Trans would be squeezed out of the rebuilt town entirely. This lie required no corroborating evidence because it operated on emotion, not logistics. Minh Tran's jaw tightened when he heard it. The processing plant had been his grandfather's. The shrimping industry had been the legitimate scaffold on which everything else was built. To suggest that the Delacroix family was circling it — during a flood, while the family was disoriented and the patriarch was bedridden — was to suggest a kind of predation that required no proof because it was axiomatically true in Minh's model of the world. Of course the Delacroix family was circling. They had always been circling. The flood was simply their opportunity.

Parador moved between the two territories on his shrimper, a man without a flag, bearing dispatches from a war that existed only in his descriptions of it. He ate what they gave him. He slept on the shrimper. He used the fuel they supplied. Each side valued him for his mobility on the water — that was the genuine utility he provided, the real service that made the false intelligence credible — and each side believed it had his loyalty, or if not his loyalty, then his self-interest, which was more reliable.

The water rose to fourteen feet six inches. A slow rise, almost imperceptible day to day, but measurable. The sandbar at the bend near the old train depot disappeared. The first floor of the courthouse took water. The current between the buildings increased by a fraction that mattered to anyone in a flat-bottomed boat.

Parador's objective was not complicated. It did not require genius. It required patience and a flood. He would accelerate the two organizations into a conflict that would damage both, position himself as the essential intermediary during the chaos, and extract — in the form of physical goods, cash, and territorial concession — what he could carry when he motored the shrimper south toward the Gulf and the open country beyond.

It was a grift. An old one. The Spanish had a word for the man who played both sides during a war between kingdoms: *emustio*, the ember-man, who fed on what burned. Or they didn't — the word might have been Parador's invention, a thing he told himself at night on the shrimper, fitting the scheme into the grooves of an older story, as a man might fit a key into a lock.

He did not think about this yet. He worked.

## III.

At fifteen feet the attic meetings began.

The Tran organization held its first high-water council on a Wednesday night in August, in the attic of the main compound building, a space that had been used for storage and was now, by necessity, the seat of governance. The oyabun — that was not the word they used, but it was the word Parador used in his head when he told himself the story of what he'd witnessed — the old man, Huynh Tran, had been carried up the stairs in a kitchen chair by two nephews, the exertion of the ascent nearly finishing what the stroke had started. He sat at the head of a salvaged card table, breathing audibly, his left hand curled in his lap like a dead bird. Around him, in a configuration that respected both the hierarchy's demands and the attic's dimensions, sat his sons, his nephews, and three lieutenants: Minh at his right hand, the others arrayed in descending order of seniority on overturned five-gallon buckets, a water-stained mattress, and in the case of the youngest lieutenant — a boy of perhaps twenty, still earning his place — the bare floor.

The attic smelled of mildew, of the wet insulation in the walls, of the old man's medicated sweat. Through the floorboards, the sound of water: not rushing, not dramatic, but a slow, adhesive lapping, the sound of the flood digesting the floor below. One of the Tran cousins, a heavyset man named Bao, had positioned a battery-powered lantern on a cardboard box, and the light it threw was surgical, creating hard-edged shadows that made the faces around the table look carved from something.

Old man Tran spoke in Vietnamese. Minh translated for the two lieutenants who didn't have the language, and Parador — who was present, in the back, on the stairs, in the liminal space between invited and tolerated — caught fragments of both. The subject was a shipment of electronics that had been en route from Houston before the flood cut the highway. The shipment was sitting in a warehouse in Lake Charles, accruing storage fees, and the Houston supplier was threatening to divert it. The old man's position was that the shipment must be retrieved. His reasoning was not financial. It was procedural. The organization had made a commitment to receive the shipment and had accepted a price, and to fail in this commitment was to signal weakness to the supplier, who would adjust future terms accordingly. The flood was not a mitigating circumstance. The flood was a test.
