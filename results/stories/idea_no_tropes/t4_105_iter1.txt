# The House on Herring Cove Road

## I.

The house was smaller than I remembered, which is something people always say, so I'd prepared myself for it, and it was still true.

I pulled onto the crushed-shell driveway at quarter to three on a Thursday, two hours before anyone else was supposed to arrive, which was the point. I needed the house empty first. I needed to walk through it alone the way you walk through a church before a service — when it's still yours, before it becomes everyone's, before people start coughing and checking their phones and the whole spell goes to shit.

The cedar shingles had gone from silver to a kind of defeated gray. The roof sagged over the front porch like a tired eyelid. There was a new dent in the gutter where a branch had fallen, or someone had thrown something, and the screen door was the wrong color — green, when it had been white, or white when it had been green, but *wrong*, detectably wrong, in a way I registered before I could name it. The hydrangeas along the south wall were enormous. They'd had fifteen years to eat the foundation, and they had done their work.

I stood in the driveway with my bag and felt the specific Atlantic dampness of Cape Cod in late August — that particular humidity that isn't tropical, isn't aggressive, just persistent, like a low-grade sadness you mistake for personality. The ocean was behind the house. You could hear it if you held still: that shush, that rhythmic nothing, filtering through the gaps between buildings. The porch — the main porch, the one that faced the water — had two Adirondack chairs on it, both listing to the left, both the color of weather. I could see the dune grass through the railing, and beyond it the long smear of the Atlantic doing what it does, which is lie there enormously and not care about any of this.

Inside, the house smelled like mildew and Pine-Sol, which meant someone had tried to clean and had only succeeded in layering one smell on top of another. The kitchen was to the left. The kitchen where — in August of 2009, the last month of the last summer any of us were still the people we'd planned to be — Marco had said the thing to Dana at the counter, the thing about leaving, or staying, or being afraid to want something, I can never remember the exact words but I remember the quality of them, the way his voice sounded like someone pulling a thread out of a sweater, like something was unraveling and he was doing it tenderly. The kitchen had a window over the sink that looked out at the side yard where the outdoor shower was, and the light came through it in the mornings in a way that made the whole room religious, and now the window had a crack running through it and the linoleum was peeling up in the corner near the fridge like a scab you couldn't stop picking.

The living room was small. It had always been small, I think, but in 2009 smallness had been an asset — it forced proximity, it made every gathering an event, there was no way to be in the room without your knee touching someone's knee, without catching someone's eye over the lip of a beer can, without feeling the whole summer pressing in. Now it was just a room with a coffee table that was scratched and a couch that was different from the couch I remembered and a TV that was a flat-screen where we'd had a tube TV that you could feel the static off of if you put your hand close, the way you feel a ghost in a movie, the way you feel something that is there and not there.

I dropped my bag in the room at the end of the hall — my room, the one I'd slept in that summer, the one with the window that looked out at the ocean, or the backyard, or whatever was out there. The bed was a double, low to the ground, with a metal frame that had rusted at the joints. I sat on it. The mattress sighed.

The whole house sighed. That was its prevailing condition: exhalation. Something had been breathing in for a very long time and now it was breathing out and would keep breathing out until there was nothing left.

I went back outside to the porch to wait. The porch — and now I was on it, and looking out — faced the parking lot of the strip mall across the road. The Wendy's. The UPS Store. The nail salon whose sign had been missing the *S* in *Nails* since at least 2009, so it just said *NAIL*, singular, like a biblical imperative. I sat in one of the Adirondack chairs and it creaked in a way that suggested it was making a structural decision about my weight, and I looked at the Wendy's drive-through and the kid in the paper hat leaning out the window handing someone a Frosty and I felt it — the thing I'd come for, the exact thing, already — that ache of a place being two places at once, being the place where your life happened and also a place where someone is right now, right this second, eating a Frosty within direct sight of the spot where you were kissed in a way that reorganized your whole understanding of what you were for.

The kiss had happened on the porch. I'm certain the kiss happened on the porch. It was dusk. It was that ten minutes of Cape Cod dusk when the light goes the color of a peach left on the counter too long — overripe, almost brown at the edges, sweet to the point of rot. Marco had been — or someone had been — or I had been standing at the railing, and then not standing, and then the light was different and so was I. That was 2009. That was the whole summer in one gesture.

I'd come back because it had been fifteen years, which is a number that means something, or because Marco had emailed in May with the subject line "Herring Cove Redux?" and the body of the email was just a link to the VRBO listing and a question mark, and I'd stared at it for three days and then replied "sure, could be fun" in a tone I practiced for an hour, and within a week Marco, Dana, Sam, and Theo had all said yes, and then it was August and I was on the porch.

Or: I'd come back because my marriage had ended nine months ago — not violently, not even sadly, just *conclusively*, the way a light turns off, and Brian had said during the last real conversation we had that he sometimes felt like he was living with someone who was in a different building, not a different room but a different *building*, a building he couldn't see but that I was always looking at, and I hadn't been able to argue because he was right, the building was this building, this actual house with its mildew and its cracked window and its rusted bed frame, and I had been living in it for fifteen years inside my head while my body moved around Portland doing the things a body does — teaching, cooking, folding laundry, kissing a husband who deserved to be kissed by someone who was actually in the room.

But that second reason isn't exactly right either, because I'd been wanting to come back before Brian left, and the wanting was older than the marriage, and the wanting is the thing I can't look at directly, the way you can't look at the sun and also the way you can't look at the sun because it might not be a sun at all, it might be a Wendy's sign with good backlighting, and then where would you be.

Inside, something dripped. The house, breathing out.

## II.

Dana arrived first, which I hadn't expected. I'd expected Marco. I'd built the whole arrival sequence in my head with Marco walking up the driveway first, but Dana's Subaru pulled in at 4:55 and Dana got out and she looked exactly the same and completely different, which is what I always think about people I haven't seen in a long time, which is probably just a thing that's true of everyone.

She'd cut her hair. Or grown it out. She'd done something with her hair — it was different from the picture I'd held in my head, which was Dana in 2009 with her dark curls with the copper highlights she got from the sun, the ones that made her look like she was always standing in a shaft of light even when she wasn't. Now her hair was shorter, or straighter, or just *older hair*, which is a thing hair becomes, and she was wearing a linen shirt that was very precisely wrinkled in the way that expensive linen allows itself to be wrinkled, and she stood in the driveway and looked at the house and I watched her looking at it from the porch and I could see her doing what I had done: overlaying. Building the 2009 house on top of the 2024 house like a transparency on an overhead projector, like a ghost being laid over its own skeleton.

"It's smaller," she said.

"I know."

"The porch is —" She stopped. She was looking at the Wendy's.

"I know," I said again.

She came up the steps and we hugged. The hug lasted three seconds, which I clocked, which I hated myself for clocking, and her arms went around me at a height and a pressure that I filed away along with the observation that she smelled like eucalyptus and the observation that the eucalyptus was deliberate, she had put something on, she had prepared for the hug, which meant she'd been thinking about the hug, which meant this mattered to her the way it mattered to me, or I was insane.

"You look great," Dana said, which meant nothing, or meant I looked old but she was generous, or meant she was too distracted to really look at me because she was looking past me into the house the way you look into a cave — excited and a little afraid.

Dana chose the bedroom next to the kitchen. The room that in 2009 had been — well, it had been Sam's room, I'm fairly sure, or possibly Theo's, or possibly it hadn't been anyone's because we'd shifted rooms that summer the way you do in a group rental, migrating by instinct, sleeping on couches, sleeping on the floor of the living room when it was too hot upstairs, though I don't think there *was* an upstairs. I heard Dana opening drawers and closing them, and then I heard her say, from behind the closed door, to no one or to me through the wall: "This was the room with the painting, right? The boat painting?"

There had been a boat painting. I was sure there had been a boat painting. But not in that room — in the room at the end of the hall, my room, above the bed. A small oil painting of a fishing boat, inept but sincere, listing in greasy turquoise waves.

"I think that was in the other room," I said.

"No, it was in here," Dana said, with the pleasant firmness of someone who isn't arguing. "I remember because I used to stare at it before I fell asleep. Every night. It was right above the —" Pause. "There's a nail hole here. See? It was here."

I didn't go look at the nail hole. I should have gone and looked at the nail hole. Instead I stood in the kitchen and felt something very small shift in the floor of my certainty, a hairline fracture, the kind of thing you only notice because you've trained yourself to notice nothing else.

Marco and Theo arrived together, which surprised me, because I hadn't known they were still in touch, or I had known and forgotten, or I had known and filed it under something that didn't require my attention because it didn't involve me. They came in Marco's car. Marco drove. This seemed important in a way I couldn't immediately articulate, so I articulated it to myself several times — *Marco drove, he was driving, he was the one who drove* — as though one of the repetitions would release its meaning like a seed from a husk.

Marco was tall. He'd always been tall, but he'd grown into it, or I'd shrunk away from it, or the house made everyone taller by being lower-ceilinged than I remembered. He had a beard now, neatly kept, and he was wearing a flannel shirt with the sleeves rolled, and he looked like a man who built things, or wanted to look like a man who built things, or was performing a version of masculinity that he'd grown comfortable in the way you grow comfortable in a pair of shoes that aren't quite your size but you've worn them so long your feet have deformed to fit.

He hugged me fourth. He hugged Dana first, then Theo hugged me, then Marco hugged Theo which I think was a re-hug because they'd just been in a car together for three hours, and then Marco hugged me. The hug was — I'm going to say it was long. I'm going to say it was longer than three seconds and that his hand rested on my lower back and that his chin grazed my hair and that I could feel his heartbeat through his flannel shirt, which I understand is probably not physically possible through flannel, but I felt it equally. His heart was beating. This is the least remarkable observation a person can make about another person. His heart was beating, and mine was, and the house was breathing out, and somewhere inside it a pipe was dripping, and the screen door banged shut in the wind, and the Wendy's drive-through intercom next door said something garbled about a value meal.

"It's exactly the same," Marco said, looking at the house.

"It's really not," said Dana.

"Sure it is. The porch. The — yeah." He pointed at the railing. "We used to sit right there."

"The railing's different," said Dana. "It was white. This is —"

"Weathered."

"Gray. It's gray now."

"Weathered is just gray that happened slowly."

They both laughed, and the laugh was real — I could feel its realness — and also performed, a laugh produced for the group, for the moment, for the mythology of people who can still make each other laugh after fifteen years. I watched them laughing and loved them and wanted to write it down and also wanted to stop noticing everything with this clenched, forensic attention that has been my way of being in the world for as long as I can remember, this relentless cataloging of gestures and glances as though if I can just record enough detail I can finally assemble the scene into something that makes sense, that tells me what I mean to people, that answers the question I have been asking the back of Marco's head for fifteen years.

Sam arrived last, at nearly seven, driving a rental and carrying a bottle of wine that was good enough to signal effort but not so good as to signal desperation. Sam was the diplomat. Sam had always been the diplomat. In 2009, Sam had been the one who poured an extra glass, who made room on the couch, who said "tell me more about that" when everyone else was looking at their phones. She was smaller than I remembered, or larger — she was *different*-sized, in a way that troubled me not because of anything about Sam's body but because it revealed the imprecision of the image I'd carried. The Sam in my head had apparently been a rough draft, an approximation, a Sam-shaped clearing in the air that the actual Sam did not quite fill.

She kissed cheeks. She said "Oh my *God*" in a way that was planned to sound unplanned. She stood in the living room and turned slowly, taking it in, and said: "The ceiling's lower."

"It's not," said Theo.

"We're taller," said Dana.

"Are we?" Sam ran her hand along the doorframe to the kitchen. "There used to be marks here. Remember? We measured everyone's height."

We all looked at the doorframe. It was clean. No marks.

"Different doorframe," Marco said. "They probably replaced it."

"Or different door," said Theo.

"Or different house," I said, and everyone laughed, and the laughter this time was warmer because it was later in the day and the first beers had been opened and the light through the kitchen window was doing the thing it used to do — the thing it had always done; light doesn't decay the way wood decays — and for a moment every person in the room believed, simultaneously and separately, that they had come home.
