# The Last Honest Nihilist

---

## I.

Look, I'm going to tell you about a job. That's all this is. You're here because you want to know how it went, and I'm here because — well, I'm here because talking to someone is part of the process, and you happened to be available, and I don't really care whether you judge me or not. That's not false bravado. That's the whole philosophy. Stay with me, I'll explain.

My name doesn't matter. I've had nine of them. Right now I'm using Margaux, which I chose because it sounds like someone who does yoga but also has a graduate degree — the exact median point of trustworthiness for a certain kind of Californian. Before Margaux I was Dana. Before Dana I was someone else. The names are tools. You pick the tool that fits the lock.

What I am, underneath the names, is a grifter. Not a scam artist — I want to be precise about this. Scam artists are desperate. They work from need. I work from principle. The principle is this: nothing means anything. I know, I know. You've heard that before, probably from some dorm-room Nietzsche or a guy in a bar who just got dumped. But those people say it like it's a tragedy. I say it like it's a weather report. Nothing means anything. The sky is blue. Water is wet. The narratives people build to get through their days — love, purpose, legacy, spiritual growth, *whatever* — are load-bearing fictions. They work until they don't. I just happen to see the architecture.

And once you see the architecture, you can walk through the walls.

I've been doing this for eleven years. Intentional communities are my specialty. Communes, co-ops, spiritual retreats, tech-bro monasteries, off-grid permaculture compounds — any place where a group of people has decided to build a shared story and pour their money into it. They're ideal targets, and I'll tell you why: the thing that holds them together is the thing that makes them vulnerable. They run on trust. Not normal trust. Not "I trust you to return my lawnmower" trust. Deep trust. Structural trust. "I will place my financial future in the hands of a consensus process" trust. Which means that once you're inside the story — once they believe you believe — you have access to everything. The money, sure. But also the information, the account structures, the unguarded moments where someone tells you their wallet seed phrase because you're *family* now and family doesn't encrypt things from each other.

I have taken, over eleven years, just under four million dollars from seven communities. I have never been caught. I have never been suspected. I leave, and they think I left because I was "called to a different path," and they send me love and they send me light and they go on with their beautiful, meaning-saturated lives, just a little poorer and none the wiser.

So here's the job. Five months ago, I identified a target. A commune in the mountains east of Santa Cruz, on 160 acres of old-growth redwood and coastal scrub, called — and I need you to keep a straight face — *The Clearing*. Thirty-one adults. Consensus governance. Radically sincere. Psychedelic-assisted everything. They meditate at dawn, they eat together at dusk, they hold weekly circles where they say what they actually feel, which is apparently a thing people can do if they've fully abandoned the concept of self-preservation.

They also sit on approximately nineteen million dollars in cryptocurrency, held in a multi-signature wallet requiring three of five keyholders to authorize a transfer.

That's the job.

I found them the way I find all of them: through the cracks in their digital footprint. A podcast appearance by one of the founders. Tax filings. County permit records for a yurt expansion that cost more than your house. A since-deleted tweet from a member who probably shouldn't have photographed the hardware wallet sitting next to the ceremonial cacao. The usual breadcrumbs. People who are trying to transcend the material plane have a remarkably hard time not talking about the material resources that fund the transcendence.

I drove up in March. The road is eleven miles of switchbacks through Douglas fir and madrone, and when you come around the last bend, the canopy opens onto this meadow — I mention this for tactical reasons, because the remoteness is a factor in the exit plan — this meadow that sits on a south-facing slope above a creek, and the light at that hour, late afternoon, was the kind of light that makes the air itself look like it has a color, like the atmosphere had been steeped in something golden and faintly amber, and the buildings — hand-built, a mix of timber frame and cob and reclaimed redwood — were arranged in a loose arc around a central fire pit, and the whole place looked like something that had grown rather than been constructed, like the land had dreamed it.

Anyway. I pulled in, I parked, and I started the clock.

## II.

Let me walk you through the marks.

The founder, the one who started the whole thing, is Oren Vassily. Fifty-three. Former hedge fund manager at Citadel, which — yes, that Citadel. Made an obscene amount of money running quantitative strategies through the 2010s, had what I can only describe as a catastrophic attack of conscience around 2018, liquidated, converted a significant portion to Ethereum and Bitcoin, bought the land, and started building. He's the primary keyholder. He's also the most psychologically transparent person I've ever targeted. The man cries at sunrises. I've seen it. We'll be at dawn meditation, which I'll get to, and the sky will do that thing where it goes from charcoal to rose behind the ridgeline, and Oren's shoulders will start shaking, and the tears just — they come down. No sound. No performance. He just sits there on his cushion and weeps because the light changed. Which tells you everything about how badly he wants to be scammed. Anyone that porous, that desperate to feel things after decades of algorithmic detachment, will accept almost any story that promises depth. I *am* that story. I walked in and I was the seeker who'd been wounded by institutional cynicism, who was ready to feel again. He practically adopted me on the spot.

Second keyholder: Lena Kareva. Forty-seven. Now here's an interesting one. Former arms dealer. Not directly — she ran logistics for a private military contractor that supplied equipment to both sides of three different African wars, and she has a very specific, very quiet way of entering a room that tells you she has seen actual violence, not the theoretical kind. She came to The Clearing two years ago. She doesn't talk about what she did before. The others know, roughly, and they've "held space" for it, which means they sat in a circle and let her cry for two hours and then someone played a singing bowl. She's the one I need to be most careful with because she can smell manipulation — she *was* manipulation, in a previous life. But her vulnerability is the inverse of her strength: she is so desperate to believe she has become someone incapable of manipulation that she cannot allow herself to suspect it in others. Suspicion would activate the old pattern. She'd rather be robbed than be the person who sees robbery everywhere. I can work with that.

Third keyholder: Tomás Abreu. Thirty-nine. Software architect. Wrote the smart contracts that structure the commune's wallet. Gentle, cerebral, speaks in long unbroken paragraphs about decentralized trust systems. His vulnerability is intellectual vanity — he believes the wallet architecture is unbreachable, and his certainty about the system's integrity makes him blind to social engineering. You don't hack the code. You hack the coder.

Fourth: June Morioka. Sixty-one. Former hospice nurse. She holds a key because the commune's governance model distributes fiduciary responsibility according to a rotation algorithm Tomás designed, and she happened to be in rotation when I arrived. She trusts with the specific, unboundaried openness of someone who spent twenty years watching people die and decided that guardedness was a luxury she couldn't afford. She told me her key passphrase within three weeks of my arrival. I'm not going to tell you how. I'll just say that vulnerability shared invites vulnerability returned, and she had no way of knowing that my returned vulnerability was fabricated.

Fifth: a collective key, held in a hardware device in the community's locked office, requiring a physical code that changes weekly according to — again — a Tomás-designed rotation. The weekly code is shared verbally in the Friday consensus circle. So I need to be present at the right circle and remember a string of numbers. Not hard.

Do you see the picture? Three of five signatures. I have June's passphrase. I can get the collective key. I just need one more — Oren, Lena, or Tomás — and I need a window of roughly forty minutes to execute the transfer, distribute across tumblers, and be on the road before anyone notices the wallet's been emptied.

That's the geometry. The human part is just: be Margaux. Be the seeking, wounded, slowly-opening Margaux who is learning to trust again after — and I will give you this one detail from my cover story, because I'm proud of it — after losing her partner in a climbing accident that she blames herself for. I invented a dead girlfriend. I gave her a name (Cass), a face (borrowed from a college friend's Instagram), and a set of memories that I can surface on command, with the right tremble in the voice, the right moisture in the eyes. I practiced the tremble in the bathroom mirror of a Motel 6 in Felton. I am very good at this.

The daily structure is useful to understand. You wake at five-fifteen. Not optional — the culture enforces it softly, through social gravity rather than rules. Dawn meditation in the meadow. Silent. Forty minutes. Then communal breakfast. Then work assignments: gardening, building, infrastructure, kitchen, childcare, administration. The consensus circle happens weekly, Fridays, in a round timber structure they call the Hive, and it runs on a talking-object protocol — you hold a polished river stone and you speak, and no one interrupts, and there is no time limit, and decisions aren't made until everyone can "stand in" the decision, which is their phrase, and which in practice means that a discussion about whether to buy a new wheelbarrow can take ninety minutes. An astonishing security flaw. You just sit there, you listen to people process their feelings about wheelbarrows, and you absorb enormous quantities of operational information because they are radically transparent about everything, including their financial architecture, because secrecy is antithetical to the culture, because the whole point is that they have built a place where nothing is hidden.

I mean, come on.

Every two weeks, there's a guided psychedelic ceremony. Psilocybin, usually, sometimes ayahuasca. Administered by Oren and a facilitator named Dessa who trained with some Santo Daime offshoot in Brazil. The ceremonies are held at night in the Hive. They last six to eight hours. Participation isn't mandatory, but non-participation is noted — softly, again, without judgment, but noted — and since my cover requires full immersion, I planned to attend. Three grams of psilocybin isn't going to compromise an operative who knows exactly what neurochemistry is doing to her perception. It's a tool. The brain generates novel pattern recognition under psilocybin. Useful for reading people. I've done it before in other contexts. It's fine.

I arrived in March. I gave myself twelve weeks. The plan was to be gone by June, with the crypto distributed across untraceable wallets, and The Clearing would wake up one morning to find that Margaux had left a beautiful, heartfelt goodbye letter — gratitude for the healing, acknowledgment that she had "more work to do on her own," all the phrases — and also that nineteen million dollars had evaporated.

That was the plan.

## III.

There is a particular quality to the air in the Santa Cruz Mountains in the early morning, before the fog has burned off but after it has begun to thin, when the redwoods stand in columns of diffused white light and the moisture is so fine it doesn't feel like rain or mist but like the atmosphere itself has become porous, like there is no hard boundary between the air and the skin and the wood and the silence.

Lena Kareva stood at the eastern edge of the meadow at 5:47 a.m. on a Tuesday in April, seventeen years and four months after the last time she had authorized a shipment of Belgian FN rifles to a depot outside Bangui, and she was watching a spider rebuild a web between two stalks of coyote brush. The web had been destroyed overnight — wind, animal, the ordinary violences of a world in motion — and the spider was remaking it with what Lena could only describe as patience, although she knew that was projection, that spiders do not experience patience, that the neurological apparatus required for patience involves a prefrontal cortex and a relationship to time that arachnids do not possess.

But she watched it anyway.

She had learned, in two years at The Clearing, that the gap between what she knew and what she felt was not a problem to be solved but a space to be inhabited. She knew the spider was not patient. She felt that the spider was patient. Both of these were true in the way that mattered, which was the way that allowed her to stand in a meadow at dawn and watch a small creature repair what had been broken and feel something in her chest that she had come, cautiously, to call hope, although the word still made her flinch, because hope in her previous life had been a commodity she sold to desperate men in rooms with no windows, and she did not yet fully trust that the word could mean what the people here seemed to mean by it.

The light shifted. The fog thinned by one degree. The spider anchored a radial thread.

Lena thought about the woman who had arrived three weeks ago. Margaux. Something about her. Not a suspicion — Lena would not allow that, could not allow that, because suspicion was the old instrument and she had put it down. But something. A quality of attention in Margaux's eyes that Lena recognized, not from The Clearing but from before, from rooms where everyone was calculating angles. It was probably nothing. It was probably Lena's old self projecting its own architecture onto someone innocent. She had done this before — had sat in circle and confessed, with shame that tasted like copper, that she had suspected a visitor of ulterior motives and been wrong, and the group had held her and Oren had said, *That's not you anymore, that's the echo,* and she had believed him because she needed to and because he might have been right.

But she watched the spider, and she thought about Margaux's eyes, and she held both things without resolution, the way the meadow held the fog, and then the bell rang for meditation and she walked toward the circle, her bare feet dark with dew.

## IV.

By week four I had Oren. Not his key — not yet — but his trust, which is the precursor, which is always the precursor. We had developed a routine: after the morning work shift, we'd walk the north trail along the ridge, and he'd talk about the hedge fund years like a man describing a disease he'd survived, and I would listen with the specific quality of attention that makes powerful men feel truly seen for the first time. It's not hard. You widen your eyes by about two millimeters. You let your lips part very slightly. You tilt your head — left side, always left; there's research on this — and you ask one question for every three statements they make, and the question always starts with the word "what." Not "why," which triggers defensiveness. "What." *What was that like? What did you feel? What did you do with that?* "What" is a door. "Why" is an interrogation lamp.

He told me about the morning he decided to leave Citadel. He'd been running a strategy that exploited pricing inefficiencies in agricultural commodities — corn, soy, wheat — and he'd pulled up a Reuters piece about a famine in South Sudan while his algorithm was actively profiting from the grain price dynamics that were connected, through seventeen degrees of abstraction, to the conditions that produced the famine. He said he looked at the profit figure on his screen and the photograph of a child on his phone and felt what he could only describe as a "structural collapse." Not guilt exactly. Not empathy. Something architectural. Like a building that discovers its foundation is made of something other than what it thought.
