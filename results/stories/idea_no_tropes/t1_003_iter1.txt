# The Pears and the Frequency of Light

The knife was a Victorinox, wooden-handled, bought at a hardware store on Atlantic Avenue in 1987 because Margot had lost the previous one — a serrated thing from a set neither of them could remember acquiring — behind the radiator while trying to pry loose a marble one of the downstairs children had rolled through a gap in the floorboard. The cutting board was maple, eleven inches by fourteen, warped slightly upward at its eastern edge from thirty years of being washed and set to dry the same way each time, so that anything round placed on its surface rolled gently left, toward the window. The pears were Anjou, four of them, not yet soft, still wearing that faint grit on the skin that would come away under a thumb rubbed firmly enough, and Margot was cutting them into eighths because that was how she had always cut them, standing at the counter with her left hip pressed against the drawer that stuck, her bare feet on the linoleum whose pattern — a lattice of pale green diamonds — had worn to a colorless smoothness along the path between the stove and the sink and the stove and the table and the table and the door.

On the fire escape, three feet from the open window, Ceil sat with her knees drawn up inside the oversized cardigan she had stolen from Margot in either 1979 or 1981 — Ceil knew exactly which but did not at this moment consult that knowledge — and her head was tilted eleven degrees to the right, which was the angle at which her left ear, the sharper one, could best receive the courtyard. A white-throated sparrow was singing from the ailanthus tree on the south side, its song the one Peterson transcribes as *Oh sweet Canada Canada Canada*, though this particular bird dropped the second *Canada* and replaced it with a note Ceil had never been able to find in any field guide, a note she privately thought of as the bird clearing its throat before deciding not to say something. Margot's mug — the blue one, chipped at the nine-o'clock position on its rim — sat on the counter near the pears, half-full of tea that had cooled past the point of being worth drinking but had not yet cooled to the point where Margot would pour it out and start again. Ceil's mug — the white one with no handle, which was not a mug at all but a yunomi she had brought back from a shop in the East Village in 1971 — was on the fire escape beside her right foot, empty, because Ceil always finished her tea in four long swallows and then held the empty vessel for warmth until it had none left to give.

The apartment was on the fourth floor and had two bedrooms, though the second had not been a bedroom since approximately 1998, when the last of several attempts to maintain separate sleeping spaces had quietly collapsed, not through any dramatic reconciliation but because Ceil had fallen asleep one night in Margot's bed while they were listening to the radio and neither of them had mentioned it and then it was morning and then it had been morning for twenty-six years. The second bedroom held six boxes of field journals — Ceil's — and a sewing machine in its cabinet that worked perfectly and that no one had opened since the century turned. There was a bathroom with a window that did not shut all the way and a living room with two chairs, the same model, bought together, that had aged into different shapes according to the different shapes of the women who sat in them. On the kitchen wall, between the cabinet with the plates and the cabinet with the everything else, a clock with a white face and black hands showed 3:12 in the afternoon.

Ceil would die at 4:07 PM today. An aneurysm — a vessel in the left posterior cerebral region, thinning for eleven years, undetected, its wall no thicker now than a filament of spider silk — would complete its slow negotiation with physics, and Ceil would be in the middle of something ordinary, and then she would not. It would not hurt. It would be faster than the sparrow's dropped note. Between the pears and the evening news, between one breath and the absence of the next, Ceil's body would make a single, irreversible decision that her mind would not have time to protest or even register, and the afternoon would continue in the courtyard and in the apartments above and below exactly as it had been continuing, because an afternoon does not know who is in it.

Margot quartered the last pear and set the knife down. A seed had split under the blade, and she pushed the halves to the side of the board with her fingertip, and one of the halves fell to the floor, and she did not pick it up yet because she would get it later, the way she always got things later, the way the apartment was a record of Margot's intentions to do things later and Ceil's inability to forget that they had not been done. The pear slices on the cutting board were pale and sweating. Margot arranged them so they overlapped, not because this improved anything but because the overlapping pleased her in a way she had never tried to articulate and which Ceil had never asked her to articulate, though Ceil had noticed the pattern every time and could, if pressed, reproduce from memory the exact arrangement of pear slices on the cutting board from any afternoon in the last six decades when pears had been cut in this kitchen, which was most afternoons in September through November, because Margot bought them by the bag from the stand on Seventh Avenue and always bought too many, and Ceil had never once told her she bought too many.

The knife had slipped. This happened perhaps twice a week — Margot held the blade too casually, angled her knuckle into the path of the steel the way someone walking a familiar hallway will brush the wall with a shoulder, not from clumsiness but from an absence of vigilance so complete it had become a kind of faith. The blade caught the pad of her left index finger just below the nail, and the cut opened and was, for a tenth of a second, a cut — red, stinging, real — and then it was a line, and then a white line, and then the skin was skin. A drop of blood sat on the pear slice nearest the board's raised edge. Margot wiped it with her thumb. She did not look at the finger. She did not flex it or hold it up or press its healed surface to confirm what she already knew. Ceil, on the fire escape, did not turn around. There was nothing to turn around for. This had happened ten thousand times. The knife cut, and the body answered, and the body's answer was always the same: *no*. Not no to the pain, which arrived and registered and was real. No to the consequence. No to the scar, the scab, the slow cellular negotiation of healing that for any other body would take days. Margot's flesh refused damage the way a lake refuses the shape of a thrown stone — there was a disruption, and then there was a surface, and then there was no evidence that the disruption had occurred at all.

From the fire escape: "White-throated sparrow again."

"Same one as before?"

"Same one as 1987."

Margot looked up from the pears but not toward the window. She looked at the clock: 3:17. She looked at the pears. She transferred them to a plate — the plate with the green rim, which was part of a set of four, of which two survived, and which Ceil could tell you they had purchased from a woman named Diana at a stoop sale on Garfield Place on May 14, 1994, a Saturday, warm, the woman's daughter eating a popsicle on the steps, the popsicle grape-flavored, the daughter's shoes untied.

"You can't know it's the same bird," Margot said, but she said it the way she always said it, which was with the full knowledge that Ceil absolutely could.

"I can hear the chip in its fourth interval. Same chip. Same bird. It was on the Hendersons' fire escape when the Hendersons still lived here. Lorraine Henderson was hanging a towel." Ceil paused. Something moved behind her eyes — not effort, not retrieval, but the involuntary opening of a door she had not reached for. "A yellow towel. Tuesday. March. I could feel the screen door because I was leaning on it, and the mesh had that crosshatch pattern pressed into my palm for twenty minutes after." She looked down at her palm as though the pattern might still be there, which of course it was not, though in the place where Ceil kept such things — a place that was not a location but a condition, a quality of consciousness that had never dimmed or sorted or mercifully blurred — the crosshatch was as present as the sparrow singing now in the ailanthus that had been a sapling when Lorraine Henderson had been alive and was now taller than the building.

This was what Ceil was. Not a woman with a gift. A woman with a window that did not close. Every sensation she had ever registered remained at the resolution at which she had registered it, not as narrative, not as the soft watercolor of recollection that the word *memory* typically describes, but as a full-fidelity record — sensory, spatial, temporal, emotional — that could be accessed in its entirety at any moment and that frequently accessed itself without her permission. She did not *choose* to recall the crosshatch on her palm from 1987. She mentioned the sparrow, and the sparrow was linked to the fire escape, and the fire escape was linked to the Hendersons' fire escape, and the Hendersons' fire escape was linked to the towel, and the towel was linked to the screen door, and the screen door was linked to the pressure on her palm, and now she could feel the mesh again as though her hand were still pressed against it, which in the only country Ceil had ever fully inhabited — the country of undiminished sensation — it was.

Margot knew this. Margot had watched it happen for sixty-one years — the driftings, the sudden clarities, the moments when Ceil would stop mid-sentence because a sound or a light or the texture of a countertop had opened a corridor into some Tuesday in 1973 and she had gone down it before she could stop herself. Margot had learned not to call her back too quickly. Margot had learned that the kindest thing was to wait, to let the corridor close on its own, to be standing there with a plate of pears when Ceil's eyes returned to the present and refocused with an expression that was not confusion but something closer to the expression of a woman stepping from a cathedral back onto a loud street.

And what Margot was — what Margot had been since before she could remember being anything, since a fall from a barn roof at seven that should have shattered her spine and instead left her lying in the hay with the breath knocked out of her and nothing else, not a bruise, not a fracture, nothing — was a body that could not be broken. Not that it could not *feel* breakage. She felt the knife every time. She had felt the barn roof's distance as she fell, and the impact, and the terror. She felt winter and she felt fever and she once, in 1979, felt what she was fairly certain was a heart attack, a squeezing pressure that dropped her to this same kitchen floor, and Ceil had knelt beside her with a face Margot could never allow herself to fully remember, and then the pressure had released, and the muscle had repaired, and by the time Ceil had convinced the ambulance to come the EKG showed a heart so flawless the paramedic looked at Margot as though she had been wasting his time. Her cells simply refused the project of death. They divided and repaired and replaced with a fidelity that mocked the calendar, so that Margot at eighty-eight looked like a woman who might be sixty, or fifty on a good day, her hands unspotted, her joints unsullied, her spine as straight as the afternoon she had fallen from the barn and the hay had caught her and the hay had been unnecessary.

They had never spoken about what these qualities were or where they had come from. There had been an early period — the first years of knowing each other, graduate students in the same program, 1963, a shared office no bigger than a bathroom — when each had recognized in the other a strangeness that rhymed with her own, and this recognition had been, in its way, the foundations. Not the abilities themselves but the *mutual sight* of them: the understanding that here was another woman for whom the body did something the body was not supposed to do, and that this doing had shaped a life into a specific and isolating form, and that perhaps two such forms, placed beside each other, might make a structure that could stand.

They had built that structure. Sixty-one years of it. The apartment was its expression — every worn path in the linoleum, every mug in its habitual position, every Tuesday with the pears and the sparrows and the light doing what the light did at 3:20 in the afternoon in late September, which was: entering the kitchen at an angle that turned the countertop the color of weak honey and laid a stripe across the floor that reached the leg of Ceil's chair — the empty chair, because Ceil was on the fire escape — at exactly the moment when the afternoon began to mean it.

Margot privately believed — and had believed for decades, with a conviction so fixed it had less the quality of a thought than of a bone — that she must be the one who survived. Not out of any wish for Ceil to die. The idea of Ceil's death was a door in Margot's mind that she approached only sideways, and only rarely, and only in the hours after three AM when the apartment ticked and Ceil breathed beside her with a thin, nasal, birdlike sound, and Margot would lie still and listen and think: *not yet.* Her wish to outlive Ceil was not morbid. It was structural. It was the conclusion of a logic she had followed to its end so many times that the path was grooved:

If Margot died first, Ceil would remember it.

Not the way a person remembers a death — the blur, the shock, the slow retreat into a version of the event that time has mercifully simplified. Ceil would remember it the way Ceil remembered everything: completely. The sight of Margot's body. The temperature of the room. The quality of the light. The sound, if there was a sound. The smell. The specific silence that follows the departure of a breath that is not going to return. Ceil would carry that scene at full resolution for every remaining day of her life, and it would not fade, and it would not soften, and in forty years — if Ceil lived another forty years, and who could say she wouldn't — the memory of Margot's death would be as vivid and as sensorially present as the moment it had happened. Ceil would, in essence, watch Margot die every day for the rest of her life. Not because she chose to. Because the door did not close.

Margot could not allow this. It was the nearest thing to a principle she had ever held: *I will carry the grief because my grief can erode.* Margot's memory was ordinary. Ordinary memory was an act of mercy she had only come to understand by loving a woman who had been denied it. Margot could grieve and the grieving would be terrible and then, slowly, with the imprecision and gentleness of a body that was in every other respect incapable of gentleness, her mind would do what minds do. It would blur. It would soften. It would replace the actual Ceil with a Ceil that was livable, a Ceil that Margot could hold without being burned. This was not disloyalty. This was the gift that ordinary consciousness extends to those who have lost someone: the slow, kind murder of accuracy in the service of survival.

And so Margot, standing at the kitchen counter at 3:22 PM with pear juice on her thumb, glanced toward the window where Ceil's shoulder and the edge of the cardigan were visible against the iron railing, and the thought that passed behind her eyes was a thought she had thought so often it no longer announced itself as a thought: *I will be here after. I will be the one who stays. And I will forget just enough to bear it.*
