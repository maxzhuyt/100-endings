# Elara Graves at the Territorial Normal School

## I.

I will tell you what was there.

The Territorial Normal School of —— sat eleven miles east of the railroad terminus on a quarter-section of mixed-grass prairie that had been, in sequence, hunting ground, treaty land, ceded land, railroad grant, and repossessed public trust parcel, each transition accomplished by documents I would later learn to admire for their formal beauty if not their relationship to fact. The main building was a two-story frame structure whose second story existed on its eastern and northern faces but not yet on its western, so that from certain angles the school appeared to be a finished institution and from others a thing still deciding whether to exist. The southern wall of the primary classroom terminated at a point roughly seven feet above the foundation in a ragged margin of raw pine and tar paper, open to the October sky like a letter someone had begun and abandoned mid-sentence. Through this aperture the prairie grass was visible, and the wind entered freely, and during my first week I watched a red-tailed hawk settle on the exposed crossbeam and survey us with the dispassionate interest of an auditor.

I arrived on the fourth of October, 1887, by stage from the railroad, carrying a trunk, a carpetbag, and documents. The documents were these: a letter of admission signed by Principal Horace T. Calloway, whose title was listed as "Principal and Superintendent of Territorial Normal Instruction" though I would learn that no territorial office had yet ratified the second designation; a certificate of completion from the mission school at ——, where I had studied for six years under Presbyterian women who taught me to parse sentences and keep my hands visible on the desk at all times; a letter of recommendation from the Reverend Daniel Whipple, who wrote that I possessed "an intellect of unusual capacity, a moral character above reproach, and a command of English composition that would do credit to any student of the fairer sex and more favored circumstances," this final clause being the Reverend's way of telling whoever read it what I was without telling them what I was; and, folded inside the lining of the carpetbag where it would not be found unless I chose to unfold it, my mother's name — Tȟašína Ité Wašté, Her Good Blanket Face — written in my mother's hand on a piece of deerhide no larger than a playing card.

I did not include my mother's name on the application. I included my father's: Graves. Marcus Graves. A white man from eastern Ohio who had come west to survey land, married a Lakota woman in a ceremony the territorial courts would later rule had no legal standing, and died of typhus when I was nine, leaving me his surname and his meticulous habit of recording things in writing, which is the only inheritance that has ever been honored in full.

The registrar was a young man with a waxed mustache and a ledger book so new its spine cracked when he opened it. I gave him my letter of admission. He read it, looked at me, read it again. I am familiar with this sequence. It is the administrative equivalent of checking a map against a landscape and finding the landscape to be, in some essential way, incorrectly located.

"Miss Graves," he said.

"Yes."

He looked at my hair, which is black and heavy and which I wore in the fashion prescribed by the mission school, pinned and netted, though it resists netting the way certain rivers resist damming — it can be done, but the river remembers what it was. He looked at my face, which is my mother's face, which is to say it is a face that answers questions I have not been asked.

"Your letter of admission is in order," he said, in the tone of a man who wished it were not.

"Yes," I said. "It is."

He wrote my name in the ledger. I watched him write it. I am a woman who watches people write things down, because I have learned that what is written can be unwritten, but it is harder to unwrite a thing that someone else has seen being written. This is not a principle I learned from my father. It is a principle I learned from watching what happened to people who had my mother's face and did not watch.

He assigned me a room in the women's dormitory, which was the first floor of a building whose second floor was indicated by the architectural plans tacked to the wall of the principal's office but which, as a matter of physical fact, did not exist. My room had a window that faced west. Through it I could see the grassland running uninterrupted to the horizon, the bluestem and switchgrass tawny with autumn, moving in the wind with the slow, rolling patience of something that has been here long enough to know that most things built on top of it are temporary.

I unpacked my trunk. I hung my two dresses on the nails provided. I set my books on the plank shelf: Wickersham's *Methods of Instruction*, the McGuffey Fourth Reader, Baldwin's *Art of School Management*, a clothbound notebook in which I had already begun recording observations of the type I am recording now.

I did not unpack my mother's name. I left it in the carpetbag.

Outside, the wind moved through the gap where the southern classroom wall should have been, and the grass leaned and recovered, leaned and recovered. The school's charter, which I had read in the copy posted beside the registrar's desk, listed eight departments: Pedagogy, English Language, Mathematics, Natural Philosophy, Moral Philosophy, History, Physical Culture, and Music. Of these, four had instructors. Two had dedicated rooms. The Music department consisted of a single upright piano that had been moved three times in two months because no room would claim it permanently, so that its current location — in the hallway between the principal's office and the supply closet, which were the same room — had the quality of a territorial dispute resolved by exhaustion rather than agreement.

I noted all of this. I note all of it now. I will tell you what was there because I have learned that no one else will, and after the last document has been misfiled and the last committee has adjourned, the only record that does not dissolve is the one a person carries in the unassailable country of her own mind.

The school was not yet finished. This is what I believed when I arrived: that it was not yet finished. That I had come to be part of its completion.

This is what I want you to understand before I tell you the rest. I believed it. The half-built walls were evidence of progress. The empty departments were yet to be filled. The charter was a blueprint, and I was a woman who understood blueprints, because my father had made them — precise, scaled, convertible into real things that stood on real ground. I looked at the Territorial Normal School and saw a building going up.

I want you to hold that, what I believed, the way you would hold a document entered into evidence. I want you to have seen it clearly. Because I will not say it was taken from me. The truth is more specific than that, and I have promised to be specific. What I believed was not taken. It was revealed, by degrees, to have no corresponding object in the world — the way my father once described surveying a boundary that had been platted on a map but never staked on the ground, so that the line existed in the jurisdiction of ink but not of dirt.

There was no building going up.

But I did not know that yet, and the not-knowing was, for a time, indistinguishable from hope, and I will not pretend it was not sweet.

## II.

The examination in Principles of Pedagogy was held on the sixteenth of November, a Saturday, in the primary classroom, which had by then acquired a canvas tarpaulin over its open southern wall, so that we sat in a light the color of weak tea and wrote our essays in the sound of canvas snapping like a living thing trying to free itself from the frame. There were fourteen of us. The prompt, written on the board by Professor Alden Marsh in a hand that betrayed either great haste or a fundamental uncertainty about how letters are formed, was this: *Describe the proper method by which a teacher of the common school should introduce the concept of written fractions to students of the third grade, with attention to sequence of instruction, materials required, and common errors of understanding.*

I completed my essay in forty minutes. The examination period was ninety. I used the remaining fifty to review my work, correct two infelicities of phrasing, and observe my classmates.

I will name the ones who matter. Ruth Delahunt, from Omaha, whose father was a judge of the territorial court and whose handwriting was exquisite and whose ideas, set down in that exquisite hand, were unfailingly conventional. Samuel Oren, thin-shouldered, from a farm near ——, who wanted to teach because he could not farm, and who worked with the grim diligence of a man aware that he was building his life out of the only materials available. Clara Bell Hutchins, who was clever and knew it and who regarded me with the particular fixed attention of a person encountering a problem she had not expected to find on the examination.

Professor Marsh collected the papers. On Monday he returned them, and if I tell you what happened next it is not from vanity but from the same impulse that compels a land surveyor to record the coordinates of a benchmark: because if the marker is later moved, there must be a record of where it stood.

He read my essay aloud.

He did not tell the class it was mine, at first. He said, "I would like to read an answer that demonstrates what I consider an exemplary command of both pedagogical method and the English language." He read it. The essay described a sequence of instruction beginning with physical objects — an apple divided, a length of string cut — before moving to numerical abstraction, and it addressed the specific cognitive error by which children confuse the fraction bar with a signal to perform division in the ordinary sense, and it proposed a method, which I had devised myself, of using shaded diagrams to make visible the relationship between the part and the whole.

When he finished reading, he said, "This was written by Miss Graves."

Fourteen heads turned. Thirteen, because I did not turn toward myself. I met their eyes. I have found that this matters — that there is a specific, measurable difference between the person who meets eyes and the person who does not, and that the difference is a matter not of courage but of information. I wanted to know what I would see.

What I saw: Ruth Delahunt's face performing a rapid adjustment, like a compass needle swinging to find a north it did not expect to be located in that direction. Samuel Oren's expression of unsurprised respect, which was the kindest thing anyone offered me that semester and which I record here because Samuel Oren deserves to have his decency noted, in case he is still alive and in case the world has not burned it out of him. Clara Bell Hutchins's eyes narrowing by a degree measurable only by someone who, like me, has made a study of the small facial movements by which white people metabolize the fact of a person like me performing above the level they have privately established as my ceiling.

"Well done, Miss Graves," Professor Marsh said. He paused. "An unusual approach. But sound."

Unusual. I noted the word. I did not yet file it. I placed it in the category of things that might be nothing and might be the first stitch in a pattern not yet visible.

In December I corrected the textbook. This was in English Composition, taught by Mrs. Alice Greenway, who had come from Illinois and who possessed a copy of Quackenbos's *Advanced Course of Composition and Rhetoric* so thoroughly annotated in her own hand that the margins resembled a secondary text engaged in a running argument with the first. On page 214, Quackenbos asserted that the subjunctive mood is properly employed only in conditions contrary to fact and in formal expressions of wish or command. I raised my hand and observed that this was incomplete. The subjunctive, I said, is also required in clauses of concession following *though* or *although* when the conceded condition is hypothetical rather than actual, and in noun clauses following verbs of recommendation or insistence, as in "I insist that he *be* present" rather than "I insist that he *is* present," and that Quackenbos's omission was not trivial because it produced, in students who trusted the textbook absolutely, a systematic error in formal written English that would mark their prose as uneducated in precisely the contexts where education mattered most.

Mrs. Greenway looked at the page. She looked at me.

"Where did you learn that, Miss Graves?"

"From reading, Mrs. Greenway."

She considered this for a moment, then said, "She's right," and made a note in her already-overcrowded margin, and if there was something in her voice — a faint surprise, a recalibration — I recorded it but did not remark upon it, because I was not yet in the business of remarking upon things. I was in the business of being right and having witnesses.

In January I gave my first practice lesson. This was required of all students in the teaching track: a demonstration of competence before the assembled class, observed and graded by two faculty. I was assigned to teach a fifteen-minute lesson on the geography of the Missouri River watershed to an imaginary class of fifth-grade students, this imaginary class being played by my actual classmates, who were instructed to respond as children would.

I taught the lesson. I drew the watershed on the board from memory — the Missouri and its tributaries, the Platte, the Niobrara, the White, the Cheyenne — in a map my father would have recognized as his own method, starting from the main channel and branching outward, because my father taught me that you map a river system the way you read a story, by following the central current and noting where it divides. I named the tributaries. I described the watershed as a system, a living catchment, a country of water defined not by political boundaries but by the direction rain falls and where it goes when it reaches the ground. I asked my classmates, in their roles as fifth-graders, to tell me which direction the water flowed, and when Clara Bell Hutchins said "south," I guided the class through the correction — "Look at the map; does the Missouri run south from here, or does it run southeast? And what is the difference? The difference is that south takes the water to Kansas, and southeast takes it to St. Louis, and those are not the same place, and in geography, as in most things, the direction you are actually going matters more than the direction you assume."

The room was quiet for a moment.

Professor Marsh wrote something on his grading sheet. Mrs. Greenway wrote something on hers. Ruth Delahunt studied the map I had drawn with the expression of a person who has just been shown a document she cannot contest but would prefer not to have seen.

I received the highest marks of any practice lesson given that term. I know this because I asked, and because the grades were posted on a board outside the principal's office-supply closet, and because I stood before that board and read the names and numbers with the care of a woman reading a survey plat, confirming that the boundaries were where she had staked them.

I am telling you these things — the essay, the correction, the lesson, the marks — with the fidelity of a person constructing a record. I did not know, during those months, that I was constructing a record. I believed I was simply doing what I had come to do: learning, demonstrating, earning. I believed the marks on the board outside the principal's office were *entries* — that they went into a ledger, and the ledger accrued, and when the ledger was full I would be given what the ledger entitled me to, which was a certificate, which was a piece of paper, which was a key, which would open the door of a classroom somewhere in the territory where I would stand before children and teach them the things I knew, which were considerable, which were documented, which were *real.*

I believed in the ledger.

Do you understand? I need you to understand this the way you understand that a woman standing on railroad tracks believes the ground beneath her feet is solid. The ground was solid. It was the train that was coming.

But that is not yet what I am telling you. What I am telling you now is only that I was the best student the Territorial Normal School had, and that I can prove it, and that the proof is in the record, and that the record is in my mind, where it cannot be misfiled.

Let me tell you about the weather shifting, though, since I have promised to tell you everything. February. The classmate — I will not give her name, because unlike the good things, the small cruelties do not deserve to be landmarked — who said, "It must be different, where you come from. The standards, I mean." She said it quietly, the way a person drops a lit match into dry grass — not to cause a fire, exactly, but not *not* to cause a fire. I said, "I come from the mission school at ——, where the standards were sufficient to produce the highest-scoring student in this institution. Where do you come from?" She did not answer.
