# Store Closing

## I.

My hand was inside the woman's bag — three fingers past the zipper, thumb braced against the clasp of a wallet that was, from touch alone, genuine calfskin and therefore worth the trouble — when the front doors chained shut and three men in Halloween masks stepped onto the marble landing by the escalators.

The masks were the first wrong thing. A werewolf, a Richard Nixon, and something I think was supposed to be a cat but had been stored folded so long the ears had creased sideways, giving the third man the appearance of a bewildered terrier. The second wrong thing was the guns. One was real — a snub-nosed revolver, blued steel, held by the werewolf in a grip that said he'd fired it before but not often and never at anything that could shoot back. The second was either a sawed-off shotgun or a length of pipe with aspirations. The third man, the terrier-cat, was holding what I'm almost certain was a flare gun, though I will allow for the possibility that it was a caulking tool. I've been wrong before. Not often, but I've been wrong.

The alarm went off. High and bright and then, four seconds later, gone — cut at the panel by someone who knew where the panel was, which meant one of these three had worked here or been inside recently enough to walk a staff hallway. The werewolf. He moved toward the panel without looking. Muscle memory. You learn to recognize it. The feet know the building even when the head is elsewhere.

I froze. Not fear — inventory. A pickpocket in a new room does the same thing a rat does in a new cage: finds the walls, finds the openings, finds the other rats. The walls were marble, old Memphis department-store marble, the kind they don't pour anymore. Two floors visible from where I stood. The openings were, as of eight seconds ago, none. Front doors chained. Fire exit to the east — I'd clocked it on the way in, as I always do, the way other people clock restrooms — now blocked by the terrier-cat, who was leaning against the push bar with the weary authority of a man who'd been assigned a door and intended to hold it. Freight elevator to the west, but freight elevators need keys, and the only person who might have one was the sales associate behind the Estée Lauder counter, whose hands were already in the air and whose key ring was, from the sound it made when she trembled, still on her hip. I filed that.

The other rats. Five of them, not counting the sales associate, who was furniture — no offense to her, but a person with their hands up and their eyes closed has opted out of the situation and is, for my purposes, a display case that breathes.

On the leather bench by the elevator bank, a woman was asleep. Not pretending. Not in shock. Asleep. Her mouth was slightly open and her breathing had the deep, even cadence of someone who had been unconscious before the robbery began and whose body had not seen fit to inform her of the change in circumstances. She was wearing slacks, a blazer with a bulge at the left hip where a holster wasn't but recently had been, and shoes that were county-issued if I've ever seen county-issued, which I have, usually from the other side of a booking desk. Her hands were large. Her nails were short. There was a callus on the inside of her right thumb that I knew from experience — not my experience, but the experience of several acquaintances who are no longer acquaintances — came from drawing a weapon thousands of times. Off-duty law. Sleeping. I moved her from the "threat" column to the "unpredictable" column, which is worse.

Behind the Chanel counter, in the position the sales associate had abandoned to go be furniture, an older woman was sitting on a stool with posture so upright it looked structural, as though her spine were a load-bearing element of the building and the ceiling would sag if she slouched. She was sixty, maybe sixty-five. Gray hair in a judicial bun — and I use the word *judicial* with specificity because I have been in front of enough judges to recognize the way they hold their heads, which is slightly tilted, not in curiosity but in assessment, the way you tilt a glass to see if there's anything left in it. Her handbag was enormous, far too large for a woman of her discipline, which meant it held something specific. The bag clinked when she set it on the counter. Not glass. Not metal. Wood on wood. I didn't know what it was yet. I would.

Ten feet to my left, in the territory between Cosmetics and Women's Evening, a woman in sequined flats and a blouse the color of an ambitious sunset was speaking to the air where the sales associate had been. She had not yet registered the robbery. She was holding an open compact and a foundation brush and she was saying, "I have been telling your people for six months that this shade is not Golden Beige, this shade is Hepatitis Yellow, and if you think I'm going to put Hepatitis Yellow on thirty-seven girls at regionals you have lost your—" She stopped. She looked up. She saw the masks. She looked at the compact. She looked at the masks again. She closed the compact with a click that was, in the new silence of the room, unreasonably loud. Then she said, "Well."

And by the fire exit on the east wall — not the one the terrier-cat was guarding but the other one, the one that led to the loading dock, the one I'd marked as secondary — a man in a gray suit was already writing. He had produced a pen and several sheets of the store's letterhead from somewhere, and he was writing with a speed and focus that was either panic or its opposite. His handwriting, from what I could see at twelve feet, was small and mercilessly neat. He was not looking at the robbers. He was looking at the letterhead. His suit was the gray of institutional certainty. His tie was the blue of a form you fill out when something has gone wrong. He was, and I knew this the way I know a wallet from a phone case by touch alone, an insurance man. Claims. Adjustments. The math of disaster. He was already calculating the loss ratio of the moment, and the moment was four seconds old.

I looked at all of them. I looked at the three men in masks. I looked at the chained doors and the dead alarm panel and the overhead lights buzzing their fluorescent indifference.

Then I looked at my hand, which was still inside the tourist's bag, still touching the calfskin wallet.

I put it back. Gently. The way you put a sleeping cat back on a couch. I even re-zipped the pocket.

Professional courtesy. You don't steal in a burning building. Not because of morality — I want to be clear about that — but because a burning building changes the math. In a burning building, every person is a variable, and a variable with a missing wallet is a variable that's looking at you instead of looking at the fire. I needed every set of eyes in this room pointed at the werewolf and his crew. I needed to be invisible, which is what I am, which is what I've always been. The wallet could wait. Everything can wait. That's the first thing you learn.

The second thing you learn is how to read a room, and this room was reading like a book where every chapter was written by a different author and none of them had spoken to each other and the binding was coming apart.

The werewolf raised his revolver. His hand was trembling. Not much. Not enough for anyone at distance to notice. But I was close enough, and I am professionally attuned to hands — their contents, their tensions, their tells — and that tremble was the tremble of a man who had rehearsed this moment many times in a bathroom mirror and was now discovering that the mirror had lied about how it would feel. I have seen that tremble. I have had that tremble, the first time I reached into a stranger's coat on a crowded bus at fourteen and felt the stranger's body heat through the lining and understood that I was crossing something that did not have a crossing back. The werewolf's tremble said: *I am here, and I cannot believe I am here, and I cannot leave here because leaving here would mean admitting I came here, and so I will stand here and hold this gun and hope that the gun knows what to do because I certainly do not.*

I knew that tremble. I knew that man without knowing him. And I knew, in the way I know which pocket holds the phone and which holds the cash, that this robbery was going to go wrong in ways none of the people in this room — captors or captives — were presently equipped to imagine.

## II.

The werewolf stepped forward. He stood at the top of the escalator landing — the escalators had stopped when the alarm cut, mid-stride, their steel teeth frozen in an expression of permanent mild surprise — and he pulled a piece of paper from inside his jacket.

He read from it.

I have been present for a number of speeches in my life. I have listened to public defenders summarize my character for judges who were not interested. I have heard street preachers on Beale explain the topology of Hell with the confidence of men who'd been given a personal tour. I once heard a man on a Greyhound bus deliver a forty-minute extemporaneous lecture on the mineral composition of the moon that was, by any objective measure, the most organized and persuasive piece of rhetoric I have ever witnessed, and also completely wrong.

None of these prepared me for the demands.

"This is a — this is a robbery," the werewolf said, looking at his paper. "All persons in the, uh, premises are hereby ordered to — wait. Okay." He turned the paper sideways. "All persons in the premises are hereby *notified* that the undersigned parties have entered into the following *dispute* — no, that's the wrong — Kyle, is this the right page?"

The terrier-cat, from the fire exit, called back, "It's the only page."

"It says 'dispute resolution' at the top."

"Just read it, Deshawn."

The werewolf — Deshawn, apparently, so much for operational security — returned to the paper. "The undersigned parties request the following remedies. One: full access to the secure vault located on the premises, sub-basement level. Two: a formal written apology from Keith — Keith Rendell, it says here — regarding the incident of March fourteenth. Three: compensation for emotional damages not to exceed—" He squinted. "Kyle, what's this number?"

"It says fifty thousand."

"This says five hundred thousand."

"There's too many zeros."

"You typed it!"

"I used a template!"

"What template?"

"From the internet! It's a professional letter!"

"This is a DISPUTE RESOLUTION LETTER, Kyle. This is what you send to a LANDLORD."

The room held its breath. Not out of fear. Out of genuine confusion. I watched the hostages' faces cycle through terror into bewilderment and stall there, like cars that had driven off a cliff and were waiting for gravity to make a decision.

The Richard Nixon mask had said nothing. He held the pipe-or-shotgun with both hands and shifted his weight from foot to foot and I could not tell if he was nervous or if he simply needed to urinate. Both, possibly. The two are not as different as people think.

The judge, behind the Chanel counter, had gone very still. Her head was tilted at three degrees. I know that tilt. I have been on the receiving end of that tilt, and it precedes a sentence.

But before anyone could respond — before the sheriff could wake up or the coach could comment or the adjuster could finish whatever he was writing — Deshawn folded the paper, put it back in his jacket, and said, "Just — nobody move. We want the vault. And we want Keith. And if you don't—" He raised the revolver, and the tremble was worse now, visible from twenty feet, and his voice cracked on the last word and he did not finish the sentence because he did not know how to finish the sentence. He turned and walked behind a pillar and was gone.

Nixon and the terrier-cat exchanged a look that was, despite being filtered through rubber and creased polyester, clearly the look of two men who have followed a third man into a building and are only now asking themselves why.

I watched. I catalogued. I waited.

And I read, over the shoulder of the adjuster — not because I intended to, but because he was writing in letters large enough to read from space and I have never been able to not read a thing placed in front of me, a failing that is adjacent to my profession but not, technically, part of it — the following:
