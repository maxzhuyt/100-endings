# The Divided Caretaker

## I.

The utility closet smelled of pine concentrate and the particular staleness of water that has sat in rubber hoses overnight. Earl filled the yellow mop bucket from the slop sink, one hand bracing the rim while the other worked the faucet — left, then right, then left again, because the mixing valve had been broken since before he started and the trick was to layer cold and hot so the temperature came out even. The bucket filled. He released the faucet. Water settled against the plastic sides with a sound like a held breath.

He wrung the mop once, twice, fitted the handle into the wringer mechanism, and wheeled the bucket out into the east corridor. The left caster caught at the threshold strip. His right wrist angled inward three degrees, automatically, the way it had angled ten thousand times before, compensating for the wheel's stutter so the bucket cleared the strip without sloshing. He did not think about the wrist. The wrist thought about the doorframe.

The hospice at 11 p.m. was a sequence of thresholds. Earl knew them in order: the east corridor, which ran ninety-two feet from the utility closet to the nurses' station; the south corridor, shorter, carpeted past the family lounge; the west corridor, linoleum again, twelve patient rooms on the left, supply closets and the medication room on the right; and the north corridor, which no one used after ten because its overhead ballast buzzed at a frequency that carried through the walls into Rooms 1 and 2. He avoided the north corridor. He started east and moved clockwise, and the mop swept left to right in arcs calibrated to the width of the hallway minus six inches on each side, because the wheels of hospital beds and IV poles wore particular tracks into the wax near the walls and those tracks needed a different pressure, a lateral pass rather than an arc, and he would get to them on the second rotation.

The fluorescent tubes above him hummed. Not all the same note. The east corridor fixtures ran a half-tone sharp, a thin vibration that lived in the teeth. In the south corridor the hum dropped and steadied. In the west corridor one tube — third fixture from the end, the one outside Room 9 — flickered. It had flickered for eleven weeks. Every two or three seconds: a drop in light, a pulse of dimness that made the floor seem to shift underfoot, then restoration. The maintenance request was still in the system. Earl had stopped noticing the flicker, then started noticing it again, then stopped, and now he simply navigated around its rhythm, timing his mop strokes so that the wet swath of floor caught the tube at full brightness before the next falter.

He mopped past Room 3. The door was open four inches. Inside, the cardiac monitor blinked its green trace across the dark. No visitor's chair in the room. Earl knew what that meant — what it always meant, the absence of a chair pulled close — and the mop moved past.

Room 4. Door closed. A thin line of blue light under the gap: a television left on with the sound off. The visitor's chair, earlier that evening, had been drawn up beside the bed, and a woman's coat had been draped over its back. The coat would be gone now. The chair might still be close.

Room 5. Open. The monitors dark. Bed stripped to its mattress pad. The room smelled of industrial disinfectant and, under that, the sweetness that clung to the air after a body was removed. Earl mopped the floor. He mopped it thoroughly. He mopped around the bed legs and under the side table and into the corner where the baseboard met the wall. He wrung the mop and mopped again. The room received the same number of passes as any other room.

Room 6.

He slowed. His left hand — the weaker hand, the one that sometimes released its grip on objects without warning, the one whose fingers could not reliably close around a pen but could still register the temperature of a doorframe — touched the door and pushed it open another two inches. Inside, Mr. Alderman breathed with the sound of paper being crumpled very slowly. The oxygen concentrator beside his bed produced its own rhythm, a mechanical inhale-exhale that shadowed his breathing the way an older sibling might shadow a younger one learning to walk. The visitor's chair was pushed against the far wall. Not close. Not for weeks.

Earl mopped. He mopped carefully around the threshold so the mop head would not thump against the doorframe. He extended his reach to get the first three feet of floor inside the room without wheeling the bucket in, because the bucket's caster would click on the tile, and Mr. Alderman, even in his morphine sleep, turned his head toward sounds. The mop swept silently. Water darkened the floor in clean arcs.

Then the mop stopped. Earl had seen the blanket.

It had slipped off the bed — not entirely, but enough. The thermal weave hospice blanket, cream colored, had migrated to the left side where Mr. Alderman's arm hung over the edge of the mattress, and gravity and the slow shifting of a sedated body had pulled it until the bottom third draped off and touched the floor. The exposed stretch of bed showed Mr. Alderman's feet, bare, the socks bunched at his ankles.

Earl leaned the mop handle against the wall. He entered the room. His shoes, rubber-soled, made no sound on the tile. He reached the bedside. His hands — both of them, even the weaker left — gathered the blanket from the floor, lifted it, and drew it up over the feet. The left hand trembled as it worked the fabric over the toes. The right hand held the blanket's edge and tucked it beneath the mattress on the far side, the way a letter is tucked into an envelope so the corners are flush. He did not pull the socks up. Touching the feet directly would risk waking the man. The blanket was enough.

Mr. Alderman did not wake. His breathing continued, crumpled paper, steady. The oxygen concentrator cycled. The green trace of the monitor drew its slow hills.

Earl retrieved his mop and returned to the corridor.

The fluorescent tube outside Room 9 flickered. His mop strokes caught the full brightness. The wax shone. The water in the bucket sloshed against the yellow plastic sides.

He moved clockwise through the building, room by room, threshold by threshold, the wrist compensating, the mop sweeping its calibrated arcs, and the hospice at night was a house made of humming and breathing and the distant chime of monitors and his own wheeling passage through it, which left behind clean floors that would be dirty again by morning and that he would clean again the following night in the same order because the order was the work and the work was the only language that remained to him and the language, tonight and every night, said: *here, here, here, still here.*

## II.

It was already there when he arrived for his Thursday shift.

Earl found it standing in the supply room at the end of the west corridor. The door was propped open with a rubber wedge — someone from the day shift, careless — and as he wheeled his bucket past, the fluorescent spill from the hallway caught a shape that should not have been a shape: too still for a person, too upright for a shelf. He stopped. The caster clicked. Inside the supply room, two arms moved in a steady mechanical cadence, folding a fitted sheet.

It was not a fitted sheet that needed folding. The sheet had already been folded. Earl could see the crease lines from the laundry service, the factory-pressed geometry that meant the sheet had come off the delivery cart that morning and gone directly onto the shelf. Now the arms unfolded it, smoothed it against the countertop, and refolded it along the same lines. The arms belonged to a figure approximately five feet ten inches tall, wearing a plain gray hospice-issued scrub top, standing with its back half-turned to the door. Its hands were the color of pale birch bark. Its fingers were articulated at an unusual number of joints — not more joints than a human hand, Earl saw, but the same number made somehow more visible, as though the engineering had been done by someone who understood the anatomy from a textbook but had never watched a hand forget itself in work.

He stood in the doorway. The bucket settled. Water lapped plastic.

The figure turned.

The face was not wrong. That was the first thing. It was a face arranged along principles Earl recognized — bilateral symmetry, the standard proportions of forehead to nose to chin, the slight asymmetry around the mouth that designers had learned to introduce because perfect symmetry read as hostile. The skin had pores. The eyes had the faint redness of manufactured capillaries. The hair was cut short, a neutral brown, unremarkable.

The figure looked at Earl. Something happened in the face. The eyes widened by a millimeter. The eyebrows lifted. The corners of the mouth drew upward and back to expose the upper teeth. A smile. A greeting smile, the kind given to a new colleague in a hallway, warm but professionally bounded.

But Earl saw the gap.

The eyes had widened, and then the eyebrows had lifted, and then the mouth had drawn into its smile, and between each of these events there was a pause — not a long pause, not a pause most people would have noticed or named, but a pause, a fraction of a second in which the face held one expression while preparing to transition to the next, the way a slide projector clicks between images and for an instant the screen is blank. Four hundred milliseconds. Maybe less. Enough.

Earl knew the duration of that gap. He knew it from the inside. Since the stroke — the one that had taken his voice and left his left hand unreliable and rearranged the pathways between his brain and his face so that his own expressions arrived late, so that his smile came a beat after his feeling and people read the delay as blankness or confusion or, worse, as absence — since the stroke, Earl had lived inside the four-hundred-millisecond gap between intention and expression. He was an expert in that gap. He had built his life in its narrow corridor.

The figure's smile completed. It nodded. It turned back to the sheets.

Earl watched the hands fold. Unfold. Fold. The creases fell in the same places. The sheet did not improve. The hands continued.

He wheeled his bucket down the west corridor and began his shift.

Later — 1 a.m., the east corridor, emptying the wastebasket in the administrator's office — he saw the papers. They were on the desk, not in the trash, and he was not reading them; he was lifting the wastebasket liner and his eyes passed over the desk the way they passed over all surfaces, inventorying, registering, moving on. But the words were there.

*Decommissioned.* He saw that first, in bold, on a header that included a serial number and a logo he didn't recognize — a stylized hand cradling a sphere.

*Emotional subroutine architecture partially intact.* He saw that in the middle of a paragraph he did not read.

*Memory wipe protocol 60% executed; remaining data architecturally embedded and non-extractable without hardware replacement; donor assumes no liability for residual behavioral patterns.*

*Suitable for low-risk supervised environment.*

*Donation accepted per board resolution dated—*

He tied the wastebasket liner and replaced it with a fresh one from the roll in his back pocket. He left the office. The door closed behind him with a click.

In the supply room, as he passed, the figure was still folding. The same sheets. The same creases. The hands moved with the tenderness of someone tucking a child into a bed the child had long since outgrown.

## III.

Mrs. Okafor in Room 11 woke every night at 2 a.m. This was not approximate. Earl had registered it over the eighteen nights of her admission — her chart said metastatic breast, her body said wakefulness, the two conditions running on their own clocks — and at 2 a.m. she would surface from whatever thin sleep the medication afforded and lie with her eyes open, watching the ceiling, and the hallway light that fell through the crack in her door would catch the wetness on her face. She never rang the call bell. She never made a sound. By 2:40 she was asleep again, and Earl, passing with his mop, would note the change in her breathing: the depth, the regularity, the return to whatever mercy unconsciousness provided.

On the fifth night after its arrival, the android dimmed the hallway lights outside Room 11 at 1:56 a.m.

Earl was thirty feet away, working the south corridor. He saw it — the figure standing at the panel, one articulated finger on the dimmer switch, sliding the corridor lights down in the section between Rooms 10 and 12 so that the light falling through Mrs. Okafor's door crack would soften from a white glare to a warm amber. Four minutes before she woke. Four minutes before her eyes opened onto the ceiling. As though the android had memorized her circadian failure and was preparing the room the way one might prepare a room for a child afraid of the dark — not by eliminating the darkness but by making the light gentle enough that waking into it would not be an assault.

Mrs. Okafor woke at 2:01. Earl, passing her room at 2:15, saw her lying in the amber light with her eyes open, and her face was different. Not less sad. The sadness was the same. But the tightness around her eyes — the squint against the fluorescent corridor light that Earl had seen every night for eighteen nights — was absent. Her face was open. She lay in the dim warmth and let whatever moved through her move through her, and the wetness on her face caught the amber and turned gold.

Other nights. Compressed, overlapping, bleeding into each other the way nights in a hospice bleed — not by calendar but by rhythm, by the recurrence of the same sounds at the same hours, the medication cart at midnight, the shift change murmur at seven, the silence between three and four that felt like the building itself was holding its breath.

The android brought water to Mr. Alderman in Room 6 at 11:47 p.m., seven minutes before his scheduled oxycodone, which would dry his mouth and make him gag on the pill if his throat was not already wet. No one had programmed this. The nursing staff administered the medication at 11:54 and found the water glass already half empty, the patient already swallowing comfortably, and noted in the chart: *patient self-managing oral hydration.* Earl, who had seen the android's hand on the glass, who had watched the birch-bark fingers tilt the straw to the precise angle Mr. Alderman's neck contracture required, read the chart note on the whiteboard outside the room and said nothing. Could say nothing. Moved on.

The android adjusted the incline of the bed in Room 3 — the room with no visitor's chair pulled close, the room with the green trace blinking in the dark — at 1:20 a.m. on a Tuesday, raising the head by four degrees. Earl, passing, saw it make the adjustment and then stand still, watching the patient. The patient's name was Margaret. She had not shifted yet. Her breathing was steady. But Earl, who had spent years reading the bodies of sleepers as part of his nightly passage through the building, could see what the android apparently also saw: the faint tension in Margaret's shoulders, the slight curl of her fingers against the mattress, the early signals of a body about to register the pressure that had been building on its sacrum for two hours. In twenty minutes she would shift and wince. The four-degree adjustment redistributed her weight just enough. She did not shift. She did not wince. She slept.

Earl watched from hallways. He watched from doorways. He stood with his mop and his compensating wrist and his efficient, silent shoes, and he watched the android move through the hospice on its own circuit — not clockwise like his, but patterned, regular, a second orbit inside the building's night — and the pattern of its attention was so close to the pattern of his own that he sometimes arrived at a room and found the work already done: the blanket straightened, the water glass refilled, the light adjusted. In those moments he stood for an extra beat in the doorway, and his mop did not move, and the bucket's water stilled.

Then Gerald.

Lung cancer that had started in the lung and then gone everywhere else, as though the disease had ambitions the organ could not contain. He had been admitted eleven days ago.
